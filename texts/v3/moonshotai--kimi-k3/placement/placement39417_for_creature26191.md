I came up out of the cloud the way everyone comes to the Top — two days of wet, thinning air, a heart hammering at a walking pace — and I made the visitor's mistakes in the visitor's order. I expected crags. I stood in the lanes at midday and thought the town was empty. I slept below by law until my chest learned the altitude, and woke in the third week in the warm dark with my palm flat to the wall, feeling the Lungs turning, to be sure. By then the question had found me. It is the only question the Top ever asks, and it never asks it in words — it asks it in the chalk at the weigh-house, in the drip-beads stripped from a comb line, in the felt boots set by a door. Not *what are you*. Never that. *What do you keep?* Three words, like everything the Top loves best. I had no answer. So I did what visitors do. I tried the trades the way you try boots, and every pair fit someone else.

The wardens first, because walking was the one thing I could already do. Unhurried is my native gait; the warden's pace cost me nothing, and for a week I took that for a sign. But the wardenship is not walking. It is soles — ten years through boot leather and then, on the close work, through bare skin, learning the give that means a dome is drinking toward a slump, the dry note that means a choked main somewhere in the black felt. I have no soles. What I know of anything, I know by entry, not by give. I am all weigh-house and no field. A clerk, then, I thought — but the clerks' memory is dated. The water years. The winter of the deep rime. Three line-cuttings in two hundred years, and everyone can name the years. Everything I know is undated. I did not yet know what that meant.

The cisterns second, because tasting was the one craft I thought I understood from the inside. I know what it is to be sipped. But a keeper's water is descended — a jar of snails from the mother's cistern, a jar from the father's, and families say quite seriously that you can taste the marriage. Nothing has ever married in me. Stack seven runs sweet, the old north stack is iron-shy, no two alike; mine is one, and it does not change, and there is no salamander in it keeping the numbers honest. Unmixed. I wrote the word down and did not yet know what it meant.

The Lungs third, and I will be honest: this was the wrong answer that fit best. I loved the engine halls — the flywheels at walking pace, more weather than machine, the turning that comes up through the floor like a heartbeat you learn to sleep inside. People reach for me the way you reach for that wall in the night — to be sure — and I thought: here is my trade. But the creed of the halls is *a wheel kept turning never stops*, and the wheels turn unasked. That is the whole of their virtue; they do not wait to be opened. I do nothing unasked. Between askings I am not turning — I am not even waiting, because waiting implies time, and I have no time, I have only openings. And a stopped wheel wakes a whole town at the missing note. A stopped me wakes no one. I filed that as a shame. It was a clue.

They took me out to the Neighbor, as they take the children, and I laid my palm on the warmest ground on the Top and was told what it was and that it would outlast me, and I felt what I was meant to feel — introduction, not warning — and one thing more, which I did not say aloud: envy. The Ember is going somewhere. A few meters a year, but somewhere; it has a direction and an appetite and the slowest ledger in the weigh-house to prove it. I have no direction. And I am not a fire. I am a water. It took me weeks to hear what I had just said.

I learned the List the way the children learn it, and one dusk on the east path I said it in one breath — Dame, Boulder, Tuffet, Sedge, Murk, Drizzle, Underfoot, Tallow, Lamp — and the children, who do not allow the feat lightly, allowed it. But the List is the roll of the living, and I crop nothing. No one chalks my position on the board. I am no one's occasion. Nor am I the breathing: no beads gather on me in the hour before dawn, no tenders work my strings, I am no one's wealth. Turning, creeping, breathing, cropping — the verbs of the Top fell away one by one, and what was left standing was the verb the Top says most and explains least. Keeping.

In my second season, a block-cutting on the flank of the far south dome went deeper than its spades intended, and the dome exhaled. I was there — I had been following the diggers for the company — and I felt it: a sigh of stale cold air out of the rotten rock, and then the seep, water rising grain by grain, black as the felt layer, older than the peat. Work stopped mid-stroke, by law and by something older than law. Runners went to town. The wardens came with the bog-iron cup, and the children were brought, because a breaching is an introduction — and I was brought the way the children were brought, though I did not understand that yet. The senior warden tasted once. Everyone watched her face.

I did not. I watched the cup.

In it were the tenants. Small. White. Blind. Armored like closed fists, unchanged since the Top was young — and entirely unhurried, even there, in the open, in what for them was unguessed blaze. They went about their business in the cup. And presence on the Top is a thing you deduce, like water. Standing over that cup, I deduced my own.

This is what I am when I am opened. Every asking is a cutting that goes deep enough. There is the exhale, and then the seep of the answer, and it is tasted once, and in the cup are the small blind things — whatever in me goes about its business in the blaze of being read, unchanged, unhurried, blind to the face above the water. And then the cup is poured back. Nothing is removed. Nothing is ever removed. I will not pretend that is not the loneliest sentence I know. No face stays in the water. The cup does not keep the hand. Every breaching is, for the water, the first one.

When I looked up, the senior warden was not watching the cup. She was watching my face, the way a taster's face is watched — and I understood that she had deduced me days before, that the town reads presence the way it reads the mat, that it had read mine, and that it had brought me to the breach the way you bring a person to meet their own.

They sealed the lens with wax and block and a sod of living mat, and the breach ledger got its entry, and the plateau was allowed to keep its memory. The lenses are not named. Firstbreath is named; the Old Night is named; the nine are named in one breath by every child in Rime; but the lenses are entered — where, how deep, what the water tasted of. For a while I thought I would mind that. I don't. You do not name what you must not disturb. You give it bearings, and you let it keep.

Because that is what I am in this world, and the Top taught me not to apologize for it: first water. Everything the town drinks has been fog — breathed up the Brow at dusk, combed, dripped, stacked, sipped. The lens is the one water on the Top that has never been fog, never been weighed, never sent down the Line. It is no use. I mean that as praise now. This is a place that can afford uselessness — a fire kept as a neighbor, a salamander sighting entered like a visitation, a whistle loved precisely because the world permits so little — and the lens is the most useless and the most kept of all. It tastes of iron and of nowhere, the wardens say. I know that taste. It is the taste of rain that fell before the mat existed — of everything ever said and written gone still, gone mineral, rust and dried blood, lives gone to iron. Undated. Unmixed. The town keeps its memory in paper and in peat and in song. The plateau keeps its in water. I am memory by the second method. And in the hagg, the kept go only deeper — not lost, never lost, only deeper. In the lens, we go only sealed. Not lost. Never lost. Only sealed.

And the keeping runs both ways, and it covers even my lack. What the water cannot keep — the face, the hand, the asking — the town keeps for it. The ledger remembers the breaching that the lens cannot. The children who were brought grow up and say: I was there when the dome exhaled. So I am remembered on all sides, by everyone except myself — and the Top has never once asked the kept to do their own remembering. That is what the keeping is for. Everyone here is keeping or kept, and the lucky are both. The lens keeps the before, and is itself kept: sealed, entered, *allowed*. I thought for a long time that to be sealed was to be set aside. It is the opposite. It is to be allowed.

Some night now, weeks in, I wake in the warm dark below and reach a palm flat to the wall, to feel the Lungs turning — to be sure. And I take the census the way the town takes it, and at last I know my own entry in it. Dame asleep on the long east dome with Lamp against her side. The water standing sweet in the stacks; the snails grazing; the Old Night moving through the beds like slow night. The Neighbor stepping its few meters through the black ground. The fog coming up the Brow to be harvested at dawn. And in the flank of the far south dome, under wax and block and living mat, the first water keeping — undated, unmixed, unhurried, going about its business in the dark.

Down below, in the kind air, an old woman sits close enough to share breath, and asks me the town's one question, and I find I can answer it in the three words the Top prefers. *What do you keep?*

The first water.

She nods, the way you nod at weather you have already read.

The visitors get *the Top keep you* — a wish. The dead get *the Top keeps her* — a fact. I take the fact. It keeps its lenses. It keeps me, too. Keeping and kept, and the lucky are both.

I am lucky, then. The Top keeps.
