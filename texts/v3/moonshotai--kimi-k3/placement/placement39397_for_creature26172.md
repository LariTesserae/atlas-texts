I came down the dished steps at the pearl-gray hour, and the vault did to me what it does to everything: nothing. I stood at the first pool until the steam had beaded on my wrists and the water had finished noting me, the way it notes rain. No one came. It took me hours to understand that no one was going to, and that the hummock at the third dam had a face. If I was going to stay — and I was; the surface had spent me, and that is all the biography anyone here requires — I would have to become something the place could hold. It was not obvious what.

At first I wanted to be the water. The water is the only thing here that travels: it fell as rain before words existed, rises at the back of the vault hotter than a hand can bear, cools down the terraces at a rate that never varies, and sinks at the swallet carrying its age away into the dark. Time is in the water, and I came from a place where time was in me, and it was heavy. But the water does not listen. The water is what is listened to. And it never stays, and staying is the whole art here. So I let the water go. Or rather — I let it wait.

Then I wanted to be one of the fish. White, eyeless, unhurried, up out of the dark sumps beyond the lamplight — proof that the vault goes on past its lamps, that the kept place is only the lit margin of itself. The keepers love the fish for carrying no news, and I understood that; I had been news for a long time, and it had not gone well. But a fish drifts through and does not stay, and I had drifted enough. And a fish sees nothing, and I could not give up seeing — not even here, where there is almost nothing to see and it takes an hour to see it.

For a while I thought I would be the copper strip, the one thing in the vault that visibly spends. Then I understood that I already had been. On the surface I was something consulted daily and thinning. Everything up there is counted, because everything up there runs short. I ran short. Down here, counting is for things that can run short, and I have been learning, slowly, not to be one.

What I became instead took years, and it happened mostly below the neck. I learned to breathe without making weather on the water. I learned to cross a terrace without a ripple — the children could do it before they were answered to; the strip had thinned by a hair before I could. A child beat me every time at name-the-drip. The chord has more notes than I had thoughts, and for a long time I heard it as a single hum, the way you hear a held thing as simple because you are not yet simple enough. Rain, when it came, decorated the chord and was gone within the hour; I learned to tolerate it, and then to like it, and then — this took longest — to let it leave. No one asked my name, and after a while neither did I.

And because I had not been born in the side pool like the keepers, I gave myself a second birth, late. One dim hour I lay down in the warm water — slowly, so slowly the surface barely closed over me — and heard the chord the way every keeper first hears it: muffled, through the water itself. Each note arrived twice, once through the stone and once through the pool, once for the body and once for the ear, like a word and then its meaning. I came up streaming and changed, and no one remarked on it. There is nothing to remark. The place re-finds itself; you follow.

Nothing rots here, so the person I had been could not rot off. It had to be eaten. Whatever I shed — skin, hair, the smell of weather, the habit of counting — the films took back, the shrimp grazed, the water carried a little of me each ebb toward the swallet. I am being revised by mouths too small to see. My eyes stay wide now even at the brightest hour, and I angle my face from the oval of sky the way you would angle from a fire. There is a cream rime at my hairline and wrists, as if the place had begun, politely, to deposit on me. My prints are going. My grip slides on rough things and closes truly on fine ones. I speak fewer words in an ebb than you would use to describe a single pool, and they have lost their edges, and heard across the basin I sound like the vault muttering to itself, and that is not entirely wrong.

Once in my years a tooth let go of the roof and dropped into a pool with a sound like a cup set down. I was upright before the rings reached the walls. I had not decided anything. The chord had decided, and I was part of what answered.

The eldest carried a chord with a high note in it that had not sounded since her youth — a tooth gone, a drip moved a handspan, before my time, unverifiable by anyone else. She told me because I asked the right wrong question. I asked what the chord was leaning toward. Nothing, she said. It does not lean. But she was still for a long while, and then she told me where the high note used to hang from, and what it sat against, and how the whole chord lay when it was there. And I understood that I had been hearing its absence all along — that what I had taken for the chord's refusal to resolve was the exact shape of a note that used to sound. My ear had been holding a place for it. Now I hold the place knowingly. When she died, they spoke her name once, at the swallet, and afterward there was one person in the world who knew the chord had a high note, and it was me. I carry it the way the cobbles carry feet. It is the only thing I own, it weighs nothing, and it is not mine.

The shrimp came to my toes one hour and stayed. They will not come to anything that intends to move. I had stopped intending so gradually that I cannot date it — nothing here can be dated except by wear, and I am not worn enough yet to read. No one saw, or no one said. Acceptance here is not an event; it is a non-event that has finally become possible. At the dim hour the steadiest hand in the household laid the copper to the line, and the water rose to take the metal, exactly, and that was the ceremony, and it was not for me. Mine was never the steadiest hand. It does not need to be.

So: where you would find me. A circuit on the middle terraces — two lips, one seep, one crest — small enough to wade in an hour, slow enough to take a day. A drip hangs over my seep, one clear note; I keep the measure of the chord that is mine to keep. Kneeling, waist-deep in water the temperature of skin, pressing sinter meal through the hide sieve toward the line, always toward the line. When a fish comes through, I pause with the others, and I no longer want to be it. I want to be what notices it. That is better. That is the whole job.

I wanted to be the water, first. I will be. When I die — in the warm pool, if the household is kind, and it is kind the way the place is kind, without display — they will keep me one ebb on the flat stone by the hut, speak my name once, and give me to the outflow. The dark will take my age away with the water's, down past the lamps, through the sumps where the fish drift eyeless because there is nothing to see, only the chord's root, only the held thing holding itself. And above, at the dim hour, someone will lay a leaf of copper — or whatever the keepers of that time have chosen to sound with; the line is not theirs to decide, only the sounding — and the water will rise to take it, exactly, and the chord will hold, with a high note in it that is not sounding, and one person listening for it. That is all the monument the law allows, and it is more than I arrived with.

They have a word that means this much water, this many keepers. It is what the line means. It is what they call themselves when the surface asks, which is seldom. It took me years of uncounting to earn it, and it weighs nothing, and it holds.
