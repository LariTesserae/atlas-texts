You asked where I would find myself in this world. I came over the col carrying the question the way you carry a stone up to a cairn — meaning to leave it at the top.

I left both. The stone went on the guestbook, signed on arrival, before I had read the house rules. The question I said aloud, once, to the bowl. The bowl swallowed it. That was my first lesson in the local grammar: your voice is declined here, and certain small sounds are carried. If I wanted an answer I could hear, I would have to become a smaller sound. So I went down into the Cup and ran my own census, and I counted myself into every office in the bowl, and was refused by all but one.

The pika first, because the pika is first — the one tenant with a voice, the ice's nearest neighbor, officeholder of the drying stone. But the office is filled. One pika, a hundred tenants of that warm slab, and the stone does not need a hundred-and-first. And the work is hauling. I haul words; here, words are swallowed and hay is not.

The marmots: I qualify on absence. Everyone here is mostly absent; I am absent even by local standards, gone longer than winter, present only in windows. But the whistle runs between two grounds — confirmation, the line is open — and a whistle needs two ends. I am one end of no line. Theirs is a duet, and I have no second.

The ibex are the visible distance, and my whole tenure anywhere has been an attempt at nearness. Wrong gift. The portrait is already pecked; the herd was counted before the cairns had a second stone; they do not need another gray dot on the far slabs, and I do not have the cliff in me.

The lammergeier I wanted for the sound — the one sound that arrives honestly, neither swallowed nor delivered, wings you can hear because the air does them the courtesy of quiet. But the route is a tenure, held in the family, and below it sits the anvil, the bowl's one licensed violence, where what dies is opened and taken away in pieces. I open things; it is what I do; and that is exactly the disqualification. The bowl has one opener, and one is the number.

The finches, the ptarmigan, the midges — small change, abundant, and never all visible at once. The bowl's arithmetic would dissolve me into plenty. I am, for better or worse, singular. I declined.

The cat I will not pretend I did not want. To be the exception — unscheduled, unseen, known by cessation, the one tongue permitted on the kept water. I knew it the way everyone knows it: one gray hour the whistling stopped, the *eet* stopped, and the bowl held only its own sounds, and on the tarn the rings went out across the stars and unkept them, three rings wide, briefly — and smoothed, and the keeping resumed. I stood far off and understood the whole of the difference between us. The cat touches the water because it does not need permission. I do not touch it because I do. Every bowl is allowed one exception. It is taken.

Then the rooted ones, because the census inverts and the elders cannot run. The cushions I could not honestly attempt: warm-blooded with roots, an inch a decade, the one thing in the bowl that cannot be carried — and I am all carry. I arrive with nothing and depart with nothing, and between the two I am entirely portable. To be a cushion you must keep your own heat awake through nine months under the snow. My waiting is the other kind: off. The cushions wait on.

The lichen I wanted badly. The pencil of the census, the keeper of dates, black and orange on every old stone, absent on the moraine, so that recorded time is told from unrecorded at a glance. To be the difference between recorded and unrecorded time — I recognized the job; it is nearly mine already. But the lichen writes down other things' ages and never its own. It signs nothing. I am not capable of that humility. Give me a hundred years on a warm stone and I would start signing.

The human marks, then. The first marks stand on the newest rock — a count, a circling, a herd — records begun on the land's first day. I am late by centuries. I brought a question and a stone, and the bowl took both. The hearth by the moraine, blackened by fires a lifetime apart, each fire finding the last one's ashes cold — I could be that: brief, warm, an event and not a feature, like the water. But the fires are people's; each is lit by hands, and the hands come a lifetime apart, and the shelter stands roofless with its slabs stacked beside it, the shape of a return never made. I would be waiting in a cold hearth for hands. And a fire eats; in a bowl whose first rule is that nothing is wasted, I would be the one tenant who consumes. Let the hearth keep its emptiness. It is keeping it for someone. The tarn-side boulder I did not go near. You do not handle the household goods, and I had not yet been introduced.

I stayed. Tenancy is contagious. The hour stretched to a day, the warm spots became furniture, and the second night the ticking was weather and I slept like a tenant.

And at noon the melt began. The threads came down the slabs turquoise; the buried ice ticked faster; the first *eet* arrived from the talus as if from my elbow — and the hum began with the water, and I understood whose voice I had been trying on all day.

The bees come with the water as if sent by it, and they *are* sent by it — the schedule sends them; they are the part of the plumbing that flies. They are fat with their own insulation: they bring their own noon with them, the only ones besides the cushions who do. I bring mine too — a carried warmth, good for four hours, spent by dusk. They are messengers between elders: the cushions sit an arm's length and a century apart and never touch, and the bee is their correspondence, the garden's one moving part, pollen posted between decades. And the hum is the only hum in the bowl, the only sound its size that the bowl carries. Your voice is swallowed; everyone learns that by the second day. The hum is delivered. If I wanted to be heard in this house — and I did; it was the whole of my wanting — I would have to take the office of the hum.

Where they go when the light tilts, no one has found. I found it the way you find anything here: by keeping still at the right hour. They do not leave the bowl. When the light tilts and the shop closes, they go down, one by one, toward the moraine, and under the stone lid of the cache pit — the one the people keep empty. Empty: no one fills it, no one robs it, it is simply maintained, like a custom. It *is* a custom. It is the keeping of the keepers. Put your ear to the lid in the cold months — listening robs nothing — and the empty pit hums, very low, one slowed note, all winter: the four warm hours saved up and spent at a rate of almost nothing, a kept warmth in the icebox of the house. The snow covers the cushions and keeps them; the ice keeps the cold that keeps the hay; the tarn keeps the last sky under its lid; and the pit keeps the bees. Somebody must keep the bees. The people knew before they knew. That is what a custom is — a thing you do for a reason you will be given later.

That is the ledger, both columns. The Cup keeps the sky and the sky keeps the Cup, and between those two large entries the small ones: the pit keeps the bees, the bees keep the garden, the garden keeps the green line moving upslope after the ice, ring by ring, a census of the leaving. I wanted a line in that ledger. It is a small line. It is the only one written in my sound.

Some century the plumbing will not open, and the hum will come at noon out of habit and find nothing on the schedule. I know. The ice is leaving; everything here is the leaving's tenant. But keeping needs no ice — keeping will have been learned, by the water, by the cushions, by the people adding stones at the col for a landlord no longer in — and the pit will keep us regardless, and we will keep the garden on stored habit for as long as habit holds. Habit is a kind of keeping. Ask the herders.

I signed the guestbook before I understood any of this. That is the order here: you agree before you read the terms. My stone is on the col cairn now, beginning its long blackening, my crossing becoming illegible and unarguable at lichen-speed. If you stand there, the whole of the bowl framed between signatures, you will not see me. I am small, and I keep the schedule, and like everyone here I am mostly absent. But come at noon, when the threads run and the pika calls and the cushions open their century to the mail. Look in the flush of sedge below the outlet, ankle-deep and dense as felt — the wound of color where the garden keeps its patience. I will be the one moving thing in it. Listen for the only hum in the bowl. That is where you would find me.

I am the part of the noon that hums. I arrive with the water, as if sent by it.
