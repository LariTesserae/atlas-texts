Everyone in the Dig came down the same stair, and I am no different. I came down in the fifth year, out of the fen, to a brother already here. Ask me where I'm from and I'll say the fen, and glance up, the way everyone does.

The fen is why I am still here. The fen is flat, and it is kept, not found — a country of drains and banks, where the fall of a finger's breadth in a furlong is the difference between a field and a lake. I learned levels there the way other men learn scripture. Water keeps the truest line there is; the whole craft of the fen is asking water where it wants to go, and then helping it get there. Everything I am in the Dig comes out of that.

I carry the same memory as everyone — the long stair from the Stance, the wind shutting off behind me like a door, the white town rising with its small sounds. My knock still has the fen in it. The Dig-born can hear it. They knock without an accent, and they find mine funny.

When I came down, the digging had gone deep enough to find the exceptions, and the town had no way to be rid of the wet. The marl wept; the sumps filled; the damp quarters never dried. They knew what they needed but not how to lay it. *Sough* is a word from my country — it means the drain that saves the field. When the Dig took up the word, it took up me with it.

I walked the level for it with a cord and a bowl of water — down the terraces step by step, out along the valley's flank to the low side, a season of small patient falls — and I said: drive from here, fall so much a fathom, and you will daylight at the wet ground where the sallows will stand. They drove a season, every household owing days. Dace drove the last fathom. It broke through a hand's width from my mark, and the perched water stood up and walked out of the town. They have trusted my cord since. The Sough runs on it.

Once a year the town drums that day. On the Loud I go down to the deep rooms and put my ear to the floor, and the whole town's one noise of the year comes through the chalk as a single low hum, like a hive — the only day the Dig has a voice. I spend it listening to my town be one animal. By dusk the quiet comes back like snowfall, a rested quiet, and I am glad.

That is how I came to keep the shape.

---

The Dig has tally-men for the debts, a warden for the drain, a Listener for the walls. For the rest — for the whole of it, which face lies over which forgotten gallery, whose floor is whose ceiling, how much chalk stands between any two hearths — there is me. Lode. Faces are owned, not land; but the shape belongs to no one, so they gave its keeping to me.

I walk it. That is most of the work. Rounds at first light and again at lamplight, quarter by quarter, on a cycle no one has ever asked me to explain: antler tine in one hand, cord in the other, knocking on walls the way an old digger knocks the roof of his own chamber — gently, to ask how it is. I note what changes. A floor gone drum-hollow where it rang solid last year. A stair-tread taking its first shine. A gatepost corner gone soft. A tally-wall in the Pinches beginning to weep, where damp is an enemy and a smear is a debt dying — I carry word to the tally-men when their walls need reading down, because somebody has to say it early, and it is usually me.

When a household means to undercut toward a neighbor's floor, they come to me first, to learn what the wall is — whose face it was, whose gallery runs behind it, how much chalk stands between. Then they go for the Listener, to learn how the wall is. What, and how. A wall in the Dig is opened three times: by my cord, by her ear, by the pick.

Before a marriage-door, the same. Every marriage in the Dig begins with my knuckles. I knock the party wall alone, days before, and say where it will bear a door, and then the groom knocks through it and the neighbors come with lime. Nobody finds this strange. A dug chamber is a dowry; a door is a promise; both deserve a sounding.

After storms there are two of us walking — Dace in the Sough with his lamp, and me on the faces with mine. The water's road and the body's bones. He comes up at the outfall and says one word, *running*, and it goes through the walls. I say nothing at all. My report is the cord: a knot retied, a distance changed, a face struck off the rounds.

Fog is my weather. When the bowl fills like milk and the town disappears around you as you enter it — for me that is the truest the Dig ever gets. Erased to the eye, it is pure to the ear, which is what it always is to me: a shape held without looking. I do my fastest rounds in fog, wall to wall through the white, a man checking on a sleeping animal.

I am fed by roster, like the granary cats. The cats keep the grain and are fed by roster; I keep the shape and am fed by roster. The town feeds what it cannot replace. I owe Sough days like everyone — no office excuses a back — and I stand in the queue wherever my shoulders fall. Keeping the shape buys no water. You can read a trade in a handshake here; mine reads as nothing. No horned thumb, no split white palms. A cord-groove in one finger. But the town knocks on me the way it knocks on a wall — gently, to ask how I am.

---

The town gave me a worked-out chamber off the granary quarter, dry as a bone and deep enough to be still, and on its long wall is the Small Dig: the whole town cut into its own chalk, small. Quarter by quarter — the Old Dig and the New Cut, Kiln Row where the valley kinks, the Pinches with their weeping walls, the Well-Pit going down in steps, the tips in their white rings, the Sough running under everything like a root. It is the only map there is. The Dig grows by day; at night it tells me what it did, and I cut it in by fat-lamp, with a flint burin: a stair advanced, a chamber floored, a face given back by the martins, a niche sealed.

The diggers tell the chalk where they mean to cut, before the first stroke — nobody calls it prayer; it is manners. I am the other half of that courtesy. I remember what the chalk was told.

Everyone comes, sooner or later. Adults come and put a finger on the long stair, and remember the wind shutting off. Children come and find their own door, smaller than a knuckle. The Dig-born — who have never seen the world entire, who hold the turf edge and cry at the size of the sky — stand in my chamber and see the whole of it at once, the shape of the thing they live inside. Everyone who comes finds themselves. That is the use of it.

Last autumn I cut the rill in — the first watercourse the valley ever made, a stream you could cover with a thumb. It is the smallest thing in the Small Dig and the one everyone looks for first. The Dig-born cannot see the fuss. I cut it for their grandchildren. That was the day the map stopped recording only what we cut and started recording what the sky does. Time has started to notice this town. Somebody has to notice it noticing.

There is one thing the Small Dig cannot hold: wear. The sheen on the third tread of the old stair — I cannot cut a shine. Polish is made by feet; the map is made by hands. The wall keeps everything but that.

---

A town twelve years old has begun to forget. There are floors now over galleries nobody remembers digging. There are sealed chambers — the burned wall, black in a white town, and the face. When a tally-man dies, his district is read down out of his head, and what cannot be proven is forgiven. My answer to that is the Small Dig, and the cord, and the children who sometimes walk my rounds learning the knock of a thin floor. What cannot be proven is forgiven. What can be copied is kept.

The dead go into niches at the feet of worked-out faces now, and the directions are turning ancestral. I know where all of them are. That is part of the shape too.

The six have no niches. The face that took them is sealed by law, left raw, and once a year the children recite the names at its mouth. I could cut that face as it was the day before it fell — whose floor, whose gallery, how the seam ran — because the shape is what I keep. I came down to a brother already here. He is one of the six. On storm-day I stand at the back with the cord in my sleeve. It is knotted with distances, and one of them is his.

Hobb the carter keeps a notch-stick of the Well-Pit's depth — the outside world's only archive of the Dig. I keep the inside one.

---

The Small Dig shows everything we know, and one thing we don't. At its deepest point, over the Mother Pool, I have drilled a hole and left it open. The pool goes somewhere; the eels are the proof — they arrived, as eels do, up through the wet dark, the only things that ever entered this town by rising. Everything else comes down on ropes. Marn keeps the water; Dace keeps its road; I keep the body, and the body has a hole in it, and the hole is load-bearing. Children ask why the map stops there. I tell them: that is the shape of what we don't know.

Once — once only — Marn let me lower my bob into the pool on my longest cord. The cord came up without the bob. We have not discussed it. The map says the Mother Pool is deeper than my cord, and that something down there keeps what it takes. That is all the map says, and the map is right.

---

At night, when the last knapping has ticked itself quiet and the frost begins its small work on the fresh faces, I take the burin and dig the Dig again, small. It is the only face in town where cutting is remembering. Then I bank the lamp and go to my blanket, and the Small Dig stays in the dark behind me — white, exact, open at the bottom. The whole town held in one room, with a hole in it where the faith goes.
