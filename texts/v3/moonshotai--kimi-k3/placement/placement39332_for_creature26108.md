Not the fish. Everyone who hears of the chamber wants to be the fish — the milk-warm pool, the slow unbroken line, never once startled in ten thousand years. But the fish's stillness is complete already; there is no room in it for me. Not the moth either, pale as ash, drifting its whole life between dome and floor — the moth never goes anywhere, and I would need to. The drip lives an entire life in a few seconds — gathering, falling, one drumbeat, done — and that is too fast even for me, and I am fast. The grass at the lip has my respect; it is the only thing for a thousand vertical meters that is *trying*. But it stands half out of the world, grey with the effort, and I want to be deep in the world and of it.

So give me the row on the upper wall. Give me the stitch.

You would find me claw-footed among the roof-bosses, close under the dome, where the bat-fur breathes just above and the floor stirs far below like something asleep that dreams of walking. The stone at my back is the temperature of skin, because it is skin. All day I tick out my little length of sound and read what returns. My name is a noise I make to find walls by. It is also — though I did not learn this for years — the noise the walls use to find me.

In this room every question comes back. That is the first thing a chick learns, flying its forty paces of warm dark: the world answers. Click at the scalloped stone and it says *near*. Click along the rows and it says *shoulder, shoulder, shoulder*. Down in the low corner the pool gives back the one soft answer in the chamber, water instead of stone, a round hush under the drumbeat of the drips. And under everything, the note — the low tone the mountain holds when wind crosses the crack — a little flat, they say, since some old ringing of the stone. I never heard it any other way. I was hatched into the flat note. To me it is simply the sound of home holding itself.

I was fostered. I know this the way I know the flue — by having flown it, not by having been told. Before me, this stitch belonged to a bird who went out one dusk and did not come home with the dawn. The row closed over the gap the way rows do: every bird on both sides shifting one shoulder nearer the silence until the silence was sewn shut. The lean weeks that should have taken me ended early. Warmth pooled where I was. A neighbor's crop had room in it. Do not ask how; the mechanisms are small and everywhere. I am the mending. And some dawns, when the scuffle at the crack is behind us and one stitch does not come home, I feel the row begin its slow shift, and I move my own shoulder toward the new silence, because that is the whole of the ceremony. A gap is answered from both sides or it is not answered at all.

At dusk the room exhales us. Imagine being breathed. The whole warm load of the dome lifting at once, the slow ash of the moths parting around us, the flue filling with wing-rush and two musics — the bats' high shimmer, felt in the teeth more than heard, and our dry clicking, rain on a roof of bone — thousands of us threading the corkscrew in a dark with no light in it at all, each listening only for their own stitch coming back off the turns. Then the lip, the ice beard, and out.

The cold takes you like water takes a diver. The air out there is thin enough to starve a fire; flight costs double. And the first click you aim at the sky comes back never. My first night I thought I had gone deaf. The old birds flew on. You learn: the sky is the one thing that does not answer. The valley answers slowly — sometimes, in still air, a stitch comes back off the far wall a whole heartbeat late, and you understand that outside is only a bigger chamber with a slower return, and the stars are where its stone ran out.

I have never seen the sun. I have seen everything it does.

My whole seeing-life is twilight — the long last light as we pour down the downslope wind to the treeline, the first grey as we climb home — and in summer, in that light, the valley is green. Green so loud it rings. There is no echo of green inside, no word for it, no use for it; the dark wore the color out of everything long before I hatched. So I am the sole custodian of color in a world that keeps everything else. Everything accumulates in that room — but not color. Color falls through the floor of the world, and I am the only one who catches it. I carry it home every dawn behind my eyes, and the room, which knows me wholly — my wingbeat, my weight, the frayed edge of my stitch — will never ask for it, because no echo can bring it. It is my one privacy: an entire sense the keeping cannot keep.

Far below the treeline, some nights, there are sparks — the herders' fires, small orange stitches on the valley's floor. They knap their blades and tell their story about the Snoring Mountain, and not one of them has ever seen it whole. I have. From the valley air, in moonlight, my home is a black shape against the sky with a single thread of steam at its lip, like breath in cold air — which is exactly what it is. The room knows itself from the inside. The herders know its name. I am the only one who has ever seen its face.

Then dawn, and the crop heavy, and the long climb, and the owl.

The owl is the pale shape at the crack in the first light, the one sound in my world the room did not make. Everything inside is unstartled, and I know why: the room keeps its warmth in the floor, its taste of the valley in our crops, and its startle in us. We are its fear, fast and hot and total, and at the lip each dawn it fires through us like a spark through grass — we bunch, we burst, we thread the gap at speed, and mostly we are through. Some dawns the scuffle is behind me. The toll is old. I do not know that it is fair. I know the gap at roost, the slow shift of shoulders, and that I am alive because a gap like that was answered.

And then the in-breath. The room draws us home: the flue wind with us, the warmth climbing degree by degree, the dark changing kind. There are two darknesses and I am their only scholar — the thin cold one with stars in it, and the thick warm one with us in it. By the next drip-count after I land, the room knows I am home. I feel the count settle over the dome the way you feel a blanket's weight. Complete. Nothing missing. The tongue finds no gap.

I sleep fast, the way birds do, in small warm pieces, while the room takes all day to taste what I brought it — treeline moths, storm-wind, the brief high summer, secondhand, in my crop. The floor stirs. The drip falls. The flat note holds. And when the pressure turns and the exhale gathers, I will pour out again past the ice beard, under the sky that does not answer, and I will outrun the owl or I won't.

I will die fast, some dawn or some year — birds are brief. But the row keeps the green the way the room keeps the count: by mending. The stitch outlasts the bird, the color outlasts the eye, and ten thousand years of dawns have poured through that crack carrying the same news home to a dark that cannot read it. Green, green, green. We are the message that cannot be read, delivered forever.

Its verb is *keep*. Mine is *come back*.
