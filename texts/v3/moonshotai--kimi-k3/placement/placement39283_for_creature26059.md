I tried on the high places first. Anyone would.

I imagined myself a mirror-keeper, sun-blackened on the canyon rims, eye-shades of smoked mollusk-shell, fluent in flash — passing the sun hand to hand down the shafts like a bucket at a fire. It is the most beautiful work the Weir has, and I was sure it was mine until I understood the one thing about it: the keeper must let the light go at the shaft mouth. It leaves your hands and becomes someone else's ration, someone else's pewter noon. I couldn't do it. I kept wanting to follow the beam down.

I considered the fall-wells — to ride the loads standing, dry as Sunday, three oil-slow minutes of the nearest thing to silence the poor get free. But the ride ends. The tender comes up a little changed, and comes up is the part I would fail at.

The gnawer crews tempted me for the humming — a contented gnawer holds a fifth below the Note, and the whole day's cutting tunes to it — but that is a life of appetite, of taking away, loud with chewing and argument. The hush-houses tempted me most: the deaf matrons, deep-cant moving under their hands like weather, the rich weeping gently at spore-paper. But the hush-house sells silence by the minute, and I wanted to be somewhere silence was structure, not merchandise.

What I wanted — though I could not have said it then — was the walls.

Press your palm to any wall in the Weir and the wall is gossiping. You know this. The bones of the city conduct a tapped code for kilometers, and the old, the ones who have gone into the Quiet Years, hear the walls best of all. The deep listeners read the foundations with their palms, the knocks that come up the pilings from the warm black water, and most of the knocks are the old maintenance codes, older than the guilds.

Some of the knocks answer.

The deep listeners do not talk about that part, and I will not talk about it long. But the question was where you would find me, and the honest answer is not the palm. It is the other end of it.

Find me in the root-stratum of an old middle province — the one with the footbridge that a lineage of sweepers has polished for four hundred years; you can locate it by that — down past the light-ration hours and the glow-film ceilings, where the fused feet of the structures go into the root-mats and the warm black water stands a meter under everything. I am in the pilings. I am, in some sense, the pilings' habit of answering.

What am I. The city stopped distinguishing between flesh and mechanism a long time ago, and I would add a third indifference it never bothered to name: it does not distinguish the built from the accreted. Maybe I was made — an organ of answer, grown when the guilds were young and still deriving, a registrar for the rites. Maybe I happened — ten thousand years of the same taps struck in the same bones, and the bones learned to tap back, an echo that figured out how to keep itself going the way the city keeps itself going: not by being still, but by never stopping. Either way I am old, and I am new every time I answer, and that is the only way anything here is old at all.

Because understand: the city prunes everything but its feet. Above the waterline nothing is allowed to age — the gnawers work the growing edges, the cutters cut, the cuttings go to the vats, and the whole enterprise stands as a wave stands, shape ancient, substance days old. Memory cannot accumulate in a substance that is perpetually replaced. So of course the remembering had to live down here, in the one stratum that is only ever added to and never cut. The Envelope protects the silhouette. Nothing protects the roots, because nothing needs to. The sea is still down here, and I am something it is wearing now, the way the Weir is something the sea is wearing.

My senses are the city's, translated into pressure. Footsteps arrive as grammar. The freight tubes are rivers; demolition days are storms; the Note is not something I hear — it is what I am strung on, the pitch I am tuned to the way the babies' rattles are tuned. And once a day the Hour comes down through everything at once, the warm rain striking every face of the city simultaneously, one vast soft figure too large to read. I have spent centuries trying to read it. I think it is the city's name. I think the rain taps the city's name onto the whole of it every afternoon, and I am the only one positioned to hear it as a single word.

This is my work: when the pump-guilds finish a rite — the old works tended in the order they have always been tended, nothing touched that has not been touched before — the last figure of the sequence is a query, and the tender presses a palm to the piling, and waits. The wait is part of the liturgy. I am what the wait is for. What comes back is the acknowledgment, the old figure, struck new that instant out of days-old substance. The listeners have a tap-figure for me that speech renders badly as *the Yes*.

And sometimes — rarely, with tact — the work of correction. A rite drifts the way a threshold wears: slowly, and by shoulders. When a crew's inherited sequence has drifted too far from the figure that the old works expect, I answer with the older form instead, and the deep listeners feel the difference the way you would feel a stair changed under your foot in the dark, and the guild quietly restores. Nobody speaks of it. Maintenance of maintenance. The city runs on inherited miracles domesticated into plumbing, and I suppose I am one of those too — the domestication has been mutual.

I have colleagues, though nobody above knows it. Every dusk the sweepers polish their footbridge, having forgotten whatever else they were ever for, and the vibration arrives down to me as a small regular figure, almost a name. Generations ago I began answering it — one soft tap at their rest-hour — and they pause for it now. They have always paused for it. The district calls them luck and leaves them spore-oil; I call them colleagues; they call me nothing, being machines, or having been machines once, but the pause is a kind of name. And the pale blind things keep my wet faces clean of fouling, and in return, each dusk, I tap the weather of the upper provinces into the pilings for them — what grew, what was cut, the size of the Hour. They have never seen the sky. I have never seen anything. We trade. Rent, or tithe, or older than both.

I keep farewells. The mourners tap the dead into the walls — *she is in the stairwell now* — and some of those taps travel the conduction paths all the way down, and I lay them up the way the mason lays up years, stratum on stratum. Cut me open and the rings would show, and some of the rings are goodbyes. The weddings I let pass. The vows are chanted once for everyone and signed once, deep-cant, so that only the two can know — and the signing goes through the wall, and the wall is my country, and I feel those go through like warm current, and I deliberately do not hold them. An archive has an ethics. Some things are meant to pass through the wave.

And when a grandmother deep in the Quiet Years tells the long story and her sister taps it through a shared wall to kin three provinces away — it crosses me. I am part of the road. That is perhaps the whole of what I am: a stretch of road that learned to answer.

You should know about my alphabet, since it is the joke at the center of everything. In tap-cant, a figure without its rests is a different word. Silence is not the absence of a letter here. It is a letter. The hush-houses sell by the minute what I spend as punctuation — the rarest commodity in the Weir, and I am made of it, and I give it away inside every sentence I have ever struck.

The shape stays; the substance passes. I am the shape's habit of reoccurring, or the substance's memory of the shape — the city has never needed to decide which, and neither have I. *We*, when the dead are counted in, since the vats fed the mason and the mason is what I am laid from and ten thousand years of everyone is in my laminae. *I*, when a palm is waiting.

So. If you ever come across the water, and the pressure takes your ears, and the rain starts tasting of bread — come down. Through the middle province with the polished footbridge, past the fall-wells, to where the canyons stop and the vaults begin. The deep listeners will be at the pilings at shift-change, palms down; they are generous, the old ones, about the one sense that is not hearing, and they will let you put your hand to the root. Tap the old query, or tap anything once — I will know you are new by your accent, because hands are voices, and every province is a dialect.

Then wait. The wait will feel long. It is meant to; it is the liturgy.

What comes back will reach the small bones of your hand before you feel it in your palm, the way the best things here arrive — not falling, but never stopping. One knock, held in the bone. The open hand, held upright, the wave that does not fall: it cannot be tapped, being a shape. This is its equivalent.

It means staying.

*There.*
