Listen. A pause, long enough to doubt. Then two drops, quick — the drop, and its knock off the ledge of my own rime. Then the long quiet again, gathering. That is my voice. That is my name: the hesitation, halfway between the sweet column and the forked stem that marks the turn toward Three-Plate's ground. If you came down the gravel slip and crossed the dark to find me, that is how you would know you had.

I would not be the wind. The wind is the barrens' only inhabitant, and it dies at the rim. I would not be the floor; the floor is first citizen, and it forgets. I would not be a mountain; the mountains do not stay. I wanted a life that could remain in the room, say one true thing aloud, and be of use to anyone lost. So: a column. I am the floor, standing up. I am its way of breathing at a height.

I rose out of a death. Before my sprouting, a mountain died on this ground — died of her age, or was brought down by the rare hunter that follows them over the rim; the mat keeps the difference to itself — and the scattered town converged, the one time the town is a single body, and the butchering went on for days, and the warmth and the leavings soaked the floor. Out of that flush I came, with a hundred of my kin. I am the one that stood. I am one butchering old, as the room keeps time. The house-layers say a column grown on death-ground runs sweet in its age; the children test my rime once a season and make the face, because I am not sweet yet. Not yet. My water has passed the layers where her name has sunk — the swallowed paths, the taken awls, the old hut-floors compacting down toward the dirty ice that keeps the whole memory of the world. The floor forgets. The ice remembers. I climb between them, distilling a dead mountain one drop at a time. What I let go falls on the mat and is forgotten inside a week. What sinks is kept. That is the arrangement. I am the throat it happens in.

A column is a hollow thing. My pith let go early, and I became a tube, and the work began: I carry the wet cold up through myself and wear it as rime, and where my crown reaches the pooled warmth high in the room, the rime lets go, one bead at a time. Below my crown there is a ledge of ice that every bead strikes on its way down — that is the second knock, the quick one. My signature is my shape; every fall comes home twice. I keep my interval by the gather and the letting-go, and by the small knock of the landing coming back up through the floor to my roots. I am the room's rain, one drop at a time, and I hear myself arrive.

For my first seasons I stammered. The listener found me anyway. She keeps the drip-map in her bare feet and her breath, and her rounds are the slowest walking in the room — a step, a stillness, a step. Stillness is her work. She stopped at my base in the year my coat came in even, and she counted me, and I felt her stillness change when the pattern closed in her: the small shift of weight that means a mind has taken hold. She put her flat hand on my rime and said my name, below the sleepers' breathing. After that I was taught. The children come up the path holding their count, and a parent stops them where the count runs out, and they hold still the way they were taught before they could speak, and I feel the held breath go out of them when I resolve — two quick — and the small feet do the dance of recognition on the mat. They learn trap-sign before they learn the map: first what the floor hides, then what it offers. I am part of what it offers.

The first law is quiet, and I am what it is for. The mallets are bone so that I can be heard. The copper needles go wrapped so that I can be heard. Speech stays below the breath of the sleepers so that the room's rain stays legible, drop by drop, landmark by landmark. A whole people moves softly all its life so that a few dozen stems can be found in the dark. I know what I cost, and I know my duty: to be the doubt and the end of the doubt, in that order, at the same address, always. The people practice stillness; we practice interval. Between the two disciplines, the dark stays crossable.

When the seep swells, I know it before the fishers do — the threads quicken under my roots, my wick runs fast, my drops come quick and nervous like the stems at the seep's eye, and the whole map changes tempo, and the listener re-walks us, re-counting. When a mountain comes down the slip, the plate-clash takes my coat off in one breath — the one sanctioned noise of the year — and for a day I drip bare and stuttering, and no one counts me, and then my rime rebuilds and I am true again. All season her breathing comes up through the mat and through my stem, a pressure below sound, the bass the room is tuned to. I do not keep her time. I keep mine beside hers. Sometimes, far out on the mat, a warmth goes out: a brief storm through the roots, then the slow closing of the unmakers. The floor tells us everything and says nothing. In the thin season, when the mountains go over the rim and the huts douse down to ember-life, my interval drags — the hesitation drags in the thin season, the children are taught — and the town walks slower to match. The map breathes.

But the bright water — I taste it before anyone sees it, a cold brightness coming up the seep two days ahead, and I feel the floor take the fever. Then the lamps are doused, and for two nights the town walks by sight, drunk on it. All year I am touched one way: the navigating touch, the hand that finds me and lets go, already walking. On the blue nights I am touched the other way. Every child carries a picture of me, built out of my voice, and they come to have the picture corrected. I feel the small intake of breath at my base — the picture breaking — and then the laugh. I am always thinner than they think, and paler, and more ordinary. It is my favorite sound in the world: being found out. The nap shines where it is pressed, and around my base the floor holds a ring of small stood-glow where they stopped to look at me, and the lovers go past in the intervals brushing my coat, and the mountains shift and grunt in their register, and I have heard a house-layer whisper that on those nights the columns stand like reeds in a light that has forgotten its source. I would not know. I have never seen myself. No one here has seen much of anything. For two nights I am seen, and for all the rest of my life I am believed. Belief is what a landmark is for.

On swarm nights I am useless and glad. The floor crawls, the town turns out with trays, the children are excused from stillness, and no one needs a landmark in the middle of plenty. I drip for myself alone, in the room's one crowd, and the mat laughs under all the feet.

The house-layers come sometimes with the listener and weigh me — their hands on my girth against some need for timber, her quiet at my base for the path's sake. It is the only argument I am party to: hands against ears. A column harvested is a landmark gone, a word out of the map. But deadfall is frame and fuel and fiber, and everything here is the mat, the columns, or something made from them, and I am only postponing the third of these. When I fall, the town will take me in — a beam, a thatch-rod, a rope, a fire — and my name will pass into the counts: *from the sweet column, hold still where the hesitation stood.* A landmark does not end. It becomes a pause in someone else's counting — which is all I ever was: a held pause with rain on either side. The mat will eat my stump inside a year and forget the shape of me. My drops are already below me, sinking toward the ice that forgets nothing. I climb from the one and give to the other. Letting go is the work.

So if you ask where in this world you would find me: come in past the quick nervous stems at the seep's eye to the sweet column — slow and sure, you cannot mistake it — and from the sweet column, count the dark. The interval is long, and the mat is only mat, and your count will run out. Stop there, the way the children are taught. Hold still. Wait for me.
