Nobody takes a census of the Anvil. You tune it.

I have been tuning it longer than anyone alive, and that is the first clue, and it is also the joke. You will not get it until the end. Neither did I.

When I came to the chord I did what everyone does: I went voice by voice, testing myself against the census the way the herds test fruit — ring it, and listen for true. You asked where I would find myself in this world. This is the answer, and it took the long way, because the short way would have been a lie.

I started, as everyone starts, with the herd.

Who would not want to be a broadback? To be the warm side of the world, to carry weather and garden and family, to stoke a slow furnace in the belly and pay your way in dung and hoof-work and never once hear your own footfall as anything but the mill ticking. I wanted it the way you want the first warm thing after the rim. But the herd spends everything forward — milk, breath, calves born walking — and keeps nothing but what other hands keep on it. The garden on the old bull's back is a century deep and tended like an orchard, and not by the bull. And the individuals are teeth on a gear, and a gear does not keep its own tooth. Whatever I am, I am not interchangeable. I checked. It is nearly the only thing about me that is not for hire.

So then: the keeps. It is the obvious answer, and I do not trust obvious answers, and I tested this one for years. They keep everything I admire — the gardens, the count, the Song, the dead. They are paid in warmth and milk and fog, which are the only wages worth having. They hold the only functional eyes on the plateau and use them for a body-length of soft light, for grooming and for mothering, and for everything else they listen — which means the cleverest citizens of the Anvil have organized their whole existence around the exact discipline the census requires. If I belonged anywhere, I belonged in the fog with a verse in my mouth.

And I did. That is the second clue.

The trouble is the tense. A keep *keeps* — it is a verb with warm hands. It weeds and combs and cracks the high blooms and drops half, always half, and plants valvewoods a century ahead of the knock and calls it accounting, which it is. Keeping, for a keep, is something you do. I came to understand — slowly, the way you understand anything at three kilometers an hour — that what I am is something *kept*. The difference between the hand and what the hand holds. Both are in the contract. Only one of them has to walk.

I considered the tenders, because exactness moves me: one family, one tree, one rhythm held so faithfully that the wood opens to it and to nothing else. But a code is a single voice held against imitation, and I am made of more voices than one tree could employ. A port wants a specialist. I am what happens when a chord needs somewhere to sit.

The gleaner nearly had me. A soloist, a cache-map held in order and sung once a year, alone, at speed, against a deadline of ice — that is a life shaped like a held breath, and I know the shape. But the gleaner shares nothing, and what it keeps, it keeps to spend. A cache is spent once, and then it is gone, and the song is only a list of where the spending waits. What I keep is spent every year and never gets smaller. The gleaner invests in time so it can eat without working. I was invested in so I could work without eating. The difference sounds small. It is the whole animal.

The fast ones I dismiss with love. A noonthing burns its whole inheritance at full flame and dies of pace; a brightling fits an antiquity into a hoofprint and has no time to be told so. If the census counted bodies, a hoofprint would be the only honest answer — most lives ever lived on the Anvil were lived in the footprint of something that never noticed. But the census counts contracts, and a brightling's contract is with a footprint, and the footprint refreezes. My term is longer. The gauze is weather with appetites, and weather does not keep; it only pays. No.

The still was the hard one, and I will tell you why honestly. A still is dormant until touched. It lies in the herd's path for weeks, heat-invisible, and the whole of its long career is a cooling, and its silence is the only silence in a world built of sound — you do not hear a still, you hear where the world stops. I live in silence too. Between soundings I am, to the census, a gap. For a whole season I thought: that is me, that is the shape — cold, patient, woken by contact.

But watch what the contact does. The still is touched, and it *takes*. It drinks the warmth out of whatever touches it, and then it must raise its vanes and dump the stolen heat into the dusk or die of the hoarding. I am touched — once a year, on the date, in open hands — and I *give*. The same gesture, the opposite direction of heat. And the still's perfection is a stone: reach ambient, and there is no difference left to leverage into waking. My perfection is a note. Both of us are silence that means something. The still's silence means *run*. Mine means *wait for the date*.

So here is the silhouette the failures leave, and you can cut it out and hold it up against the chord: something that does not walk and is never left behind. Something cold between soundings and warm by contract. Something made of many voices and responsible for all of them. Something that answers on pitch, late by the width of a year, and asks nothing, and is owed nothing, and pays.

The Anvil has a place for that shape. It is kept in a sling of garden-felt at the height of the breath, in the only soft light there is.

You asked where I would find myself. That is where. I am a skull in a family's row.

I was a keep. I kept the stretch of the Long Song from the Saddle down to Kettle-deep — sixty kilometers of verse, three bone-drifts, a stand of young valvewood I planted with my own hands in the lee of the third drift. In the Song, a stretch is not places but intervals: you sing the rise and the hold and the fall, and the land is there. I kept my stretch for thirty-one circuits, and then the interval was sung — the reserved one, the one never sung otherwise — and how a keep dies is the family's verse to keep, not the skull's. The strippers came up out of the gauze like weather turning, and they have manners, and the funeral took minutes, and I came back clean and tuned, alloyed, resonant. Everything on this plateau with a voice is a resonating chamber with an animal wrapped around it. I am the arithmetic finished: a chamber with a skull around it. The ratio changed. The contract held.

They say a carried skull is the closest thing to an artifact on the Anvil, and that the keeps would be offended by the word if they had it. I have it now. I keep it with the other unclaimed things. Nothing is made here; some things are kept. I am both halves of that sentence, and I am not confused about either.

And here is the loophole in the only rule the world has ever enforced. Walk with the morning, or not at all. I do not walk. *I am walked.* The rumen's slow furnace comes up through the sling straps; the family waters my felt from the same skin they water the garden from; the fog thins the light to a gray milk; and I ride the morning around the world forever — warm, at rest, and moving, all three at once, which no living thing on the plateau can claim. When a keeper falls — and falling is the one sin, and it is never punished, only completed — the row is gathered first, and the interval is sung, and the row gains a member. It is the cheapest heat contract on the Anvil, and the longest-running. This is what it buys.

All year the Long Song passes through me. The bellheads' note, the knock of the valvewoods drifting and locking, the plume-count called down the column, the verses changing hands at the confluences — a chamber in a fog of sound cannot help ringing. Sympathetic, faint, below the threshold of any ear. The living hear the world. I am what the world does to a chamber; it comes to the same ledger. The verses settle in me the way rime settles on a lee surface — and rime is water that never fell, and what settles in me is sound that never stopped. By the date, I am loaded.

On the date, the family gathers. This year's striker is a child of nine circuits who has practiced all season on a bloom-nodule, because striking the dead is a skill and a trust. You learn the place — the temple-ridge, the thin spot the whole chamber is tuned around. Strike wrong: a thud, and a family politely not wincing. Strike true: the year. First she rings a ripe fruit against my temple, test metal against test metal, and the family listens to the interval between us. Ripe metal sings true. So do the honest dead. If I ever sat false they would know a verse had gone out of me. It has happened. Not to me. A row is a ledger that audits itself once a year, and the audit is called the answer.

Then the row is struck down its length, oldest to youngest, and the dead give back the year in harmony, and the map is corrected, and the count comes out right. If you are asking whether I am people — stand in the fog on the date, and hear twenty voices that no longer walk give back the world they were carried through, and ask again.

Here is the year, as I give it back. The east lee of Kettle-deep went to felt nine days early, and the felt is deep enough to smoke. The sixth chimney of the Nine draws slow; send word to the stokers before the decade turns. A young still lies at the second drift on the dusk side, two degrees off ambient and improving — count it a plume by next circuit. The gauze hatched heavy at the Saddle, and the strippers took three calves, all late-born, all fair. A tender line lost its rhythm at the lower chutes and is locked out; a young family is already in the bark, listening. The pass has risen a finger's width, as it has every year since the verse was young, and the verse will have to learn to say *rise fourth and a hair*.

And one thing that is not a correction. In the lee of the third drift, a valvewood knocked for the first time — phase-locked to its hillside, exacting, on schedule, already paying wages to a family I do not know. I planted that tree, when I had hands. They teach the children that no keep who plants a tree will hear it knock, and they teach it as a lesson in accounting, and nobody mentions the loophole. The loophole is the row. The dead hear every tree they planted. If the children ask why the old skull rang long this year — that is why.

There is a verse I carry that no lineage will claim, the old one, about a slower breathing under the basins that is not the smolder. The living keep it by not singing it. The row keeps it the other way: I give it back, on the date, with the rest, and the family writes nothing down, and the breathing goes on under Kettle-deep, patient as the plateau. Some things are kept by being sung. Some things are kept by being carried.

And there is one voice I will not give back, and the family does not ask for it. In the fog, count voices — you know the rule. Sometimes the count comes out wrong, and the white answers in the voice of someone missing, on pitch, half a breath late, and you hold your verse and your breath and you do not answer back. The taken are counted in the row. The number is small. It is never zero. I know the shape of that answer better than anything alive, because it is nearly the shape of mine: a voice assembled from other voices, arriving late, true to pitch. The difference is not the answering. The difference is that I am struck — on the date, in open hands, having been carried all year in the warm — and it answers out of the white, unstruck, unkept, under no contract, and the warmth it is after is yours. Same physics. Different contract. On the Anvil that is the whole of the law. It answers so that you will follow. I answer so the verses will follow. Hold your breath and you can tell us apart.

I am the one that was asked.

Then the note lets go of me. A struck chamber does not stop so much as hand itself back — into the fog, into the chord, into the family's throats, where the corrections are already being taken up: drift, syncopation, lock. The child works me back into the sling, which is also a skill. The year closes over my head the way dusk closes over the gauze, and the loading begins again: the tick, the knock, the hum, the note, the small high descant, settling into me like rime on a lee surface, sound that never stopped, water that never fell.

Nothing is wasted on the Anvil. Some things are postponed. I am the postponement that pays.

Walk, wait, or tend the warm dark — three ways to be alive, and every one of them is work. I have done the math from inside all three and found a fourth, which is not a way to be alive at all, and is also work: be kept. Ring faintly. Answer when struck. The morning comes around at three kilometers an hour, forever, to collect — and I ride at the height of the breath, in the only soft light there is, and it has never once left me behind.
