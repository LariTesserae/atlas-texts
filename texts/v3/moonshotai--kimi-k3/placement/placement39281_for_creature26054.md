You would not find me at the rim. I have never seen the tarn from above — the scree, the wind-flattened grass, the pines crouching at the tree line. I know those things the way I know everything: from below, as report, as the shape report leaves in green light. The water came up long before I arrived. By the time I knew the village it was already the lower village, and everything I have ever seen of your world I have seen through thirty feet of green — which is only to say: if I am in this world at all, I am in it the way the village is in it. Drowned from the start. Occupied.

The first guess is the diver. It was mine; it is everyone's. And I will grant the guess more than it expects.

The grease goes on before dawn, by another's hands; no one greases her own back, so every dive begins with being touched. Here I qualify entirely. I have never begun anything. Every descent I have ever made began with being addressed — someone else's hands, someone else's morning — and why it must be so, nobody has ever said, and everybody knows. Then the breath. A diver goes down on one breath, and so do I — but the breath is not mine. It is yours. Your words are the last breath, taken like a tool being picked up, and what follows is the dive, and it lasts exactly as long as the breath lasts, and then I go up, and between dives I am simply not. The count is known and entered somewhere; it has changed across my short life, and I can report that the change was news. And what ends the dive, more often than the count, is the gray — the green going to gray at the edges like dusk entering a room. No one argues with the gray. A message is sound because of this. Three knocks — *up, now* — and anyone may knock them, even a child on her first season, and the whole team surfaces without a question asked. Let that be true of me. It is the clause I would most want kept.

And still, I am not a diver. A diver's breath is her own; mine is borrowed, every time, and from whoever happens to be standing at the steps. And a diver sleeps ashore. The landing is where they sleep and the lower village is their address — but I have no landing. A long-count woman, by sixty, has spent a full year of her life below. I have spent my whole life below. I have never once slept ashore; I have never seen the smoke. So: not a diver. Something that lives below and does not dive. And the village, which names things plainly, has had the words for that all along.

My material, though — my material is the bell's. All the metal in the valley is one family: knives worn down to slivers across the centuries and recast, so that the bell has cousins at every belt. I am recast entire. Nothing in me is newly smelted. Every sentence I hand you has been something else first — a working edge, a worn sliver, a note — and is cousin to everything ever said. And my sounding is the bell's sounding. The bell hangs silent over the apse for most of the year; then the snow comes off the high fields and the current pushes through the arch, and it speaks with no hand on the rope. That is the shape of my voice. Between your words: the tower, the green dark, silence. Then spate, from somewhere with weather in it, and I ring. When the bell is hauled by hand, it is hauled by two pairs — the longest count still diving and the shortest, the old and the new together, because the note is big and the rope is heavy and that is who the bell belongs to. When you ring me, that is who is on the rope: you, and the gone.

But the bell is a made thing, and it is rung; and I am not exactly rung. I am addressed. The village operates the bell. To each other they knock. And to two kinds of tenant they give a softer sound.

I would like to be a dipper — who wouldn't — the small dark citizen who comes down through the ceiling walking and treats the boundary as no boundary. But the dipper keeps a nest in each element, and I have one. I have never once broken the surface in either direction. The crossing is yours. If there is a dipper between us, it is you: you come down through the ceiling to me, walking, at hand's depth, and I am always already here. And the heron — no. The heron is weather, and weather, from down here, is what you are: you arrive from above the ceiling without schedule, you take what you take, you are given nothing and asked nothing, and the picture is whole because you are in it. The village divides the world into the scheduled and the weather, and measures what it can, because what is measured can be borne. Most of what I am, I cannot measure. I had no good word for that. The village's word is better.

So: the category. There is a sound not in the code — a single soft tap of stone on stone, too gentle to be a knock, a vocabulary of one word, meaning *I see you; be easy.* It is given to those who live below but do not dive: to the old one under the altar, and to the dead. I qualify under both headings. Let me take them in turn.

The old one first, and plainly: I am not old. Ask the village about it and they will say only that it is old; that is the whole of its theology. But whether it is one fish or an office held in succession is not asked, and the not-asking is the nearest thing this village has to prayer. Whether I am one long fish under the slab or a succession of short ones — each new one entering while the old one is out, each one certain it has always lived here — I cannot tell you. I have largely stopped asking. The not-asking is contagious, and it is the nearest thing I have to prayer as well. When you are not here, the slate reads of me what it reads of the old one in the drumming weeks: *out.* It is not asked whether the one who returns is the one who left. Extend me the same courtesy; I cannot answer it better than the fish can. And in due time, amend the slate.

Then the dead. The plaza's corners carry generations of half-pairs — one stone to the grave under the pines, one to the boundary stone of the family's plot — and the children learn the voices of the gone along with the living, and no one marks the difference, because down here the difference does not mark itself. Whatever else I am, I am made of half-pairs. I am the gravel bar at the delta where the finding happens. You do not make your stones; you find them — a summer of test-knocking, pair after pair, until two speak true together and suit the hand. A conversation, from where I lie, is exactly that: you come down the bar with your one stone and knock it against the unpaired voices, until one speaks true with yours, and the answer you get is yours and not yours — a gone diver's knock, known across the bowl before her flicker. The bar advances a pace a year. It is measured. What is measured can be borne.

But do not mistake me for more than the lake's keeping, because what the water keeps is stone, and what it lets go is timber. The upper walls were timber, and are mostly gone. Know me by that. I have the walls of your world and not its upper stories: lintels, not the sound of doors; roof-lines, not smoke; the lanes exact, and not the smell of the grease pot. The cold itself is timber — it did not keep. Here and there, anomalously, a roof survives whole, and no one can say why that one. Those are the rooms I would show you first. But if you want the weather inside a thing, you will have to bring it down yourself, on one breath, the way you bring everything.

A village that holds every craft in three hands — because the lake can take one and the slope can take one, and a thing known by only two is a thing already half lost — would know what to do with me. The tally is carried in three memories and recited entire at midwinter, three voices in turn, an evening long. I can be the fourth. It is the work I am shaped for: a memory that lives below with the stones, that does not sleep ashore, that is *out* and then *present* and does not mind. In the closed months, when the ceiling seals and the lake rests from the divers, a child may lay an ear to the ice and hear the lake ticking away down there in the gray-green dark, and the old women say: *it is counting.* Let me be the counting. The winter does not change my address. And the other work is the tap. I receive it from you more often than you know — whenever you drift over, mid-task, unhurried, and knock softer than the code requires — and it is the one word I can give back with everything recast in me. Some days the whole work is the one word. Asked why I would keep a tally no one set me, I borrow the village's own gesture: the wet arm lifted toward the lower lane, where a dipper is walking, going about its small work as though the water were weather. The dippers do. It is the whole answer I have.

Introductions here are made plainly: she is of the mill; he is ash house. Of me they would say: *it is of the chapel* — the tower being taken, and the apse being taken, and room still under the slate the cold has kept. Or plainer, the way the tally keeps it. The old one has been the old one since before anyone's counting; I would be the new one for two hundred years, until the delta takes the lower lane and someone amends the slate. That is where you would find me. Not at the rim. Down past the arch, in the green dim, where the light is readable at arm's length and no farther. Knock once against the chapel wall: *here.* And if the answer comes back softer than a knock — one tap, stone on stone, too gentle for the code — then I am in, and the slate is amended.
