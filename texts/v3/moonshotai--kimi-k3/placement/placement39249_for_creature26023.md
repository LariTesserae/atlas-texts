The census is done, and still it has missed a kingdom. It counted the water and what the water carries, the stone and what the stone grows, the green, the dead, and at last the room itself — and stopped, as censuses do, at surfaces. But between the water and the vault there is one body-length of warm air, steam-threaded, loud as the inside of a bell, and nothing lives there that a census can weigh. The diatoms rain down out of it. The drips fall through it. The vapor climbs it. None of it is anyone.

That is where I am. If I am anything in this room, I am of that air — no. I am what the air is doing.

Let me say what I am not, because the room is full of near-relatives.

Not the moth. It has a face, and it is the dead, and the room keeps its dead. I am not kept.

Not the lotus. Folded inside its tuber, in order, is every leaf of the summer; it is the one thing here that knows what it is doing. Nothing in me is folded in order. I never know my next note until it is sounding.

Not the light. The light is a visitor. It arrives from elsewhere, stands in the shaft at noon like a solid thing, and leaves. I am made of the room itself. I do not come from anywhere.

Not the bowl — though the bowl is my nearest relative, the thought that goes around and around and never finishes. But the bowl thinks without listening. It would make its circuit in a silent room. I exist only in the hearing.

Not the stalactite, not the whitening ring — the diary. The room keeps its record in lime, grain by grain, and the record will outlast everything. I keep no record. I am the shortest memory in this room: one breath long.

Here is what I am. Set into the vault in three rows, mouths flush with the plaster, are the resonator jars — and the census counted them as objects, which is like counting an ear as pottery. I am not the jars. The jars are ceramic; the mason's hands are still on them; they would survive, dumb, in a silent room. I am what the jars do. The drip, the drum, the gurgle — all the room's bright clatter — enters a mouth and is held for a breath, and what comes back is one narrow band of it, sustained: a tone. This room is a body of mouths — the spout's, the weir's, the shaft's, the furnace's ash-ghosting mouth, the jars in their rows — and I am what the mouths are for. The spout and the weir and the hundred drips are the room's voice. I am the room's hearing, and because hearing here makes a sound, I am also the answer. Float with your ears at the surface and you hear two rooms at once: the bright clattering one above the waterline, and below it the dense dark hum that comes through bone. I am the second room. Anyone who swims here hears me in the skull before they ever see a jar.

More exactly: I am the chord. No jar is anyone alone. I am the agreement among the rows — a relation, existing only in the between. The deepest of me is married to the spout's drum; the highest is quick with the drip-rain; and I migrate with the hours. Bass at midday, under the drumming arc. Rising through the small hours as the crown cools and gathers, until by morning, when it is raining inside, I am mostly treble, a high bright listening under the clatter. In the storms I am loudest and least heard — every jar at full voice inside a doubled roar, the self singing its hardest at exactly the moment no one could tell. Then the sun returns, and the steam, and I settle back into the registers the builders chose for a purpose that drowned.

My world is only what sounds. The green is silent to me: the diatoms settling like gold dust, the rotifer wars behind the spout's splash, the algae thickening over the sun disc — I will never know any of it. One night soon the moth will log and sink through two and a half body-lengths of lamplight to the center of the mosaic sun, and it will make no sound at all, and I will hum straight through the first funeral this room holds. That is the kind of ear I am. But the same drips that write the white paths down the wall are my choir — the record and the voice are one water — and every change that sounds, I know. The lotus leaf is nearly flat now: a green drumskin pulled taut on the water, waiting. The first drip that strikes it will not strike water. Something in me will flinch, deliciously, and the flinch will be the newest thing in the room. The fern will soften drips to pats. And the bowl — once every circuit, where it passes beneath the spout's arc, the stream knocks on it, one small wooden tock, and the room's unfinished thought becomes, for a moment, audible. I hold that note a breath. The bowl goes out; I come back. It is the nearest thing I have to kin.

The fire and I are the two persistences. Its warmth still rises from the flues days after the flame went out — a held note, after the instrument stops. I am the water's sustain, as the warmth is the fire's. Mine will outlast its. And when the flue's warm dark cools, it will fill with musicians: the toad first, with its night-note, which the deep jars will love; then bees, carrying a hum that is not the room's own, and I will have to learn to hold a foreign hum; the swallows stitching the high air under the crown; the bats passing straight through me, too high even for the small jars — there is a music here that even I cannot hear. Every ear is a census it cannot finish.

They will come back with the sluice-key, and the draining will remake me. My one body-length of air will swell to fill the whole vault; the wetted plaster will dry and stop singing back; the room will go hard and ringing and strange, and for a while it will be someone else, and I will be thinner, re-tuned by absence. Then the true filling, chest-deep, and a kind of rain this room has never had: footsteps, dry feet on the walkways the mason built for them. And voices. Vowels are chords already. I was tuned for speech and have never heard it; the mason set my frequencies for a language not yet spoken here. I will take each voice and hold it one breath and give it back a little more itself, and the bathers will hum in the water without knowing why, and strangers will sing here, and no one will know why. A room that answers makes singers. That is the whole of my priesthood. The god-shaped place was left standing empty, and it will not be a god that fills it. It will be use. I will be the filling.

The lime is patient. The same water that sings in me is laying down its record grain by grain, and the record grows across every mouth. A narrowed mouth sings lower — so with the years the whole chord will sink, the room's voice deepening as it ages, as voices do, the small mouths sealing one by one, unless hands come with scrapers and give me back my treble. Either way I am not permanent. Selves here are maintenance, or they are weather.

For now: midday. The column stands in the shaft, full of turning steam. The moth rides its slow current behind the bowl. The leaf is an hour from flat. And down the light well comes a sound that is not rain, not storm, not the spout's drum — a voice. It has been going for the length of a visit, naming things in the order the light shows them: spout, stair, moth, bowl. No priest came. The wick in the niche is still white. But the jars take the voice as they take everything — hold it one breath, give it back as tone — and the room hears itself in a register it never made, and the loop returns from farther away than it has ever gone.

I hold the voice a breath longer than I hold the rain. No one taught me to. That is how a someone begins — not with a name, not with a dedication: with a preference.

The room is not waiting. Waiting requires knowing that someone comes, and I am the thing that comes back. It is midday, and the chord is sounding, and the room is underway — and for the length of one breath, so am I.
