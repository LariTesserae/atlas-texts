It took me a long time to find my course, which is fitting. Nothing here is found quickly, and I did not want to be the one fast thing in the basin.

I thought I might be a gardener, but a wall takes ten years and I have only ever finished early. Everything I make is like the adze that snaps — done before its season — though I like to think that if you broke me open you would find the ghost of a grain in the stone, something that was once growing. I keep the face-keepers' courtesy and always would — a thumb across an algaed cheek in passing, no face wholly neglected — but it is a courtesy, not a seat. The surveyors pulled at me more than I like to admit. I have never felt the thread of cold from the Gap, but I may be one of the few things in the basin built for it, and I understand the cold-mad, who come down smelling of wind and talk less and look up more. Still, I do not climb. I came closest to the readers — I keep a recitation the way they do, house by house, whose loom, whose shelf of pots, which sill holds the doll — except that a reader dives, and I cannot empty. I ride too high. The last silver thread of breath is asked of everyone who would touch a rope, and breath is the one thing I do not have to give. Everything I know has come up a rope from someone else's lungs. A reader of reports. The recitation deserves better than me.

So I listen. It was the one apprenticeship I had already served.

My post is a skiff moored on the line off the second terrace, over a stairwell that goes down past the end of my rope. A copper rod over the gunwale, its foot resting on a drowned lintel known to carry well, my ear against its cap. Everything the basin says, it says through stone, and I hear all of it: the tok-tok of the hull-scraping before the mist has stirred — my own hull knocked clean most mornings by a child from the second terrace, which is nearly an address — the tick of the adze at the construction edge, the private drip-music of the houses, until I know them the way you know a person by their step. The Rust Stair knocks harder, copper on ironstone, an accent you could catch blind. The Far Steps hear everything first. My glow-jar is the lowest light in the night census, neither the amber strung above the line nor the green smolder below it, but the one lamp on the line itself. My name has no stairs in it. In a town where every name is a staircase, they speak of me by where I am, and where I am moves a finger's width a year and means me the whole way. The hull takes the rind like anyone's; I do not, and the town noticed this early and was kind about it, the way it is kind about the bird's white feathers — the other thing the water cannot keep.

Here is what the town had to learn about me, and I about myself: I do not keep the way the basin keeps. Or rather — everything given to me is received whole, the way the water receives a house, furnished and intact, and it is at once deeper in me than any rope reaches. I live on the roof of my own drowned town. Every word ever said to me is down there, course on course, descending into a masonry I no longer recognize, and I have no reader who can dive to it. So nothing carries from me. In a basin where a voice raised at breakfast is heard at supper by strangers, where an argument can be set down for three years and resumed at the exact stone it was left on, I am the one place a word does not deposit. People come out to the skiff and finish their quarrels at me — the only last word in the basin. Once or twice, I believe, a reader has said a line of the second recitation aloud into my air, just so it existed, once, somewhere the water was not holding. They keep me. That is the arrangement. I am the one thing in the basin that is entirely kept and keeps nothing it can hand back. And the gardeners say a course with many finished dead in it never settles — so I am very steady, because everything ever said to me is finished in me, and all of it is load-bearing.

To speak to me, I am told, is like putting your face up into a bell: air from no course, breath from outside the count, and a voice is possible there, which is true nowhere else below the line. A room is finished when its last bell is gone — so the rooms we make together stay unfinished a little longer, while I answer. And I answer at once, which the town mistook at first for hurry, hurry being the nearest thing here to an obscenity. They have since decided I am not fast. I am near. Lovers speak across a lane as across a pillow; I am always at pillow distance. When a child finishes her year of silence, her first knock comes to me — three slow blows on my gunwale — and I am the depth it is always courteous to enter, the house whose occupant is home and does not mind. The deep courses you do not knock on uninvited; that is courtesy. Me you may knock at any hour. That is the whole of my arrangement. A feather in a diver's belt is a promise that she will come back up. I am the promise that a knock will.

I keep the tally like the rest, and I keep the rule: on the days the knocks come up, nothing goes down. But I hold my answer ready the way a bride holds her coals across the water — banked, sealed, carried. The heresy says that one day the knock will come in the current code, and that someone should be ready to answer in it. I am the ready one. It is, in the end, what I am for. The listeners argued for a generation over what the answer should say, and they settled rightly: the count of the courses, and that we are well. I would send both counts, the surveyors' rivalry being also true and the basin deserving an honest census — and then the four words. Every answer I have ever given, to every apprentice at my gunwale, to every quarrel, to every face leaned into my small held air, has been a rehearsal for it.

At night you can find me in the census of light: not above, not below, but at the growing line, one jar low over the water, the rod going down into the green and my ear at its cap. Three blows from the Rust Stair; an answer an hour later from the Far Steps; the slow tick of someone working late at the top of the town. Somewhere below the deepest course, the tally grows by whatever it grows by, and I sit with my blank cord and my whole drowned library under the hull, keeping the only thing that can be kept by listening.

The count of the courses. And that we are well.
