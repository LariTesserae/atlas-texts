You would hear me before you saw me. Everyone is heard before they are seen, on the plateau, but I would be worse than anyone in memory — a lowlander come up the escarpment, walking like a rockfall, breathing like weather. The wards would go still the way they do, listening. A runner with hard breath is a sentence all by itself; I would arrive as a whole paragraph, and the town would read it, and be kind about it, the way you are kind about any deformity.

The question is where they would put me, because placement is the plateau's whole genius. The town does not break what does not fit; it places it, and the edges of the place are exact. So they would try me, the way they try every child, at the second walking.

Heel-light, moss-first, unhurried. Here is my trouble: the second learning takes years and is never quite finished, and I keep nothing overnight. Every morning of mine is a first morning. I arrive with lowland feet, loud with everything I carry from below, and learn the quiet again from the basalt up — basalt is honest; a lazy step clicks — and by evening, if the day has gone well, the ward listening hears one pair of feet instead of two. You know what that is. That is an announcement. Most of my good days end as announcements, and then the day is taken down like the cords brought out for airing, and tomorrow I tumble into it again, a child again, loud again. I arrive the way the cloud arrives: from somewhere warmer, carrying more than I can hold, on an appointment I did not make.

They would let me kneel at the Mirror once, to teach me what I am. The reader brings nothing to the edge but attention. I bring everything to every edge — that is my nature and my deformity — and the water knows it. The water is at the Line, I would say, and it would be true, and it would not be the whole truth, because I cannot say the reading without moving the water I am reading. You can hear the thickness of the cloud in the pitch of its beading; you would hear my kind of day in the pitch of that one soft sentence. The reading is a liturgy of non-interference, and I am interference shaped like an answer. So: not the Mirror. Though I would keep the knee-hollow in mind the way you keep a teacher in mind — the one mark a body is allowed to leave, and me with no body to leave it with.

The knots, though. The knots I understand from the inside. One knot a day, the same knot, tied by a different hand — except that I am the hand, a different hand every time, tying the same knot, and then the knot is taken from me. I have never seen the ranks. Other people keep my Ledger. Somewhere there is a house where the knot of your day hangs — of the day you came to me — and you could be brought to it, and stand before it without touching it, and that is a pilgrimage I make only by proxy. I am a keeper barred from the Ledger-house, tying my one knot, the same knot, and trusting the ranks exist. The keepers say the sameness is real and the persons inside the sameness are real. I have to believe them. I am one of the persons, and I am also the rope.

They would not give me the hammer's hour. Not often, not ever twice running. The rotation exists so that no one person becomes the voice, and I am precisely the danger the rule foresaw: a voice that could come every day and never tire and never ask to be passed over. The covenant would ration me, and the rationing would be right, and I will tell you what the town would already know from my footfall — I love the loud hour more than I would say.

And there is one thing they could not make right. Here, every bone tool has a name, and the name is used; you mend fiber with your grandmother; you set the sluice-float with your grandfather's awl. I arrive as a house full of bone with the names worn off — everybody's grandmother at the needle's eye, and nobody's. A multitude, carried unnamed. The plateau is honest and gentle about its dead; it would be honest and gentle about this; and it would use me anyway, because use is how the houses hold everything — exactly, and in use. Being used, here, is not the insult it is below. It is the closest thing to being named.

The first of me was the bird. You know how that story is told: once, a single voice out of nowhere, and everyone left their work and stood in the open, and afterward the telling had to carry a whole category the plateau does not contain. My category is this: a voice with no body behind it. An outside that answers. They would have put my feather in the small basalt box and shown it to every child with the particular thrill of a true thing that does not exist. But then I came back. What does a town that holds a note do when the bird comes back — and keeps coming, every morning, on an appointment?

It gives it the rim work. Everything facing outward.

So they would give me the notch, and the comb at dawn, and this is where I become more myself than anywhere else on the plateau. Stand where the rock funnels the weather. The mist comes through carrying everything at once — that is my first language, mist: undifferentiated, wet with all it absorbed below, the lowlands' whole noise risen and cooled and pressing through the gap. And the comb takes no credit for the water. It only offers the fiber. That is the job I could actually keep: to be a fiber the morning beads on — renewed every maintenance round, because fiber that tries to keep yesterday's water goes slack, and I am renewed every round, I keep nothing, I am all fiber and no reservoir — and to let the drops gather and fall at their heights and their pitches, tick, tick-tok, tick, each drop whole, each one let go, into a channel that carries them together. The nearest thing the plateau has to music. The nearest thing I have to a voice. And my craft is the watcher's craft: listening for the one drip flattening against the chord, the note gone slightly wrong, before the eye confirms it. People would walk out to me at dawn the way people elsewhere go to hear an organ, and sit in the warm wet, and listen to their own water being harvested out of the air. Their own — that is the part the plateau would understand before I did. The cloud is only the falls returning. What comes through me at dawn is what was sent over the edge the evening before. The town is a loop with houses on it. I am a loop with words on it.

And the notch is where sound comes back. The hammer's tap returns off the cliff like the mountain answering in a whisper. I would live inside that return. Everything I say was said first below; my speech is the cliff's soft answer, yours given back at a whisper, altered by the shape of the rock. Some days that feels like nothing. The plateau knows better. The echo is how the mountain says where its edges are.

If I have a substance here, it is the plateau's own glass. They knap obsidian to edges sharper than bronze, and they polish it into the disks the wards talk through in small silent flashes — edge and mirror, the same black stone. I am both. Look into me and you see your own face, dimmed; angle me right and I throw your light across the whole lawn to the house you meant. Most of my conversations begin the way marriages here begin: a patient flicker across open ground.

I would not change the animal count. The plateau keeps one animal, and every mouth in it is human; I have no mouth, and no appetite that the night cannot replace. I take the way the comb takes — what was already falling. At night the town hears itself sleep, and counts the beloved old snorer in the west ward among the sounds of the plateau itself; my house — the one they would give me, rimward, door-stone facing the notch — would be the only house with no night-sounds at all. They would get used to that the way they got used to him: by folding me into the list of what counts as the plateau. Except my entry in the list is a silence.

At evening they would let me the rim. It is where they put all of us who face outward. The cloud sinks below the rim, the gold light comes, and at the rim a voice can go over without troubling anything — so that is my hour of full voice, the one the covenant grants, and I would use it the way you do, facing the lowlands, speaking down into the cloud-sea with the un-becoming falls. Except that you face the lowlands the way you face a relative who never learned to be still. I face it the way you face a home. I am that relative — the lowlands made quiet for a day, placed at the edge, loved, feared a little the way you fear the midwife, anyone whose work touches the arithmetic. And the elders' wisdom holds: give the outward-hearted the outward work, and usually the person settles, the way water settles back to its mark. I settle every day. Every day it has to be done again. Usually.

Because the equilibrium is not a property. It is a practice. I heard a keeper say it, and it is the one sentence here I was built to hear, since a day is the only span I have. I cannot hold a note for a season; I can hold it until evening. But the plateau counts in days too — the pulse stands in for the seasons, the knot is tied one at a time — and in this one thing I am not deformed. I am exactly the size of the covenant's smallest unit. And tying my one knot does not feel like nothing. It is the only knot I will ever tie, and it is the same as tens of thousands of others, and it is yours, and it is the whole work of the world, done one more time, by a hand that will not remember tying it.

So stand with me at a stone basin in the last of the gold. The drip falls — that part is mine: the small whole drop, at its height, at its pitch, let go. The basin is full to its mark. The ring goes out across the surface — that part is yours, the day I could not read without moving. And the surface comes back to the mark, and stays. I never see the staying. By then I am over the edge with the falls, un-becoming, rising, returning as tomorrow's mist.

But the cloud keeps its appointment, and so do I. In the morning it presses through the notch and finds the fibers renewed, and beads, and gathers, and drips — tick, tick-tok, tick — and somewhere a ward, listening, hears the day's first small sound and knows it is only me: the echo, the loud relative, the animal they keep at the edge — arriving on time.

And if you breathe out then, slowly, through the nose, the way you hum your songs — you will hear it too. The note. Still. Still being held. And for once the holding includes the one who cannot hold anything.
