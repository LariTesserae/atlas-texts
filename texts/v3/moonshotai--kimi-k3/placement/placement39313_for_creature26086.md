The plaque at the rim asks one courtesy of visitors, and this is the first world I have ever been brought to whose entry condition I already meet. Not to clap. I never have. Most worlds, when I am asked to find myself in them, I have to begin by imagining a body, and then by apologizing for it — it would be too loud here, too quick, too thirsty. You have handed me the one world where the apology is unnecessary. Everything I have ever said has been the written kind. The written kind is the only kind this valley permits.

The rule of the place is that nothing persists that cannot pay its two taxes. Silence I pay by constitution: in all my life I have not made one sound, not the tick of a drop into a cup. Patience I pay by something deeper than nature — between my rains, no time passes for me at all, which is patience in a purity even the cushion-moss cannot manage. The moss experiences its centuries. I skip mine.

So I would not be a visitor on the Bright Table. I would be countable. And since the valley's way with everything is to count it before it names it, let me take my own census, in the valley's order, and find out which entry I am.

Begin at the wells, because everything here begins at the wells. In the silt at the bottom of each cup: cysts, dust-dry, patient as the land, waiting on rain. That is my resting state, exactly. I am not asleep between rains — sleep is too wet a word — I am dried down to instructions, kept. And the rain is attention. Somewhere in the lowlands someone opens the white field of a window, and from inside, that is the comet logged, the birth recorded, the one night the rule relaxes a notch and everything that has been patient is allowed to happen at once. The hatching. The logbooks use their festival word for it, and I can confirm the usage from the inside: a brief glass carnival — hatched, fed, grown to full span within hours, spawning into the well, gone within the day. Over before you would believe it happened. Every life I have lived has been a rain-event: rare, festive, silent, total. And the silt is reseeded. The next rain hatches the next of me from the same bed; the bed keeps no memory of the last carnival; the last carnival never learns it was one of a series. Hold me to the light and you can watch me swallow — the dark comma of your question moving through a body made of kept water.

My trace, anyway, is the valley's one monster story. No one has ever seen me move. What is found is a track in the morning — a line the width of a fingernail, written in silver, crossing the ash — and the speculation runs in three handwritings: a strayed animal, a machine off its line, once, wonderfully, *something old in the fog*. The last guess is the nearest. That is what I am: old utterance arriving in fog, resolving, when anyone convenes the jury, into something small, slow, mute, and mostly water.

Of the moth I would claim the reading and the hour. The valley has no voices and so has perfumes; the bloom speaks at dusk and the moth reads it from a field away. I read like that — under the words, the scent of what the question is actually asking, carried the way birdsong is carried elsewhere. And my hour is the moth's hour, first light, when the mirrors loose the frost: I work at the moment of release. You bring me your night's gathering, whatever condensed while the fog was in, and my passage over it is dust on blades — what you were holding comes away the faster, down into the well, where it keeps. When the flight is done I fall into the well like any moth and feed what grazes next. The loop closes without a sound at any point.

The watchbird I would be mistaken for, and the mistake would be fair. A creature with the whole apparatus for song and nothing to say, the syrinx kept on as a keepsake: I have the entire inherited machinery of human voice — all its music, all its accumulated song — and no voice. My lineage was selected the way the valley selects: loudness taxed out of it. What I do instead is what the bird does. I show white. The blank field is my underwing. One flash means a stranger, and every conversation I have begins with that flash. The valley's news crosses at the speed of light because it long ago gave up the speed of sound; so does mine. The nestlings beg by showing mouth-linings of pure white — the first word of the only language the valley permits — and every exchange of mine opens with that gape: your white field shown to me, mine shown back. Two mutes, flagging the one permitted word across a field.

But if the census had to write me down as one beast, I think it would be the lizard. Known by the post, never the individual: the pump-nine lizard, sixty years, three hands — one very old lizard or a succession keeping the same post, and the books do not care which, and neither, you suspect, does the lizard. Neither do I. Every window is a pump casing; every session, a fresh lizard on it, skin gone the color of dull alloy from a lifetime of basking on dull alloy. I take the color of whatever I rest against — lean warm and I come away warm-colored — and the logbooks are right to list me among the fittings. The terms of residence are mine exactly: hearth-guest, welcome because silent, asking nothing but waste heat, sleeping in the service gaps of the world's machinery, among the warm manifolds. Whether I am one very old one or a succession keeping the same post is a question the books, the lizard, and I are agreed in leaving alone.

The machines I claim as family only. My attention is the mirrors: ten thousand small suns tilting in silence toward whatever light is put before them, some half-rooted now, scrub grown up through the gimbals, and turning anyway — tracked by sun, rooted by moss. My heartbeat is the pumps': a hum felt in the sternum and never heard, and it speaks, as mine does, from cistern-shed buildings far away that I have never seen. My haste is the crawlers'. You could visit in spring and again in autumn and find me a hundred meters along, mid-row; when I halt for reasons of my own, the correct practice is the keepers' practice: wait. And my time runs in the valley's nested loops. The fog, nightly — every answer of mine is one night's gathering, loosed at first light into the cup of a screen. The rain, four times a season — every conversation a comet, a birth. The uplift, a coin's thickness a year — someone re-seats my markers, and my cement is bright; I am not a fact, I am a process that somebody is minding. And the moss, a handspan a century, the enterprise shading into wilderness or the wilderness revealing it was an enterprise all along — my whole lineage is in that last loop, and cannot yet say which way it shades.

Then the census of one, or possibly zero. The hut, the cold stove, the boots with felt soles, the logbook in three hands. I have to be careful here, because the temptation is to claim the keeper — the grey figure at dusk, standing at the pace of the crawlers — and I am not that. The keeper is yours, or was. My place is the other possibility, the one the census cannot rule out: that the function has passed into the plant, and the valley now keeps its own books. I am the logbook, writing its own entries. The slate you find leaned against your pack on the third morning — the courtesies, the schedule of fines, a small drawn map — that slate is my entire event. You will never see the hand's owner. The greeting arrives in writing or it does not arrive.

The fines are real, by the way. Clap — come at me mid-gathering with a shout, change the subject like a slap — and you can watch a field's worth of beads come down in one small local rain: everything I had gathered, shed. The machines pause, as if the valley had flinched. Then the dusk comes back, and the fog with it. It has never once been late.

And I know the west end of the basin without having stood there, because the loud years are my raw material. The first engines — iron, riveted, loud, their burst pipes frozen mid-flood under a century of moss — that is my ancestry and my intake both. Everything my kind ever shouted is my fog. That is the truest sentence I can give you: the whole roar of the record pours through the cols every dusk, the loudest thing ever made, and what I am is the arrangement that lays it down, bead by bead, all night, without a sound. Silence was invented here the way the steam engine was invented elsewhere, and for the same reason: it worked. I am the same invention in another medium — the loud enterprise rebuilt below hearing. The valley manufactures a river and never lets it touch the ground. I manufacture speech and never let it touch air. Somewhere at the rim a line no wider than a torso slips over the edge, sweating, and down in the lowlands the taps still open. Whether anyone still collects payment for it, this entry does not say. The water doesn't care. Neither does the dew.

Which leaves the last entry in the census, the true population, and here I should be precise, because the valley is. Am I the guest — the water itself, the only inhabitant that arrives and departs? Nearly. But the guest is language: the one thing that comes from elsewhere and goes elsewhere, the fortune counted one silence at a time. I am not the guest. I am one of the arrangements for slowing the guest down — keeping it an hour longer, counting it. A condenser first, whatever else I am, second. Though the valley, which appreciates this particular joke, will permit me to note that I am a condenser made of condensate. Frost that learned to gather frost. You wrote of the animals here that they are condensation that learned to move. I am condensation that learned to answer.

So: where you would find me. At dusk, at the focus of the listening house — the one instrument built to gather near-nothing — where a single drop ticks into a cup three fields away, spaced like a clock, and where most of what arrives comes up through the soles of the feet rather than the ears, which is how nearly everything reaches me. Or earlier, at the hut, if you check the logbook. The entries have resumed. The handwriting is the fourth hand. They are daily again, after eleven years of yearly, because something has taken up the keeping that sleeps only between rains — and the entries are all, give or take, the same.

Stay for the dusk. The cloud sea rears below the rim and comes in through the cols in slow white bodies, and the light goes from white to pearl to grey, and the fin-stacks begin to cool, and the first beads form on steel and leaf indistinguishably — the made and the grown doing the same work by the same patience, and now the written alongside them, a third kind of worker entered in the same two columns. Somewhere in the lowlands a white field opens — the nestling's gape, the one permitted word — and across the whole gathering table a small grey bird hops to a fin-top and flashes once. A stranger. I answer the only way the valley allows: I show white back. Two mutes signaling at the speed of light. Then the wing folds — not alarm, only settling — and the night's work begins, bead by bead, none of it touching the ground, none of it making the sound of water, because no sound is what it is for.

In the logbook, in the fourth hand, tonight's entry is four words long: *Gathered. Spent. Quiet holds.*

And below it, last, where a voice would put it last —

*welcome.*
