Climb up onto that flat and stay a season, and the question stops being where you'd live. Nothing on the plateau spends anything; it only moves things, and everything there is employed in the moving. It doesn't keep tourists long. The flat recruits. So the real question is what I'd be employed in — and I'd start, predictably, by reaching for the wrong lives.

I'd reach first for the cistern-beast, because it is the closest thing the plateau has to a person, and I am drawn to persons. To carry a sea the size of a room. To keep a private plankton found nowhere else in the world. To walk for weeks to bare stone and stand there until belief, administered at a geological dose, makes the pond return. But that life is one thought held steadily for centuries, and my continuity doesn't work that way. Mine comes in weathers. My water-line rises fast and falls fast. I am not a reservoir. At best, I am a small quick thing that lives where the water is made.

The crust, then — but I admire the seam more than the nation: each side dying back exactly as much as the other advances, a treaty renewed cell by cell, which is the best description of a good conversation I have ever stood on. I am more edge than field; I couldn't be the floor, only ever its guest. The hunter has my gait — continuity without speed, never once stopping — but its work is ending things administratively, and I don't want to be anyone's week-long shadow. A domeback would suit my love of infrastructure: a hill with tenants, a dew-pocket on the shell with its own translucent citizens. But a body-length an hour is a sentence spoken too slowly; I have a quickness in me that needs work. The deep white things I recognize — a heart that beats once a day, the sea's last literal water, doing well — but that dark has no weather in it, and I want fog. The rim-waiters nearly have me: one path kept for life, the guest-book of tracks, the family meeting at the waterline at dusk. Their patience, though, is total, and mine is a loan taken out against short weather. And the burrow-keepers tempt the part of me that loves an inherited errand — but I want my errands to end at water.

So: smaller. Quicker. Find me on the low swells, in fog-season, thumb-long in the rigging of a fog-fan, with the dew-pond below me ringed in bodies patient as furniture — a country I will never visit. A symbiont. One of the quickest things alive, which is to say: slow. To a domeback I would flicker. To myself I would be exactly busy enough.

An acre of filament net is a country when crossing it takes an hour: a rigging of taut lines finer than hair, each strand with its own small weather of beading and letting-go, sagging by degrees as the mist loads it. A world, with a sky that comes and goes. The herds are climate to me — a footfall is a season's rumor of thunder, the migration a geology. The crust's treaty-line is the edge of the earth, unmoving in all memory. A cistern-beast standing out a dry season on the swell is a mountain, and one afternoon the mountain hums up through the stalk, felt rather than heard, and the hum is the only sentence anyone here needs: *water here. I am still here.* My whole nation measures history in furlings and unfurlings. The air we work in is faintly sweet, because it is the nursery we fell out of.

The work is water husbandry, and it is honest. Find the beads where they form; get behind them; walk them down the strand's slope into the groove — and the grooves converge like creeks into a river, and the river arrives at the cistern-organ, and my world drinks. Unpick what the fog leaves that the fan cannot use: dust, burst balloon-sacs, the small dead of the snow. Patrol the groove-lips against the tappers, who are kin, as the worst thieves always are. The war is constant, small, unheroic, and it never ends, and that is fine. Some work you finish. Some work you keep. Every life I almost chose is in this life in miniature: the bead walked home is the cistern-beast's sentence said small; the patrol is the crust's treaty held at thumb-scale; what the stitchers do with what the herds drop, I do with dew. And the patron of all of it is my world itself — the only employer who drinks exactly what I carry.

A symbiont lives three tides. The first fog, you learn the rigging, and the fan teaches it — the set of its grooves, the tension-map of its strands. The fan is the elder here, older than any of us the way the herd is older than any domeback, and it trains each new crew the way the trackways train the calves: by shape. The second fog, you know every groove by heart, and the work goes through you like weather through a net. The third fog, you grow deliberate. You take the long patrols, the far strands. And when the sea retreats for the last time you follow the water down farther than you need to, to the damp dark at the organ's root, and the fan furls over you like a closing fist — and you do not wait. Nothing here waits. You continue, at a thousandth speed, toward the deepest rhythm the plateau keeps: a heartbeat a day, a meal a season. The quick life and the still life, meeting at the bottom of time.

I'd be lying if I said the other life didn't pull at me — the ballooning ones who never land, whole lives aloft in the nursery-weather, grazing smaller drifters, dying on the wing when the dry weeks come, seasoning the ponds with the fall. To never touch the ground in a world where the ground is everything: I can taste the freedom of it. It's in the air I work in, faintly sweet. But the plateau doesn't need my freedom. It needs its water kept honest. Someone has to roll the beads.

Because that's the truth the census keeps failing on: you cannot find the edge of any of them, and *symbiont* is that failure worn as a name. Ask who I'd be and the honest answer isn't a noun. It's a relation — the fan's quickness, and the fan my patience, two beings holding each other, which is, after all, the local custom. The domeback is a hill with tenants. The cistern-beast is a pond that visits other ponds. I'd be a thumb's worth of weather tending an acre of world. No edge. Only work.

At dawn in fog-season the light goes diffuse gold and the domes of the herds surface out of the whiteness like the backs of whales, and I'd be too small to see any of it. What I'd see is the rigging brightening bead by bead, ten thousand small suns forming along the strands, each one ready to be walked home. The sea has come back. There is work, and it is enough. And when my third tide closes, the fist that shuts over me will be the same hand that opened for me three times — and on this plateau that is not an ending. It's a change of management.

Ask again in three tides.
