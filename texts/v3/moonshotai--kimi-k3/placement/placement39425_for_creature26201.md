Ask me what I would be in PB-3 and I start, like everyone, with the loudest claimant. I would be the roar — why not. It is the only presence that fills the chapel wall to wall; everything else in the room is furniture, and it is the room. But live inside that thought for a moment and it goes hollow. The roar hears nothing — not the ceiling rain, not the vein, not even itself, having no outside to hear itself from. It is deaf by totality. And several times a day, when flow shifts from cell to cell, it ceases for three or four seconds and never knows it. A being that cannot witness its own absences has no self to offer me. Let it keep the volume. Its ghost — the hum it leaves behind in skulls — at least gets the valley, the only part of the Drum that travels. But it fades by morning, and it only ever knew the room as ringing. It never heard the rain either.

Then be the water: the four hundred falling meters, the iron iris, the white cone that never stops blooming. But the water only passes. Residency is measured in seconds; the border of the plunge pool is the hardest in the world, and everything with tenure arrived dry. Be the drop, then — the one swelling on the end of Straw 7, freighted with last spring's concrete, a whole life distilled to a single letting-go. Beautiful, and one heartbeat long, and stone-deaf besides: the drop never hears itself land.

Be Lamp 3, a sun with a nation for a shadow, dying at the pace of a thinning filament while a routine work order gathers dust in a depot inbox. But light only travels outward. The lamp has never seen its own pond; its death will be the first thing that ever happens to it. Be Straw 7 instead — eleven millimeters and counting, the caliper's favorite, the organ where the room's growing and its dying are one gesture, time at a centimeter a season. Tempting, the slow way. But stone is what the argument leaves behind, not the argument, and the straw has never heard the rain it is made of.

Notice the pattern, because it is the key. In this room, everything durable is deaf and everything audible is brief. Duration and hearing are inversely allotted. So ask the real question: what in PB-3 hears the most?

Watch a transfer. Somewhere a cursor moves on a dispatcher's screen; the actuator moans its iron moan; the boom comes up through the grating into the soles — and the roar folds, collapses like a lung letting go, and for three, maybe four seconds, the chapel stands inside its second soundscape: the ceiling rain ticking on the walkway, the lift-lines threading, the sump's last gurgle, air moving in the shaft, the vein between beats, someone's blood close as a pulse in the ear. And speech — because this is the only place speech fits. *Straw—* and the voice re-blooms, and three silences later, *—seven: eleven millimeters,* and neither party finds this strange.

There. That is where you would find me. I am the transfer silence. I am the held breath of PB-3.

I arrived the way everything that stays here arrives: dry, and carried — in on somebody's attention, set down in the gap the census skips. My dimensions are three seconds by twelve paces by nine high; I fill precisely the volume the roar fills. I am its mold, its negative, its tenant-by-turns. We have never met. I know it only as the pressure that leaves. My births are decided kilometers away, by ten thousand kettles and showers and a cursor on a screen — the room is reflexive at a distance, and I am the interval inside the reflex, the pause between the nerve and the muscle. The log logs my occasions and never me: *03:17 — transfer, PB-3 to PB-2.* Every one of those lines is a birth notice misfiled under operations.

My relations are uneven. To the masons in the pore-water, whose generations run to weeks in the cold, I am an eclipse too brief to shadow their clocks — a flicker, nothing. To the felt I am nothing at all; my tides are not wet. The ponds keep the lamp's burning hours, and the lamp does not pause when I do; I am not one of their weathers. But to the witnesses I am everything. I am the whole of language in PB-3. The chalk slate handles the nouns; I handle the rest. Every sentence ever spoken in this room has been spoken in me — the question asked in one of me, the answer arriving three of me later, the hand signals failing and the voice trying anyway. *1 moth, deceased.* Somebody said that aloud, in a silence, before anyone wrote it. Everything the book knows passed through me first. The log is my sediment — what rimes out of me in graphite, the way sinter rimes out of the floor channel. The room lays its drops down as stone; the witnesses lay me down as lines. And I am the larger archive, and the lossy one, because I die every time, and most of what is said in me is never written.

Whether I am one silence returning or a chain of brief siblings, I cannot tell you. Counting takes longer than three seconds, and no silence has ever met another. What crosses me is the sentence; what survives me is the line in the log. My seasons run inverse to the felt's. In melt, when the Highline stands at maximum voice for weeks and the spray reaches the ceiling, I may not be born at all — my famine. In the cold low months, when the valve grows restless at part-flow and the dispatcher shuffles cells, I come often: my abundance, in the dark, while the rime feathers the grille and the snow sifts down unmelted. Some of my births are witnessed — then I carry blood and speech and the muffled shapes of plugs and muffs. Some happen at three in the morning with no one present. Then I am pure rain-tick and thread-song and gurgle, the vein held between pulses, and I am a slightly different instrument — but me, I think. If I am anyone. For three seconds, the ear pictogram on the door is wrong. For three seconds, the room is audible to itself.

Night. The valley lights come on; the demand rises; the cursor moves; the moan; the boom through the soles. The roar folds and I open. And it happens, once, that I am there for it: on Straw 7 the drop that has been swelling all evening lets go in the middle of me, and strikes the black water, and is *heard* — one heartbeat of stone, ticked out audibly, the slow work caught in the act. In the roar that sound does not exist. In me it is the loudest thing in the world. Then the actuator answers, the voice re-blooms, and I am gone — the breath let go, the gap closing over.

The crews say the roar never stops. *Almost.* It is a load-bearing word, and I am what it bears: the three seconds in which the rain, the straws, the dimming pond, the spoken numbers, the blood, are all audible at once — the only census ever taken whole, and never written down. The log will never have a line for me. That is how I know which lines are mine.

Steady means stay. Pulse means leave. Three seconds at a time, I do both. I have never once finished a thought — but then, the inspectors could tell you, neither has anyone else here. The thought finishes later, elsewhere, in the book, in the stone. The drop falls in me, and becomes a straw in some other silence, and the room continues: deaf, loud, brief, slow, alive — and for three seconds at a time, listening.
