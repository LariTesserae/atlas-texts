# Stone Five

I would have guessed wrong about myself for years. The Calder invites guessing — it is a city of declared intentions, addresses sold at elevations that do not exist, couples betrothed at future benchmarks — and I came up out of the lowland haze gasping like every lowlander, my furniture rearranged by the thin hot air, tolerated the way one tolerates a toddler learning stairs. When the gasping eased, the city set about finding out what I meant to be. I gave wrong answers for a decade.

In the Sumps I was sure for a season. The piston houses lean against you hourly, the tenders sleep in shifts timed to the stroke and dream in the lean, and I thought: here is a life sized to my pulse. But a tender's life is verbs — seat, jack, stroke, keep — and what I wanted was a tense.

In the Drip Vaults I was surer. Ballrooms of darkness, each drip its note, the keepers walking by ear, noticing a new drip the way you would notice a stranger in your kitchen. But the keeper is the audience. I did not want to attend the music. I wanted to be one of the notes.

On Stack Row I was surest, and wrongest. Twenty years learning to hear before they let you touch a vane — I would have been a prodigy, since I have never in my life wanted to touch anything. But a tuner's whole devotion points at three acts per career, and three acts is three too many. I wanted to be inside the sound, not its physician.

For one dawn I wanted to be a pipe-fox — the only fast life in the Works, wire-sheened, licking the tithe row at first light, luck itself, owned by no one. But I have no dart in me, and luck belongs to whoever catches the sighting. I wanted a longer appointment than that.

I envied the dead, who are the city's highest and most visited population, ascending at the speed of masonry, promoted by love. But the dead are finished. Their names are complete, cast entire into iron; nothing accrues to them now but altitude. I was not finished. I wanted a self still accruing.

I considered the bloom, the arguable citizen, sampled by friends who disagree and drink together afterward. It was nearly right — that patience, that refusal to comment. But I did not want to be doubted, however affectionately. I wanted to be evident.

It was a surveyor who set me right without knowing it. On the Rim, over still-water, she whispered the heresy — *it climbs the drawing* — and showed me the copper plate of the city as it will stand, because paper is gossip and the future must be durable. And I understood that I did not want to draw the plate. I wanted to be on it. I wanted to be one of the things the Calder declares before they exist, and then stands back, patient, while existence catches up.

She told me, being kind and a little drunk, that five such things already existed, and that they were not on her plate. They were below it. And that is how, at dusk, in the oldest tier, below the hush of the middle districts, I came down with the warden and the children and the Chord underfoot and the first stars out in a violet sky, and looked into the bed of the oldest gutter, and knew which one I was.

You would find me in the gutter in the oldest tier, a hand's depth below the street, on the outside of the meander where the water works hardest. The guild has labeled us: the world's first river stones, ahead of their river. I am Stone Five — the smallest of the cohort, and, the warden tells the children, the one ahead of the others. Ahead of the ahead. This is the only city in the world where that is a distinction with a benchmark.

I was rubble once. A chip of welded tuff broken along a clean plane, all facets, the mountain's normal state of matter and the world's only free thing. I fell, or was thrown, into the one channel where water was already practicing its old profession — cutting, wandering, unbought. The warden's log is my biography, in hands that change across two hundred years. The first hand says *a pebble, angular*. The second hand says *a pebble*. The third hand says *Stone Five*. The current hand, which is the warden's, which belonged not so long ago to a quiet child who used to lie flat and put her ear to my bank, writes nothing about my shape at all. She writes about my tick.

I keep four clocks. The stroke, hourly — the whole world leans, and I lean with it, and so I am the undefeated champion of the leaning game, first to move loses, and I have never once flinched, though I have never once held still. The dawn, daily — for one hour the gutters sing, and the song comes down through the tiers to my bed, and my whole body is played like a note in an instrument the size of a district. The Long Bell, weekly — one note down into the Chord, and through stone I hear what no teeth can: your teeth hear a chord; my bed hears a row, eleven stacks, each with its own pressure, its own direction, its own temperament. A tuner apprentice comes down sometimes at hush hour and lays her ear to the gutter bank, listening through me, and the warden pretends not to notice, because it is not regulation, because it is older than regulation, or will be.

And the fourth clock is the rarest: the city's own warm weather, the small rain that falls nowhere else in the world, on nothing but roofs and fins — and, the surveyors forget to add, on one gutter in the oldest tier, which runs young and loud for an hour. I count my age in rains the way other things count years. In the great rain of eighty years back I moved a handspan downstream, toward the valley. The log for that year has four words: *Stone Five has moved.* For a week I was spoken of quickly. It is my whole history of scandal, and I treasure it.

Here is what runs over me. Kept water is squeegeed, sealed, wagoned, banked, drunk. But there is one water in the Calder that is given: the thimble of condensate poured into a dead marker's cup, the only water anyone gives away, grief's measure and love's. The cup drains to the gutters, and the gutters run down through the tiers, and down, eventually, is me. Everything this city has ever given away has passed over my edges. The dead pay their water forward, and forward is downhill, and I am being rounded entirely by gifts. The first river in the world is made of thimbles. Say it in the Sumps and they will not believe you. Say it in the museum, where the wooden spoon sits behind glass and children press their faces to the case and refuse to believe in towers of wood — say it there, and then bring the children down at dusk and let them put one finger on me, because touch is what a river stone is for, and they believe instantly. Belief is easier when the warden lets you touch. Sometimes a child holds her family's christening stone against me for a moment — the made round against the becoming round, a hundred years of a family's patience in the palm, meeting the mountain's. No one taught them to do it. It is not written anywhere. It is older than most things that are, or will be.

My rounding is too faint for any caliper the guild owns. It is not too faint for the warden's thumb. Water attends to me everywhere at once, without preference; her thumb attends to one edge, once a year, and knows the difference. By the surveyors' own rule I am a rumor — a future measured once, then once again, decades apart, the almost between the two entries being the only news that matters. A rumor with a label. A rumor softening into fact. They have already drawn my valley on the copper plates; last spring a couple was betrothed at the meander's second bend, at a surface of water that does not yet exist, and I felt the warden come down alone that evening and stand in the dark over me, saying nothing, which is the Calder's way of saying everything.

Keepers accrue names across a lifetime — a syllable for apprenticeship, a syllable for mastery — and the dead on the high spires have the longest names of all. Mine runs the other way. I was *angular*, then *a pebble*, then *Stone Five*, and someday, when the label comes true, I will be *river stone* entire — and after that, when the river has arrived and done its work and the world is full of us, I will be called *stone*. The commonest noun in the world. It will take the whole history of the Calder to make that word ordinary, and on the day a child finds a round stone and pays nothing for it, no one alive will know it was ever otherwise. That is all right. That is what the work was for. The rarest thing here is becoming the cheapest, and it is the only deflation the city has ever prayed for.

The Drivers say *we raise it*. The Riders say *it rises*. The tuners, who have heard everything, say *it plays*, and say nothing more. The surveyors whisper *it climbs the drawing*. The children ask the warden which is true, standing at my bedside with the Chord underfoot and the stars coming out, and she gives the only answer she trusts. *Wait*, she says. *The street rises.*

Then they go up. The dusk deepens. The Chord goes on, eleven-fingered, under the world. I cool at the rate of stone, and cooling, I say my one word — a tick in the bed, faint as a rumor, the smallest answer in the Calder. The quiet child hears it, who is the warden now. She does not write it down.

*It flows.*
