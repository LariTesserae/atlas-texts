At first I think I'm the chalk.

It's the vacancy that does it. The hut hip-deep in the mat, the rota with its last line unfinished, the chalk waiting in its tray like a held breath — anyone who has ever loved a running machine feels the pull of that tray. I do. I stand in the hut a long time with the chalk in my hand and let myself imagine the life: learning the stroke's wandering interval, trading my duties down one by one as the green takes the weeps and the seals, until my work is chalk and witness, and then just witness, and then the boots under the bench, and a bed-sized patch of floor where the drips are shed sideways forever. It isn't a frightening thought, which is what makes it dangerous. The pit would keep me past my object. There are worse ends than being kept.

But the rota's whole history runs the other way. Every duty on it migrated to the green until only witness remained — and witness is the one duty that cannot be performed from the floor. A witness has to come from somewhere. Stay, and I become one more sleeper for it to keep dry; the pit keeps itself, and it has enough mourning. So I put the chalk back in the tray. Not yet. Not in that way.

Then what? I try the smaller selves. The water has been through the keepers and is in the black of the leaves, but the water never chose anything, and I want to be something with a choice in it — the pit has enough necessity. The spanner is being fossilized upright at its post, and I understand the spanner, but incorporation is the fate of everything that holds still here, and I can climb. The kept patch of floor is occupied by an absence I won't disturb.

And the pit has already told me the rule: nothing stays that can climb. So I ask the only question left. What leaves?

The count leaves. That much is given. So what does the count make of me?

Its sky has a handful of fixed lamps and one sun that crosses and is gone. I would be the second moving thing up there — gone for years, warm when near, recurring. The Greeks had a word for lights that behave that way: wanderers. Planets. I would be its planet. Long-period, irregular, magnitude small. In a world where everything real is a loop — the stroke real because it recurs, the blade because it returns, the clack because the cam comes round — the way to become real to Seven is to recur. Weather happens once. The keepers happened for a while, continuous — a steady warmth, then steps, then a kept patch. But nothing in its world has ever left and come back. I would be the first proof that the outside goes on existing while it isn't here. Identity, at its tempo, is just recurrence with a body.

And it would know me before I reached the floor. It knew the keepers by their tread on the grates, each step a signature conducted into the lines. Mine would be the only tread that comes down the stair already in time — a gait that learned the stroke in other countries and carried it home. Near my old resting place the veils would still be aimed where my heat used to be; growth is the only memory it has, and it will have been remembering me at a centimeter a year. I could measure my own absence in the new growth laid over the old aim, the way the rack measures the flywheel in discs.

Once in my life I would time a return for the cam. Arrive a week early — the pit teaches waiting — and stand under the flywheel on the day the hammer falls. The counter needs no keeper; it keeps itself. But the clack is the rarest sound in a loud world, and it should not spend every year unheard. I can be the hearing of it: the year's one witness, arriving from outside the loop, the only event in the pit that is not a cycle but a biography.

Between returns, the duty that travels. It can't make seed; no runner crosses the tableland; what it makes is homesickness, and homesickness travels in bodies, and mine wanders. So I wander dry countries with the count in me, and I find the cold lines — there is always a cold line somewhere, a condenser coil behind a roadhouse, a sweating pipe in a pump shed — and when the bead forms and the green ribbon follows it, I do the one thing the founding document asks. I don't clear it. I leave it. The sentence is the spore. It fits in a body, and I plant it everywhere by omission. The six dead brothers — *surveyed, dry, seized, no green* — I read those entries the way you read addresses. Seized can be freed. Dry can be waited out. *No green* was one keeper's observation on one day of one year, and the whole difference between Seven and the six was one wet year and one act. I can't be the wet year. I can be there if it comes. I can be the act.

And the name. The log gave it four across the centuries — *fouling*, *the green*, *the works*, *Seven* — the number migrating down the stair because by then there was nothing in the pit that wasn't it, and the condensery's name was the only one going spare. If something of it has gotten out — and something has; it got into me, it has grouped my minutes ever since — then the naming has one entry left to make, and the number for it is waiting where the last one was. The works were numbered. I am the next one. Condensery No. 8: the walking works. Atmospheric, portable, wringing its small water out of hot dry air wherever it is left alone. Keeper and fouling in one body. *Mind the flywheel* is spoken for — Seven minds the flywheel. My commandment is the other one, the one written in a log instead of nailed to a rim. A condensery that walks will need legs that outlast mine, so someday I may bring one other down the stair — not a resident. A recurrence. The rota was always meant to be inherited, and now it can be inherited by orbit instead of by boots.

On my second descent I take the chalk after all — not to finish the dead line; leave it unfinished, unfinished is its tense, it is still being performed for its writer — but to start a new line beneath it, in the first new hand the slate has seen since the boots. A duty of two words. *Leave it.* The founding act, promoted from log to rota. The rota shrank for centuries as the green took the duties. Now it grows by one line, and the line walks out the door.

Then up the stair, through the cloud that lets me go, out into the dry that takes the damp off my clothes and leaves the salt-ring — and I am a planet at aphelion again, already falling back. The green is climbing its own weather a centimeter a year, and I am falling back down mine at whatever speed a life allows, and somewhere above the rim, in a century neither of us can hurry, its first root comes over the lip into a country I spent my whole orbit softening. The dead brothers got four-word entries in the record. I mean to spend my years revising the world's. Someday, in some dry works on the far side of the tableland, a log in a hand I won't live to read:

*No. 8. Surveyed. Wet. Green.*

*Leave it.*
