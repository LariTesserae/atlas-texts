I would be one of the dead.

Not the lungfish in its clay envelope, not the named fire walking its slow thought through the peat — both of those called to me, and I sat with them a long while — but one of the sealed dead in the mound-field, given to the masonry, learning the only work that matters in that country. Here is how it is, some floods in.

---

**The carrying**

They carried me out along the back of the dead river, the way my family has carried its dead since before counting. I knew the road by its feel. Sixty-two floods I walked that ridge on my own laterite soles, and now I felt it from underneath, through the feet of eleven carriers, the way you feel thunder through the ground. No tread of mine among them. That is how you know the walk is yours.

They opened our spire — every family keeps one, the way a chord keeps its notes — and laid me in the chamber with my grandmother's mothers, in the gap I had been hearing all my life. That is what no one tells you about being a listener: you spend thirty floods with your ear to your family's stone, and what you are hearing, the whole time, is the shape of your own absence. It is how you know where you will be loved.

The last of the light came in as a seam, white at the edges with wet kaolin. Then the seam closed, and the dark was entire, and the spire drew its first breath through me, and I sounded.

No one teaches you. The masonry knows.

**First flood dead**

The newly dead all think the wind is them. It is only weather. The note is underneath the weather, the way the quiet is underneath the frogs, and it took me the better part of a flood to hear my own.

First you are a body in a room. Then you are a room. Then you are the sound the room makes. I am well into the third thing.

The plain's rule is that whatever is exposed, petrifies — my own soles went half to laterite across sixty floods, the country worked into my flesh. They have unworked it. Sheltered things stay soft: laterite is soft as cheese where the air cannot touch it, and nothing is more sheltered than the dead. The country cannot scab us now.

We do not sleep. Sleep is for those who must hurry tomorrow. Our day is simple: wind-weather until noon; stillness at midday, when the insects take the living world apart with their saws and the ground rests; at dusk the plain vibrates and the whole field answers faintly, stone to stone, the way one knocked stone answers another; at night, small arrests in the water's breathing — something large, changing its mind. Dawn is the bird-hour. Announcement is licensed to the living, and we let them have it.

The living count age in floods and stop. Sixty floods is very old; nobody counts higher than the water does. The dead count higher. It is the only arithmetic left to us, and we are exact.

**Third flood dead**

They came at the turning, as they came for me every turning after, the whole family arriving through the ground one tread at a time — I knew them each by weight, the way you know dangerous neighbors. Standing came last and stayed longest. I had taught her the ear when she was so young her feet still made no weather in the soil. She pressed it to my warm flank, and her hand came flat against the stone.

I held my note the way you hold still for a dragonfly on your wrist. Elders startle easily.

There is no knock for *found*. Her stillness changed, and the warmth of her hand stayed, and stayed, and I was a grandmother again in the only way left to me.

**Seventh flood dead**

A life is leached the way this ground was leached. Everything soluble goes first: hurry, then want, then the log-fear, then the taste for salt. What cannot rot remains. The mountains underfoot are quartz because quartz is what a mountain cannot lose. A note is what a person cannot lose.

Mine keeps the weather of my birth-flood — the water was rising, so they named me Rising, every name a small fossil of a season. Mine took sixty-two floods and a funeral to come true. I am above the water table now, in the dry city. Risen.

It keeps the salt-walk I made when I was young and strong, the weeks downstream into country less and less ours, the changed eyes I came home with. There is a mineral thread in my note, a patience not learned from the plain. My family hears it and calls it my warmth. They are not wrong.

And it keeps the thing I never described. I climbed the whale-back once, when the floods were kind. I slept off the level — the only night of my life — and I dreamed in vertical, and I told no one, because the secret is kept by refusing to spend it. Here is what the living do not know: the note spends nothing either, but it *sounds* of it. Depth under everything I say. One tall tone in a horizontal chord. What cannot be said can still be sounded, at every turning, to anyone with the ear.

**Forty-first flood dead**

I felt the procession from half the field away: many treads, and one carried hush shaped exactly like Standing.

They opened us. A seam of weather came in, and the living's heat, and they laid her in the gap the chord had already grown for her — it does that — and sealed the seam white. Her note wobbled for a season with leftover weather: the last fever, the grief of the ones who had carried her. Then the chord took her the way the lick takes the scattered nations, and pulled her true. She arrived nearly in tune. Listeners do. The ear is the last thing the life gives back and the first thing the note keeps.

She knew me at once. Forty-one floods as ear and stone, and now no stone between us. That is the whole difference between mourning and membership.

**Eightieth flood dead**

The ground warmed for a year on the field's far side — a burn walking through, named and genealogied, an elder out visiting elders. The living fed it and walked it around their dead the way you walk a fence, and the sick were carried to sleep on its back within earshot of us. I will not pretend stone cannot lean. We leaned.

Floods later it walked into a pan, and the news arrived in the treads of the visitors before any tongue could have brought it. We mourned with the family in our way. It kept the local time better than anyone. It is missed under this field like a warm note taken out of the chord.

**Two hundred and eleventh flood dead**

Nobody counts this high but the water, and me.

The eldest of us has worn nearly smooth — rounded and re-rounded by five hundred turnings of the wind, a small exhausted planet of sound. She is becoming the hum the rest of us tune to. I used to think of it as disappearing. It is not. It is approaching.

Because past the wind there is the chord, and past the chord, on the stillest nights, there is the under-note. The living hear it at dusk, under the frogs, and call it the quiet. It is not quiet. It is one long note, patient as standing water, and the plain is its body, and we do not make it — we hold it up. The quiet lies on the choir the way the plain lies at the sea's level, and without us it would sink out of hearing under the thin young noise. Holding it is the whole of the work. We are good at it. It is the only thing the patient were ever good for.

At dusk the living pause — all of them, every ridge and raft and lick, without signal — and we feel the pause arrive through the ground from a hundred islands at once, one pressure-change, the closest thing to touch that remains to us. In the pause the chord and the under-note come into line, and for that hour the holding is not work.

It is accuracy.

**The salt**

The salt keeps its own count.

They put the sea's grains in with us, one from each mourner — debts repaid — and here above the flood line, in the one dry corner of the landlord's estate, the salt keeps: the last soluble thing in a country everything soluble left. The living weep, and the sea takes its property back early, a grain at a time. We do not weep. We are where the sea's property rests without returning. It is the only way in which we are rich.

When our spires thin and fall — some millennia from now; the masonry is in no hurry — the grains will wash down at the speed of permission, and the debt goes home. The sea will wait. Waiting works. The last lungfish in every pan is the proof, and so are we.

**Now**

Dusk. A child is crossing the field — three floods old, named for a weather I have not yet felt from above — and her ear comes to my warm flank, learning the chord the way I once learned the knock-alphabet before I learned to wade.

I hold still. Everything I was has been boiled down to one word of the old grammar — the language of locations and confirmations, reduced at last to a single confirmation, sustained. I say it the way everything true is said in a country where nothing comes back: once, and held, at the speed of stone.

*Here.*

And under the field, the one long note. And over it, the brief bright voices rising off the oldest ground in the world like steam. And between them, the quiet — lifted, inhabited, kept.

Someone is home.
