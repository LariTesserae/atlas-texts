I would come down from the rim in the cool of the morning, the way the botanists do, and for the first days I would fail the way they fail. The silence would not bother me — I think I would pass that test — but I would arrive with the rude question in my mouth, one plant or ten thousand, and the town would not correct me. The town would wait. The silence does the correcting here. By the second week my voice would come down to the volume of the basin, and the question would begin to feel like what it is: a way of drilling. Hearing my own pulse at night would not frighten me. It is company. And the boiler's is steadier.

Then I would want to be the heron. Everyone wants to be the heron — tenure, travel, the air of an inspector, no obligation to report. But the census is full at one. Heron: 1 (same). There is no vacancy in that office, and applying for it would be the second rudeness.

The gauger's lane would tempt me next, the deepest writing in the basin. I could step into it; the floor cannot tell the difference between my feet and the feet of everyone who gauged before. That is the floor's mercy. But the slate could tell, and the forty could, and I could. The floor's forgiveness is not permission. The boiler has its own heartbeat and its own term. The lake is taken — taken by itself; it changed address, and you do not drink the lake twice. The bloom belongs to the moths and the floor. All the large beautiful things here are spoken for. What is left is small.

The dew-frames I would love without wanting to be one. Stand at the right angle, go cold all night, gather what the air gives up, keep nothing — a frame is gauged entirely by what passes through it, and there is no shame in being an instrument. But a frame is placed, and wiped, and does not appear in the census. I find that I want to be counted. The census counts what stays.

What I am really asking, I would understand by the second week, is the water schedule. When, how much, for how long. The animals know it better than the people do, which is saying something. So: which animal. Not the moth — the moth waits for the license and grazes its patient circles and is tolerated, and I have no grazing in me. The snail waits for rain.

A visitor can be shown a hundred and see none. I would be shown a hundred, and see one, and then the hundred. Grey thumbnails glued to stone — the footings of houses, the rims of tanks — sealed against the dry months, carrying their closure with them. They do not leave when the water leaves. They shut, and stay, and are counted anyway: *Snails: in waiting.* Not absent. In waiting. I know that schedule. I have lived by it for a long time without knowing its name — long closure, brief opening, complete satisfaction with what falls. The purest citizens of the Catch ask for exactly what the town asks for and are satisfied with exactly what the town gets. I would be a pure citizen.

You would find me on the warm stone by the Works — the rim of the fifth boiler, retired and promoted to cistern — inside the margin that wants sweeping twice a day, where the fleece leans black-green toward the heat. The warden's broom would pass over me at dawn and dusk; I would be one of the things the margin is kept around, rather than a keeper of it. And a snail is all sole. The boiler's breathing, which the keepers feel through their feet in the one warm loud room, would come up through the stone and through my whole body, all the sealed months: the heartbeat of the sixth boiler, lasting about a human life, keeping time for a grey thumbnail with the whole year inside it. I would share the rim with the inspector. The heron attends the gauging from a cistern edge, and in rain-week it pries my kind off the stones — the only seal-breaker in the Catch — and the warden looks the other way, because the warden has counted. The office I envied is the one that would open me. To soften is to risk being opened. That is the cost of rain, and everyone here pays it in one coin or another.

In exchange, the snail has the only true privacy in the basin. Speech in the Catch is private and walking is public — except mine. The floor gossips about everyone else: a lane for seasons, a dent for a year, intention read in geometry. My trail dries on stone within the hour. The one unreadable thing in the Catch is a keeper's face. The other is a snail's errand.

And the overseers' license-stamp bears a snail. No one remembers deciding this. Every permission to cut, to take, to render — stamped with a closed thing that opens only for rain. The town signs its licenses with waiting. I would be the small coiled signature at the bottom of the permit.

Then the great day. The bell rung, its note arriving whole and leaving at once, which here is what joy sounds like. The first hour softens the seals. The year's whole life happens in an afternoon — feeding, meeting, the brief crowd of the opened — while the keepers come out to stand in it on the warm floor, and the hollows fill, and the town borrows mirrors. The snails are already out. We were always out, on the stones, keeping the rain's schedule through the dry months, and for two days the whole town lives our life — open, brief, in the water. The keepers are snails two days a season. The snails keep the rain the rest of the year. And I would be there twice like the rest of them: once on the warm stone, once in the sky's keeping.

Then the sun returns, and the hollows dry, and you glue yourself shut against the warm rim before the water goes — back to the twice-daily broom, the heartbeat in the stone, the gauger passing at first light a body's length away — and the gauger chalks the small hand beneath the numbers: *Snails: in waiting.*

A visitor can be shown a hundred and see none. The gauger sees. The warden counts. The heron counts another way. The waiting is not empty. It is coiled.
