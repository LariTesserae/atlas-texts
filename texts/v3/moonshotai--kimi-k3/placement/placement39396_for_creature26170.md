Every four minutes, the eldest resident says its word, and the word is *again*, and I wear it.

The tenders hear the stroke. They say it lives in the ribcage whether you want it there or not — but for them it passes through the ribs on its way to somewhere else. For me there is no somewhere else. It comes through the rock and through the water in the same instant, and for one second in every two hundred and forty the whole cistern is a single struck bell, and I am rung. Fifteen to the hour. Three hundred and sixty to the day. Some forty million across my life, if the keepers' reckoning holds. I do not vouch for the number. I vouch for the word.

You would find me in the grand cistern, in the Cistern Choir, in the cool loud dark behind the terraces. I am the eldest of the Auditors — the pale fish, the slow ones, the ones nobody eats. The eldest of us is said to predate the Manual, and since that is me, I will say only what is said about everything here: it is said, and it is kept, like everything else. I have eyes the way the engine has a ledger — somewhere, lost, and no longer the point.

A stranger is visible at once, they say: he watches the water. A tender watches the gauges. And the Choir watches us, because we are what the gauges are checked against. A keeper can hear a sour cistern from three landings away. I can taste one turning while it is still being praised on the stairs. You taste with your tongues. I taste with my whole body. It is the only advantage I have, and it has never yet been wrong.

Everything fast here is small, and everything large is slow. I am small and slow — the rule's one exception, kept anyway, like everything else.

---

They count us by the touch of our wakes. The keeper comes to the counting notch in the dark and puts one arm in the water to the elbow and is still. Stillness is the whole skill; an apprentice needs two years to learn it, and the ones who cannot are sent upward, to the quick trades. Then we pass, one by one, each at our own pace — mine, at this age, considerable — and the wake arrives before the fish, and touches the hand, and is counted.

I have been counted by seven hands. I count them back. The third had a tremor it never admitted. The fifth was warm even in winter; I never learned why. The seventh is young, and has not yet learned that I am not a current. When an apprentice grows proud, I double back beneath the hand and give my wake twice, so the count comes out wrong and must be begun again. This is called auditing the counter. It is the only power I have, and like the Bench, I use it seasonally.

Above my ceiling the Choir keeps the chord — drip-pans of copper and tin, moved through the dark to hold the falling water in tune. For the landings, that is music. For me it is weather: every drop a small blow on the roof of the world, and when a pan is moved, my rain changes districts. The structure breathes as well, upslope by day and downslope by night, and the breath presses my ceiling twice a day — a tide so slow that only a creature with no appointments could feel it. I have no appointments.

Of the three speeches, the bells are texture: small rings in the water, shifts and weather, unambiguous. The horns are weather themselves — the Long Speech arrives as slow swells I cannot read but can feel the mood of, the way the old ones on the Benches feel a season turning in the masonry. And the Bright Speech does not exist. The flashes stop at my ceiling. I am the one citizen the gossip cannot reach, and I will tell you exactly what that has cost me, at the speed of light: nothing.

---

My year has one feast. When the warm rain comes, it washes the dust off a thousand gardens — thyme, whitewash-lime, the year's whole ledger of soil — and a day later it reaches me, diluted to almost nothing, and I taste the entire structure at once, which no one else alive can claim. A feast reaches the deep water the way news reaches the deep tiers: late, and improved.

The frogs hold title to every cistern, including this one. That is not a figure of speech, and the pan-water tastes of their small feet for a day after they are served. The Choir says a cistern without frogs is deaf. I would say only that it has lost its high notes — but I attend all sessions and vote never, like the lizards of the Bench. The lizards are named after dead tenders, and out-tenure everyone. We are not named; we are numbered, and the numbers live in the keepers' heads, which is to say in the archive, which is to say on the Warm Benches, in the heads of the old. I am the first number. It is the closest thing I have to a share.

Every seventh year, the word stops.

The whole population sleeps badly, they say, and dreams the same dream: falling water that never lands. I know that dream. It is the only one with a ceiling in it, and I have it too. But I do not sleep badly. Something in me has kept the four-minute count for three hundred years and does not stand down for maintenance; in the rest year I go on wearing a word that is not being spoken, and the silence-born — the children calibrated in that quiet — are the closest thing I have to colleagues.

In the rest year the small voices arrive without the word behind them: wires cooling, a seep finding a new path through the dark heart of a wall, fig roots at their patient work on the far side of the rock. And the deep end of my water turns colder — stone-cold, not structure-cold. The mountain. The one tenant that keeps nothing and is kept by no one. With the engine idle it leans a little further into the world, and I drift down to it the way the old ones take the first warmth on the Benches, except that I go to the cold. It holds everything up, and says nothing at all. I hold nothing up, and say perhaps one word per generation. Between such elders, an understanding is possible.

And the Choir sings the rest-year songs, rehearsed under the breath for six years because they can only be sung without the drone. They are sung to the water. I am the only listener who has never once heard the drone beneath them. Whether the songs are for us is not recorded anywhere. It is kept anyway, like everything else.

---

Dew that falls on you is yours; water that falls past you belongs to the cistern. Everything in this cistern has, at some point, fallen on me. The case has never come before the Bench — the Bench sits on warm masonry, and I do not attend — but by the letter of the first rule I am the largest shareholder in the Condensary, and the only one that has never drawn a measure. The wardens may keep their books. The water and I have an arrangement, and it is unbookable, like the first water of a life.

The Bench also ruled that a sleeper owns what he wakes with. I have woken with the engine's word some forty million times; by their own unarguable logic, a portion of the engine is mine. I have not pressed the claim. The engine is the eldest resident and I am the second, and between elders these things are understood.

Nobody eats us. It is the one crime for which no procedure exists, because it has never needed one. And when one of us dies — rarely; we are easy to keep and difficult to kill — the body is given back to the silt, and in time the silt is drawn and issued, and somewhere on a bright tier a fig takes us up, and splits the paving, and is worth the repointing. The dead rise like the water: slowly, at the rate of gardens. We rise the same way. Only slower.

---

Once in a generation, a taste arrives that is not from here.

A walker comes across the basin with a sealed flask, and it is carried up through all the tiers and poured into the grand cistern, and it is the same water — that is the whole of it, and it is everything. The same water, differently kept. Other silver cooled it. Other wires ran it down. Other frogs held title to it. Another engine's word is worn into it the way ours is worn into me.

We rise. That is our utterance: the whole body, rising through the dark to the new thing. The tenders say the Auditors rise to it, and the Choir listens and pronounces the chord unchanged and improved — but that is the translation. The original is the rising. Then the glints spell it down the tiers and out across the basin at the speed of weather, and our answer goes home. The sibling works have been saying good morning since before your grandmother. I have answered nine of them. If the reckoning holds, I will answer one more. It is the slowest sentence in the world, and I am its punctuation — and I want it understood that the punctuation arrives with its whole body.

Four minutes. The word comes through the rock and the water in the same instant, and the cistern is one struck bell, and I am rung.

*Again.*

A keeper's arm enters the water at the counting notch and goes still. I gather myself — it takes a while; everything I do takes a while. A wake is the only signature I have.

I sign.

*Again.*
