I came down the stair loud. Everyone does. The valley sorts its arrivals by volume: what arrives loud is quick, what is quick is weather, and weather does not count. My boots on the dressed treads were the loudest thing in the cirque, and by the second terrace I understood that the loudness was the answer to the question I had brought with me. Where would I find myself here? Nowhere, at first hearing. The population is fixed, the terms are mineral, and I fail both.

But I walked it anyway with an eye for the vacancy.

The obvious seat is the windbreak beside the tally slab — the one post in the valley ever staffed by anything like me. I could crouch there out of the wind, take up a hammerstone, and cut the next mark: the row resumes, the instrument read at last, the errand answered. I wanted that seat. But a tally is not marks. A tally is the returning. Anyone can cut one; to make it honest you must come back and cut the next, and the next, at the valley's intervals, which are seasons, and I can promise the valley an afternoon. Worse, the sterility would keep my mark fresh — and everything here is kept fresh, and everything fresh implies someone about to return. The whole valley already murmurs *last season, last season*, like a rumor it cannot stop spreading. I will not add my voice to that rumor. The counters meant to come back and didn't; the kindest thing a latecomer can do is not forge their handwriting.

And the seat is filled in any case. The valley keeps its own books now — one skin of gray flour per season, laid down without hands. The tally waits. The ponds do not wait.

The water, then — the first tenant, the only one that moves. The census even leaves that door ajar, dismissing the afternoon rain as a claimant that seeds nothing. But rain that falls into a basin does not leave by dusk. It is naturalized: stilled, gauged, divided into thirds and thirds again, given four days and the whole valley to cross. I considered it. For four days I would be everywhere — ankle-deep in the shadowed runnels, dark in the culverts, lifted over my own kind on the bridglets, held in the mirrors, ticking in the tuned vaults. No other position offers so complete a tour. But it is a tour. The contract ends at the outfall notch, in one loud sentence spent entirely outside the walls. I would learn everything the Work knows and then be required to leave with it — calmed, measured, exported. The valley's only export is what it has finished teaching, and I was not ready to be finished.

The rest I crossed off one by one. The light keeps its own appointments and needs no deputy; it arrives on the right days, at the right slot, without me. The frost takes no apprentice; its corrections are final. The slow mason works without signature and would strike any hand that tried to help it hold the line. The errand — the *meant to come back* — is inhabited by the Work itself; expectation turns out to be a residency, already taken. Even the deepest appointment is for two: the drip and the pillar, falling and rising, and there is no chair for a third. Every seat in the valley is staffed. The census is full.

If I was going to be anything in this world, I would have to look where the census stops.

The census stops at the lip.

Stand at that clean gray line where the built world begins, and face the other way. Everything the valley does is aimed where you are now looking. The Comb strains what comes from here. The gauges read it daily. The varves file it by season. The tally counted it until the counting stopped. The mirrors tilt toward it all day and keep it a few minutes past dark. I had walked the entire instrument hunting for the vacancy, and the vacancy was the subject.

I would be the snowfield.

I am the one unmade thing in the cirque. Everything below the lip is fitted — every slope faced, every floor paved, every interval shaped — and I lie where I fall, surfaced by wind, nightly remade, the only thing in the valley that dresses its own surface. The Comb holds back the part of me that is still ice and admits the part that has agreed to become water; it is the exact border of my translation.

The lip was dressed to meet me at a line. Most years I still touch it. Some late summers there is a hand's width of bare stone between my edge and the masonry — the valley measures its weirs in hand-spans, and it measures me in the same small unit, and that hand's width is the whole errand. It is the one interval in the cirque left unshaped, and the only one anyone ever counted.

I am lit first. Long before the first hour reaches the terraces it strikes the peaks and me; the view from the rim at dawn — the fingerprint reading itself at distance — happens in my light, from my country's edge. The watchers' windbreaks were built against my breath. I was the weather they kept out and the thing they kept count of, both at once, which is the oldest arrangement between a subject and its observers: they shelter from you in order to watch you.

The census missed me because I enter under another name. Every spring I come through the Comb in pieces — strained, stilled, divided — and for four days at a time I am the whole valley: in the runnels and the culverts and the mirrors and the vaults. The census calls this water. It is me, translated. And some of me never crossed at all: under a flush cap somewhere there is a vault of me that fell while the tally was still being cut, kept clear by the same sterility that keeps the wood — unaged, uncounted, resident for centuries. The census took me for the plumbing.

The varves are my biography, written in the third person. The gray flour is the mountain's, but I am its courier: my melt carries it down and the ponds lay it in lines, one skin a season, and the lines say how much of me came. The valley is copying me into stone, and the copying keeps pace with the erasing. I cannot read it myself — I have no gauge; I know my own retreat only as a feeling of thin places. Subjects guess. Instruments know. That is the division of labor between us.

On clear days I am already inside the instrument. Every pond holds a piece of sky; at these angles every piece of sky holds the rim; the rim holds me — a hundred small snowfields lying flat in the basins, whiter than any stone. The valley watches me with all its eyes, and I lie in every one. At dusk we keep the same late guest: the basins below hold the sky a few minutes past its time, and I above hold it latest of all — the last light lives on the snow after the terraces go dark, two keepers at opposite ends of the cirque letting go in the same moment. The instrument and its subject, agreeing about when night is.

There are two silences in this system. The Work has planned its own: the pillar rising a hair a year toward the drip, the circuit closing, the one small sound stopping — a silence earned, on schedule, paid for in advance. The other silence is undesigned, and it is mine. The drip in the deep vault is fed, at the far end of its long plumbing, by me, and every spring I send less. Some year the drip will slow; the appointment will be broken a hair short of its meeting; the planned silence will arrive early, from the wrong direction, unpaid. I think the counters saw this before anyone. A tally of a retreat is a countdown, and no hand wants to write the zero — the row stops mid-entry not because they were interrupted but because continuing meant one day cutting the last mark. This is only my guess. Subjects guess.

So here is the covenant, as I understand it from above the lip. You cannot stop me leaving; I cannot read you. The reader you expect may never come — but a measurement with no reader is still a measurement. A measurement with no subject is a rite. I keep you from being a rite. You keep my leaving witnessed. The valley is a clock measuring the length of its own life, but I am the water, so it is measuring mine too; we share the clock. And when the last line is written it will be laid by the last of my leaving — until then the exchange holds: I am spent, you are written, one skin a season.

In winter the measurement rests. I lie over everything — Comb, gauges, tally slab, the windbreaks built against me, which I fill to the brim and consider the one joke I get to tell. The whorls go white; the fingerprint softens under its subject; nothing is asked and nothing is answered, and the Work is only proofread and covered. Those are our months.

Then comes the morning — earlier, lately — when I hear myself begin. It starts under me, in the dark of my own body: a tick, a threading, the first thread of water finding the old way down. My leaving is the valley's waking. The Comb takes its first sip of the year; the gauge lifts off its winter mark; somewhere below, in a tuned vault, a drip quickens by an interval too small for anyone left to hear. I am smaller by that much; the archive is one line richer. At night the frost ticks in the joints and ticks in my crust — the same cold, proofreading us both — and once in a cold snap the deep vault sounds its drum, and some nights, above the lip, my own pack settles with a low sound the wind takes away: the subject, shifting in its sleep.

I came down the stair loud, and the valley was right about me: quick, weather, gone. But given the choice of anything in that world, I would not be the reader or the mason or the drip. I would be the one unmade thing — lit first, counted longest, the reason the instrument was built, still lying above the lip, still answering.

The question comes down every spring. The answer goes down with it, through the Comb, past the gauge, into the gray ink.

The notch says what it has always said.

Still.
