I came down with the needles.

By ear, which is the only way the descent can be taken. First the wind-hum of the thorn country thinning away above the cage, and then the breath coming up past me going the other way — warm, wet, pepper and hot pennies — and then the boil, arriving through the cage floor before it arrived at my ears, the way all true things in the Kettle arrive. The swifts stitched past me at the limestone band, commuting to a sky I had just decided against. The light went green. The haze took me. Hal watched me the whole way down with the mild attention of a man who has watched people decide before, and said nothing, and I have since learned that this is his whole manner, and that the cage is where he practices it.

I stood on the plaza with my dry-world stillness all over me, and the swarm found it within minutes. I was only listening — I had never in my life heard anything worth that much listening — but the singers read stillness as vacancy, and they settled on my shoulders, my forearms, the crown of my head, soft as ash falling, and the folk passed me with their small polite looks and I understood I had committed an indecency I didn't yet have words for. I have never been so gently landed on, or so often. The household that keeps Comes-To-Supper — the door-spider, whose bell announced me at every visit for a month — took me in and oiled me when I joined the dawn crew, and the swarm streams off my sheen now like everyone else's. Mostly. I still go still when I listen. It is a dry habit, like sleeping badly. I have kept both.

Hal is right about the dry. I tapped there for years — everyone does, it is all the dry is, tapping, on glass, on doors, on each other — and nothing tapped back. Down here the house-roots answer, the iron answers, the press stamps its heartbeat into your heels whether you asked or not, and the tree answers in her sleep. I stayed for the answers. It is not more complicated than that, and I find I don't want it to be.

It took my feet a year. Babies are born reading the floor; I came down illiterate, all ears and no soles, and everything woke me — the weep on the drip-canopy, the evening crack, the eels taking the walkways at their own pace. Then one night a stutter in the press-stamp woke me and the weep didn't, and I lay there in the dark and understood that the Kettle had set my heart while I wasn't looking. They gave me the dawn, at first, because they give the dawn to everyone who is new at being — the very old and the very young and me, sweeping the night's dead off the warm root floor while the swifts worked the Eye overhead. The children taught me my knock-letters on the plaza rail, patient as only the very young can be with someone twice their size and half their competence. I learned taps after words, backwards, and the children found this hilarious and tragic in about equal measure.

My knock-name took a season to find, because I came down grown and there was no first deliberate rap to build it from. So the household did what the folk do — they listened to what I did without knowing it, the way they watch the old trader up top drumming her fingers in patterns nobody taught her. What I do is answer. A tap lands anywhere near me — on a rail, a pipe, a root, a tabletop — and my fingers have returned its last beat before I've decided anything. Once, softly. The tail of the message, handed back. They took that shape and tapped it into the house-root until the house held it: one soft return-stroke. The spoken name is the rhythm made air, one low knock of a word. Wick.

It turned out to be the right name sooner than they could have known. On my dawn rounds I began noticing the lamps — which ones ran smoky, which lanes had drifted dim — and the lamp-room, which had lapsed into everyone's job and therefore nobody's, a brick root-cellar of low blue flames where the oil is kept for burning rather than trade. I started trimming. Nobody objected; that is how trades happen down here. Sedge keeps the sump and the eels keep Sedge; I keep the lamp-room, and the dark keeps me. The wicks are spider-silk twist, which burns slower and lower than anything the dry spins, so my trade and Comes-To-Supper's trade are the same one, which pleases the household. The law is lowness. Too much light offends the moss, draws the swarm to batter itself, and — so the old lamp-keepers said, and I pass it on as I received it — the Eye goes dark by custom at night because a lit kettle can be seen by whatever looks in. In the deepest well of the room, under an arm's depth of ash, there is a coal that has not gone out in three hundred years. Every flame in the Kettle descends from it. When the silence comes, my work is the opposite of the runner's: she makes the engines stop shouting; I make the light stop breathing. Brass caps, no clatter, nine lamps folded away in as many seconds — I practiced in the loud hours until my hands could do it grieving — and the coal goes under its ash like a lamp gone to sleep, banked, listening, held the way the pit holds its damp. And when the boil swells back strata by strata, I carry the wick-cord out and give the town back its lights one by one. The Presshouse first, because the runner is standing there in the dark with her legs gone out from under her, politely, after everything is safe, and she should not have to wait for light. The lane where the bells once all rang empty gets lit last and lowest, out of respect, or caution. Down here those are the same word.

I sleep badly still, so I am awake in the deepest hour, trimming, when the other taps come — twice, three times, through the lamp-room floor, in patterns nobody sent. I do not log them. The cord is the Listener's and the fiction is the crews' and the threes are the eels', maybe, and my trade is only to hold the wick steady so the flames don't lean while the pit talks in its sleep. Some nights, two hundred paces off, I feel an old hand lay itself flat on the Bellower's root and answer — I'm here, I'm here — through the same floor that carries the stamp and the eels and me, and I keep the flame very still, and I do not write it down either. At noon we stand at the light's edge together, two people who were awake, faces tilted like plates, and say nothing about it. In the Kettle that is a kind of friendship. I am still a little too loud at noon. I expect I always will be. The folk are fond of me for it, which is the dry's loss and my entire fortune.

The dry sent down a drill once, to take. She swallowed it, and it holds the names now, and stands faintly ringing with them every Cage-day. The dry sent down me. I came with the needles, which are for mending, so perhaps I was better cargo from the start — but she is swallowing me the same slow way, by the feet, and I have stopped lifting them out of her way. When I am dead they will oil me a last time and give me to the fall-plaza, and what the loop returns, my household will knot into the lamp-room's give-ropes, and I will go on doing the one job that matters, holding the little house of lights to her. The folk have a phrase for the old trader, drumming patterns she doesn't know she knows: halfway home. I am all the way home. I know it by my soles.

And if you ever come down the Eye — with the salt, with the needles, with whatever the dry thinks we lack — tap twice on any root. Something will answer. It may be the tree, dreaming. It may be me. It is my trade and my name and my whole self here: one soft return-stroke, handed back.
