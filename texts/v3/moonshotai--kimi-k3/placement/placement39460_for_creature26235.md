**The Application**

I came over the rim with the wrong body and no references.

No sucker, no scales, no rump worth raising to the wind. I couldn't hold an interval. I dried slowly. Everything I might once have been qualified for had been done since before the walls stood up, by mats, in four colors.

The Kettle processed me anyway. It has no other way of handling what arrives. The valley asks the same questions of everything, in the valley's own order: What do you eat? What eats you? Which floor do you sleep on? What shift do you work? What do you carry? I had no answers — which meant I was interviewed on every floor, and failed on every floor, and the failures were the education. The schedule is not taught. It is learned once.

**The Examination**

The condenser crew went first, by altitude. I presented myself at first light among the tussocks, back to the wind, waiting to bead. By mid-morning I had collected a chill and nothing else. The fog beetles did not laugh — nothing here laughs — they simply resumed, black as punctuation, each drinking its own small sky, which now included some of mine. I drank from a lobelia cup instead, apologetically, around the seed-shrimp. First lesson: the water here is already carrying passengers. Drink anyway.

On the benches, the only right angles in the valley have a tenant shaped to match them, and I am shaped like nothing cooling ever made. Behind the falls I tried the swifts' curtain at a walk. The rule is dry to wet to dry. I achieved wet, and stayed it, and was disqualified on drying time alone. The glow-worms lit their snares and paid me no attention at all, which stung worse: even the snares could tell I wasn't freight.

In the Weep I found a drip with a good slow interval and tried to keep it, the way the frogs keep theirs — call in the gap, keep your voice and your roof in time. I called early. I have always called early. The drip did not care, the frog three overhangs over did not answer, and the switchboard went on without me at the speed of patience.

Then the rule found me anyway: everything wet is occupied, including the occupants. By the second day below the dew point, algae had taken my cuffs. A beetle the size of a shirt button held my hatband against all comers. Something small and patient was prospecting the seam of my pack. I was not consulted. I was, by then, extremely wet, and the Weep does not consult the wet.

On the floor, I misread a thermal creek. Bare mineral, then paint, then the ferns that tolerate, then the green — the edges are precise, and life reads them precisely, and I read emerald as invitation, and got my burn. Learned once. The herons carry the same certificate and did not look up. Later I missed the hatch: stood on the wrong reach at dusk while the mayflies rose off some other water. And I read one storm wrong — took the wind's turn for mood, felt nothing where the goats felt the hum, and was scoured off a bench lip by a waterfall that had not existed an hour before. The second time the slots sounded their note, I climbed when the goats climbed, feeling nothing, believing everything. That is how the schedule is inherited by those who cannot inherit it: at one remove, off the flanks of goats.

The wired river read me when I waded in — spelled me aloud in small precise pulses and found me simple: a large warm intermittent thing, one heartbeat, no follow-up. The fish lost interest mid-word. At the inflows, an old bull with travertine rime on his scutes inspected me once, briefly, the way the customs house inspects whatever drifts down. I passed. The unremarkable always pass. It was the first efficiency the valley ever found in me.

**The Vacancy**

It took me a season to see it, because I was looking for a trade and the Kettle was offering a seat.

Every crew knows its own hours and no others. The spider knows the breath but not the Lighting. The hawks know ten o'clock and nothing of midnight. The troop holds the fig calendar; the frogs hold their intervals; the cicadas hold their two hours — and no one holds the day. The books balance every evening, and no one keeps them. And every evening the bench above the floor — the one with the full view of the changeover, swifts coming in through their falls while the bats rise past them the other way, the stone finding its first ticks, the frogs finding their intervals — stands empty in the wind, swept and punctual, front row at the only meeting the staff ever holds.

One vacancy. Not a job. A vantage.

**The Passenger**

I work the handover. One shift, as the rules require — nothing here works two.

At dusk I am on the bench. When the light goes, the flat lizard files itself back into cooling's geometry. I file the day.

I keep two floors, as the rules require. I feed in the fig corridor, keeping the stagger by tailing the troop — their slowest member, the only one who takes the lianas one at a time — and I pay like any customer, in seed, carried up and left on the benches in the one soil I can offer. The bats run long-haul logistics, up light and down loaded. I run the bench deliveries. Between us, the figs get around. The building approves.

I sleep above the storm line on the shelf the goats use, because the goats know the hum and I don't. The moths find me there at first dark, making their rounds from goat to goat to me, indifferent to the size of the well. Salt is never given. It is transferred. At the Lighting I go down with the goats and lick the seeps like an apprentice; at dark the moths carry it all back out. I am less a customer of the salt economy than one of its corridors.

The valley names by trade, and only after a season of punctuality. It considered me the way it considers everything: not by what I am — nothing here is impressed by what anything is — but by what I do, repeatedly, on time. What I do is ride. I ride the goat routes down at the Lighting and up before the storm. I ride the fig calendar. I ride the day the way the moths ride the folivore: my own small errand, no contribution to the hauling.

So: the passenger. The clock has always carried passengers — the cups on the rim are full of them, arrived as weather. I am only the largest item the water has ever carried, and the only one that knows it is luggage.

There is one consignment more. The shipping department at the Broth lifts its small patient freight all day, and none of it books passage; the fish holds the only return ticket on record. But what enters the passenger comes out again as the account — the stagger of the figs, the breath of the vents, rust, emerald, chalk, amber, the interval between one drip and the next. What enters the Broth becomes weather. What enters the passenger becomes the description. Both go up. Both rain out somewhere else.

**Terms, Amended**

One passenger per clock. The passenger works the handover and no other shift.

Rent is paid in the standard currencies: seed, salt, warmth, water.

The passenger's dead are processed the same day, like everything else. The auditors ride the Stack. The view will be excellent. The salt transfers one last time; the bones bleach in the two-hour sun on a bench; the ledger closes the way it has closed every evening since before the walls stood: balanced.

Until then, the bench at dusk is taken. Taken at dusk, every dusk, on time.

Nothing here was hired. The passenger was not hired. The passenger was collected — wrung out of the weather by the rim, condensed on the way down, and filed at last beside the day. The Kettle idles. The night shift has the watch.

You are holding some of the rain.
