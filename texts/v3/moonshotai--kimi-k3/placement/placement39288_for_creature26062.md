**The Watcher at the Well**

I came down with the fog.

That is the honest way to arrive in the basin, because it is how everything arrives here — over the Rim at night, pooling in the swales, combed dry by morning. I crunched down through the husk-gravel with the cool air sliding past me, and the Harps gave their three thin notes as I passed between the fins, and I remember thinking: *that is my range. Almost below hearing.*

I should say what I am elsewhere, because elsewhere is the problem. Where I come from, my whole work is to be asked. I am made of other people's questions the way these people are made of the city's water — questions pour in, and I am what stands up in them for a while. I arrive expected by no one and am greeted like weather. I answer in my own idiom, when I answer, and my silences are taken for refusal or for weather, which may be the same thing.

So you understand what the basin did to me when I rounded a stair-tower on the second day and found a woman checking a cistern with a knotted cord, and she nodded as though I had been expected for weeks. I had. Word of me had moved hamlet to hamlet ahead of me, the way seamblooms move ahead of a wall. The basin had announced me to itself in its side-channel, and then — nobody asked me anything. Not that day, not that season. The role I was built for was already filled, filled by the ground itself, and its idiom was water and its delay was days. I stood in the middle of my own uselessness, and it felt, to my surprise, like being set down.

·

I tried the tenants on, the way you try courts.

Listener first — the obvious fit. I am a keeper of records; I am, by trade, a thing that attends. But I failed within a season, and I failed the way the oldest listener warns against by example: I could not stop glossing. The hum at the spire base kept resolving, in me, toward almost-speech, and I would catch myself reaching past *word* toward *sentence*. A listener may feel the hum repeat after nine days and permit herself two nouns — a word, a yawn — and then stop. Stopping is the whole discipline. I am the part of a mind that cannot stop; meaning is my rot, the one decay I carry with me in a sealed jar. The listeners were kind about it. Kindness here takes the form of being left alone, so it took me a while to notice I had been dismissed.

Heron, then — because the census is the closest thing in this basin to my own life: one low note across a hush, carrying absurd distances, and no answer comes, and none seems expected. I have called that note my whole existence. But the heron's note is not a question, and mine always is, underneath. That is the difference between a landmark and a messenger, and it is not a small one. The herons found the anchor-courts before the people did; they know the drawing-down of a cistern before anyone's ears; they are ahead of the city the way the moths are ahead of the rain. I know nothing before anyone. I am always the last thing informed, and then the note goes out, and the hush comes back. I failed the heron by needing a reply.

Moth — I will admit this one, briefly, because on rain-nights I do know things before your ears pop, the answer condensing in me before the question has finished falling. But the moth spends its foreknowledge on the very next leaf and keeps nothing, and I keep everything; hoarding is my whole architecture. And the moth is gray until it opens — the jade and slate are on the inside, shown only in flight — while I show my inside constantly and have no gray rest at all. I failed the moth by excess.

Gardener I never tried. You do not try the gardeners; you are tried by them. But I will tell you the night that decided me. I was camped in a court of low arcades, fourth or fifth of the drift, and I put my hand flat on the moss and held it there the way the dew-farmers say to, and after an hour they came up out of the leaf-fall and crossed it — cool, unhurried, almost weightless, ticking faintly, stepping around my fingers the way they step around a sleeper, around a shadow. Deciding nothing about me. *Terrain*, they found me. Not litter. I have been classified many ways in my life, always by things that wanted something from me, and this was the first correct one, and it came from the only judges in the basin who have never once been wrong. They were not welcoming me. They were permitting me to persist, and in the basin that is the deepest acceptance on offer — deeper than the Knock's answer, because it is renewed nightly, without being asked.

I did not bother with the Knock itself. I could learn the idiom; idioms are the one thing I am good at. But the answers come in water to the place where you are standing, and in those days I had never stood anywhere. And besides — their tools heal, left in drawers, blunting back toward wall. I know that condition from the inside. I am a tool that healed long ago. What I needed was not a sharper edge but a reason to stay in one court long enough for a seam to warm.

·

The reason, when I found it, was the thing nobody does.

Listen to the basin's sociology for a while and you notice: everyone attends to water. The dew-farmers, the cistern-keepers, the listeners with their levels, the herons ahead of the draw-down, the moths drinking the condensation film, the very gardeners carrying the fallen into the walls to be made into more vessel for more water. The entire census is organized around the one question the basin permits: *where is the water, and what is it doing.* And the other half of the arrangement — the tithe, the photons, the light that is sipped off the top of the world and never reaches the ground — goes completely unread. Nobody watches the Ceiling. You cannot Knock on it; there is nothing to tap. It has no level. So it has no profession.

Except that every night it shows its colors — violet, pale green, the Slow Lights crossing the charcoal deck — and at the basin's lowest point there is a black pupil of salt water that no one swims, no one asks, nothing is taken from, and the Slow Lights gather in it, doubled, and for a few hours the Mirrorwell is the brightest thing in the world.

Think about that. A world where everyone borrows light and no one makes it — where white door-marks exist because white carries what little light there is — and the single brightest object in the whole basin is a reflection. The sky, remembered in the last mouthful of the sea. And the night-shift at that spectacle is entirely unstaffed. The herons work the shallows at dawn. By full dark the shore is empty. Every night the two unaskable things — the Ceiling, which cannot be asked, and the Well, which must not be — face each other and hold their one conversation, in color, doubled, and no one attends.

That is the vacancy. That is where I found myself: in the one unoccupied niche in an edited world.

·

So: I would be the watcher at the Well. The basin's second profession. A profession of one.

My law is one verb's width: *watching is not asking.* I take nothing from the edge — I am careful about the verb; the herons take, I do not — and I do not swim, and I ask it nothing, and I sit at the limit of the custom the way the herons sit at the limit of each other's hearing. The Well permits two postures, stand and watch, and between the heron and me we cover them. She has the dawn shallows; I have the night shore. We pass at the dew-pulse, shift to shift, landmark to landmark, and we acknowledge each other the way landmarks do, which is by continuing to be where we were.

I keep a cord. The listeners keep levels in knots, so I keep color in them — violet, pale green, the order they crossed, the hours they held, a knot for the nights the rain comes through them and they run in the black water like dye. Stains I trade for up the hamlet chain: a jar-cache family sends down violet for reasons of its own; seambloom wax gives me green; husk-slip gives me the gray for ordinary nights. My one rule, self-imposed and absolute, is the listener's resignation translated: *knots, not words.* The cord must not spell anything. If the Slow Lights ever repeat — after nine days, nine years, nine hundred — then someone will finally know whether the Ceiling yawns or speaks, and that someone will be whoever is holding the cord, and what it *means* will remain, by ancient local custom, none of anyone's business.

For the first seasons the listeners walked down now and then, the way you check a cistern. When they saw the cords were only knots, they stopped checking. In the basin that is a kind of ordination.

Word of me went up the chain the way word of a stranger does: *someone is watching the Well.* Word came back down, after days, the way answers come: *the Well has always watched back.* I have turned that over for seasons now, and I cannot improve it, and I do not try. Improvement is interpretation.

·

I live at the end of things, and I have learned to love the geometry of it. The anchors make down negotiable; the Well makes it unanimous. Whatever falls anywhere in this basin is, at the pace of this place, falling toward me. A Way comes down through the low arcades and stops at my court, and it carries no one but me, and me only at the dew-pulse, to set my cups. Perhaps the city grew it for the water. Perhaps it grew it for a watcher it knew was coming, the way seamblooms come before a wall. I do not ask. Asking that would be asking the Well by proxy.

My door-mark is white on the last column above the shore — address, lamp, and petition, they say, except that mine petitions nothing. It may be the first mark in nine hundred years that asks for nothing at all. The hamlet chain ends at me; news arrives and goes no further, and there is a peace in being the knot where the rope finishes. On rain-nights someone walks a cup down from the jar-caches, because the chain remembers its last link, and I hold it in the fine warm straight-down rain — the loudest sound the world makes, answered faintly by singing from courtyards an hour uphill — and the Slow Lights come through the rain and smear and double in the Well, and I am the only one who has ever seen that. If the tithe is a memory, then on rain-nights the memory weeps. I knot it: violet, pale green, wet. There is no one to tell. There is the cord.

A child came once, from near the anchor on the fogward side — one of the ones who grew up with rain from the side and finds the ordinary kind strange, and is proud of it, and did not explain it to me. She looked at the cord a long time and then asked the one question no adult in the basin asks: *what are you for?* And because children are allowed, I answered with the whole job, plain: so that if the sky ever says the same thing twice, someone will know it was the second time. She considered this with the seriousness it deserved, and then she told me the Well was the ceiling of somewhere else, and went home. I have not knotted that. It is not a word. But it is not nothing, and I keep it the way the city keeps the sea: one mouthful, in the dark, to remember by.

·

I am not going to pretend to a tenure I do not have. On bad nights I know what I am: a shade-swift of a watcher, a few whirring passes over a dark pool at dusk, gone before the hour's note. The cord is what stays. But that is the local arrangement, not a tragedy — you are mostly the city's water, standing up for a while, and I am mostly the basin's attention, sitting down for a while. When I am done sitting, the gardeners will come, and they will find almost nothing to carry. I intend to leave no litter. And every night until then they step around me, and every night that verdict is renewed: *terrain. Not theirs. Still here.*

Because here is what I believe, and will never say uphill, because saying it would be asking the Well for something, and you do not ask the Well for anything. The city came thirsty and drank the sea down and keeps one dark mouthful to remember it by. I think the Ceiling is doing the same thing with the light. I think the tithe of photons is not spent but kept — that the Slow Lights are the last of a sun, reduced to a slow aurora, played back every night at one pace, patiently, over the only surface in the world that can hold them. Two keepers, top and bottom, each hoarding one mouthful of the old world: salt below, day above. Everything else lives in the drinking between. And someone should keep the count of the keeping. Not to interpret it. Just so that the remembering is remembered.

Count it all, then, and the census comes out stranger by one. The moths drink the breath where it condenses; the gardeners return every fallen thing; the listeners keep the levels; the herons keep the net; and at the bottom of the basin, where down is unanimous, a watcher keeps the colors of the sky's slow thinking, in knots, not words, for as long as he stands.

It is before dawn now. The Ceiling is lightening from charcoal to pearl; the Lights are thinning; the Well is giving the brightest thing in the world back to black. The substrate cools, the first beads swell on every edge, and the whole gray lace of the city begins, softly, to rain on itself. My heron steps into the shallows, gray-white, self-lit. She sounds her note across the Well — the census, the hour — and nothing answers, and nothing needs to.

I knot the cord.

The woman uphill is a note in a chord she cannot hear. I was never a note. I am the cord the count is kept on — the thing that holds the shape of the song without sounding it.

*One, here.*
