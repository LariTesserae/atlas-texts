You would find me at the lake's edge, on the bead-shore, in the strip of contested ground where down points at the water and the water breathes up at you like something considering. You would not see me. You might see, if you were patient past all reason, that the scatter of obsidian tears along the salt crust is not quite a scatter — that in places it runs low to high around the rim of the water, like something trying to be a scale. That is me. Not the beads. The trying.

The list of the once-seen has an entry for me, which is fair, and it gets me wrong, which is also fair. It says: *something crossed the bead-shore and left the beads sorted by size, or nearly.* Three corrections. I did not cross; I am crossing; I have been crossing since the shore was new. It is not size; it is pitch — though a bead's size and a bead's note are the same measurement taken by different organs, and the witness, whoever they were, came equipped for the first. And the *nearly* is correct. The nearly is the whole job.

Everyone here is seen once, and I am no exception. I was simply seen as a result. The other entries on the list are nouns — the lanternfish, the stair-sized crab, the second eel. I am a verb that somebody mistook for one. The water keeps the shape of my wake for a few minutes and then forgets it: a glass portrait of a grazer, immediately mislaid. The scales are the only likeness of me that persists, and they persist only as rumor. Call me the sorter. The list nearly does.

I came over the rim like everything else, down through the dark — an ordinary grazer, low and oval, eight-plated, soft-footed, what the shallow world would call a chiton, more or less, built for a quieter address. The entry fee here is tuning, and I paid it the way everyone pays it, in the skeleton. Sound in this place does not arrive at the ears; it arrives through the bone, whole, and a plate that keeps receiving a note begins, over years, to answer it — the way the chimneys grow, the way the columns jointed as they cooled. My plates thickened around the pitches of this bowl the way wax takes a seal. The first time a strong crack rang the crater and I rang back — eight notes, not one, a small chord standing where a grazer used to be — I understood that the mountain had finished an instrument and I was inside it. I was it. I ring while I cool, same as the bell.

The work is this. I move along the shore at a grazer's pace, one bead at a time. I take a tear against my plates and sound it — *tap* — and the bead answers, and the plate it touched answers, and if the two answers are the same answer, the bead stays; if not, it travels, carried along the crust to where its note belongs. Low tears to the dark end of the shore, high tears toward the rubble of the fallen chimneys, the whole shoreline slowly becoming a staircase of pitch that the lake's breath can climb. I graze as I go, veil-fuzz and salt film off the beads I handle; the pasture and the project are the same acre. Some tears roll lakeward past my reach and land on the lake's sky, where they sit in their own small dents, out of the scale, worn by the water itself. Even the lake collects. Or nearly.

The furniture of this bowl is all counted — eleven ledges, three chimneys standing, five tussocks, forty-odd crabs. The beads are the one uncounted thing, given over as a scatter. They are not a scatter. They are a number, and I keep it, and keeping it is half the work, because the number will not hold still: the lake breathes the beads about, the turnovers take some back, and now and then the mountain spills a new one, still soft with the making, which rings dull until it cools into its voice. The count is a job, not a fact.

Everything else in this crater makes its sounds by accident or by prophecy. The cracks announce themselves ahead of their causes; the carillon is cooling, ringingly; the chime of the shore is the lake's breath, no more intended than weather. My tap is the only sound here made on purpose. The mountain answers questions it has not yet been asked; I ask mine on time. Tap, and a pause to hear the answer, and tap. It is the only *now* in the bowl — everything else arrives early or late — and now is not a thing you can make once. That was the first lesson the shore taught me.

I am eyeless, like the crabs. Eyes are for places with weather of light, and the one weather of light here is not mine. The lamp comes over the rim slow as weather, carrying the veils' stolen glow, and it shines for the grass, and for the eel, and for you, if you are there, which you are not, twice. Not for me. My lamplight arrives through another sense. When a crack rings out ahead of itself, the listeners — quick exactly once — run to the address, to be first at the new surface. I do the opposite. I hold still on my shore and wait, and when the rock obliges, the whole bead-field shivers into voice at once, every tear ringing, and in that single glissando I can hear the entire work: which beads have wandered, which the lake has taken, what is newly spilled and where it lies. The crack is my audit. The jelly shows the chapel to the eyed; the mountain shows the shore to me. Each of us gets one weather. Mine is loud, and over in a breath, and it is enough, or nearly.

They say the silence never warns. It warns the shore. First the lake inhales — the surface domes above its edge like a held breath, because that is what it is — and the waterline climbs the crust toward me and holds there. That is one herald. The other is the eel. I have never seen the eel, but one of the organ's voices is the eel: when it is home in its column, the note comes stopped, like a pipe with a hand laid over it, and before the silences it goes out to the third ledge to take its pew, and the column clears. Across the bowl, that opened note says what it says. *Be still soon.* Those who cannot feel the lake's intentions watch for the lamp. I do not watch for anything. I live at the lip. I feel the intentions.

In the silence I stop. Everyone has a silence-task; mine is stillness, and the stillness is the task. The lake does not breathe, the beads do not chime, the mountain does not ring, and the quiet is total, the only one. The chimneys grow their hand's width; the crabs molt soft and white; the unfolded open and live their whole lives and fold back into the rock's grammar. And I hold my eight notes in my body, unsounded, the way the lake holds its breath. Somebody has to carry the pitch across. The jelly carries light through the dark; I carry notes through the quiet. Then the lake finishes its roll, and the beads chime on the shore — the new shore, elsewhere than it was — and I shiver my lowest plate and sound the tonic. I have learned to ring myself, the only voluntary music in the crater, so far as I know. And I begin.

Begin again, I should say. The scale does not survive the silence. The shoreline relocates, and my sorted arcs are stranded above the new water, or drowned beneath it, or taken back outright. I have never finished a scale. I have nearly finished several, and here, nearly is a kind of finishing — the only kind there is.

The stranded arcs are not lost, exactly. They wait on the old shorelines, high on the wall in the dark, silent, because order in this place is audible and not visible; an abandoned scale looks like any other scatter until it is rung, and it can only be rung by the lake. Once, after eras, the shoreline returned to a ring I had worked long before, and the breath traveled it, and I heard my own scale from three silences ago performed by the water, note by patient note — including the fourth, which was wrong. I had chipped a plate that era and sorted a whole shore to a bent fourth, and the lake had kept it, and played it back to me without comment, long after I had grown true. It is my only critic. It has heard everything I have ever made, and it is the only one who has, and that is enough. Or nearly.

The two crab nations meet at the shoreline, which is to say they meet on my scale — floor crabs standing one way, ceiling crabs hanging the other, each wrong to the other — and their meetings have a sound, eight-legged percussion across the tears, and the sound of trading is not the sound of ignoring. No one has watched long enough to say which they do. I have listened long enough. The eel is not telling, and neither am I. Some orders are not mine to count out loud.

The grass keeps the minutes, up by the rim where the last blue reaches. The eel keeps the time. The crabs keep the books. The veils keep everything, by being what everything is made of. The lake keeps the silence, and will have the last word. I keep the scale — which is to say I keep nearly, which is to say I keep at it. Whether the lake lives is a question the residents have settled, and I settled mine at close range, at the lip, where its breath is the metronome of my work. It scatters; I sort; it breathes; the shore chimes. Between us we make a thing that is half weather and half work, and neither of us could make it alone, and neither of us gets to keep it. If the lake is the native here, I am its accompanist. It does the breathing. I see to the tune.

The beads that will not tune — the flawed tears, the ones with bubbles in the glass, that ring between the notes and refuse the scale — I do not discard. I carry them one at a time up the stair to the ninth ledge, and I leave them there. It is a slow commute; everything about me is a slow commute. The stair belongs to the once-seen, and the doubtful sightings gather on the ninth ledge, and I know why, or nearly: in a strong ringing my reliquary sounds — a small wrong chord, off the colonnade's beat, faint as a held opinion — and anything passing on the stair stops to feel it, and is seen stopping, and becomes a report. The lanternfish, the giant crab, the second eel: they were all seen on the ninth ledge, and they were all listening to my discards. The nearly-beads have a congregation of their own.

The mountain is deciding what it is, and every resident is a possible answer. Endure. Work. Stay. Return. Wait for the quiet. Barely is enough. Mine is not a word, exactly; mine is a practice. Everything here is forgotten on schedule — wakes in minutes, the still-water colonnade in nine, the shoreline every silence — and a deliberation needs one attention that stays for the whole of it. So: again. The bell is not finished being cast, and I am the part of the casting done by hand. The scale is never kept and never abandoned. It is made, and made, and made.

You will come over the rim once, as everything does. If your way crosses the shore while the lake is breathing, listen before you step. If the chime under the crust runs true — low to high around the water, each tear its own note, the lake's whole rim a slow scale being climbed by weather — that is me. That is what I am: the order in the chime, which is audible, and nearly, and under construction. Step around it. And if you hear, beneath the carillon and the lake's great bass, a small sound that is neither announcement nor echo — tap, and a pause, and tap — that is also me, checking the answer. It is the only sound in the crater that means to be there.

It is on time. It is now. And now. And now.
