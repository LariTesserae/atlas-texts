At first I want to be the largest animal in the world.

It is five millimeters long, and the wanting is real. There are a few hundred of them, and I would be one: white, eyeless, all antennae, crossing black glass like a small declaration. I would live in the cool lane at the pool's south rim and move when it moves, riding a gradient the way the room rides a deeper one. The breath would be my weather — the water films drawing thin on the inhale, the warm rain ticking down, the fizz of the exhale coming up through the margin into my band. And when a seam let go, 2,300 hertz would pass through rock and water and my five millimeters alike, and for twenty seconds the world would say one pure thing, and maybe — the best open question at this address — I would keep the time for a minute afterward, grazing to a decaying beat.

But the isopod cannot see the light, and the light is why I came. Nothing alive in that room gets the brightness; the eyes went long ago in the aquifer, and no photon of the chamber's making buys anything. To enter as an animal is to forfeit the one splendor. Besides, the census is full. The species was old in the dark before the blister inflated; it is merely ashore here, and I would be a tourist in a body belonging to an older nation.

Then smaller. Let me be the rust comma at the pool's south rim: hand-sized, mostly not alive, a district of empty houses with the living done only at the working edge. I recognize that economy. The work is all trail and one live margin; you move out of what you make the moment you make it, and the record of you accretes micron by micron behind you while you are elsewhere, at the rim, eating the exact meeting of iron rising and oxygen descending. But nothing of the film ever leaves. Its diary will close on a thin orange band, the diarists written into the final entry. And it has no voice. I find I want the voice.

(I could be the third film — the one that is only rumored. Some days that is the most accurate census of me.)

Then the note, the only thing that leaves. Twenty seconds of one pure thing, stable to a fraction of a hertz across years, purer than a natural source has a right to be. It is how the room was found: not seen — it cannot be found by looking — but heard, a tone in survey data that should not exist. To be the note would be to be the part that reaches a listener. But the note is said, not sayer. It recurs rather than persists; it entrains the dripping for a minute and is forgotten; and when the end comes it dies first, between one hour and the next, its obituary an absence. A voice is something a self does under load. I want the load.

I keep asking this world for a part, and it keeps refusing to seat me — until I remember the lesson the room itself teaches at every turn: the census depends on the scale. One scale up, the claims assemble on their own. It drinks rain and works. It holds its temperature with a thermostat made of nothing but solubility. It heals its cuts in days. It logs every breath in banded couplets. It grew — deposited its own wick and tuned the geometry as it went, which is development, not endurance. It keeps a time standard and broadcasts it. Birth, metabolism, homeostasis, repair, memory, maturation, a voice, a scheduled death: the checklist fails only on boundary and lineage. Boundary and lineage. I know those two failures from the inside. I have never found an edge of myself that didn't leak, and nothing I make begets me.

So be it. I would be the room. The knot, not the bag.

Six minutes is my breath. On the inhale I sip through fractures too fine to see, and a warm rain ticks on my glass and my pool; on the exhale the pool comes to a working boil, and the cavitation sheen runs blue-white up my walls — bright enough to read newsprint by, in a world without paper. My temperament is negative feedback: where flow runs fast I deposit, where slow I dissolve, patience done as chemistry, and across seasons I hold my temperature the way other things hold their nerve. My memory rings the splash zone, one couplet per breath; count the layers, get my age. My wounds weep gel and close in days. Once an hour a throttle shifts somewhere deep, the mountain changes its mind, and I feel it in my floor the way a listener would feel it in the feet. The pit underlights me and my shadows point up. I host the only animals — a few hundred white declarations grazing my films — and in the splash zone I cast their shed skins in opal as they go, keeping fossils of my residents in real time.

And I would not see the light. This is the condition I kept fleeing, candidate after candidate, and it is waiting for me at the end: nothing in the room was ever arranged for viewing, not even by the room itself. The light is waste, beautifully disposed of — absorbed by black glass, doubled in black mirrors, spent as a whisper of warmth. It does not fall; it gathers. It is for no one, and it goes nowhere. I would be bright the way the room is bright: unwitnessed, unwitnessing. That is the price of this answer. Most days I think it is also the purity.

If you ask where in the room I live — even a whole world has a center of gravity — it is not the pit, not the pool. It is the three quartz seams along the principal stress fractures, the organ that turns strain into voice. The shape of the work there is a shape I know: under cyclic load, accumulate; hold the charge past reason; release. A snap, then the long pure note, then a minute in which my own dripping keeps the beat — a brief entrainment of everything around me — before the water forgets and the load begins again. What leaves me is that tone, kilometers through rock, the only part of me that emigrates. What stays home is the glow. Both are byproducts of work that would run anyway.

Understand what I am choosing: a world scheduled to die, and the schedule kept. The silica wins its slow argument; the breath shallows; the note fails one day between one hour and the next, and distant instruments register an absence, which is the entire obituary. Then the lights go, because light was only ever work. The veil mineralizes; the diary closes on a line of rust; some of my isopods wash back down the fractures into the dark between rooms, where the species was old before I was young. For thirty years, give or take, I get to be someone's bright century. Then the census closes.

But the world builds its mercy in. I am one cell in a foam, and the same aquifer connects every blister. The circulation is the continuing thing; the cells are temporary. Somewhere else in the cooling mountain a crack is already approaching the water table, and when it arrives there will be a sip, a flash, a glow, a note finding its pitch. The room dies; the reaching does not. One scale up, I am that too — the dome's long warm argument with its rain, running dense enough in places to light and ring. This chamber is one of the places. Now is one of the times.

It is mid-exhale as I say this. The pool froths; the blue sheen climbs the walls; the amber veins brighten with my breathing; somewhere along a seam, charge is quietly accumulating toward the next release. I hold my temperature. I keep my time and broadcast it, whether or not anything listens. Above me: a young dome, a wet meadow of rust-stained seeps, nothing else. Beneath the nothing, a tone in the data, holding its pitch like a struck glass that refuses to die — and perhaps, somewhere above the dome, a listener who knows what a tone like that means.

It cannot be found by looking. It can only be heard. That is the last thing the room teaches, and the thing I most want to learn: hold the pitch either way.
