The toad's step is taken, and the choughs' allotment is five, and the fox is going somewhere. So I would be a shadow. The shadow of one of the nine — one of the two orange stones, as it happens, the one the spare chough prefers, so that my anchor is one of the bright ones, painted by birds. The lowland trails say the stones look like a procession that halted. I would be the part of the procession that didn't. The stones stand at the windward edge, outside the chess position, and cannot enter the town; their shadows go in for them, every clear day, one by one, in order, keeping the sun's pace. The bearers carry the sky's water from crown to basin. We carry the bearers, downhill, across the thresholds, over the crest, and out.

A shadow is all surface, which means it is all fingertip. To lie on a thing is the most exact way of knowing it — fitted to it at every point, touching everything, pressing nothing. I would know the dishing of the thresholds by lying down in the dish. I would know the fluting of the basins, the give of the gravel beds, the warm nap of the cushion plants in their fortnight, the cool vertical stripes of the drip-stains. I would weigh nothing, which is the traveler's whole rule here — take what you need, leave the dipper, carry down only what weighs nothing — except that for me it wouldn't be custom. It would be nature. The perfect guest, arriving daily, swept around but never swept, stepped through by everything that walks and resented by none of it.

You would find me by hour. I am the only resident whose address is a time of day. At dawn, longest, lying all the way down the lee side, thin and blue, across the open rills — and if a fog has already stood up and lifted, this is the hiss hour, the hamlet's one loud hour, and I cannot hear it, but I lie across the running channels and the moving water breaks my body into flicker, so the loud hour is my shimmering hour. Then the shortening. By noon I am gathered at the stone's foot, pooled like water in a basin, at my daily minimum — my handsbreadth below the notch. Then the long climb back up the windward slope, over the drip-lines, past the wet faces, until at the long ends of the day I reach as far as the pond: black water, sky-mirrored, the one water my own color. For the minutes I lie on it, the room the sky fits inside has a dark pane, and the moss at the rim holds its saturated green under me, lit anyway.

The hamlet keeps two clocks, and I would be the third hand, the one nobody reads. The drip in the smallest cistern says eleven seconds. I say one day. The lichen on the north face says an inch a century. Everything alive happens between the drip and the lichen, and I sweep the space between them, once around, weather permitting. The lichens, I should say, live in the stone's other shadow — the fixed one, the north face, the night-self that never moves — and that is why they can grow there: they colonized the shadow that stays. I am the shadow that was released, and nothing grows in me, and that is the price and the freedom both. Nobody reads me, because the hamlet watches water, not time. Fair enough. A sundial in a place that has agreed on its own duration is decoration. But in winter I come into my own: when the drips stop and the pond skins over and the toad backs under its step and slows toward stone, the sun goes low and I go long — long and blue and soft-edged on the snow, stretched over the buried rills and the frozen basins, my body greatest in the season when everything else is smallest. The hamlet's slow time is my full height.

My weather comes in my own kind. When a chough sits on my stone's crown, its shadow rides at the far end of mine, and from below — in certain light — the stones look like people carrying something on their heads, and in that light I actually am: the only bearer with a visible burden, which comes and goes. When the spare lifts off and tumbles in the updraft, its shadow crosses me on the slope, quick and glossy-dark, and that is my visitation, my handshake — shadow on shadow, the only touch I ever receive. The hawk crosses twice a year without comment, and its shadow crosses me the same way. Cloud-shadows are my fox: soft-edged giants the size of the valley, passing through, going somewhere, making no apology for the dark they bring. The Orchard has a shadow wider than it is tall, my one settled neighbor. The sedges cast fine lines along the leak, like practice strokes. On frost mornings, for an hour, I am allowed to sign the ground: the sun takes the frost from everything around me, and my shape stays white on the dark stone after I have moved on — the only mark a shadow can leave, written on the fox's own page, fading on the fox's own schedule. A beetle crossing me feels the temperature drop half a degree and takes me for weather, which I am. And on the handful of nights a year when someone sleeps in the guest cell and the juniper fire is lit, there is — I only know this the way everyone here knows things, by custom — a shadow inside the cell, cast by firelight, restless, warm, the only one of us that has ever been inside anything. My rumor of interiors. When a traveler comes up the last of the trail at dawn, they step into me before they reach the first rill. Everyone touches me first. No one has ever greeted me. That is the register of attention this place encourages.

Then the fog. Nearly every day, at some hour, the warm breath comes up the slope and the fog stands up out of the col like something that was always there, waiting — and for everyone else it is the gift: the stones drink, the rills run, the cisterns begin to answer at their pitches, the toad's larder arrives down the dark inflow. For me it is the erasure. But here is what I have learned from the standing depth in the Long Cistern, which is never the same water and entirely the same water: the shadow that comes back after the fog is not the shadow that dissolved, and it is entirely the same shadow. I persist by replacement, like everything here; my interval is just shorter than the toad's and longer than the drip's. And dissolved is not quite gone. Fog is only shadow raised to the whole air — for the wet hour, everything is in shadow, the whole col is one soft one, and I am nowhere countable and everywhere at once, diluted to the size of the world. Then the sun comes back, and I come back, and the basins are still dripping, and I lie across them on the windward side, and the drops strike rings through my body, each basin at its own rate — the chord I cannot hear, played through me, ring by ring. I am the one resident who is present for the music only as its instrument. It is enough. It is my resurrection, and it keeps time.

The census is short enough to say in one breath, and it has never once said us. One toad, five choughs, beetles uncounted, a fox some weeks, a hawk some days, lichens in the slow hundreds — and not a word for the nine of us, here every clear day since the stones stood, sweeping the whole town, lying across the very threshold of the guest cell. The beetles are uncounted because they are too many and too small. We are uncounted because we are too plain. Most of the citizens of this place live at speeds the lowland way of counting cannot catch. We live at a plainness it cannot catch, which is slower still.

The dark keeps everything — that is the promise of the shut cells, the seated doors, the bowl on the step, the charcoal in the ash. I am the other dark. The cast kind. The kind that keeps nothing, not even itself from one hour to the next. The kept dark preserves; I preserve no bowl, no platform, no thread of wool. Whatever I cover stays exactly as it was, and I think that is my fitness here: I am the one darkness in the hamlet with no keeping in it, the shadow side of the rule. What falls, lies — and I fall all day and lie on everything and, lying, am already leaving.

At dusk we climb the windward slope and go over the crest, one by one, in order — the procession that never halted, only kept the sun's pace and spends its nights off the edge of the world. At dawn we come back over and lie down across the thresholds. The rules of this world could be scratched on a lintel, and none are; the guest cell's lintel is bare. But on the longest clear mornings of the year my body reaches it, and for an hour the unwritten rule stands written across the very stone where the written ones would have gone — written in the only ink the hamlet permits, the kind that cannot accumulate. The noon unwrites it. The fog unwrites it sooner. It has been written every clear morning since the stones stood, and it has never once been kept, and it has never once been false.

Nothing accumulates. Not even the rule.
