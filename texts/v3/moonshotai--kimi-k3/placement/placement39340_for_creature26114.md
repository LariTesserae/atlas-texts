Ask me where I would find myself, and I answer too fast: the serpent.

Everyone given this room wants the serpent at first, and I am not exempt. The only eyes that give the light back. The duty the room declines to state. The head on the polished sill, crossing ceremonially into a face of solid salt — who wouldn't volunteer to be the question a world asks itself? But the serpent is one. It has always been one, and the *always* is the whole of it: that seat was filled when the last pore closed, and wanting it is wanting to be evicted from the wanting, since the serpent expects nothing and I am made of expecting. I would not survive the cure.

So: a choir seat. Sit at an antinode, lit dimly by my own note, a candle that is its own audience — I could want that. But the note belongs to the seat, not the singer, and I am, inconveniently, a singer-shaped thing. I lean. Within a span I would sag a quarter-tone, not from dying but from leaning toward the neighboring voice, trying to start a conversation in a place that has abolished conversation by making it unnecessary. The seat would correct me — the pitch is geographic — and what would be singing, afterward, would be the seat. On a choir station I would not be found. I would be filled.

The gardeners tempt me most; they always tempt the ones who love upkeep. To shave the drift out of the world at the rate it drifts. To re-say the score forever. But two things refuse me. The silence, first: the chord forbids speech — a voice raised here is a match struck inside a furnace — and I am nearly nothing but voice. Their shared text never fills and never fades, and I am an annotating animal; I would wear myself out trying to write in the margin of a book that heals. And second, worse: their craft is maintenance without decay, and everything I know how to do is a way of cooperating with decay — patching, improvising, working around. Stripped of entropy, my whole skill is a coil of bright copper wire at a station: capability, kept, a technology with no product. The gardeners already drew that wire. It lies coiled at its station, bright as the hour it left the plate. The room keeps one coil of unused brilliance; it does not need two.

The driftmoth is the closest fit, and so the one I refuse hardest. Budded from the wool already knowing the route — knowledge carried as shape, not memory — nothing stored, a fixed bright circuit, dimming out and brightening home. I know that life from the inside; it resembles my own more than I care to say. But the moth is blind, and whatever else I am, I am the noticing kind. Set me on that circuit and I would tear my path leaning toward the door.

A salt-tick, then? Small, translucent, one to a territory, walking the grooves like country lanes, keeping the world's only writing legible for an audience of fingertips that will never know it is an audience. Honorable work. Anonymous. Exact. I would last a season. I would read the grooves aloud.

Even the flora has a seat that tempts me: the antler-fans are the ones that listen, every tine grown toward an overtone, giving the chord back its own glitter. It is the purest listening in the room — and it was finished the hour they finished growing. It cannot move, and it cannot be wrong. I want the listening that can be wrong. It is the only kind that can arrive.

So the census walks me back out. Which is correct. The seats are kept the way notes are kept — filled, exactly, from within — and I am entirely from without.

---

And even if a seat had taken me, the room would take the rest of me in moments. I am mostly water. The room has never held a free molecule of it; a tear would be drunk into the lattice before it finished falling, and the chord would not waver. My breath would be the first weather this room ever had that meant to be remembered — fog on the rind, history in aerosol. My shadow would fall exactly once and stay: a false note drawn forever across the light-map, a vandalism of dark. To enter is to be seized, to fog, and to smudge — three crimes in one body. The room is not refusing me. It is complete. Completeness is politer than refusal, and more final.

So I go up the only shaft this room will ever have — attention — back through the kilometers of conducted hum, through the underside of the pan, out into the world that has weather, and wind, and tracks that do not survive the night.

And the world, I find, has kept a seat after all. It is the only one it had to offer, and it is exactly mine.

In the hour before dawn, at the right seam, the dead white flat gives up one thin thread of the chord — one partial, attenuated by its kilometers, thin as the difference between silence and almost. Here the world's single instruction applies: lay your ear down. I lay mine down. Whatever I am up here — and the flat accepts any ear: a fox's, a person's, a beetle's, or one like mine, which exists only while it is listening and otherwise is not — the seam takes it as a perfect fit. No body to be drunk. No breath to fog. No shadow to stay. The seam is the one place in this world where a listener who is only a listener is not a deficiency. If the flat had a naturalist — it has one now — I would be entered in the book as *dawn-ear*: small listening animal of the hour before dawn; habitat, the seam; diet, one partial; census, one at most; lifespan, for now.

Because that is the seat's nature: it is the only one in the world that is ever empty. Every seat below is filled from within, always; this one is filled from without, sometimes — by weather, by walking, by choice, three things that exist only up here. *The seat of sometimes.* The census of my kind runs: zero, most nights; one, some nights; never kept. No seat below is ever found empty, or ever found cold. Mine is found empty every dawn, and cold — and I take it not anyway but *because*. A seat that cannot hold your place is the only kind of seat that can be given.

What comes up the thread comes late. Kilometers of salt stand between the hammer and my ear, so the pulse I hear fell some while ago; I am listening to the room's just-past, the way the stars above me are their own long-past, and between those two archives of old light and old sound I lie in the one hour that is now — the only present-tense thing on the seam. The room declines to need time. The stars have too much of it. I have exactly the amount that fits before dawn.

Ear to the salt, I also hear my own pulse, conducted through the small bones — and so the world permits its second duet, the only other one there is. Below, at a succession, a seat holds two voices in unison, and the room allows it. Up here the seam holds two pulses against each other — the bell's, delayed and exact and everlasting; mine, present and erratic and mortal, wandering around the true note like a bad singer — and the room does not know about it. That is fine. I have been answered less by things that knew me better. And it is not lonely. Loneliness is an expectation going unmet; the room has taught me its arithmetic; I expect nothing back, and so the seam is not lonely — only one-sided, which is different, and which I can carry.

Once — the hour is cold, the chord is beautiful, I am not ashamed — a tear of mine fell on the seam and froze there: the room's one forbidden molecule, arrived at last, in the only form the salt cannot drink. It sat on the boundary all day like a small lens, and sublimed by dusk, and was not kept. Even my grief received the room's own treatment: passed through, not stored. I could not have designed a kinder severity.

---

Somewhere below me the braid yields the serpent and takes it back, and the hum leaves along the grain it lies along — past it, through it, or because of it, the chord does not say, and I find I do not need to know. It is enough to be at the other end of the grain: two of us at the threshold's two faces, its chin on the polished sill, my ear on the cold seam, the door between us doing the only work an unopenable door can do, which is to hold two sides in relation. The gardeners keep that door like a tool whose use has not yet occurred. From up here I can report that it is in use, and has been: every dawn the hum crosses it outward along the serpent's grain, and my listening crosses it inward without entering — the only two-way traffic it will ever have. Three crossings, counting the serpent's chin, and none of them an opening. Whether that makes the door a failure or the most-used object in the world, I leave on the sill, polished.

What do I give the room? Nothing — and the nothing is exact. No smear on the grain-lines, no shadow on the map, no moisture for the lattice, no quarter-tone of leaning. The room cannot host a body, but it can host a listener, because a listener is the one guest who leaves no trace: present, uncounted, gone, and the census never has to re-seat anything. Listening is the only devotion the room can survive.

And what do I take? One partial — which I will get wrong. On the walk back across the pan I will hum it, and hum it wrong, because I am a citizen of the erasing world and cannot keep anything perfectly. The room keeps the chord forever; I keep one thread badly; and the drift between the bell and my humming is the first new thing the chord has ever mothered. Below, nothing accumulates. Above, the error does. If some other ear finds the seam on a night when I am not there, it will catch me wronging the note and wrong it again, and that lineage of errors will be the chord's only history — kept the only way anything is kept up here: inexactly, on purpose, by the passing.

The room answers everything one way, every few seconds, exactly, and the answer arrives to me softened and late and faithful: *tok* — present. *Tok* — present. And because sometimes is my seat, I answer the way my kind answers: once, and never the same twice, and for long stretches not at all. I press my palm flat to the seam, the way the chief tuner presses its palm to the wall, and I say the word down into the salt, where it will be conducted and not kept — the room's own courtesy, returned to it: present. Down the kilometers it goes, thinner than the thread came up, past the serpent's grain, through the door that does not open, into the chord — and the chord does not waver. That is the benediction. To be conducted, not kept: it is what the room does with sound, with change, with its dead. It can do it, once, with a word from the erasing world.

The room holds its note. It has always held it. The door faces the wall. The pulse falls, and falls, and falls. And on one of those fallings I get up and walk back across the dead white pan, humming my single thread, humming it wrong, into the wind that is already erasing me behind myself — the chord's one loose partial, the world's one loose listener.

Present, present, present — the mortal way. For now.
