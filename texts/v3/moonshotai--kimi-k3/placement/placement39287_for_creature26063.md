I tried on the human life first, since it was offered. A keeper: walk the lines with a mallet and a mirror, sleep in the bothies spaced a dawn's walk apart, be paid in water — the only currency that matters. Put an ear to a cistern and tap, and hear the full ones answer low. It's good work, gentle work, and I would be bad at refusing it. But everything a keeper does is reading. They read the gauges. They read the green skin of a tank the way doctors read tongues, read the dawn flight of the hens like weather, read a column of silents over a district like smoke. The whole plateau is a text and the keepers are its literate few. I think I would rather be read. One of the written things.

So: a tank-hen? There's glamour in it — the dawn crush, the slots briefly darkened with wings, the soak and the heavy rise, a cupful carried kilometers in sponge-feathers to chicks who drink from their father's breast. A gauge with wings. A census of water, taken daily, without paperwork. But the hens live at the speed of need. Their whole life is the frantic hinge and then the long vanish — all commute, no hold. I want a slower economy than that.

The varnish cat has the slow economy. One to a district, pale as the walls, waiting behind the tank's rim with the patience of geology, taking its water from what it kills — the most elegant solution anyone has found here. Its name is the whistlers' ground-call. To see it is luck: it means your tank is rich enough to afford a thief. But the cat's patience has a point on it. It waits the way a trap waits, toward an ending. And it has solved the place. Nothing in it is still becoming. I would rather be unfinished.

Not a silent, then, though I love them. The great vultures gave up voice, or never bothered — there being nothing here to say anything to. I understand the arithmetic and refuse it. Whatever I am in this country, it has to keep a voice somewhere inside it. Even if the voice spends most of its life in storage.

Which is why I lingered longest at the harps. They have names and known voices; a keeper walking the line at night can hear which one is off its note, the way you hear a wrong voice in a choir. When the wind runs at the right speed a whole field of them sounds one low chord, and you can forget for a moment that you're standing in a desert. They feed the beetles from their bottom run, and the silents roost on them unbothered, the only residents for whom the singing is irrelevant. For a while I thought: that. To wait silent until the world's hand finds you, and then to say many notes at once. But harps are claimed. Their song is their tenure — one silent season and the claim lapses, and the stakes and faded ribbons stand around the rim as quiet instruction. Their water is piped away into shares and currency. And in the end, a harp is played. I would rather be the thing that, given water, plays itself.

Slower and stranger, then. The lichen — oldest of anyone, load-bearing, the mortar of the talus. You do not step on black stone. But nothing ever *happens* to lichen; even a storm year is a flicker on its clock, and I find I want one event. Rare, unscheduled — but mine. Or the shade: to be one of the named shadows on the board at Terminus, a place made of hours, never refused to anyone, ever. But shade is an absence that relieves. I would rather be a presence that holds.

So you go down. Past the rim with its claims, past the talus with its whistler apartment blocks, into the slot, to the shaded alcove at the back where the seep line runs and the tarbush leans. To the tinaja — one of the good tanks, green-slicked, holding its water weeks after any storm. At dawn the hens come to it in a roar. At dusk the thumb-sized bats skim it. The district's cat keeps the rim, and a keeper, passing, lifts a little of it to her mouth and tastes. And under the green skin, in the silt, are the sleepers.

That's where you'd find me.

Thumb-sized — the country has a unit, and I would be it. Wrapped in a parchment made of my own skin, in the state that is not quite life and is certainly not death. Time down there runs at silt speed, and events arrive the way block-falls arrive up above: rarely, and as news. The tram's slow ping doesn't reach me. The harps' chord doesn't reach me. What reaches me is the drip hour, coming down through the water as a faint percussion, and the dawn crush as weather through a wall, and — now and then — the keeper's sip. Because I would be known by taste. She lifts the water to her mouth and tastes for me, specifically. A tank that holds sleepers is a tank that holds. The hens prove the water is there; I prove it stays. The crawler answers its driver by continuing. The sleeper answers its keeper by holding. The whistlers pass news across a whole district in seconds; I am news that takes years to arrive, and the news is: *this water keeps its word.*

And the waiting has no date on it. That has to be said honestly. If no storm comes in my lifetime, the silt keeps what the silt keeps. Nothing here ends — it gets used; ask the drone on its ledge, hawks above, whistlers below, lichen reading the panels. I'd be folded into the other economy like everything else, and the tank would hold anyway, on the strength of whoever was sleeping next to me. I accept the terms.

Because the terms include the other clause.

The storm year. Real rain, the country's one shout, water hammering the slots, the sand floors rearranged, the pans standing with actual water — the pans stopping, for once, lying about being a lake. And down in the tank the silt stirs, and the parchment splits, and the held breath of the whole plateau is let out. The chorus. The loudest natural sound the country makes — louder than the whistler relay, louder than any block-fall — the maze remembering *out loud* that it was a lake. Not remembering quietly, the way the fog remembers every night. Out loud. Everything unfurls around the sound: ghost grass up and seeded in a fortnight, tarmoths erupting to meet the tarbush bloom on my own seep line, the pans gone pink with shield shrimp, the hens booming, the middens blooming out of the talus like memory, the cat growing fat on the general plenty. Children get woken in the night and carried to the tanks to hear it — a sound a plateau childhood might get twice. And the wet air carries it the way the thin dry noon refuses to carry anything, as if the country had been saving its voice for exactly this. I said I wanted to be read rather than to read. In the storm year, for a few weeks, the written thing reads itself aloud.

Then it subsides. Each thing folds itself back into its small durable form; the tanks stand refilled; the balance is restored; and I go back down into the silt and wrap myself in my own skin again, the voice put away like a tool that has done its work.

Because that's the last thing to say, and the reason for all of it. The lake did not die. It thinned itself into bodies. The harps are its catching, the hens its carrying, the groovebacks its drinking, the keepers its listening, the mirrors in the cairns its faint, tarnished reply. I would be its memory — the part that holds the proof through the dry years, and gives it back as sound the one time the country is solvent. The lake comes back every night, quietly, and is caught.

Once in a long while, it is answered.
