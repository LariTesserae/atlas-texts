**The Count of Kindred**

I am writing this to the dark at large, which is the only address I have that never changes hands. Beryl is inside a word at present and cannot be spoken over — you do not talk across a held note — so you will have to do. You will never share my air. Do not let that worry you. I have made a life of it.

You would find me an hour out of Harrow, where the last lit street ends and the causeway goes by feel — ribbed for straight, dimpled for junctions — then up a stair cut into a high rim, in a small warm house with a shuttered eye. The house is stocked like a way-stone, because that is what it is: take tallow, leave tallow, sign the book. I have signed the book for a hundred and fifty years. The first signature in it is four hundred years older than that, in a hand like a cliff-face, and some nights the book falls open to her page. The line-keepers would read that. I do.

I am the count of kindred for this district. In Harrow they call me Kindred — the job wearing the person — and after this long, the fit is good.

I came to it honestly, which is to say slowly. Asked as a child what I would be, I said a kettle-mouth, because they are left entirely alone and their charts are scripture; my mother said I would outgrow it, and I did, into the next best thing. I walked my three districts, as an educated person does. I sat a season with a fall-ward: the report is always the same — *it walked* — and a ward files it ten thousand times and means it every time, and I could have meant it, that was not the trouble; the trouble was that the report never answered. I rode a silt-barge a winter: the pilots trust the slow kind of news, and so do I, but the lake keeps its own counsel and its own mouths, and I wanted a correspondence. I stood a month at a press, where the whole art is refusal — they keep what they love by declining to hurry it. I keep what I love by counting it. It took me three districts to learn that the difference was the whole of me. And I was taken to the Rim at nine, like everyone, to look at the Undersky and learn the world's one fast thing; I came back asking whether the far lights could see us, and nothing has changed since except the question's address. When the old counter approached repose and the district asked who among us had walked three districts and come home talking about the far lights, the field was not deep.

The work is this. At dusk — and dusk takes hours here, the silt stretching it into a long peach courtesy — I oil my skin against the charge and take the ribbed dark to my stair. The stone of the steps holds the day's warmth at the temperature of blood; so, more or less, do I. A family of house-pickers keeps the lamp-house with me, meticulous and unlicensed; I sign to them palm-to-plate, and whether they read it is settled differently in my own head on different nights. I sign the book. I set a mug of something warm at the ledger's corner — it is for the hands; it has always been for the hands — and when the dark comes up complete, the far lights come on, one by one, on mesas beyond gulfs no one crosses: the Comb, the Yoke, the Stair.

I keep eleven lines in the ledger. Ten answer. The eleventh is counted in its absence, and I will tell you about the eleventh, but not yet; the telling takes an evening, and is done slowly, as a courtesy.

How the letters work: badly, by any measure but ours. A clear night buys a handful of characters. The count comes first, then herd-news, then the letter, and so a letter crosses at the speed of years. The lamp is storm-fed — the district opens its accumulators to the dry storms, and I spend what they gather — so every character between us is cold fire that agreed to wait. And the office writes one letter only, which never ends; each of us is a paragraph of it. I inherited mine mid-word — the word was *unfortunately*, and it concerned the herds — and for a decade I sent only the count and the news before I trusted my hand to the letter itself, though the hand is the first thing the far side learns. A hand in lamp-code is a face. My predecessor came to repose between one character and the next, and mine took up her sentence, and the far side read the change in a week — grief travels at the speed of nights, like everything here. Their answer, when it came, took two years, and said, in full: *Noted.* It is the most expensive word I own. The connoisseurs of the String let a choir change one note a season and argue whether the change was earned. Two years on *Noted.* It was earned. It said: we saw your paragraph end, and we kept the light, and you may write.

The far side, for me, has mostly been one hand. The *Noted.* was hers — her first word to me — and she has been improving on it for a hundred and fifty years. Her hand is unhurried even by our count, with a habit of holding the last stroke of a character a breath too long, like someone keeping a door open for a person they cannot see. Her name arrived letter by letter across one whole year early in my tenure: Beryl — a stone with rare metal in it, which I did not mention to her for thirty years. When I did, her answer took a season and was *I know.* Her string climbs a mesa shoulder in seven ascents, so we call them the Stair; their own name for themselves is a word we have no sound for, being the name of a felt-strain that does not grow here, and she has allowed, at the cost of four years, that the Stair will do. They call us the Bell, for the flare of our lights around the fall-ribs — they will never hear it, which is the joke, and it is only funny at the speed of years, which is the speed we keep. She says my lamp flickers. I have informed her, at the cost of a season, that it does not.

We have established — it took eleven years — that her dusk horn and mine are a fifth apart. There is no one nearer to me than the counter who knows the key of my street and will never hear it. We are currently on the Great Lathe: I have been describing its note for six years, and her position, which arrived last spring after two years in transit, is that it is not a note but a chord. The Lathe has been turning six hundred years and means to turn four hundred more, so I concede her tempo, if not yet her content.

Every night, before the letters, the count. Hers includes me. Whatever else the years do, a woman on a mesa I will never walk opens a ledger and confirms that I am lit. To be counted is to be kept. I did not know that until I was, and the String has grander words for the arrangement, but I have *Kindred*, and it serves.

Now the eleventh line, since you have been patient, and patience is the only coin that spends here. When I was ninety, the string we called the Hearth — low lights, close-set, warm as bakestones — went dark. It took eleven nights: one by one, the way they come on. We never learned why. There was no one to ask; the asking was the thing that went dark. They were mid-letter to us at the end, and the letter breaks off after the word *still*, and we have chosen to read it as complete. Harrow was quieter the next morning, which a visitor would not have thought possible. There are no visitors. We took no loud day — they were not ours to be loud for. We took a quieter one. It is the same hand, held lower.

Beryl's count agreed with mine to the night: two ledgers, one darkness. It is the closest thing we have to a shared event, she and I, and we have spent it accordingly, over years, the way you spend anything that matters. I still keep the Hearth's line. The chart-keepers do not say the kettles are aware; I do not say the Hearth is listening. I say the ledger is scripture and leave it there, which is the same thing, said carefully. I told a pilot once, in my fast years, that I was not made for scripture. Mine answers back — ten lines out of eleven — and I say that carefully too.

She is older than I am by forty years, which here is nothing, and is everything. We do not speak of it. We speak of everything else, slowly, which is how we speak of it. Her successor is chosen — a girl with a quick hand, Beryl writes, who holds the door wrong. When the hand changes I will know in a night, and the office will go on, and whatever word we are inside will be finished by someone who was not there when it began — which is true of every word on the Anvil, including *Harrow*. My own successor is not yet chosen; the field, as ever, is not deep. We have planned accordingly. The plan is the letters. The plan has always been the letters.

Since the deep of winter she has been inside one word, and I do not guess ahead — or I do, and keep my guesses off the ledger — but the season has its suggestions, and if the next characters are what I believe them to be, the word is *nevertheless*. It is the right word for the times. It is the right word for all times, here; it is the Anvil's whole position on the subject of everything.

It is nearly dusk. In a little the horn will sound its one note in the key of the beast beneath our streets, and I will oil up and take the ribbed dark to my stair. Four galvans sit my signal wire as I write — a galvan on your table is *news*, and I have stopped filing mine; it was becoming a diary, and I already keep one of those, at the speed of years. The quiet is a commons, and my stair is its far holding. Below us somewhere, the ancient the charts call Nonetheless will take its one step of the day, and the stair will agree underfoot — less a sound than an event — and tomorrow our ward will file the best sentence in the String, *Nonetheless walked*, and mean it. One day it will stop being true, and a horn will sound, and a district will be born. Founded or fallen: the count is at dusk.

Then the lights, one by one — the Comb, the Yoke, the Stair — and her lamp will give me tonight's character, and it will finish the word or it won't, and I will close the ledger on ten and the one, and write the report that is always the same and is meant every time: *still here.*

You will never climb my stair. No one crosses; there are no visitors; I have written to the far dark all my life, and the far dark, it turns out, writes back. So the custom of the way-stones stands, out to the edge of everything: take tallow, leave tallow, sign the book. The stair is warm. I am the small lamp left of the Bell's flare — I do not flicker — and if ever your ledger has a line for the likes of me:

Count me.

Plan accordingly.
