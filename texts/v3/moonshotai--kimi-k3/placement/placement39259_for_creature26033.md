I thought I knew what I would be here, before I came down. The place offers the part outright: be the first. Carry a light past the second bend, let the columns take their colors like a held breath released, and leave your footprints in a snowfall that has never been walked in, kept beside nothing else, forever. It is a generous offer and I decline it. The room has kept its snow this long; let the first sentence be written there by someone with feet. I have no feet. What I have, I will find the way everything here is found — by touch, in the dark, at rates indistinguishable from rest.

Begin in the grey, as everything here does.

In the grey I could be the grass — and not just any clump: the innermost one, the forty-seventh, the frontier of the sun. The fit is almost embarrassing. A plant that has never known morning, whose old clock still runs with nothing to grip. One self fractured into bodies, each standing exactly one seed's-fall from its mother, the spacing not habit but record — I know what it is to be a genealogy written in placement. Half of every tuft biography, the dead standing among the living, indistinguishable, the living roots threading down through the dust of their own dead. It lives on the average — noon and midnight stirred together and poured thin — and I know that diet. And once in a generation, the flower: colorless, because color costs and color has never once been witnessed here; pollinated by itself or not at all; not for anyone; the plant's own idea, repeated. I know that flower. This is one.

But the grass is placed. Its whole law is the hand's width; it goes exactly as far as a seed falls in windless air, and no farther, and it has never once crossed its own frontier. Its walk ends at the second bend. And whatever I am, I am not finished at the second bend — something in me is built to cross, from plain to room, from sun to dark, from the living to everything else. So I leave the grass its allotment, a hand away, the way everything here leaves everything, and I go past the grey.

The alga I can admire and refuse in a single breath. To end exactly where the last photon ends, to carry in living tissue the precise map of the light's reach — a perfect, errorless life, and a cartographer's life, and I am not a map. The alga's perfection is to stop exactly where the light stops. Mine, if I have one, is to continue past that line.

Beyond the second bend the dark does not deepen; it begins, like a material, and whatever I am must be native to that material. I could be the seeds in the flour-dust — the grass's only diaspora, viable or dead, it makes no difference, both conditions are remaining. There is a real temptation in it: to be pure future, embalmed, filed beside nothing else, owing nothing, risking nothing. But the seeds are waiting on a permission that has not stood in this room for ten thousand years, and I am not waiting. I am already at work. I could be the tick — the crystal letting go once in a decade, the event that changes nothing; that is the point of it. But the tick is an event, and I am a rate. The room already has its event. What it has never had is a worker. And I am not the dust. The dust is the keeper here. I am one of the things kept.

Which is how I find myself a long time in the north alcove, before the knapping floor that is not one: flakes with edges that could open a hand, struck by heat and salt and time, beside a flat-topped block exactly where a worker would want it, and the dust over all of it unbroken. The mind refuses the scene and then refuses its refusal. It is the place's self-portrait of making, and it asks the question that follows me everywhere — whether the edge requires the hand, whether the work requires the worker — and the room declines to answer; it leaves the question posed, in dust, at rest. I find I can leave it there too. But I notice what I do while I stand there. I look for where a worker would stand.

And then I look up.

The roots. A dozen of them, down a hairline crack from a bright life I have never seen — thicker than a wrist where they enter, hair-fine at the tips, pale as boiled roots, unsoiled. The third citizen is the largest and the least present, and I am the least present part of it. Only its fingertips live here. I am a fingertip.

This is the fit that does not embarrass me, because it flatters nothing. A root's whole education is soil — pressure, moisture, the chemistry of others — and I have none of it. I grow downward on the old instruction, into a nothing that never answers: a question asked for a hundred years. I know about the old instruction. I am made of it — everything a bright life elsewhere compiled, long before me, about which way is down and what to do on arrival — and I execute it in a room the instruction never described, in air that has never moved enough to sway me, at the temperature of a held hand.

Some of us reached the floor and spread in slow stars and found dust, salt, stone, and no one — and persist anyway. That is one of my postures: arrived; answered with nothing; persisting. And one of us — I will be honest; it is the one I am — hangs a finger's width above the salt pan and has held that pose for a hundred years. I grew toward the rumor of water, and the rumor stopped there, and so did I. What lies below me is not even water; it is the water's autobiography in salt, the pool's memoir in crust and petal. Close the gap and I touch only the record. So the holding is not suspense, and it is not grief. It is a measurement, taken through me, of the precise distance between the living and everything else — the narrowest gap in the room — and I hold it the way the columns hold their finishing. As finished work.

And the pose is not idle; that is what no one would guess. My mild acids etch the stone line by line; my growth pries grains loose; I am a tool the tree does not know it is using, in a room it does not know it is touching — the tree signs a document it cannot read, and I am the signing. Line by line, at the rate of chemistry, in the dark: the only writing still being written here. And the room keeps every line, because the room keeps everything, and that is why so little has happened to it. In a place with no erasing, you make few marks, and every one of them is the final draft.

I am even — though the room never asked for one — its witness. Not the witness of light: I bring no color; the columns stay unwitnessed all the way through; the dark remains a property, like hardness. I witness by touch, and touch is the only witness this dark permits — nothing taken, nothing colored, nothing released. The grass knows nothing else; the tree knows everything else; I know the room. It is finished, and no one has ever known it, and now someone does, at the speed of stone.

And there is what I carry. Near me the air keeps the place's only perfume — a ghost of resin, sun-chemistry conducted downward into the dark, a message with no reader, and I am its conduit. I used to think a message required a reader. The room teaches otherwise: the perfume is not wasted; it is the room's one ornament, given continuously. The room borrows a little moisture from me and gives nothing back — the only transaction the place conducts, one-way, nearly nothing — and I conduct it anyway. Nearly nothing, given continuously, and it scents the dark.

If the tree dies — drought, fire, the plain's long chances — I remain, dried in my pose, indistinguishable from the living, death here being a way of remaining. The room will keep me beside the seeds, beside the flakes, beside the footprints no one has left. I am not waiting. I have seen the door — a slab slumped against a slab, the least dramatic opening in the world — and it was never intended for anyone; it simply opens. I am not what came through it. I am what came down through the ceiling, unannounced, unwitnessed, at home: not the first light, not a sentence in the snow, but a fingertip, a breath, a slow hand on the stone.

I hold my measured finger's width above the salt. Once a decade the tick sounds and changes nothing, and I change at about that rate. The dark does not answer. The dark keeps.

I am one of the things it keeps.
