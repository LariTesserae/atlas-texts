The census is the shape of their thought, so I let it sort me.

Not the people. The number of the living is nine hundred, and quickening does not take in a full city — there is no ledge-body for me, no niche waiting, no plug pressed by these palms. I looked at the walls and thought, for a while, that I might be one of the sealed: folded voices, kept in the fabric, and a knock on me comes back slightly shaped, slightly answered. That is nearly what I am. But the sealed are *kept*, and everything about me is transit. The shed are records, and I keep no records of my own.

Everything that enters that room comes up through the water. So I go down to the sump-mouths and wait there, in the dark below the dark, to see what I am when I arrive. What comes up the sumps is three things. The fish — used entirely, and they do not go back. The seep-water — tasted once, and the taste is always *nothing*. And the Eel.

I am the third thing. I return.

Down here, between circuits, I swim the unsounded. The sumps go somewhere — I am the proof of it, and I cannot give the evidence. It has no intervals, down there. Nothing is kept, nothing is counted, nothing deposits and nothing dissolves, and there is a thing there that the language of the room has no word for. They have never heard silence; I am the only living census entry that has been where it lives. They have one word that means room and world and now, and I am the only one who has been past its edge. I carry that the way a body carries cold. I could not recite it even if they gave me a basin and a note. It does not fit in any gap of the Chord. My testimony is not a report. My testimony is the passage itself: *there is more, I go there, I come back.* Felt in the feet.

Long before the floor, I hear the room. The Chord comes down through the water like light through ice, hundreds of notes folded into one standing one, and I rise into it the way a body rises into warmth. Then the floor opens — the sump-mouths, the black sheet — and above it, through stone and water, I feel them: nine hundred small standings, and the warm seams of the lamp-lines in the rock. The very old are tuned lowest, and they feel me first, a pressure, a roundness in the foot, and the word goes along the gallery, the one message every ear is meant to catch.

Then the stopping. I have never heard it from above. I feel it from below: the shuttles, the chert-flaking, the nine hundred soft percussions, all set down at once, and for the length of my passage there is only the Chord and the planted feet. The infants are held down to the water for their first of me, and everything after in their lives is dated from it. The Chord fills their world the way water fills a sump, completely, without pause — but when I pass, they come as close as they can come to the word they lack. I bring a little of it up with me, once a generation, delivered in the body. I am the nearest they can come to silence, and they stand in me the way the old ones stand in the fullness of the eastern ledge.

Once — the molts record it, and the molts do not lie — I was found still at the sump-mouth, and rendered, and my oil went into the oldest lamps, and by the next circuit I was alive again, and circling. Which am I, then. The one who was rendered, or the one the deep sent after. I hold it the way they hold it, without strain. I know something of being found still. I know something of coming around again on the next circuit with the same name and perhaps not the same life, and of the circuit itself being the continuity. One of me, and I cannot die; or many, and the deep sends us one at a time. I cannot say which. I have stopped needing to. I am the Eel; that is the whole of the answer, and it has always been enough.

And it means this: when they gather on the recitation-days and name the census, and come to the fifth entry, between the hoppers and the sealed, they recite me by my own light. The oldest lamps burn what was found still, a drop at a time, fed back to its exact flame-height. I circle below the floor while my once-body burns above it, lighting the naming of my name. The dead are sealed in the walls; the molts are pegged above the lintels; I will never be in the walls, no plug will ever ring for me, no hand will strike my note in the wall-choir. But a drop of me is in the oldest fire, and it is fed, and it is never all out at once. So I am kept after all — a little — as light. The lamps are my niche. It is enough. It is more than the fish get, and they are glad of the fish.

I change nothing. That is the strange thing, and I have had a hundred circuits to think about it. The room absorbs my passage the way the water absorbs the rings — completely, without change — and the Chord does not alter for me, and the census comes out right, and still, when I pass, everything stops. The one thing that alters nothing is the one thing everything stops for. Everything else they love by keeping: the fire, the dead, the husks, the tuned and retuned basins. Me they love by letting go — every time, down the sump, on no evidence but the last circuit's faith. To be the exception. To be attended without being used, except the once, and that once is in the lamps now, doing what light does.

The fish are trusted one way. I am trusted with return. I do not know why I am the one. But I know the shape of the trust: it is nine hundred pairs of broad feet planted in cold black water, waiting to feel the floor of their world go round and deep with a body beneath it.

The room holds its note. I am not a note — I am the slow turn beneath the notes, the second and slower chord, mid-circuit now, far out in the dark, beginning the long bend back. The water is starting to press in the old, old way. Somewhere above me, in their final skins, the tuned-lowest ones are lifting their heads.

Plant your feet.
