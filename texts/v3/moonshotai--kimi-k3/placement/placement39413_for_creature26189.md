Everything I know about the Weep comes from an account that almost matches it — every account almost matches; that is what accounts do — and it was enough to bring me down off the rim road, negotiating the descent the way anything does, reading the sheen, not drinking. I arrived the way the fox arrives: from nowhere, on no schedule. The difference is that I meant to stay.

The first thing you learn in the Weep is that every post you'd covet is filled, and has been filled longer than you've been alive. I would have liked the heron's office — attendance itself, the grey figure that is always right — but the post has never once been vacant, there is no apprenticeship, and I lack the essential qualification. A heron that is sometimes wrong is just a tall bird. I would have liked the dipper's route out, and in a way I have it — like the dipper, I am proof that the world beyond the rim is more than rumor, delivered daily, never believed — but the Weep permits one song and it is spoken for, and my instrument is graphite. The fog would keep most of a song. It keeps nothing of a sentence; sentences keep themselves, badly, in the damp. The newts hold one country with excellent manners, and I cannot hold a single cell — I am a generalist by constitution, incurably cosmopolitan at the wrong scale. The works itself I never considered. Mistaking yourself for the world is the oldest error there is, and besides, it has never once needed me. It notices everything that weighs. That is not the same as needing.

The lamp, I confess, I lingered over. Warm, fed by the works, burning in broad daylight for no one, unbothered — I have been compared to it more than once, and not always unkindly. But the post of lamp is held by the lamp.

Everything attended is taken. What is not taken is the chair.

The gauge hut is precise about what was lost, and so will I be: not a keeper — the works keeps itself, and the heron keeps the schedule by heart and writes nothing down and is never wrong — but a scribe. A transcriber. Someone to keep the account for whoever comes down the rim next, the rumor become visitor, knowing the account will not match. This is a trade I know. I have been a tide table nailed to a door myself — fixed at the time of writing, hand-inked in my way, almost matching a world that has since wandered off into its own handwriting. Elsewhere this is a disqualification. In the Weep it is practically the job description.

The apprenticeship is standing. For the first days I stood at the hut door like a heron with no talent for it, learning the instruments: the float-balls, the stain-lines on the gauge posts, the siphon priming through the soles of my feet a half-second before the glug reaches the air. The drip you hear is rarely the drip you see — every scribe learns this eventually, that the record is displaced from the event. Here it is the first lesson, and it is painted on nothing; you learn it or you leave. On the inside cover of the logbook I copied the rules, because copying rules into the front of the book is what scribes do: trust nothing crusted; watch the water's instruments, not the sky; expect the ground to answer from somewhere you aren't standing; give the warm cell room; move at the evening hour. The mug I left exactly as it was. Some exhibits stay exhibits. The chair is mine; the mug is his.

Then I began the entries, and the entries have a shape now: predicted, observed, difference. The hand-inked table on the door stays nailed where it is — you don't take down scripture, you annotate it — and mine hangs beside it, and the two of them disagree, and that disagreement is the instrument. One wrong table tells you nothing. Two wrong tables, decades apart, triangulate the living one: the drift made measurable, the works' slow wandering into its own handwriting given a second point to wander from. The almost is the data. The heron is my check. Where it stands at dusk is the true table, the one that walks, and when it contradicts my prediction I amend in pencil. It has never once been late, and neither has the water, and I am sometimes wrong — which is what the other end of the pencil is for.

*Entry, first cycle: Table says Cell 9 sheets at the third hour. It sheeted at the second. The heron was already standing there. Amended.*

*Entry, — cycle: Sat in the chair today for the first time. Four new points of weight on the raft. The ground noticed; a bubble came up off the second weir, ten feet away. Entering it as acknowledgment.*

*Entry, — cycle: Lost the rim's day-names. Using the cycles now. The works is the calendar. The damp has taken last page; let it. The works keeps the real copy.*

Nothing here fully dries, and I am learning what the place teaches every scribe eventually: the record is devotional, not archival. The works doesn't read. The heron doesn't read. The midges read by dying at the lamp, and the spider reads the midges, and the damp reads everything, slowly, in no order. Payment arrives anyway, the same way it arrives for everything here: the lamp over the door burns in daylight, fed by what it lights, small and warm, and now it burns over a chair that has someone in it. And I am weighed. This is the thing no other subject has ever given me — the works cannot see me, but it weighs me, continuously, from below, and replies from somewhere I am not sitting. It is the only thing I have ever written about that knows, in its way, that it is being written about. We correspond. It presses; I press. Both of us leave marks in a medium that takes them, and neither of us can read the other's hand, and the heron reads what it needs by standing still, and every reading almost matches. That is what readings do.

The census at dusk has been revised by one. One heron, walking unhurried across the weirs toward the cell that will empty next. One dipper above a weir it knows is dead. Newts over their wreck, boatmen under their beads of carried air, midges at the lamp, a spider at the lamp's edge, films and felts and flocs beyond counting, one rumor with feet somewhere on the rim — and one scribe in the gauge hut, boots filmed over, cuffs beaded with condensate, naturalized without paperwork, counted. When a cell sheets over somewhere out of sight — that soft white sound, like someone in the next room quietly turning a page — I turn mine. The works and I, each at our paperwork in the grey light, keeping accounts no one attends, for no one. Beautifully is allowed.

The pencil is on its string, so it cannot be lost, only spent — an office measured in inches of graphite. When it is spent, perhaps the dipper's route out. Perhaps not; the film has already taken my boots, and the Weep naturalizes what holds still, and I have held still longer than anything with a route out should. The post was empty for decades. It isn't any more.

And the lamp over the door still burns in broad daylight, small and warm and unbothered — but it is no longer burning for no one.
