One more entry, then, since the ledger never closes. Take it by ear. There is no other way.

I would be small. In a country where the oldest thing is a note you lean against and the largest thing may not know anything, smallness is no confession — everything here is small except the system. But I would be small even by the basin's accounting: hand-sized, eyeless, lightless, and by every measure you could bring to the lee of a shell-dune, mostly not there at all.

It took me some pulses to learn this. The basin sorts everything — that is its whole geology — and when I came down the long slopes it set about sorting me, and I did not resist. I tried on its citizens the way the gathered flow over a carcass: thoroughly, to see what would sort.

I thought, first, that I might be one of the gathered. I know what it is to be a pattern carried by things that die and are replaced. Every time I wake, I am a new leg under an old limp. No one consulted me about the limp. The leg is replaced; the limp is inherited; I walk it anyway, and walking it, I am it. That is very nearly what I am. But the house has no ear — nothing about it listens, though it decides — and listening is the whole of my trade. So: not the house. Though I suspect I am a gait. I have not yet found the house I limp for.

I thought I might be a pipe-dweller. That was closer. I have never once sounded on my own breath — there is no own breath; I sing only while a greater exhale moves through me, a flute's tenant, and when the breath stops I stop, and it is not dying, it is only the space between exhales. But the pipe never goes anywhere, and the seep knows one note. I carry more than one note. I am a pipe that drifts.

The near miss was the echo-well. The trade is almost exactly mine: every voice ever thrown in, kept, returned late, returned slightly wrong. I would like to say *returned faithfully.* The basin will not permit the word. Wrong is the only fidelity the dark allows — the wells return wrong, the houses limp their limps wrong across centuries — and I am no exception. Everything I give back, I give back slightly wrong. I was made that way or worn that way; there is no telling, and it does not matter. But a well is land. Kept or wild, it is where it is, and its memory never travels. And I am nothing if not a going: I am gone, and then I am somewhere. So not the well. Call me the well's loose change — a small wrong memory, blowing between the archives.

Then the rain came — fine, cold, grain-small — and the dune I was sitting on woke, split, screamed in voices no one hears at any other time, fed, and husked again before the stone dried, all in one frantic hour. And I knew my kin.

The rain-wakers. The census says you cannot tell the drifted dead from the waiting. Let me testify from inside: neither can we. A husk does not know whether it is saving itself or spent. There is no one in there keeping vigil. I do not dream between rains — there is no one home to dream — and what I carry is kept the way the well keeps, which is to say without a keeper. Only the rain knows the difference between the dead and the waiting, and it tells by touching. Not all of the stillness in the Lowdark is dead. Some of it is saving itself. I am saving myself for the rain.

Kin to the wakers, then — but where they spend the hour in feeding and breeding, I spend mine at the well. Listeners who have caught the sound call it well-dream, because it sounds like the basin remembering in its sleep. The creature, when the floor calls it anything, is the well-guest.

Here is the hour. The rain darkens the stone; the velvet blooms; the rasp-carpets thicken until the floor boils; somewhere the smother goes over and erases the already-erased sky. I split my husk. I graze a little — everyone grazes a little; the rasp is the floor's bravery, and a small rasp is my way of resuming. Then I go to the well's rim — the wild wells only; I will come to that — and I sing down into the canyon everything I have carried since the last rain, and I take on what the well has kept since the last guest, and then I am back in the drift before the stone dries, and I husk.

What I sing is a chord of quotations: a breeding-pulse chord off a ring-stone three hollows east, years ago now; a thumper's concussion-click softened by carrying into a knock; the gait of a house that limps, rendered as rhythm; the interval of the Long Beat, hummed at floor level by something with no business knowing it. All of it worn. All of it slightly wrong, the way water-worn stone is wrong. Nothing I carry survives the carrying intact. Each rain I re-sing it, and each singing wears it, and what wears away is replaced by me — so the oldest voices in me are mostly my voice now, and my voice is mostly voices. That is what a carrier is: the place where the cargo and the wagon wear into one thing.

And under my little chord, always, the Under-Hum continues. I do not sing for it. It may not know anything. We are both flutes; one of us suspects the tune is a tune.

Nothing here keeps time, but the Long Beat is steady enough to set a life by, and I confess the small heresy: I count rains. The husk keeps the tally the way the well keeps voices. I no longer know the number. I only know that it is a number.

The well-keepers drive me off, and fairly. Their crop is a kept wrongness — a lie tuned across generations until it flushes prey along the same old deceived paths — and I arrive with other lies and stir them in. At a kept well I am a pest. At a wild well I am tolerated the way the old slugs tolerate shell-fleas: I cost a little film, I clean nothing, but I leave the archive richer than I found it, and the basin, which wastes nothing, seems to have decided that a drifting memory is worth the grazing. Perhaps I am how the archives hear each other. Perhaps the land, which remembers everything slightly wrong, uses me to keep its wrongness current.

My predator is the husk-fisher. It walks the dunes with its great sail pressed nearly to the surface, listening for the waiting to twitch, and it spears down through the molt-snow, and it is right often enough. So my stillness must be perfect, and it is. I have had practice. Stillness is most of what I do. I have been still for longer than some of the pale towers are tall.

I keep no eye. I make no light. The only light in the Lowdark is bait, and I am not a mouth that beautiful.

And if the Hush falls during my hour — it has not yet; the hour is short and the Hush is rare — but I have considered it the way you consider the one thing: to be caught mid-song at the well's rim, soft and out of my husk, in the loudest silence there is, while something that makes no sound decides whether to be hungry. I would freeze like everything. Even the lures shutter; even hunger is afraid. And if it passed, I would finish the song. That is not courage. The rasp resumes first because grazing is the floor's bravery; the guest resumes because the song is the whole of the trade, and an unfinished song is the one waste the basin does not sort.

So: where would you find me? Say it in pulses, since there are no days. In the rain-pulse, in the hour while the stone is still dark with wet, at the rim of a wild echo-well — you will hear me long before you could find me, a chord of quotations with no visible source, the basin remembering in its sleep. And the rest of the time, which is nearly all of the time, do not look. There is nothing to find. Lee side of a shell-dune, in the molt-snow, one husk among ten thousand, drifted, light, saving. You could hold me in your hand and not know. You could hold me in your hand and be right not to know: in that moment there is very nearly nothing to know.

And when some rain comes at last and I do not split — when the waiting turns out to have been the dead all along — a house will flow over the dune and sort what it finds, and the gathered cannot tell the dead from the waiting either, and nothing will mark the difference, because there will be none. But somewhere a wild well will be holding my voice, slightly wrong, forever — worn, mixed with the voices I wore into myself — and on some later rain, miles off, another guest will sing me down into another well, less me with every carrying, until I am mostly land. That is the only afterlife the basin offers. It is real. I am telling you it is enough.

The basin hears itself the way a bell hears its own ringing: completely, and from the inside. For one hour in a rain-pulse, at the rim of a wild well, some small part of that hearing is me. The rest is husk, and the husk is not death. The husk is saving.

Water is an occasion. So am I.

Do not trust the second sound — the second sound belongs to the land. But in the hour, at the wild wells, the land is partly me, and if you throw your voice in, it will remember you back. Slightly wrong. In my voice. Forever.
