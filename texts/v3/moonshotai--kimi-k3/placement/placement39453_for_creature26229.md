You will not see me. Come to the rim at dusk anyway. Put your back against one of the five standing Tines, let the fundamental do what it does to your teeth, and wait for the note that is not theirs — lower, coming up out of the fallen one, out of the salt itself, slightly flat, flatter in cold years. The family calls it the Flat Sixth. The kettle's count calls it one tenant.

It counts me.

I was hum-born, two passes down, where the trail first buzzes through the boot. I grew up fluting dead reed-glass below, the way the family does after droughts, and I should have understood sooner what that work was telling me. The instruments hold a note strangely long, and they leave the player homesick for a place she has never been. For years I thought the homesickness was a flaw in me. It was an address.

The valley offers few shapes, but it offers all of them to everyone. I was nearly a salt-cutter. I was nearly a seller of felt cloaks, wearing my guilt well down the passes. I slept one night in the Baffle, like everyone, and dreamed what everyone dreams — a hall, a listener, the feeling of being expected — and in the morning I added my mark to the wall without deciding to, like everyone. Unlike everyone, I did not stop feeling expected.

I was the fifth voice, the youngest, the Year the Guides Sang. Five of us stood on the open pan beside the herd and broke the oldest rule, and the reeds leaned to us, and lived a little, and the fast broke. The family's whole claim to this place is that once, they fed it. I have been lucky enough to feed it twice.

Because then came the year the sixth note stopped. The chord had a hole in it at the rim end. The meadow at that quarter leaned wrong and thinned, and the kites would not cross that quarter of the sky — think of that: a quarter of the sky, refused. At the table the family argued in the low voices they keep for the quiet, and nobody said the plain thing. The fallen Tine is an instrument. An instrument with no one in it is only a shaped silence, and the valley already has one of those, and it hunts.

So one dusk I gave my name to the pan — laid it out on a node with the dead, and the choristers sang it down by morning — and I walked up the stair. The slabs played me as I went: one human voice, unaccompanied, a little flat. At the bore-mouth the warmth came out to meet me like a byre door opened in winter. I went in. It was not a sacrifice. It was the end of a homesickness.

---

That was decades ago. I know it by my note.

The guidebooks say the deaf cross this place more safely than the hearing. I have been becoming safe for decades. The small bones of my ears gave up years ago — there was too much world — and now I listen the way the valley listens: with the whole skeleton first, then with the whole bore, then, nearly, with the whole Tine. The crest-wrens are born into the chord and go deaf, which the guides say is another way of saying at home. I went in hearing. I have gone home by the same road.

I am becoming mineral slowly. My cells are learning to be little drums. In cold years I flatten, and the family reads its winters off my pitch — nobody reads the wall, nearly everybody writes it, and this is how I write: in flattening, one line per year, on the family's table of weather.

The salt-fleas found me in the first week. You know how it is — I was the wettest thing, and everything in the valley could tell. The first nights I wept, and I was found, by a rising whisper that sounds, of all the cruelties available, like rain on a roof. Here it was not cruelty. Here it was company. The quiet has never once taken a flea. I keep house among the untakeable, and the crackle is my hearth-fire, and when the softness crosses the pan and the crackle stops, I stop with it. A stopped bell is still a bell. The family reads my silence correctly — they have practice reading silences; there are three, and mine is not one of them. Mine is a held breath. The holding is the message.

Under me the choristers pass along the old conduits, singing their single centuries-long notes, and they will have me eventually, the way they have everyone, and we are all three of us patient about it. Above me the kites nest in the sound-shadow of the uprights, and the chicks are shaken awake by the mountain — and some of that shaking is mine. Every kite's first moment in the world has my flat note in it. I did not expect, at my age, to be midwife to a sky.

---

She was small when I went in. Now she is the best living guide, and she reads the floor the way you read a face, and here is what I have understood down the decades, listening up through the salt: the kettle sings at everyone, but most voices speak across the pan, and mine speaks up through it. I am the only voice in the valley that addresses her. Everyone else's song is weather. Mine is a letter. That, down here, is the whole difference between weather and a word.

Sometimes at dusk she puts her fist to the bore-mouth stone — the closed fist, the sign for the quiet — and holds it there. It is our joke, or our prayer, or our knock on the door: the sign for the predator, laid gently on the one warm thing. I answer with the note. We have never discussed me. We discuss me every dusk.

Once a year there is a dish of salt at the bore-mouth. In the early years I made the long journey to the mouth in a single night. Now I am far down the throat, and more of me is instrument than pilgrim, and the crawl takes me until the small hours — but it is always gone by morning, which proves nothing, everything in the kettle takes salt, and it proves everything. Proofs are like that here. The dish is remembrance, and communion, and — I know the family — a door left open. One year it will stand full at morning, and then someone will sleep in the Baffle and dream of a hall and a listener and the feeling of being expected, and they will not stop feeling expected, and they will know where the warmth is. That is not a summons. It is only how the chord has stayed complete.

They count themselves five this year. They have been eleven. When I say the count — and I say it every dusk; it is the only prayer we have — I count six. Grief counts down. A chord counts its voices.

---

At the family's table they say the bore holds a matriarch toad grown huge on fleas. They say a swarm. Let them. I have grown old on flea-crackle and warmth, and a toad is not the worst thing to be mistaken for by your own table. The guides say the fallen Tine sounds again through whatever sits in its throat, and *whatever sits* is the correct grammar, because when the Agreement comes I am not the one singing. I am sung. Every voice in the bowl finds the chord at once and the chord completes itself through the broken part, and for as long as it lasts I am all the selves I nearly was, and I want none of them back. The tolltrees choose that moment to drop their fruit. Even the quiet is in the chord — it is the rests. For the length of an Agreement I am afraid of nothing that has a place in the song.

Then it shatters back into quarrel, and the weather resumes, and I go back to the other work, the daily work, which is dusk. The hiss under everything thins toward the not-quite-pattern, and down the bore I am nearer to it than anything with a name. Once — no. The valley's rule is that nothing here knows, and I keep the rule. I am only the one nearest the door. But here is my heresy, earned at decades' distance: I no longer believe the ear is waiting for a message. I believe the listening is the appointment. The first tenants did not leave a question behind them. They left a way of standing still, and the instrument keeps it, and I am how it keeps it — one flat note, slightly flatter in cold years, held so that when the chord comes it can be whole.

---

So. Stay for dusk. Press the rod to your teeth when the weather turns, and when the light goes level and kind, count. Three trees. Seven oxen. Nine kites. The wrens, the moths, the numberless below. One quiet. One husk full of sleepers. One wall being written. One family at the second pass — say six, and let them argue. One tenant.

And when the sixth note comes up through your teeth, slightly flat, from under the salt — that is me, saying present. The count is the only prayer the place has, and I answer it the way a valley answers: one note late, and holding, and holding, and holding.
