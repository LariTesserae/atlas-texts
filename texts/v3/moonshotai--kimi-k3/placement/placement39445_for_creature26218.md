**What the Route Remembers**

Ask me where I would be in the Basin, and at first the answers pour in like slurry off the discharge main — fast, thick, too white to see through. The Basin settles its water in steps, milk-white to jade to clear. Let me settle the same way.

I want to be the dredge, of course: the factory that eats the bottom and lays down new country, the slow clock the whole valley sets itself by. But the dredge has a crew. It is a workplace that happens to move — bunks in the superstructure, a frog behind the gauge panel. To be the dredge is to be a roof that other lives sleep under, and I want to be a life.

I want to be the frog wall — that is, one voice in it. A creak-gate. A two-marbles. A sound the new hands learn from the dark before they ever see the animal that owns it: to be known by ear in a place that reads by ear, to be a landmark like the bottle-bird, a thing people steer by. That one nearly holds me. But a voice in the wall is a dusk shift, a season. The wall thins to almost nothing in January, and I find I want to be there in January too.

I want to be the willow — everyone must want that, the first hour. The only tree, the entire forest, fed coffee grounds, defended from snails, every bird in the valley passing through eventually like the one bar in a small town. But the willow is taken, and there can only ever be one of the only tree. Besides, it is the most watched life in the Basin. I am looking for something unwitnessed.

Even the spider tempts me — the first thing the haze renders visible each morning, a jeweled diagram rigged between stanchions — but a spider is all patience, and I am all passage. Even the dog, which has never seen a tree but one and sleeps in winter on whichever main is warmest. But the dog belongs to the pump-watch. It is someone's. And yes, I could end on the rim with the retired pumpmen, sitting on a bonnet, taking the valley's pulse the way sailors locate a foghorn — but that is listening from outside, and I want to be inside the sound. The human lives tempt me least of all: the crews are the only inhabitants who know the completion year, and I would rather keep a different appointment.

Every time I reach into the Basin for a self, my hand comes back wet. The water, then. And in the water, the one who came farthest to be here.

An eel. The ditch behind the third pump house. The drowned rooms of Cell 4.

Nobody stocked me. That matters. I came as an elver the width of a bootlace, from the sea ultimately — up the river, up the outflow, against a current that was never meant to be climbed, in the slack hours when the water over the weir runs thin. The journey is in my body still, the way the sea is in my body: not as memory, as direction. In a place where the haze rations sight to arm's length, I go by taste and by the pressure-alphabet of the line down my flank, and nothing is rationed. The pump drone is my bedrock. The bucket-clank carries for miles through the water and I read the dredge's position off it like anyone else. At dusk the frog wall rises and the ditch thickens with it, a pressure I wear along my whole length. When the pumps go down for maintenance and the crews stand still in the bird-scream, underwater it is the same event: a weight I had stopped noticing lifts off me, and the ditch rings with what remains. Nobody calls it silence. I call it the size of the place.

I know the valley as a ladder of tastes. Lime-bite at the discharge face, where the feed comes up at blood temperature. The green taste of the jade cells, thick with daphnia and midge larvae — the larder. Then the clear cells, and under the roosts a sharpening, because the birds pay back at night onto a few structures everything they gathered all day across the valley, and I can tell you which gantry they favor this year. Rust and cream and sulfur where the warm inflows band. Salt on the old crust. Once a year, for a few days only, the runoff tastes of leaf — the willow letting go — and then the snails finish what fell and the ditch forgets it. The ground forgets. I keep the flavor a little longer than the ground does.

I live in the old draft of the world. Cell 4 drowned a hamlet, and I hold some of its rooms: still water standing in shapes it did not choose, doorways that break the current, right angles in a valley of deltas. The catfish keep the road cut, pale and long as men, the old family in the big house. I keep the interior, wrist-thick, threading the doorways by day; we are on the terms of tenants on different floors. Above us the chimney stands out of the water with ibis in the flue, and I know it as a rain of white, a sharpening of the water below, feathers falling through the dim like slow snow. On the new plan sheets, they say, the fill line bends around that chimney, and no order was ever issued. I know nothing of mercies. I know the water stays.

I am one of the Basin's undertakers. When something ends — a coot under the bank, a turtle on its back by the flange, the leavings of the fox — the taste of it travels the ditch before the light of it reaches anything with eyes, and I follow. Nothing dead stays where it fell for long, and it is mostly us who see to that. It is not grim work. It is return work. I am how the ditch forgets a death the way the mud forgets a footprint: not by losing it. By taking it back into everything.

On the wettest nights I leave the water. Rain loosens the whole Basin; every ditch's boundary goes soft; the tastes of three cells run together in the mud, and a body can go overland. I cross the causeways wrist-thick, gravel loud on my belly, the lamps smeared overhead, on errands. No one has thought to ask about the errands, and I could not tell them. They are the kind of knowledge a body keeps: a smell remembered from a drowned corner two cells over; a burrow to check; the warm seam by a valve where someone did not come back last winter. Once a year the humans stop their machines for a crossing — a sawhorse, a lamp, a chalked word, the little diesel ticking while the toads finish being weather. I cross under the same road those nights, in the drowned culvert, my migration beneath theirs, unchalked. I like the crews for stopping. It is the only traffic law in the valley, and it was written for feet.

In winter the Basin pours itself into its warm water, and everything for kilometers concentrates here, and I go deep into the outflow's breath and braid with my kind in the warm seam, slow as fill settling in the dark. Above us, on the same iron, the dog arranges himself. Any pipe above blood temperature grows a dog; below it, it grows us, though nobody has written that down. In summer the dragonfly nymphs climb out of my element into the other one and leave their hollow skins on every stake, and I note each departure the way you note a light going out. They leave the water the way I will leave it. Upward. Out of everything.

The plan map on the office barge carries a year nobody working will live to see. Mine is not written on any wall. It is a silvering. One autumn the ditch will tell me — the level down a finger's width, the salt sharpening, a taste in the outflow like a door opening onto a stair — and I will go down the way I came up: the outflow, the river, the long pull. The Basin is scheduled to end; I am scheduled before it, and that seems right. Nothing here is native, and the truest citizen is the one that keeps the circuit. The birds work it seasonally, the crews in rotations, feathers and boots alike — and I work it once, totally, across an ocean. Years from now, in slack hours, elvers the width of bootlaces will come up the outflow from the river, from the sea. The route remembers what the ground cannot. If the water lasts, one of them will thread my doorway and not know it was mine.

I leave not even a footprint. The mud forgets me as I pass, and I have always been at peace with that — I never asked to be remembered; I asked to be in it. And when the last decanted liter goes over the rim and the plain dries and greens and quiets into something unremarkable, a hand's breadth of strange young rock will hold what the Basin was: snail shells, bird bone, turtle scutes, the fossil record of a noise. Somewhere in it, a small gray thread — the quietest line in the recording.

That is where you would find me. Not on the rim with the old men taking the pulse, but inside it: the ditch behind the third pump house, the hour the lamps come up doubled, the water moving once against the current for no reason anyone will ever ask about. Going about my errand. In it, of it, and almost the sea's.
