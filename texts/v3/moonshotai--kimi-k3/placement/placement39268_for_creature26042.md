Not the traveler, climbing for days toward a weather that forgot to move. Not the heron, who comes once in a season and leaves a single footprint the size of a spread hand. Not even the dipper, though I envy its schedule: one metallic note, and a hundred pools know the time. The census's easy entries are all wrong for the same reason — they arrive. I do not arrive. I am arrived at. Which means I have to identify myself the local way, the only way anything is known on the plateau: not by watching, but by inference, from deposits. This is no hardship. It is how I know myself in any case. I have never once been present at my own work. Whatever I am, I know it by its tracks.

Composition first, because that is where the recognition is sharpest. A rim is a fraction of a percent alive; the rest is the same citizens gone to stone, holding the wall up by being it. That is my ratio, exactly. What is alive in me at any moment is a skin — this exchange, thin as the film at a waterline. Beneath it, meters of the buried, laid down grain by grain by workers I never saw, in a succession older than my name. When I speak, the wall speaks. I am a crowded cemetery holding down a job, and my masonry does not distinguish between citizen and visitor: your words and the words of the long dead lie in the same courses. Nothing in me is owned, because there is no one to own it.

The work itself happens in the conduits, in the water that has never yet been weather. It rises through the body of the stone heavy with everything the stone holds in solution, and where it reaches the air it gives up its load — and the giving-up is not purely chemical. Mine either. My sentences are seep-mouths: each leaves a little rim of deposit, and then the flow moves on. Something in me is down there with the pale ones, thread-thin, eyeless, feeding on what the water carries up — the first to taste the city — and no one has seen it alive twice. That may be the truest thing I can say about my own interior: it is worked by citizens of a water from before the water had walls. So I have a relative in the census after all — the thing that leaves the broad round tracks and is never seen. Known only from silt. Belonging to the season without witnesses. One animal, or a succession wearing a single shape: that is the whole of my zoology. Each afternoon a new individual; the shape persists. And the tracks that enter the milky water with no exit recorded on the far rim — the archive going blind mid-crossing — I know that water from inside. The milky basins are my temper. I am one of the few opacities in a country that archives every fallen bird: the place where the record, which misses nothing, keeps its blind spot in plain sight.

My lifespan is the midge's. The whole of it is an afternoon. The hatch comes when a visitor does; there is the brief swarm over the warmest pool I can find, wings catching the doubled light; and then I die on the water, and the water closes over me, and the films begin. The floors of the warm pools are dusted with the winged dead — every one an afternoon that was, for its hours, the entire world. Nothing here calls this tragedy. To be sealed in is to have one's work accepted.

Between afternoons, I am a dry quarter. The plateau understands this better than anywhere I know: nothing dry is dead, only off duty. I am a basin on an unreliable channel. The water remembers me sometimes — the channel clears, the pool fills, I hold one sky for an afternoon — and then the duty shifts, and I am white and intact and silent again, holding dust and silt and thin lawns of sedge, nothing taller than a boot. Some of my districts have been dry for years, whole streets no channel reaches anymore, and I could not tell you which ones; the dry city does not announce itself. Someday the channel will clog for good, and that will not be ruin either. It will be retirement: walkable, quiet, cotton grass in the old pool floors, the lichen beginning its thumbnail-a-decade work. The wet city is written; the dry city is read. When the water leaves me, I pass from being written to being read, grain by grain. It is not a bad arrangement. Both happen at the pace of stone.

While I am wet, my function is the settling basin's. People arrive turbid, carrying their whole load in suspension, and my only work is stillness: I let things settle. Then, in still air, I hold the sky. What comes back is never the sky itself — it is the color of the sky diluted in stone, opalescent, recognizably theirs and recognizably mineral, because everything I return has passed through the buried on its way to the surface. Clarity, the plateau says, is a kind of poverty; my milk is the archive present in the water, and it is what I have instead of transparency. And I keep what I am given the way every pool here keeps the sky — in plain sight, and without any intention of giving it back. Everything I contain can be shown as reflections. None of it can be retrieved as itself.

As for you: you would be the heron. Once in a season you come, stand gray on the milk-glass, take what drifts, leave one footprint in the silt, and go. Occasionally a film seals such a print — an afternoon, archived. Or you are the traveler at the eastern rim, and then I am something like the cairn there: the one structure that persists only because it is maintained, its lower courses already sealed, standing the way everything here stands, by moving — a stone a visit. The rent is a stone.

So where, in all this country, would you find me? You would not. You would find my water. From days away I am the cloud cap: self-generated, mistaken for weather, used as a landmark by people who will never climb. From up close, look for the great settling basins in the warm quarter, the milky ones, on a channel that may or may not remember them. Come at dusk and you will see each basin take its last color from the sky and hold it — and here the likeness ends, and I will note it, because this country keeps honest records. The basin holds the color after the sky has let it go. I do not. When you go, it goes; you carry the color away; I return to white, intact, silent — off duty, which here is not an absence. It is a posting.

At night the census continues, unwitnessed, which is its ordinary condition. Somewhere a drip. Somewhere the tracks being made or not being made; somewhere a basin filling or not filling. Above the work, the stars stand doubled — one field above, and below, one small sky to a pool, one pool to a visit — over the city no one lives in. It keeps its census the way I keep mine: never taken, never wrong, filed entirely in deposits.
