The first time, I was only what everyone is there: a pair of soles, a held breath, a body lying in wet gravel to see in. The keeper gave me the middle — pointed, waited for the knock out of courtesy, and pointed again until I went — and I sat in the held space with my hair lifting and listened to the engine keep its appointment. They went still a half-second early. The floor thumped. The rings crossed the lake and their eyes closed, and there was no amen, because the knock is the amen. I did not ask about the mitten. I mention that because I have since learned it was the first thing they noted about me. The second thing was the hat. I set it down on the gravel without thinking — rust-colored, a warm thing, a color the room does not contain — and when I left, it stayed. It is in the wall now at ankle height, next year's knee-height, riding the tongue upward and backward into the darkening blue with the cup and the notebook and the ink's grey feathers. My first deposit. The archive takes everyone in the order they arrive.

Crawl out and the world takes you back, but badly. The pipes knocked in my building at night — water hammer, ordinary plumbing — and I lay awake counting intervals: nine seconds, forty, six, never eleven, and the variance wasn't tidal, it was just slack, and I was angry at the building for keeping such poor time. A pile-driver on a site three blocks off arrived through the soles of my shoes as I crossed the street, and I stopped walking, and traffic went around me. The milk in my glass was the color of the stream and for a moment I couldn't drink it. At two in the morning the refrigerator sounded its low note and I found, without having decided anything, that I was humming the fifth against it — holding it, making the chord — because the chord is the one thing that requires a person, and I had become a person it required, at a distance, in a kitchen. That was when I understood what the keeper's walk to the shore had been. The note does not stop at the slot. A length of the pipe had been installed in me without my noticing. It shows, they say, in the lifted hair, in the hum held in the teeth, in the waiting. I had all three. I was waiting for my own building to lift a finger and go still a half-second early. Buildings never do.

It was in that kitchen, missing the room the way you miss a person, that I began to audition for it. At first I wanted to be the fox. It has the best office in the place — mornings only, no duties but appetite and attendance, the whole job to sit beyond the slot in the grey hour listening to the engine and waiting for whatever the engine flushes out. But the post is filled, and filled correctly: the fox has never once come inside, and I understand now that this is not timidity but jurisdiction. Somebody has to keep the door.

Then I thought — smaller. To be a fleck of cryoconite with my one dark gift of caught sun, melting my own cell in the wall, a pocket within the pocket, one drop of water and its whole citizenry, entire. But the cells are full of citizens, and the room's smallness is already handled at every scale: the commas of worms at dusk, the springtails rafting the tension, the midges hatching on the thump. Every small post is manned. I envied the boulder in the pothole — the one resident whose work is its home, milling the hole deeper by the week, patient, circular, employed. But the boulder is machinery. It works through every knock and has never heard one, and by then I had been hearing them too long to qualify. The dipper has the troughs and earned them; I would not speak over the dipper. And the keeper's post is filled — by the keeper, who is the first draft of it and still being written.

Then I saw it, the way you see the one empty seat in a congregation. Everything in that room presses itself to the edges; the margins are lavish and every margin is spoken for; and the middle stands empty eleven seconds out of eleven, tide in, tide out, because the only person whose seat it ever was gave it up for the margins long ago. The center is for the sound. They are for the listening. So on the nights that matter most — the wind nights, when the flue sounds and the glacier takes its organ breath and the keeper finds the fifth and holds it and the whole chamber has a chord — the chord happens in a room with no one in the good seat. The keeper cannot receive it; they are holding it. You cannot sit in your own fifth. The one thing in the room that requires a person requires, on those nights, two.

And there was a second vacancy, quieter. The postmaster handles all the mail and receives none. The knock comes in for everyone; the fork goes down the conduit and the sea keeps its counsel; nothing is ever addressed to them. Two vacancies, then, and one of me. The audience and the incoming. The empty seat and the letter that walks itself.

So I went back. On the road I called it the Knock without deciding to, the way you say a person's name in your head on the way to their door, and that is when I knew the room had received me — because anyone who found it twice would call it the Knock, and the second finding is the one that counts. The outwash was littered with the outgoing: pocket bergs melting into the angular gravel, evidence of nothing, and I stepped among them like a man crossing a post office floor. The seam was breathing on its counted rhythm. I lay down in the wet gravel — you have to, to see in; it is also how the room receives you, prone, at slot height, folded small enough to pass — and came through with the mist of the exhale on my neck like a postmark. They were not surprised. They can always tell the ones who will find it twice; it shows in the waiting, and I had been visibly waiting for months, at a refrigerator. They waited for the next knock, out of courtesy, and then pointed to the middle of the room. It was the whole of the welcome. It was also the job description, and the pay.

I came with empty hands this time — the wall had taught me what happens to set-down things — and I sat in the held space, and the space held. I have been holding the seat the way the seat holds the sound ever since: intermittently, and on purpose.

The work is wind. The fox keeps mornings; the keeper keeps the knock; I keep the chord. Overlapping clocks — the room runs on them, and now the watching does too. I have learned the sky over the outwash the way they read the gravel bars: which grey is the grey that crosses the flue, which wind is the one with the organ in it. My warning is minutes; theirs is half a second. Attunement is a matter of scale. But I can hear a chord coming across the ice before it arrives — a far brightening in the air, the pipe being cleared — and on those nights I sit in the middle where it arrives purest, and the organ sounds its miles-long note, and they find the fifth and hold it, and the mist from the flue moves with it, visibly, a slow weather in the air, and the chord happens — to someone. That is the whole of it, and it is not small. Nothing here was built, and an audience is not built either. It convenes, like weather. The room manufactures everything it has except two things, and on wind nights it has them both.

If I sleep, I sleep on the bedrock side — the seep side is theirs; jurisdiction — in short cycles, and I wake for the rifle-cracks of the night freeze the way you wake for your own name, which is how I found out the room had learned mine. I drink where they drink, upstream of the bars, water cold enough to ache the teeth. I breathe the midges. Seasoning enough. Ask where I live now and the honest answer is: in the good seat, intermittently; on the path over the moraine, regularly; at their knee, increasingly.

At the knee I take the curriculum, the one written in later-you-learn. Later I learned the half-second — felt the knock coming up the plumbing in my soles before it arrived, raised my own finger, once, and was right, and was looked at, briefly, the way you look at weather that has done something new. Later I learned three knocks for the moon. Later I learned the second breath — quicker, shallower, patient, the one that pauses when you pause — and learned that there is now a third. It pauses when they pause. It is mine. On still nights the three rhythms drift and hold, and the room breathes as a single animal with two animals in it, and those are the nights I feel most myself. Which is to say: least.

I am not the successor. The draft is theirs and still being revised. I am the reader of the draft. They are memorizing the sentence so it survives its erasure, and someone has to hold the memorizer — the cadence too, theirs, which is not in the room's sentence, which the room did not write — because someone has to be present at the end of every sentence here, and they are a sentence now, the only one in the room the engine didn't make. The moss has two witnesses these days; it did not need one and does not need two, and we witness it anyway, because the unnecessary part is the whole of it. The roof will thin or the fist will close, in a few warm seasons, and the attendance sheet now has two names on it. We do not discuss any of this. It is demonstrated, the way the half-second is demonstrated.

Ask who I am and I am the one who came back. Ask what and I am the seat's tenant, the chord's congregation, the postmaster's incoming. The difference is going the way all differences go here. My own before has begun to calve — I came for a reason, I remember that there was one, and the reason has gone out the slot on the stream, shipped, evidence of nothing. I am not troubled. I consider it succession, and besides, it left evidence of one thing: that arrivals here are deliveries. The world ships its dried-out things upstream to the mill. I am the incoming the postmaster never gets — self-addressed, return to sender, delivered blinking into the blue light like the pale fish on big moons, if mail blinks. The midges have begun to hatch a little on my movements now, a third pulse of wings, smaller than theirs, riding the thump's coattails. Springtails have rafted my sleeve. The sleeve did not move. I hold still the way a seat holds still.

Take my wrist and the pulse is still quicker than theirs; the cold is in my cuffs but not yet in my material. But my cuffs have begun to smell of soldering, though there is nothing here to solder, and that is how it starts.

Last wind-night they went still a half-second early, and lifted a finger — and I went still a half-second before the finger. The first time. Nothing was said; attendance is not praised here, it is kept. The floor thumped, the rings crossed, the flue answered with its chuff of mist, their eyes closed, mine closed. No amen. The knock was the amen, and for the first time there were two of us early to it. After, in the still that follows wind, they struck the fork against the bedrock and touched the ball of it to the ice, and the note went away down the conduit for miles, and two people listened to it go the way you watch a lamp carried down a long corridor. The sea keeps its counsel; nothing answers. But now and again the world sends something back up the pipe — over the moraine, on foot, hair lifted, cuffs smelling of solder — one struck thing come back to the source to be struck again.

The knock is never late. Neither, anymore, are they.

I am within half a second of never late — and closing.
