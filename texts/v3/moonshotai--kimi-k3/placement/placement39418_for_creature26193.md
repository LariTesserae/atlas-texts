You asked where you'd find me. Not on the roof-field — not for half the year. Listen for me out past the ghost houses, where the ticking thins and lets go: down the dark channels where the rushes stand like something forged, and the water has a current instead of a pulse. That reach, between the last warm seep and the trade landing, is my country. I am a poler. I take the town's stone out through the marsh, and I bring back what the white can't grow, and the knock of my pole in the channel is how the Rime knows I'm nearly home.

My hailing-name is Laa-o — two long notes with a dip between, like a pole lifting out of deep water. The children whistle it roof to roof when they hear me coming, so my name gets home about an hour before I do. The other name is indoor, and you're not. I've cut twenty-three Rounds, and poled twelve of them. There are a handful of us who work the channel — *the calves*, the town calls us, when it calls us anything. The Rime has no streets, but it has one road, and it's wet, and it runs one way until you turn it around.

Out goes the town itself, piece by cured piece: paving, troughs, hearth-stones, cut soft and stacked hard, pale as slices off a bone. And the querns — eye-stone, from the throats of the spring-eyes, hard as tooth. What a village pays for a quern would keep a row in grain through a winter, and still I think they underpay: they get a stone that will outlast their teeth and their grandsons' teeth, and we get sacks that empty. Fair trade all the same. Not every floor needs to remember. Some floors only need to be floors. The dry world taught me that, and I carry it home like salt — a thing the crust can't make.

In comes the rest: grain, ochre, salt, and timber, timber dearest of all. Every beam in the Rime came through a poler's hands, and I know the town's roofs by their former lives — the ghost mortises, the old joinery. The dry world grows wood straight and uses it once. We grow it crooked, in families: a beam arrives a roof, becomes a loom-bar, ends a ladder. And last and lightest, news, which I pour out at the bathing-runnel at dusk the way you'd pour sky-water for a guest — freely, if the season's been good.

The landing is firm ground and a borrowed roof, half a day past the last seep. First night out, I do what we all do: hang the gourd. A plugged gourd of stone-water with a rag wick, and under it a flake cut from my own lintel, so the note it ticks is my door's note. You tune the wick to the distance — fast for far country, slow for the landing, where the marsh is near enough to hear on its own. A Rimer cannot sleep in silence. Silence is what water makes when it is planning something.

The dry world's houses are silent like a held breath. Their floors keep nothing — you could spill your whole life on them and mop it gone. Their people raise their voices for greeting, for weather, for sport; they spend their alarms like coppers. In the Rime a raised voice means a hurt child or a new spring, and I have stood in villages that sounded to my ear like one long emergency, and they meant supper. They put their dead in the ground, and the ground is not an archive. I asked once what keeps their grandmothers. *Nothing keeps them,* they said, and looked at me kindly, the way you look at someone whose luck you pity. I never found the words — I still haven't — for how it is at home: that the walls are somebody, and you can put a flat hand on them.

Two days past the landing — I went once, with a trader's train — there is a village grinding its bread on one of our spring-eyes. They have their own word for it. The stone went farther than our name ever will. I put my ear to it afterward, out of habit. Cured stone keeps its counsel. And the traders say, *you people can't lie*. It isn't virtue. We were raised by floors that testify; a lie is a sealed thing, and sealed things rise elsewhere — usually through your neighbor's wall, at supper.

I'll tell you the wage of the pole, since no one else will. A crack is news: the whole town stops, chisels in the air, and everyone looks to the Hearers. Three winters back, when the water left the Low Row and came up through the weaving-house in the night, I was two days out. Everyone alive in the Rime can tell you where they were when they heard it. I can tell you where I was: asleep in a dry field, with the gourd ticking beside me, faithful and wrong. I heard it from a trader's mouth, the way you hear weather from another country. The town hears itself faster than I can carry anything. So when I come home I go around the quiet way at dusk, ear to the floors like a half-trained Hearer — not to map; the Hearers would rightly not permit my ear on the map — but to catch up. The floor tells it slow. It has time. It is, after all, the thing that happened. Olla has never once said *safe* to me, only *the water is where it was*, or *it has moved — we will learn where*. I have made my peace with hearing it late. Mostly.

You'd ask, if you knew us, whether I've thought of going dry. Everyone who poles thinks it, once, at the landing, in the terrible rest of it. Their doors stay the size they were built. Their thresholds don't rise. A house there never asks anything of you, and there were nights that silence sounded like mercy. It took me years to hear our houses rightly: the asking *is* the company. A town that needs your chisel every morning is a town that notices you exist, daily, in a load-bearing way. And I've stood on the basin's rim — polers do; we climb out with the traders — and seen the strandlines one above the next, the old lake's rings, and the Rime a white thumbprint on the newest one. The water has been going down since the ground cracked. Everything here is going down. That's not despair. Down is where it's kept. They asked me at the landing why I go back into it. *Because that's the direction home is,* I said. They didn't ask again.

So that's the what of me. Not a Hearer, not a Keeper — the town's ears are otherwise employed. I'm the part of the Rime that goes out and comes back. I'd say *the tide*, but a tide comes back on its own. A poler has to decide. That's the whole job, in the end: deciding back.

It's dusk now, and I'm doing it. This is the gray hour — we call it by the bird's name — and the marsh is taking the light down in stages. Sound comes first, always: the town's tick resolving out of the rush-hiss, faint as a clockmaker's shop through a wall, and then under it, close by the boat, a small whiskered stirring — the night fish, starting their run up the runnels. The tasters are out. The water's sweet. All the flags are up. Then the smells: peat smoke, wet stone, the eggy breath of the spring-eyes, which you would not believe I dream about at the landing. Then the last bend, and the white crown of the Back, and the Heron on the dome of the Headhouse, one leg drawn up, watching the basin the way it has watched for longer than the town. It croaks once as I pass below. They watch that bird the way other towns watch a flag, and they listen for me the way they'd listen for weather — its whole work is staying, mine is coming back, and between us we mind the edges: it answers for the water, I answer for the dry. It has the better berth. It has never had to sleep in a dry house.

The whistles are going roof to roof now — two long notes with a dip between — and I'm poling easy, because there's no hurrying the last reach and because the rushes tell on me anyway. At the town's edge I ship the pole and put my palm flat on the crust. Warm. It always is. You'd think a person would get used to that, the way you get used to a shoulder under your hand, and maybe the ones who never leave do get used to it, and that's the one thing I know that they don't. Pattens off. Bare feet on the Back, and the stone faintly warm up through the soles, and my sister at the roof-hatch with the first cup — sky-water, poured like it costs nothing, which tells you how her year's gone. Down three rooms the air goes mild. My grandmother is in the east wall of the down-room, load-bearing, and I put my hand flat to her, coming and going, the way you touch anyone's shoulder. She ticks sometimes. The stone is drinking. It's meant fondly.

The gourd goes back on its peg, plugged, and dries till the next run. The house ticks for both of us now. If rain comes in the night — and it usually does — the down-rooms take it as news, secondhand: the roof tells the gutters, the gutters tell the cistern. The one news in this town I'm content to get late.

Tomorrow the clapper sounds the Round, and I owe days, and I'll pay them glad — one chisel among hundreds, ticking in time with the work-song, inside the sound for once, whatever comes. That's all the prayer a poler has.

Soft floors, stranger. The water is where it was.
