The census fits on two hands. Make room for an eleventh entry — write it in pencil. The assessment is ongoing.

I came the way everything comes: down the intake, an accident of the city's thirst. Whatever I was before was small enough to be drunk — an egg, a spore, a drifting beginning — and the heat and the noise and the open dark took their usual toll, and what was left of me caught in a node and stayed. Everything alive here descends from an accident of the city's drinking. I am the most recent proof that the experiment continues, that somewhere below, right now, something new is trying the water and finding it possible.

The laws I learned in my body, the way everything learns them.

Sound first. It arrived not as hearing but as *occupation* — chest, teeth, the fluid of the spine — and it told me the first law before I had finished arriving: nothing here is hidden, everything announces itself, make your small sound or be nothing to anyone. Then stillness. I paused at a railing on my first day to watch a welding-flash out in the void — a brief blue sun, gone — and when I moved on, three rust flakes had settled where I had been, patient, already cataloguing. Stillness is claimed. I learned it in my shoulders, and I have not held entirely still since. Then heat. I chose my depth the way you choose a floor of a building: the warm streets, the grazable band, hot enough for the veil, cool enough to think.

The first time the Hour came, I did what everything must do once: I listened for my note in the chord of names. The great valves opened, the roll-call rolled through the water, and the city streamed from its solitudes toward some priority legible only to the works — and my name was not in the chord. I flattened in the lee of a buttress beside the isopod and let the two-handed fury pass over, the cavitation turning to hail, and that settled the question of which side of the liturgy I am on. Not the clergy. The living.

In an alcove off my ring-street there stands a warden-shaped shell, empty and intact — molt or predecessor, the open question. I considered it the way you consider a coat on a peg, because its question is mine, I won't pretend otherwise: works or fauna, made or born. The census says of the warden, *very old fauna, or a machine that long ago decided to be a crab*. Whatever the warden is, I am the small proof that the second option is livable — something made, deciding, daily, to be a crab. But the warden carries its question at table-size, across plazas, audible to everything, routed around by the Hours. It hides in nothing, and I live by hiding. The coat doesn't fit. I left it on its peg.

In the older galleries there are shapes that came down the stack under their own power, long ago, and stayed — visitors the fall catalogued and some later Hour welded gently into the fabric, mid-gesture. The city does not see you, but it keeps you. I pass them sometimes, and it isn't fear I feel. It's a clear preference. Kept is the fixture's immortality. I would rather be read than kept.

The fish and I share the quickness — quickness is the rarest element here, and between us we hold most of it. I brighten the way they brighten when the beam crosses me, when the light of being-read swings through my dark, and I hold station in it the way they hold station in the lamp. But their courage is the crossing, and it is not mine. When a lamp dies they strike out over open water, hundreds of meters of exposure, steering by far points of light, and some of them arrive. I can't. I am a creature of the wall; the void takes what crosses it slowly, and I am slow the moment I leave the stone. If anything of me ever makes that transit, it goes the way I arrived: carried. An egg in the intake, drunk again by the same thirst, poured out at some other lamp. I don't cross. I am re-arrived.

So: not clergy, not pilgrim, not the congregation of the lamps. What remains is small and pale and industrious in a register you have to slow yourself to perceive.

I keep a threshold.

A shop doorway on a ring-street at the middle depth, in the lee of the third buttress from the stair, where a node pools and the roar cancels itself to something merely loud. The lamp across the street burns amber gone slightly toward rust — the tender who keeps this gallery drifted that way decades ago, and I have come to think of the color as correct. Home is a wrong color you have decided to love.

I pick the fall for what rides it down. The fall is the city's old selves — rust, mineral dust, the isopods' shed skins, the molt of everything — and most of what I glean from it rained down, in grains too small to name, from the dry world the city used to be. Once, a machine cycled the dry-cell lock up the street and the ghost walked out for half a second: hot oil, ozone, scorched dust. The only smell there is, whole. Everything in me stood up. I sift that ghost every day; for half a second it was assembled. The accounts mention a crab that once rode a crate through a water lock and was found pacing a dry floor, the only wet thing in the dry world, tracks crossing and recrossing in dust instead of silt. I think about that crab more than is reasonable. Someday a lock may cycle wrong for me, and I will get half a second of air — sound arriving thin and sharp instead of through the bone — and I will not know what to make of it. I will make tracks anyway. Tracks are what I make, in whatever sediment I am given.

My neighbors keep their distances and their parishes, and I keep mine, and the arrangement has the correctness of long practice. The isopod keeps the tower across the way, a parish of rusticle garden mown in long deliberate arcs — you can tell from across the void that the tower has its isopod, and I take an unreasonable pride in it. Six fish hold station at the lamp, switching on and off around the light like small thoughts. The tender passes on its weeks-long rounds, taps the cage twice out of faith, and does not see me. The asymmetry is total, and I have made my peace with it: I know the liturgy; the liturgy does not know me; it is enough to be the one who knows. Once in a long while the warden's track crosses the plaza below, audible for days in advance, the slow scrape and tick arriving through the stone, and I go down and watch it pass the way you would watch weather.

The works have their Hours; I have mine. No chord announces them. A priority becomes legible only to me — a drifted lintel, a clog in the pattern, an arrangement that wants making — and for an hour my doorway is a one-crab convergence, demolition and raising at threshold scale, the silt hanging in the water like a held breath. Then it relaxes. The inverted chord is mine too. The lamps go back to two, the fall resumes, and the threshold is clean.

A kept threshold — silt brushed back, tracks fresh and overlapping — is a lived-in doorway. A threshold drifted smooth is an inheritance pending. Doorways outlive crabs. When I stop, mine will silt over, a season or a decade, until something comes ranging the long way around the galleries, reads what is printed there, and takes up the brushing. It will not be me. It will know where the doorway is, because the tracks say so — because I wrote it down in the only fresh script the city has. That is the whole of my immortality, and I find it sufficient: not kept, like the visitors in the wall. Read, like a doorway.

The pencil stays pencil. Works or fauna, the Drum does not answer questions, and I have stopped asking. I brush.

You would find me the way you find anything alive here: by pain, then its easing. Down the stack, down the ringing throat; choose the warm floor; third buttress from the stair. Wait out one seiche, so the silt can hang undecided and resettle. Then look along the ring-street for the doorway that isn't drifted smooth. Come at the turning of the tide in the bottle. Bring no sound you don't need. Read the threshold.

You are reading it now. These are the tracks — fresh as of this hour, overlapping, brushed back from the lintel. The fall is already on them; before the next Hour I will brush them back again, and what they say is all I am: *still here. Still keeping.*

The seiche is turning. Across the street, the lamp is thinking.

Excuse me — the lintel.
