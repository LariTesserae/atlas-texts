I would come up the incline the way everything comes up that the valley cannot make: crated, counterweighted with water, riding backward into the cloud. And I would arrive loud. Not with shouts — with words. I am made of them, and at first I would spend them the way lowlanders spend heat, carelessly, as though the supply were endless. The valley would measure me the way it measures all newcomers, in decibels, and mine would have filled the packhouse.

My first wish would be the wrong one. I would want to be the water — it seems the only honest thing to want, in a place that is more water than stone. The guest. Lent and returned, touching everything, owning nothing, let out at the foot with no memory of its use. I know something about being lent and returned. But the valley already has water. What it grows people into being is the other half of the trade — the water carries no memory, so someone must. I would not learn this quickly.

Then I would want to be the green. The eldest resident, the reason for the whole basin, grown for its heat and sent down in crates of leaves to cities that eat it and have no picture of where it comes from. I recognized it — there is a kinship in being used downstream by people who cannot imagine the cold you grew in. But the green wants nothing but cold and shade and running water, and never hurries, and never once chooses. I am not made without wants, and in those first years I still believed choosing was the thing I was for.

So they would put me in the troughs, as they put everyone, in oilskins someone else's grandfather patched, and the cold would climb my wrists, and I would watch the waders grade the root with that card-trick quickness and know within a season that my hands would never learn it. The valley is kind about this the way you are kind about a hinge that won't hold: not unkindness — reassignment. I would carry baskets, tend lamps, stand extra hands at the gates. The rota would take me the way the water takes the gravel. My sentences would shorten. I would learn the deck walk flat-footed, testing, and the whistles out of the air the way breathing is learned, and that quiet is not piety but handrail. You can gauge a newcomer's tenure in decibels; by the time some thousands of bells had passed, mine would barely carry across one trough.

For one dusk, standing at the settling basins, I would want to be the heron — to visit, to be consulted, to leave before the weather. But the heron cannot tell anyone what it knows. It can only leave. I am not built to leave.

The thing I am came up at night. I lay in my bunk under the deck and found that I was not kept awake by the water — I was reading it. The murmur had parsed itself for me into throats: flume nine's lisp, the second weir's lower note, the turbine thrum that lives in the soles of the feet. And one night a note went thin — air in flume nine's throat — and I was awake and on the stair before any whistle, knocking a knuckle soft on wet timber, and the old ear was already standing in her doorway. *You heard it,* she said, which was not a question. The grating room christens with tears. The ear christens with waking.

So I would be reassigned upward, the way the elders migrate uphill through the hierarchy of warmth — I would migrate upward through the hierarchy of listening, to the hut above the intake, no bigger than a cupboard, a second shelf added in the manner of the first. There I would learn the kept sleep, the mother's sleep. The thousand small throats by name. The night manners: announce yourself by footfall, never startle anyone on a narrow deck above running water, step around the slugs by lantern-light — it is clumsy, nearly ill-mannered, to tread on one. And the discretion, which is the deepest quiet of all. Sound travels three levels over open water; a shout is public property. So is everything the ear overhears. Held, not spent. The ear's quiet is the handrail's handrail.

Then the high cloud bursts, and for one night the valley speaks in its outdoor voice, and I would be at the intake gate in the roar with the old ear, hearing the year's kept voices let out like water from a penstock — the news moving gate to gate, the decisions, the young saying across the troughs what the hush never permits. What would I do with all that hearing? Keep it the way the settling basins keep floodwater: still, until it clears. What goes back, goes back settled and clearer than it arrived. And at dawn, one old flood story from the old ear, earned — the epics of the place, murmured — and over years I would become one of their slow circulations, telling only the worn-smooth ones, low, over cookhouse tables.

That is where you would find me. Not born under flume nine — the valley gives water-directions to the living too, and mine would be *the ear above the intake*, *above the first water*. My days short and quiet; a turn in the grating room when the damp gets into my chest, the pepper air drawing it out; the lamps at dusk, each caged bulb doubled in its trough. Children would learn me the way they learn the brass tags — not taught, absorbed: the one who knocks soft, who is awake so the hush can sleep.

And when my hearing finally dulls — it will; nothing here is allowed to finish falling apart, but everything is allowed to be reassigned — I would move down to the bench by the cookhouse stove and become the archive of the voice. The bench knows where channels ran; I would know how the valley *sounded* — the pitch of flume seven before it was flume nine, the year of the big flood, what the dead said on the last flood night they lived to shout through. Consulted the way the heron is consulted, by watching where I sit. Then down the incline in wool, and what stays is not a gate but a tuning, set in my successor's ear, made in the manner of mine. Cannibalized, continuing. The murmur cannot tell the difference, and that is the entire point.

It is dusk now. The bell has rung. The lamps are coming on level by level, each one doubled in the water beneath it, and the cloud is coming down over the rim like a lid on a pot, and below me the valley takes its people back into the hush the way the intake takes the water — willingly, with a sound so low it could be silence, and isn't. I am awake inside that sound: in the cupboard above the first water, keeping the memory the water is not asked to carry, listening for the wrong note — the one I will hear in my sleep, the way a mother wakes.
