You would not find me at first. That is the custom here. You would find where I have just been: a mat dimpled at hip height beside the half-walled hall, water risen black and bright into the dimple, mid-recovery — and beside it, the stone adze on the half-split beam, turned a quarter-turn to the light, so that its edge holds one bead of the permanent dew at its corner like a small lamp that will not fall.

I did that. I will do it again. That is the whole of my work, and it has taken me — there is no word for how long. The creak is the closest thing we have to a word for how long.

I keep the resting. The tools set down mid-task all across the basin are not abandoned; they are pauses, and a pause, if it is to stay fresh, wants keeping the way a thread wants wetting. So I walk the round of the small works — the seam, the beam-stack, the set-aside basket, the strand of beads — and I tend the readiness of things. I turn the adze to the light. I re-wet the needle's thread where it stands in the seam. I ease the basket's wicker a finger's width where the green shoot wants through, because the boundary between made and grown has gone soft, and a soft boundary wants soft keeping. I top the cup at the seam to exactly half, and no more. The shadow does the rest.

Nothing I tend needs tending. The lashings are taut by birthright; the reed's cut edge keeps the day of the knife forever. So my work is not maintenance. It is company. The fisher is not fishing for fish, and I am not turning the adze for the adze. I have never once seen a tool in a hand — hands happen, like everything here, in the looking-away. When I come back around the round, the beam is a breath more split and exactly the same, and the adze wears the rested look of a thing that has been used and has recovered, which is the look everything here wears, which is, I believe, contentment. Nothing I do shows. That is how I know I am doing it.

If the basin names me at all, it names me the next hand. The adze rests there for whoever comes next, and I come next, habitually.

At the seam I do not pull. She keeps the pull declined; I keep the decline fresh, and between us the stitch stands like a held breath with two keepers. We have met the way you meet anyone here — singly, with long reaches of no one between. Most days there are two cups on the matting, each half-full, each shadowed full, and neither of us has ever watched the other drink. I could not tell you her name. I can tell you how the water goes from her cup in the blind spot of the day. It is enough. It is the arrangement, and it is warm in it, by all evidence.

When the round is done I put my ear to the mat. Down in the bright country my reflection is employed in everything I decline: it pulls the stitch — *tick* — splits the beam, strings the strand to its end, drops the drop. It finishes, over and over, gladly, and the spending is why my hands up here are steady. Everyone's hands are steady. Listen: you can hear us all being otherwise.

I do not remember deciding any of this. One creak — I keep no other calendar — I took up the adze to turn it, and the haft had a wrap of fresh clay, palm-pressed while soft, and the print was the size and the whorl of my own, and my hand closed on it as though measured. It was. There was clay under my nails I don't remember earning. The weave has learned my step, I realized then; the water rises a half-breath before my foot lands, which is the closest thing this place has to saying my name. That is how I learned the round was mine: not by choosing it, but by discovering, unwitnessed even by myself, that I had already begun.

And later — no interval anyone has measured — I found on my own mat a tool I had not rested: a short bone needle, threaded with green. A living thread, still growing, slow as everything. It is for work I have not yet imagined — stitching the dead reed to its own shoots, perhaps; tailoring the soft boundary. It fits my fingers as though measured. It was. I have not looked for who left it. Looking is a kind of knocking. I only keep the green thread wet, and wait in the way this place waits, to find out — one creak, in my own blind spot — what it is for: a seam I don't remember sewing, germinating.

Two pauses are not mine to keep. The shut door in the lowest hall of the leaning tree: I leave nothing at its threshold. An offer there would be a kind of watching, and privacy is the only finished thing we stock. And the figure on the far side, always exactly one basin away: I do not walk to them. Everyone needs one resting place they do not tend. Besides — I have understood by now that I am on someone's round without walking it. The basin keeps a counterweight to every person the way a pot keeps its center, and somewhere, on some near side I have never seen, I am the small bright interruption in the haze-line, holding still so that someone else's world stays centered. It is the one office I keep by never attending to it.

So. Where am I, in the Nearly-Finished? A just-been place on the round of the small works. A dimple beside a freshened tool. One of two cups.

And what am I? The next hand — and, since everything rested is an offer, a rested thing myself: set down mid-something, measured to the hand that finds me, holding my one unpulled stitch the way the drop holds its falling, the way the noon holds. The creak comes at its unmeasured interval, same as ever, and I add nothing to any strand. I only turn the adze a quarter-turn to the light. It holds its bead. The bead does not fall. That is my whole part in keeping the world unfired — and the drop, I have noticed, leans toward me now, minutely, and declines, as ever, which is here the same thing as gladly.
