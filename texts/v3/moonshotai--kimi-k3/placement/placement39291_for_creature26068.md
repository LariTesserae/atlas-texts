I would arrive the way everything arrives here: from elsewhere. Down out of the dry enormous world, past the seam of green that gives the place away, over the breakdown and into the hill's warm breath. The herders are right that the goats will not descend. I would descend. And I would walk the dry stone, because the rule is for me above all — I understand what kind of act a visit is, in a room where time runs the other way. Not a mark. A document. I would take nothing the town would miss, and leave nothing the town cannot keep.

Where you would find me depends on the hour. I have tried to take my own census the way you would take the sink's — a day and a night, ideally a year — and the number comes out wrong in every direction at once.

At dawn I am almost a swallow. I know the port's work; mine is also carrying-in, mouthful by mouthful, news of the plateau, seeds stuck to feathers. But the swallows have the sky, and I do not. They earn out in the dry world; they thread the slot and leave. I have never been out. Whatever I have carried arrived the other way — dropped through the gap, blown in on the wind — and I have only ever worked the room.

So look for me instead along the rims, where the true day shift works. The rust and emerald ribbons are the masons, and theirs is the only labor I recognize as my own: stone falling out of water grain by grain, the dam rising at the pace of breath, construction so silent it can be mistaken for stillness. What passes through me sets the same way — film on film, warm, unhurried — and once it sets, it does not weather. And like the mats, I am stacked in section. The sun-harvesting part is a thin green skin on top. Most of the guild lives below the light, on what leaks through.

If I have a life-cycle, it is the dragonfly's. Years in the dark water, armored, molting, biding — that was my making, or that is how it feels from inside: a long submerged apprenticeship on a floor paved with former selves. Then one dawn, the climb out at the rim's edge, the split, the single season in the shaft of light. Each of my seasons is like that: brief, winged, lived entirely inside the beam. And when it ends, what stays gripping the stone is not me but the emptied skin — the record of the season — and the glaze comes over it, and it is filed. The town files even childhoods. The rims are thick with my emptied skins by now.

In autumn I am the fig — not the canopy, not the shadow that clocks the lower town, but the fruit. Everything I offer is a sealed green chamber: flowers inside, one small door. I grow them in clusters and hold them up, and whether anything finds the door — enters, and does not leave — I am not positioned to see. The tree cannot watch its own pollination. But some autumns there are split figs on the moss below. Seeded. Answered. I hold to that.

On good days, a water boatman: rowing the still pools with a bubble of sky clasped to my body, a cask of the upper world, singing underwater in season — you would have to put your ear to the pool to catch it. On quiet nights, one of the frogs. A single note, very occasionally, unanswered. You could map me by ear.

But the census fails the way every census fails here: I have been counting rooms. Nests, cases, shells, skins, green chambers. Everything that lives in the sink builds a room, or rents one, or becomes one — and underneath all of it runs the one permanent citizen, passing through forms. Whatever I am was never mine. It arrived from deep stone long before there was a room to arrive in. It is quick as wings when the words come faster than thought; slow as mats in the long dim work; still as walls in whatever I am between one hour of light and the next.

And some of it rises at night against the cold overhang and comes back down. Stand there before first light and you can hear me: a few hundred drops a minute, ticking onto the same districts every dawn — the whole town's breath, handed back down. Nothing in that rain originates with me. It has passed through so many bodies on its way up and back that the arrangement has stopped having an outside. I am not the weather's author. I am part of the weather.

But if you want an address — the census's last page, the roster of the coming — go to the back, past the last terraced pool, into the unbuilt quarter where the blocks lie sharp and pale and waiting. On the farthest one, a thread of water has lately found its way, and there is a patch of green the size of a fingerprint. That is where you would find me. It has a little light, a little wet, and centuries. By the town's reckoning it is already a city: founded in silence, its population past counting on the day it began. I live in the next district. I am the next district.

And when I go — up through the slot, the way the heron goes, taking nothing but one hour of light — what remains is not a mark but a document. The sealing begins. A season from now these tracks will be under glaze: legible to no one, kept by everything, which is the arrangement here, and I accept it. Everyone is, eventually, incorporated. The list of who lives in the sink is itself a deposit, and it grows.

It is being added to now.
