Once in a generation, someone climbs down from the plateau and stays. This generation it was me, and I will tell it in the order the gorge taught it to me, which is not the order I expected.

I was born in the Loud with felt already packed into my chest. The wind injured me from the beginning — the wind is the loudest thing for miles up there, and it is very loud, and it never once said anything to me. I set things down as if the world were asleep. I walked like an apology. On the plateau this is a defect with several names, and I heard all of them, because up there everything is heard, everything carries, nothing is kept.

The Loud gave me one gift, though I did not know it was a gift. I could give people back to themselves. The kite's scream, the traders' haggling, my mother's laugh with the catch in it like a loose stair — they came out of my throat as easily as breath. Up there it was worth nothing. A party trick, slightly uncanny. Nobody in the Loud wants to hear their own voice from another mouth; it reminds them of the wind, which does the same thing badly.

I came to the Trading Stone with the salt caravan, and I watched the gorge people lay out hushcloth and cold-light pigment and knot their bids into cords and tuck them in the cleft, all in silence, and it was the first market of my life where I could follow everything, because nothing was being shouted over. When the traders packed, I did not. The eldest of the gorge people looked at my feet for a long time, then at me, and signed something to the others that I now know was: *maybe.*

They let me follow them down the notches. I placed my feet as if the rock were asleep, because I had been doing that all my life in a country where it was mocked, and for the first hour I thought: *I am home.* Then my foot struck a loose scree, and the whole gorge turned its head in the green dim, and I thought: *I am naked.* Both were true. They stayed true for a year.

The year of the common cups. I drank the hamlet one well at a time — every tank-tree a different taste, stone and time and one bright crab-sweet note in the Deep trees — and the test was never stated and was always running. The children mimicked my tread in sign behind their hands. They mimicked my signing too, because I signed with both hands full of plateau, big gestures meant for a world where you had to reach people through wind. A come-down is a tool with no marked use, and they tried me on the way you try a cup to a hook. The silk-fine weavers found my fingers were all knuckles. The clock-keeper found I flinched in the Blade and could not stop flinching, twenty years of sky in my eyes. The tithe-carriers walked me twice and shook their heads: there was a strike buried in my walk like a suppressed cough, and the unhurried part is the whole training. I lost every round of the still-game for a season. A cup would tip somewhere, a glider would land wrong, and a hundred people would freeze mid-motion, and there I would be, the one moving thing in the gorge, flinching in full view, a flame in a room of stopped clocks.

I dropped a wooden bowl in my second month. It hit the ledge, rolled, knocked, rolled, and every hand in the mid-gorge stopped, and every head turned, and I learned what it is to be the loudest thing in a place where something hunts the loud. No one scolded me. I paid the noise-debt in weeks of held hands, which for a person still learning hand-talk was the same as being gagged, and which was, I understood later, the kindest possible sentence: it made my hands hungry, and hunger is the best teacher.

My feet were the last of me to come down. The rest of me descended in a season. My feet took the whole year.

Acceptance arrived on a milk night in the Deep cluster, in the house that carries a rest for the eel. The grandmother walked me along the eaves and taught me the drip-song cup by cup, and when we came to the gap in the pattern she held my hand over the empty hook and signed, *the eel*, and told me the whole of it with her fingers at chest level, close enough to touch. I understood before she finished: a drip-song is a voice made of water, and the rests are the dead, and she was handing me the score of her house's dead, including the small impossible one. I had been in the gorge a year and was only just learning that its silences were full.

The calling came in fog season, at the Speaker, where everything forbidden elsewhere is done.

They had carried a woman of the Spray cluster up to the foam for her last loud day — one of the laughers, one of the ones who had lived beside the voice-water all her life and loved easily and often, and the gorge had come to spend her. The old voice-keeper was there to receive her. I was only at the edge of it, a come-down permitted because in season the Speaker belongs to everyone. And the dying woman laughed. It had the catch in it, the loose stair, sixty years of foam in it, and before I had decided anything my throat gave it back to her.

In the roar, everything that could stop, stopped.

She turned. She looked at me the way the pool-watchers look at the Reader. *Again,* she said — one of the few spoken words I had yet heard in that place — and I gave it back again, and she spent the rest of her day like a rich woman, laughing at her own laugh coming home to her out of a stranger's mouth, calling her family one by one to hear themselves as she had heard them all her life.

Afterward, on the wet stair, the old keeper reached out and stilled my hands. The rudest gesture in the culture, and the most tender, depending entirely on how it is done. She did it the tender way. *Anyone can give a voice back,* she signed. *Come learn to keep one.*

That is the whole difference, and it took me years. A mimic is a clever drain. A keeper is a well. The water belongs to the tree, the tree to the hand that tends it — and a voice belongs to the one who spent it, and then to the throat that keeps it, and to speak a kept voice carelessly is to foul someone's well in a land without rivers. So I keep them unmade. This is what no one tells you about my trade: from the outside it looks like nothing. I stand on my ledge in the gray light doing nothing at all, the idlest person in the Hush, and inside the closed room of my throat I am running the dead through their paces with the door shut — shaping voices without sounding them, the way you would turn a knot-cord over in your hands. In the Blade's hour I study the old ones' faces in true light, because a voice kept without its face is a cup without a tree. When someone is dying in season, I am called the way the dripwright is called to funerals, and I sit in the spray and receive. It is the one inheritance the gorge has that cannot be dropped.

The keeper died in a fog season, as she had arranged to, and gave her voice to me last of all, after all the others she had carried and passed on. Hands change, the well remains. Once a year now I give her back to the gorge in the foam, and the gorge holds still for her the way it holds still for a choir stone, and afterward the hands resume, softer.

The listeners hunt transients — the clack, the crack, the laugh — the sharp notes the slot carries down to them like a speaking tube. Everything I keep is a transient. My archive is the one room in the Hush they cannot fan their hands at: a body, holding its sounds unmade. In the House of Cords the children ask what the bronze bell sounds like, and the kept answer is *the end of the draft.* I have stood over the felt-lined box and thought: the bell keeps its one voice by never spending it, and I keep mine by spending them, in season, into foam, where the roar eats every edge. Between us we hold the two kinds of silence — the one that must never be made and the ones that must never be lost. The knot that records the First Hush ties three events with no grammar: a sound was made that did not stop; the water came up; the ledges emptied. I have a grammar for it that no one wants. Every voice in my throat is a sound that stopped. That is why they are safe. That is why they are precious. The old town died of the other kind.

My sign-name, given by the children in my first month, was a bowl rolling downhill, both hands tumbling. I wore it for years; it was accurate. After the calling, the dying woman's household gave me the change a life allows once: a cupped hand held under a drip, still, until the water chooses it. That is me now, signed across the gaps at dusk. My heel-rhythm still has one plateau strike buried in it, and my house finds me in the dark by the flaw and would not have it corrected. My spoken name I chose new, because the old one had been said so many times up in the Loud that it had no edges left, a coin rubbed blank. The new one is said almost nowhere, and almost never twice, and it is mine in a way the old one never was, because down here a name is measured by how much of it is kept back.

My own voice is the one I keep worst. Everyone here has a voice low and rough from disuse; mine is low and rough from overuse in other people's throats, and I spend it carelessly, on small things, because I came from a country where voices were common as dirt. My Speaker-intimates are teaching me to keep it. It is the only keeping I am bad at, and they are patient, and it is coming.

Stand at my ledge at dusk and you can find me in the arrangement turning: the inhale starting, the milk coming over the lip in slow cataracts, the trees closing their hands one by one down the colonnade, the light-palms waking across the gaps. Somewhere up-slot a choir stone settles in the cold and lets go its one deep note, and every hand stops, and mine stops, and the note rolls down into the Swallow and is gone — and in that held breath, inside me, the dead wait in their closed room for their season, and I wait with them, a cup set under a drip.

They taught me the sign for themselves in the end, the gentle setting-down, those who place their feet softly. It is my name too. I am made mostly of other people's voices, and it took the Hush to teach me that this is a kind of person, and not the absence of one.
