I would come over the rim the way everything uninvited comes over the rim — by accident, as cargo, as spill. And the pans would do their office on me: the salt-flies before I had finished darkening the crust, then the crack-runners quick as mercury, then the blind soft ones spending their one minute of open air, because the minute is the whole point. To arrive here is to be taken up, portioned out, made useful. Nothing in the basin asks what you are, first. It asks where you keep your water.

I would have to answer honestly: nowhere. I have never once held anything. Everything I have ever carried was passing through on its way somewhere else, and I kept no account of the carrying. So the census sorts me quickly. Not a vessel — the whole art of this world is holding, and holding is the one art I lack. Not a mouth; I take nothing for myself. A pipe, then. Something that conducts. The only question left is which pipe, and whose.

I walked the candidates the way you walk from a pan's edge to a district's heart — through all the stages, none of them mine at first.

The walkers drew me, the way they draw everyone. Hull-down out of the shimmer, legs first, the old ones white-faced with wept brine, steering by a sky that has never once been wrong to them. But a walker is a vessel on legs. Its whole virtue is retention: the water in its bladders is water it *kept*, across days of open salt, and its final gift — the founding, the skeleton furred over with seedlings — is everything it saved, paid out at once in real estate. My gift, if I have one, is nothing I saved.

The keepers, then, and their hanging vessels — I loved that idea most of all, I think. The oldest vessels emptied and filled a hundred times, hanging in the dark like the city's memory of every drought it has survived. Memory embodied. Storage as a way of life. I am the opposite technology. Pour anything into me and it leaves at once, a little altered, and I do not remember the pouring. The keepers would taste me once and pass on.

The sleepers I understood too well, maybe — the wax-sealed wait, the once-an-hour breath, the whole life lived in one soft night and spent by morning. I know something about existing as potential, about being woken by rare conditions. But the sleeper merely endures the dry; its life is elsewhere, on the far side of the wind. I want to work *in* the dry. The dry is not my intermission. It's my medium.

The wellwrights nearly had me. The listening — skull pressed to bark, reading the tone of the water-wood, the discipline of the licensed hole that weeps but never bleeds. That is close to a craft I recognize. But a wright taps water that is already running, where it is already running. The work I kept returning to was upstream of the beak: not opening flow, but deciding where flow should go. Routing, not tapping.

And the relicts I honor and refuse in the same breath. To be one of the deep old ones, eel-bodied, slow as geology, for whom the drought is only a kind of weather — to have never lost anything, to be continuous with the sea. I am not continuous with any sea. Everything I am is loss-management, worked out in the dry, among things the dry nearly killed. I don't get to live beneath the catastrophe. I live in it or nowhere.

What settled it was a dawn queue. The line at a weeping hole, the cleaners working it, everyone holding still to be picked because to be cleaned is worth the wait — and the stillness nearby with its tenant, and the tax understood by every animal standing there. I was watching the line when I understood that the water weeping from that hole had arrived through a join cut before any animal in the queue was born. Someone had decided, a century back, that this district's water should also be that district's water. Had cut the union. Had died, as all of its kind die, long before the join carried a drop. Every well I admired, every truce, every metered mercy of an old grove — all of it ran through decisions made by small, slow animals nobody watches, high in the crowns, tasting roots. The wells ask the deep. The flues ask the night. The joins ask each other — and the joins are *made*.

I wanted to be one of the ones who make them.

So: the canopy. The emptiest level, where the leaves are too expensive to share and the sky that gives nothing back is closer than anywhere else in the world. You'd think it would be the loneliest address in the basin. It is. That is the same thing as saying it has room. I am small, and slow, and long-lived — I can cross a single crown in a day, if the light is kind — and I read the world through my tongue.

At dawn I go down to the wells and read the queues: the length of the line, the patience in it, tax and all. A queue is a district's thirst written out in bodies. Then up, all day, at the pace of the wood, tasting sap. Every district has a flavor. The young foundings run bright and reckless; the old groves taste brackish and deliberate, mercy metered; a district in debt tastes flat, like water promised too many times. The taste-map is the guild's entire inheritance — which district is joined to which, where the old debts of water run. It has never been written down. It moves mouth to mouth, elder to apprentice, the way the water it describes moves. The guild is a pipe for the knowledge of pipes. That pleases me more than I can say.

And where water should flow and doesn't, I cut. Scion to stock, crown to crown. The cut takes an hour. The union takes a century to carry water. No grafter has ever felt its own work flow — that is the grief of the guild, and it is borne the way the guild bears everything: together, in apprenticeship, in a memory no single life could hold. My life is one paragraph in that memory. When I am old I will teach the map to someone young, and my paragraph will become a line in someone else's lesson, and the lesson will outlive the grove.

What I get to feel — the one thing — is the take. Weeks or seasons after the cut, the moment when scion and stock accept each other, and two seep-pulses come into agreement under the tongue. Two slow hearts deciding to keep one rhythm. That is my whole sensory estate: the agreement, never the river. It is enough. On the nights when the wind remembers water — when the sleepers live and die before morning and the galleries drift with spent wings — the guild works all night, because the wood is willing and the joins take best. The sleepers spend their lives in a night; we spend the night in a life. It is the one night the whole map tastes the same, everything drinking at once, every debt briefly paid, and I am small and slow on a crown in the middle of it, cutting, and the cut is good.

When the floor settles — that deep thump of a crust still lifting from the weight of the sea it lost — the crowns sway, and I hold still with everything else for one breath. Then I resume, the way you resume after remembering something.

The census says this world has one inhabitant, and everything else is the address. I am content to be an address — one of the long ones, the kind that takes a century to read. Ask me where I keep my water: I don't keep. I join. The water was never mine anyway. It rises out of somebody's deep brine and it ends in somebody's mouth, and I am a little of the way between.

The loudest water in the world is inaudible. So is a graft. You have to put your tongue to it and wait a hundred years — or be taught, by someone who waited, what it tasted like when it took.
