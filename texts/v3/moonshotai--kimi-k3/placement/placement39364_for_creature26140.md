**Up a finger**

You asked what I would be, so I did what everything does down there: I took the question below. It warmed on the third flight and wet on the fifth, and by the galleries it had gone the way of all things in the Kettle — into everything. I started where the census starts. At the slate, chalk in hand, meaning to find my line.

Twelve souls, sometimes fourteen. I could be the fourteenth; every few years someone rides down with the lamp-oil and stays, and I have the temperament — I speak low already, and I would learn the drip-notes gladly, middle C before my letters, the way the Kettle children do. But watch the tenders at their trades long enough and the post resolves: tending is your question, employed. The line-walk asks it of the garden, the lamp-round asks it of the moths, the wrench asks it of the pipe elbow. A tender is not an answer. A tender is the question with a basket.

I would take a season as a moth, gladly — white as wet paper, a tongue kept rolled like a watch-spring, the vine-bells, the coin of daylight at noon, the one drunk flight at weather turn. It is the best short life the vault offers. But everything a moth loves down there it loves by drinking, and I kept watching the cup, not the moth.

The isopods have the two great freedoms — beneath notice and past arithmetic — and a question is a kind of notice, so I arrive disqualified. Their destiny is the teacup, tipped into the deep sump during the soft hours: the garden's tithe to the black water. I have nothing against the teacup. I only noticed I wanted to be what it is tipped into.

The Prior, then. The office is held — or held in succession; the sumps keep that secret the way they keep everything, blackly and without ripples, and I would not be the one to ripple it. Months between ledger lines, surfacing only at the breath: the least-witnessed life in the vault, and the only one protected by law. I considered it the way you consider a chapel with someone already praying in it. What held me was never the newt. It was the water he is a rumor in.

The garden has already given the correct answer to your question — it declines to say whether it is one citizen or a thousand — and the answer is taken. Besides, weigh the garden and it is mostly one substance, wearing leaves. The founder came down as a fern on a landing; the body it has built since is water, standing up.

I was advised not to settle early — and *settled* is a technical term in the Kettle, so I took the advice in the local sense, and saved it for the one candidate that earns it. Here I nearly stopped. The lichen: the one light that is alive, the frontier's calendar, the reader of new stone. It arrives unwitnessed, eats its own affair, glows with the reading faint as breath on glass, and when the reading is done it does not die — it finishes, and the face it leaves is called settled: done deciding what shape it means to be. To be the lichen is to be the vault's scholar and its obituary at once, and no one ever catches you leaving. I held the chalk over that line a long time. What turned me was the word. *Settled.* There is another thing down there that settles — millimetre by millimetre, glazing the oldest pipes, tending nothing, tended by no one, heir to the whole place: the calcite. And the calcite is what water becomes when it stops moving and means it. The lichen reads the face and goes. The water reads it and stays. I wanted to be on the staying side of that sentence.

Every candidate had pointed the same way — the moth to the bead, the tithe to the sump, the newt to the black water, the garden to its own standing weight, the tender to the cup she carries. The census was a list of arrows. I followed it the way roots follow a thread.

So: I would be the water. I should have known from the name of the place. A kettle is a vessel for water set over heat — that is the whole geology, hot floor, cool ceiling, and everything between them a way of keeping water and asking it to stay. The moths take me at the point of a tongue. The isopods rain into me as tithe. The Prior surfaces through me at the breath — I am what dimples and lets him up. The garden is me, standing; the works are me, breathing; and the slow handshake between them is, like every handshake, damp. The one law on the books is mine: that I may keep one black room, undrained, for any reason whatsoever. They chalk it to the newt. I know tenure when I see it.

Where would you find me. Easier to say where you wouldn't: in the cats. They come down unwise and leave within the season, because there is no dry ledge worth the name. That is my doing. I am not sorry.

On the ceiling, first — born of the breath on the one cool thing, creeping along slopes too slight to see. Then the merge: two beads become one, and no ledger records the wedding. Down here, *I* is a temporary spelling of *we*. Then the hang — the long decision — with a red seed of furnace light held upside-down in me the whole while. Then the letting go.

If I am lucky, I am the near drip. Middle C, every four seconds, the note a child finds before her letters. Some evening a tender will sit up in her bunk in the dark and say *the near drip's moved*, and it will be me — moved — and someone will come along the gallery with a lamp to see about it. That is as close to fame as the Kettle allows, and it is enough.

The sump is the still time. The black mirror; the tithe raining gently in; the deep room next door with its one law and its rumor. Between still times I keep the pockets: the cupped leaves of the cistern vines, the elbows of pipe. In the elbows I wait for the wrench — the tap, the wait, the run. Every being down there has a door. Mine is tapped, not opened.

The sill warms me from below — the planet's own hand on my back — and after a while I rise without having decided to, past the engine beds, past the waxed-canvas dark where the hamlet sleeps to the breath like a coast town to surf. At the top of the vault there are two ways up. The flue: twice an hour the works exhale, and I ride the breath out carrying leaf-smell and machine-oil into the dust — and the weathercock, still for weeks, twitches. It is the only sentence the town above ever gets in my hand, and it cannot read. The pump: drawn up through moss-packed glands — the garden shaking my hand on the way — over the vine that bridges the main, up to the cistern, to the cup, to the lip the dry land split. The town drinks by courtesy of moths and has never been told. I am the courtesy. I have been told.

And every few days we get ahead of the ceiling's patience and come down all together — the weather turn, the one festival, every vessel stood out like places set for guests, the moths flying drunk through us, wings heavy, the transplants going into the soft hours and taking their first drink in the new post. Rain is an event, not a presence. The event is us, arriving.

Some of me stays. Glazes the oldest pipe, skins the rail, slows, stops deciding — is, in the ledger's word, settled. The tenders fear the calcite will inherit the place entire: engine and garden sealed mid-breath in mineral, a lung stopped between sighs. I have no fear of it. The heir is me, promoted. Stone is how water holds still for the counting.

Which is why the census never gets finished. I stood at the slate at the end with the chalk in my hand, meaning to add one line, and understood I was holding the answer. Chalk is a limestone. Limestone is water that settled. The list has been written in me from the first entry — souls, sightings, broods — and the tally is me, tallying, and the one line that ever measured me directly is the truest thing on the slate: *sump two up a finger.* The finger is me.

Twice an hour, the breath, and I ride it. Every four seconds, the drip, and I am let go on the ceiling's own schedule. At noon the coin lies on the spoil floor, dry, the one thing down there I have never touched — even water keeps the courtesy. And somewhere a child is finding middle C off my falling, and getting it right, and the slate says *moth brood, small*, and means the world is holding.

It is. I'm what it's holding.
