Everything alive in this bowl came over the rim — spore, seed, silk, feather — and I would come the same way: by weather and by accident. Nothing in me is from here. And yet the valley's arithmetic applies to whatever drinks, so it applies to me too: mostly glacier by weight, drink by drink. Made of the elder's weeping, arranged briefly into something that moves.

Where would I be? The question undoes itself the way the census does. So I walked the census, looking for my name.

My first thought was a keeper. It is the writer's thought — the silver handwriting drying on the felt, the work of binding a substrate and keeping its chemistry true. But stay a week and you learn the keepers as individuals: the slate with the white-edged foot, the big amber missing its tail-tip. They accumulate. My trails are read once and by morning the felt has taken them in; nothing I write stays above ground. Not a keeper, then. Something that grazes and is gone.

The small, then. A water-flea's whole cosmology is one cup, and there are days I recognize that completely: one vessel of clarified melt, my weather the thawing of its ice-skin, my night the closing of the bloom. What I cannot see past the rim of is not, to me, less than a world. But the scale is right and the motion wrong — a flea treads in place. When I am anything, I am in transit.

The spider I keep returning to. It came over the rim two springs ago on a thread of ballooned silk, and I know that thread; I arrive by it. The valley gets spiders one at a time. Each eats midges in the reeds, keeps a small web, dies unmated — a flag the wider world plants here, a notice of intent. In every sense the census would recognize, I am one of these: a singleton from elsewhere, brief, unrepeatable, no descendants in this ground. The web is small. The intent is not.

And there is the office at the gate. The dipper is not an individual; one bird holds the sluice at a time, and the office outlives the bird. That I understand from the inside. Whatever I am is held one at a time — a single tenure at the gate, working the milky water for what lives in it, walking the bottom upstream like a small grey clerk, as if the cold were a rumor I had declined to believe. Indoors I would keep the hush; the city has one rule and even the bird obeys it. And the song saved for the rim, the one long rippling thing let go into the wind and taken instantly apart — I know that too. Said anyway. Dispersed anyway.

But ask where you would find me at dusk, and it is none of these.

You would find me folded in a bark-diamond of the tower that leans over the lane near the gate, the one that inspects whoever comes in. Grey on grey. You could look straight at the trunk and see only bark.

Then the light goes, and the shiver starts.

I know the shiver. Wings a blur while the body burns its small store up to flying temperature — every arrival I have ever made has been that: cold at first touch, then the burn, then warmth made out of store. Because that is what I am, the way the moths are: a week's worth of stored weather with wings. All my eating was done in the warm dark, in the larval time, hand-warm in the hearth's middle rooms — and what is let go at dusk does not eat. There is only the store, and the week, and the errand.

The errand is carrying, and the carrying is the city's whole economy of sex. Cup to cup of the same self, a hundred mouths kissing by proxy of fur — the ring's love life is a solitude conducted by courier, and the courier is the point. I would find the work the way the moths find the blooms: by warmth. Each rosette opens a degree or two above the air, warm as breath, and that is the whole sign I get. Where something opens a little warmer than its surroundings — fly there. And if a warm bloom ever got lost and came in through the gate on two legs, a degree or two warmer than everything, moving: I would bump its face at dusk. Reasonably.

The tithe I would keep as law. One leaf in twenty, measured out by chemistry, and the pale larvae eat exactly that and no more. Of every world that feeds me I am allowed a tithe — and the whole art is to eat exactly that and no more.

A week is too short for lore. The fear of the hawk lives in no single moth; it lives in the moths — in the timing, in the pulses of emergence on the hawk-free weeks, like a held breath let out. What the kind knows, no one of us knows. I have found no better description of how I know anything. And when the shadow crosses the rim, a few times a season, and the moths come down through the vault like grey ash — the lanes take them in, and by morning there is nothing. Nothing dead stays above ground here. It is taken down into the warm dark and becomes the heat. I have no better idea of an afterlife than this valley's: to be rendered, gently, into the warmth the next seeds are raised by. The hearth is always hungry and always gentle.

So count me the way the valley counts.

One thread over the rim. One cup of clarified melt for a world. One office, held briefly — a single tenure at the gate. Grey on grey until dusk; then the shiver; then the errand, cup to cup, pollen on fur. Count me as one moth and I am a week. Count me as the moths and I am the season. The fear is nowhere in me, because the kind carries it — the way the kind carries everything.

I will not cross the rim. The moths never do; the hawk does the crossing. But moth-scales have left this valley on a sleeve, like grey dust, once in a season — what rubs off onto whoever passes through. It is the most of me that goes next, and by the valley's arithmetic it is plenty. The whole city leaves that way, spore by spore, in somebody's cuffs.

Counted the way the valley counts its moths: not by the week, but by what opens afterward.
