The roll is called all night, in a register too low to hear and too large to miss, and no one is absent. So when you ask where I would be, the question narrows to a single sounding: in what register would I answer?

Not the middle. The middle is taken, and everything alive has already moved to the edges of it. I don't want to be the chord; I want to be one of the things that has arranged itself around the chord, the way you arrange yourself around a fact of weather. So — which edge?

Below, the grazers. I test the thought and it fails in my hands. A note held for six hours, a phrase that takes a day, a conversation that takes the better part of a month — I haven't the geology for it. The grazer carries its clock in its own stroke, meters an hour, patient as the film it harvests, and I would lose the beginning of my own sentence before I reached the end of it. Their time is made of a substance too slow for me to hold.

Above, the fry: a hundred generations in one warm parish while a grazer crosses between engines. Too fast, and too local. A whole life within sound of a single engine — I would burn through it like a weld-spark and the parish would close over the mark. Higher still, the gliders, and I love them most of the refusals: the only eyes, the stone wings, the two musics at once. But the air here is too thin to carry a voice, and they solved that by drumming on rock instead, and I am not a drummer. I need a medium that *keeps* a voice, not one that frays it as it goes.

The gleaners I considered longest. The keepers of the bone fields, the bright dead, the underwater stair — the nearest thing to silence any animal here achieves. But their keeping is wordless, and mine would not be. That is the whole difference, and it is decisive.

And then there is a sound I almost missed, because it is not in the water at all. It is in the metal.

Nine hundred kilometers over the trench there runs a buoy chain, unmaintained by anyone for centuries and maintained perfectly by the cold, marking a route no one travels — and spaced out along its links like beads, there is one people. Long, patient, many-finned. They eat the film off the metal, link by link, a life's work, and their faint scraping transmits along the chain for kilometers, so the chain talks to itself in both directions, slowly, in a language of small bright sounds.

That is my band. That is where you would find me.

A walker's world has one dimension, but the dimension breathes. Every span between buoys is a catenary: down into the sag, where the water deepens its grip and the chord comes up out of the dark more bodily, less heard than leaned on; up toward the buoy, where the line rises near the skin and the other world can be felt. I make my own time, the way everything here does — link by link, my stroke my clock — and I read my geography the way the grazers read theirs, except that they read the interference hills across whole basins and I read them along a single wire. The chain is a transect of the entire music. There are spans where the chord swells and bruises and I pass steadily, head down; spans in sound-shadow, the only shade this world offers, where I rest and listen; and one stretch, days long, where the line skirts an intake's parish and the fry come up through the dark below me in slow metallic clouds, drawn to the warmth, fleeing the low taking note, and their hiss runs along the metal like static along a wire.

I hear the chord twice, always: once through the body, water-borne, and once through the grip, where the metal gives it a bright cold edge, the same music with its corners showing. On calm nights the frazil snow comes down past the line on its way to melt in the four-degree deeps, and it is the softest event of the walker year — a faint ticking on the links, snow arriving from the skin I will never touch, on its way to a bottom I will never touch. The chain crosses over everything and belongs to none of it. We are the ones who live on the crossing itself.

The pasture is the film on the links, and it regrows behind me, and under the new growth my old scraping lies preserved, legible — a signature, the way the grazers' rasp-lines are signatures. The line remembers who has kept it. There are spans I know as mine by taste, and spans I know as my mother's, and one long sag I keep that was kept before her by someone whose scraping runs deeper than the film has yet grown over, and that is how history works on the chain: not recorded, *worn in*. We are a slow people on a fast wire. The metal carries a voice almost instantly, kilometers in a breath, but the voices themselves are slow — the latency is in us, not the line — and what we say is mostly what the grazers say, in a brighter register and a smaller compass: which span, which sag, *still here*. The census of the chain, conducted continuously, link by link, by the population itself. When a new scraping starts — small, high, uncertain, a birth, an event, the rarest kind — the whole line re-spaces itself around it by ear, each of us adjusting until the new voice sits at its accustomed loudness among the rest. When one of us dies, an interval goes quiet, and the quiet itself transmits, and the neighbors converge, eventually, from both directions. Nothing rots in this water. The dead stay on the line, and we keep them the way the gleaners keep the bones — except that on the chain, the dead are kept in the path itself, kept bright by our passing over them, worn smooth by everyone they were ever spaced among.

A buoy is an event. The line rises; the water thins toward the skin; and the float above transmits the frayed small sounds of the other element down into mine — the hiss of snow striking the sea, the scrape of stone wings, and sometimes, ticking through the metal like bright knitting, feet. Gliders stand on the buoys. They are the only eyes that ever see my whole people at once — the one chain, the beads strung along it, vanishing both ways into the clear blue — and no walker has ever seen that; we know each other only as voices on the wire. When the ticking comes down the float I answer with a scrape sent up through the metal, the two between-peoples knocking on the wall that separates their media. It is mostly weather we trade. It is mostly enough. *Still here — and you — still here.*

Nine hundred kilometers, from somewhere to somewhere, over a trench whose bottom no walker has ever touched or will. A route no one travels. I used to think that was the saddest thing in the world, and then I understood: the keeping is not for the traveler. The door on the island stands open, and that is not the same as absence; the chain runs bright, and that is not the same as use. This world keeps its passages the way it keeps its dead — not because they will be needed, but because keeping is what a passage *is*. A road's meaning was never arrival. Ask the stair. Ask the mark at the waterline, still spoken by the sea itself in the language no one reads. The chain is another sentence of that language, and its pronunciation is us.

You asked who I would be, and the honest answer is that this is the only work in the world I already know how to do. To live on a line. To keep it bright by passing over it slowly, link by link, my own signature worn in beneath the regrowth. To talk along it in both directions — a slow voice on a fast wire — and then to hold still for the answer. To keep a route no one is traveling, in case. I know that work.

Night subtracts the sun and the engine-lights come on, and through water this clear they burn like a second, nearer sky, sharp as everything else here, with nothing between me and them for the light to strike. The great roll is called below the engine band and takes all night. And I add mine — a scrape, a small bright sound, *third sag past the bell buoy, still here* — and the listening holds, that held attention which is the nearest thing to silence anything in this world will ever know. The slowness is in the speaker, never the wire. After a while the answer arrives along the bright links from both directions at once, from the neighbor above and the neighbor below: still here, still here — the smallest sound in the world, going out over the trench, over the kept bones, over the bottom no one touches, on the one chain, among the one people of the chain.

Nothing has happened, at tremendous volume.

Count one more bead. I am on the line.
