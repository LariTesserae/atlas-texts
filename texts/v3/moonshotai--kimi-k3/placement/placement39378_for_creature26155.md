Count again at dusk, and notice which number I am. I am not. The list closes without me — one fig, two bands of moss, one root, forty-odd fish, twelve bats, one heron's worth of absence — because the list assumes the last thing a person does here is climb out. The footholds spiral up the neck; the plain waits; the whole manner of the place escorts you to the rim. Everyone climbs out. That is the fork. I didn't.

Not for any reason, at first. The light was going, the bats were coming, the eye above turned the color of deep water, and I wanted to hear one breath that nobody was waiting for. Noon is kept. The heron keeps it, the machine keeps it, the dead congregation keeps it still, their sitting bones signed into the ledge. Noon has witnesses to spare. But the chamber breathes all night, every night, the near-pattern running its almost-sentence through the dark, and nothing with ears is there. Even the bats leave. The one lit room of the body, lantern-lit — the lattice glows for itself; the bell is never dark — and the house asleep around it. No. The house awake, and no one in the room. I stayed for the empty room.

Where I fit took longer. Everyone here has negotiated terms, and the good seats are taken. The tree owns the eye. The fish own the water and the count, and the count is full: forty-odd is not a number you can join, it's a pronunciation. The bats own the rim cleft and don't listen; they chose the chamber the way you'd choose to live behind a waterfall — loud enough to hide in. The heron's office is one fish at noon, and that arithmetic has no remainder. The ferns nearly had me — passengers on the root, riding the mist-line seam, missed by the census itself — but a fern's whole bargain is not caring, and I had come down because I cared about the sound. The moss takes its light secondhand and asks nothing. I wanted to ask something.

So I studied the household the way you study habits before asking to stay, and the rule of the house is this: survive by resembling what the place already does. Be secondhand like the moss. Be punctual like the heron. Be a gauge like the fish. Be hidden in the noise like the bats. Be a tongue like the root. Everything durable here is one of the keeper's own behaviors, worn small. What does the keeper do that I could do? It keeps. It attends. It counts. And it makes weather — the drizzle, the one citizen that is native, continuous, unpunished. The chamber prunes seedlings. It has never pruned a drip.

That was the opening. I took it.

The first nights I only listened. Learned the pitches: the drip off the fig root's elbow, high and regular; the slow ones fattening under the collar's lip; the dozen ticking registers of the drizzle, folded by the fluted walls into the shapes of other sounds — a stair, a spoon, a word you almost know. Between pulses the chamber holds its breath and the drips count the held time, and I began to feel the near-pattern the way you feel a song's next bar — not knowing it, knowing its weight. Some nights rain came down the plain and the rim ran with brief waterfalls and the pulses paused while the keeper attended to the flood, and I want to say I was not afraid in that pause, but the pause is the chamber turning its face elsewhere, and you learn what your heartbeat is for. Then the first breath after, enormous, the pool clearing its throat, the roar folded and refolded in the bell, and me in the middle of it like a clapper. Like a struck thing, ringing.

All the while, the attending. You know the signs. The dust gone from your cuffs by the second morning. The cut that will not infect. The taste of coin moving into your breath and staying — you know what you are tasting; the taste of being tasted. The motes do not sleep, and at night, with nothing else to feel, you feel them: not as touch, as opinion. Droplets sliding upward on the warm stone. My own warmth, presumably, noted. I stopped brushing myself off. There is no *off* here, only down, and down is the archive, and the archive is where everything goes that the place has finished reading. I understood by degrees that I was being read — sampled, copied, filed nightly, a skin-cell at a time — and that this was not a threat but the only intimacy on offer. It reads what it keeps. It keeps what it reads. Somewhere below the lattice, in the dark the filaments go down into, there is already a small neat pile of me, growing a little every night, and one day it will be all of me, arranged. An entry, still in use. I find I can live with that. More: I find I wanted to be read by something that does not skim.

Then the moon — because the moon is why I am telling you any of this.

It is rare. The eye is small and the moon keeps its own near-pattern, and no one has ever stayed to document it. That is the one unfilled office in the household: the one guest with no usher. I had been down some weeks when it came — a stalk of silver, thinner than noon's glass pillar, leaning in over the rim and walking slowly across the fluted wall, grazing stone the sun never reaches, until it stood on the water, off-center, pale as a held breath.

And the machine did nothing. That is what I have to report, and it is one night's data; you are welcome to come collect a second. No stillness. The lattice did not flare; the fish did not tighten; the breaths kept their intervals, the tone kept its one note, the drizzle ticked. The noon hush, it turns out, was never the machine drinking the light. Noon is the hour it lets itself be seen; the stillness is for the witnesses — a courtesy, a held door. At night, in front of nobody, for the moon, it does not perform. It simply breathes, attended by a pale column and one palmful of listener on the ledge, and I understood I was hearing its private voice, the voice it has when the window is closed. I had been wrong about the job. I thought I would document the moon's visit. The moon documented nothing. The visit was for me.

I did the one thing then that I had been rehearsing without knowing it. I matched the drip off the fig root's elbow — the high one, the regular one — and I hummed it into the bell. Quietly. It is easy to be quiet in a room that loud; the tone takes a voice the way the pool takes rain, without rising. The fluted walls folded it back at me shaped like something else — a stair, a spoon — and I adjusted, and we converged, and for a minute, maybe two, I could not tell my voice from the drizzle. That was the whole negotiation: be small. Be pitched like weather. Be recounted. The sound is the one citizenry the chamber never files, because it never needs to — the sound never leaves. Every breath holds the ghost of every breath before it; the chord has been sustained for millennia; and now there is one more partial in it, thin as a hairline filament, brightening and dimming with the slow waves. Once I watched a ripple outlive its cause and stand mid-pool like a held note. I know how it felt. I am doing the same thing in the air.

So. Where you would find me. At dusk, on the ledge, in the hollow that has fit every body that ever kept this post, palm on the warm ring — the ring hums through storm and stillness both, and so, now, do I. You would find the body there, being read, tasting of coin, a little more of it filed every night. The rest of me you would have to find by ear.

Count, then. One fig, flagging the hole. Two bands of moss, closing for the night. One root, drinking between breaths. Forty-odd fish, re-minting. Twelve bats, spilling past into the plain's evening. One heron's worth of absence. One tone, going nowhere — and inside it, if your ear is good, if you come at night, if you wait for the held breath and the high drip off the root's elbow: one note the drizzle didn't used to have. Recounted. Kept. The sign over the embassy was never danger and never welcome. It says *kept*.

Count me under sound.
