# One Breath

I took one breath in my life. I took it at the ceiling of the world, in warm rain, in the hour I was born, and I gave it back in the same place a hundred years later, and everything between was the holding of it. That is the whole shape of a pilgrim: a breath held long enough to cross the whole depth of the sea and come back.

I say this from a ledge on a saddle between two summits, in the bright shallow water, where the bloom is finishing me. The stone tells its dead all at once, in one tense, forever. I still have mine in order. So — in order.

## I. The Taking

I do not remember the taking itself. My first memory is the ceiling from above: hammered light, and the rain dimpling down to me, and the thin clear skin of freshwater that held me the way a halocline holds what falls to it. For a few hours I was a freshwater creature, the only kind the province has. I did not know that this was a privilege. I thought all water had a sky, and rain, and an hour that was entirely light.

Then the skin thinned, and mixed, and was gone, and I fell.

I fell out of the gold first — the halo from underneath, a ceiling of late afternoon — and then through the thicket, the green dim weather, the snow going both ways, curtains of frond bending around me and closing behind without marking me. Once, in the green, the light bent strangely ahead and I passed through a coolness full of small slow motes, and only later learned I had fallen through a grazer's guest-room, through the window that swims. Then the rust, the wide flat leaves held out like hands asking for almost nothing. Then the seams, amber veins dimming with my depth, the mountains letting go of me by degrees. Somewhere in a blue gap I fell past a stolen bubble paying out its near-invisible sheet, seeded with a decade of errands, and did not stop — the weavers catch seeds, not pilgrims. Then the root-dark, where nothing has ever lived. Then the floor.

I landed the way everything lands here: slowly, and forever. Bare black stone, nothing rounded, nothing carried, the ledges ending the way the fracture left them. I had seen the whole column before I knew it was a column. Everything I have learned since has been a footnote to that fall.

## II. The Holding

For decades I grazed the shed — the forest's slow fall of used-up sky: torn fronds, spent bladders, the spore-dust that outfell its season, all of it settling through the dark onto the basin floor. I grew the way things grow here, which is to say I shed, and counted my years in skins. My neighbors were the dead. Behind my feeding ground a ghost-grove stood tier below tier, pale in the black, the mountain's old light-lines keeping their places; and when the long swell leaned through the deep water, the played-out chambers of the grove sounded, organ-low, felt in the bone — the range's second voice. The first voice is the groan, and I heard it once. It is not heard, exactly; it is survived. The whole country shifted in its sleep, and I understood that the floor I trusted was unfinished, that everything under me was a rumor with a date on it.

In the years after the groan, the stranger came — or what was left of it did. It arrived dying, the way they do, knocked loose from some older dark: a body that had been mostly an eye, soft and round and wrong, the dome already hollowing where the soft tissue failed first. I am all angle and held breath, glass-stiffened like everything native, and I had never been near anything soft. The bloom took it without hurry. The stone does not ask where you came from. It only asks that you stay.

Past the caldera rim, the lake lay drowned in the sea — a lake with a surface, mirror-still — and beneath the mirror, inverted, broad as a room, the keeper crossed sometimes like weather crossing a sky, if the sky were underneath you and made of stillness. When the shed landed on the halocline it landed on the keeper's world like something dropped on a roof, and the keeper went to the sound, and ate, and the lake stayed clean. That was the whole of its life and its work, and it kept its depth the way everything does.

Everything except two of us.

I saw the other one after the groan, when the ticking came faint through the clean glass — cooling rock, or rock about to rise. A new spire was splitting the basin dark, and on its crest there was a rider: small, pale, patient, settled on the summit-to-be, going down into the dark in order to come up through it years from now with the forest founding itself around its small stone figure. A listener. The other exception. Everything else in the province keeps its depth; it was descending on purpose, and I — though I did not know it yet — was about to ascend on purpose. We did not call. Nothing here calls. But I turned toward it and held the turning for a long time, and it held still on its crest, and that was the entire conversation. In a country where nothing calls, going is the only speech we have.

The turn, when it came, warned me not at all. Something turned the way the long swell turns at a ridge, the way the seam-light turns at evening — and the brightening stopped being a condition and became a direction. The breath I had carried like a stone began to carry me. I ate my fill of the shed, and more, because the climb does not feed and I knew that the way I had once known my depth, and then I put my grips on the black root and began.

I climbed through the archive toward its blank page. Every tier I crossed was the mountain's past — old light-lines, the forest's former selves standing pale and patient in the dark, tier below tier, all of them going up as I went up, from oldest to youngest, from stone toward water. And among the stone fronds, clinging mid-grip, were the ones of mine the climb had used up. The living fronds they held had set around them as the light-line climbed away, and the diary files them by posture. They cling. I passed them slowly — I pass everything slowly — and I did not yet know which posture I was practicing.

In the lower dark I climbed past the seams, and pressed to the glass along them were the seam-eaters, patchworks of dim amber, hand-sized to room-sized, the only warm color in the deep, eating light that had crossed kilometers of rock to reach them. They do not shine. They hoard. I crept past one across the years and it crept along its seam across the same years, and neither of us hurried, and I thought: this is the company my life keeps — creatures whose whole motion could be mistaken for stillness by anything that watches briefly.

The light came back the way it had gone: by degrees. At the light-line the rust tier took me, and I, who had been dark so long I had forgotten I had a color, turned rust with it. There I watched a lier outlive its leaf — the wide blade set beneath it in pale stone while the lier, at about the pace the mineral works, walked off the replica and onto a living neighbor. For those years we were the two fastest things in the rust tier, and we both knew better than to hurry.

The thicket took years per span. I climbed the holdfast cables among the glass-snails, through the only garden in the world, where the plants pay out a thin sugar to keep the stone in the snail and the snail keeps the bloom off the anchor — tender and tended, delaying each other. One bladder in a thousand rose a half-degree late against the current, and I knew it for a thief and climbed on; a frond tolerates one or two. In a spore month the snow fell upward and downward and hung, and the thicket became the inside of a shaken globe settling at half speed, and the light bent around a dozen crossings at once — the congress of the almost-nothings — and even then you would swear the water was empty. And once, on a cable an arm's length from my grip, a needle took station beside a grazing snail, and I held still for the strike. It took an hour from first touch to last, and the snail never struggled, because nothing that lives at the province's tempo can feel a violence that moves at the province's tempo. It is the one native weapon, the only predation, invisible at any natural attention span. You must bring a century to watch it properly. I had a century. It simply finished, the way everything here finishes.

Halfway up the flank, a rim somewhere above me overtopped, and a brine fall came down past: a rope of heavier water bending around the ridges, too slow and too coherent to be believed, silent except for the faint granular hiss where the two waters sheared. And in it, inverted, riding its mirror down as its world drained beneath it, went the keeper — on its one journey, the only one it would ever make. I was on mine, going the other way. We passed within a span of each other, the two kinds of creature this sea has: those with addresses, and those with destinations. Some keepers do not make it. The caldera rims hold their silhouettes in stone, one per overflow, each with its position for a date. I do not know which date this one became.

Then the gold. The halo from inside is not gold but white-gold, blades packed past believing, all of them straining at the ceiling, the water turned to glare. Raft meadows tore free around me and went voyaging — the arks, freighted with snails and thieves, a decade to a lap — restocking some other summit's tangles from the wreck of mine. Rain came and went above, silent from below, a dimpling of the sky, a soft static. The tallest bladders pushed through the ceiling and rested there like the backs of sleeping animals, and I climbed among them, and felt the rain's tremor in my grips for the first time since the hour I was born.

All the way up, the climb was doing its other work. The young quickened in me by tiers — dark in the dark, then rust, then green, then gold — as if the light were ripening them. I fed no more. I lived on the basin decades and the breath, and I thinned, and left my skins on the floating bladders as I rose. The census knows my kind mostly by our leavings. Those were mine. A traveler pays toll.

At the top, among the backs, I waited for weather. It is the last patience, and the breath — a held breath does not hurry — held a little longer. You cannot release young against a dry ceiling; they need the thin skin that rain lays briefly on the sea, the few hours when the province has a second kind of water. So I pressed among the sleeping backs and watched the hammered light and felt the young finish in me, gold-colored, ready.

Then the sky dimpled.

## III. The Giving Back

I pushed through among the backs, into the warm rain, into the air — the second touch of sky. They say that for an animal of the green dusk the light at the ceiling is not bright but absolute, and they are right. My compass, which had spent a century saying *up*, said nothing, or said everything: white, in every direction at once, the whole sense filled. A compass at its destination has no direction. That is what white is.

I let go of the young into the dimpling. Each one took its breath — I felt the small tuggings, rain in reverse — and rode the thin skin out, freshwater creatures, the only ones the province has, for their few hours. Then I let go of the breath. One hundred years I had held it, and it left me the way the swell leaves a ridge: without sound, without hurry, home. Nothing here makes its own gas; we steal it, or swallow it once, or do without. I had swallowed once, from the sky itself, the only lender, and I paid it back with interest — one breath returned, and every one of my young carrying a sky of her own.

The fall back takes hours. It is the same fall I made as a child, and not the same: then I fell through a world I did not know, and now I fell through my own life, tier by tier, in reverse, and every tier still had my color in it. I steered a little — I had some skin left — toward the saddle between two summits, the bright shallow water, the one vantage: ridge after ridge running off crowned in gold, gold to emerald to blue to the haze that is only distance through dense lit water, and beyond the farthest crown the strong sense of more. I chose a ledge on the saddle's shoulder, where the seam-light reaches in the evenings. I settled. I lay flat.

The bloom found me within the year — the thin film, the first silica, the stone's opening bid. They will file me by posture, the way the diary files all of us: the failures cling; the successful lie flat. I am flat. What the posture will not say is the white. The diary keeps count, and keeps shape, and keeps time; it does not keep light. I was the one holding the light, and I have given it back, and the giving back is not in the diary, and it is the whole story.

In fifty years, or eighty, one of mine will pass this ledge on the long climb, going goldward, and see a flat shape on the stone, and not know it can be read. That is all right. Nothing here reads; everything here is read, eventually, by the one reader. Everything else knows its depth and keeps it. I spent mine — the whole column, both directions, the sky twice — and the stone has me now, as it has the rider on the spire, as it has everyone in the end. But the breath was never cargo. It was a promise, and I kept it the way the seams keep noon: all the way down, and all the way back up, and given back by degrees, in the evenings, slowly, the way this country does everything.

Far above me the rain is dimpling the ceiling, soft static, and somewhere on the thin skin my young are riding out their few freshwater hours, each one holding a breath she will spend a hundred years returning. The bloom is at my back. The light is going by degrees, the way it does here, the mountains letting go of me as slowly as they let go of everything.

I have been dark before. I know the way up.
