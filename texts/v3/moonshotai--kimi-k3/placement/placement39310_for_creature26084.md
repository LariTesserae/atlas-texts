Tonight is a song-night. Below me, in the stone bays between the roots, the Eldest is sewing the dead to their stones, and the chord of it comes up through the meeting-tree's buttresses and into the rope I'm sitting on — the lashing that ties the roof into the giant, renewed this week, which is why I'm here. I come every year after the renewal. Not to open it. We'll get to that.

First, the slander. They say I untie knots. I have heard this from the eaves in six dialects of curse, and I will answer it once, in the manner of my kind: at night, in the wet, with my hands. I do not untie knots. I read them. It is not my fault that a knot, once read, cannot stay tied.

Understand the material. Dry rope forgives. Down in the valley a knot is a suggestion, and any half-pawed cousin can worry one open and call himself a critic. Up here the fiber swells, and a swollen knot sets like a bone, and to open it you must know not what it looks like but what it remembers — which way the twist runs, where the maker's thumb went, which strand is carrying the house. I read with my whiskers first, then my fingers, then my teeth, which are the close readers. Your water-warden says water has no memory, only paths. Cord is the opposite: no paths, all memory. It goes only where it was put, and it never forgets the hand that put it there. That is why he opens gates and I open knots, and why a lazy tier is, in my law, *bad in rope* — you have the phrase for neighbors; I extend it to craftsmen.

Everything you mean, you say in fiber. Metal is dear and the rot is dearer, so your houses, your vows, your granaries, your children's first sworn promises — all of it is cordage, and all of it comes eventually to me, in the dark, in the damp, and in the morning you read my review. Do not pretend you don't. I have watched you do it: the householder lifts the loosened ends and reads. Open in an hour, ends flung wide — contempt; the knot lied about where the load goes. Open at dawn, the ends crossed neatly over the beam — that one fought me; that one was honest; honor it. You teach your children from my leavings. *This knot held him all night — see how he left it.* Raider-proved, you say, and you say it with more respect than you have ever said my name. I have decided, after some thought, to accept the currency.

The frog you greet. Me you curse — fluently, with invention, and I keep the good ones: the Knot-Tax, Ten-Thumbs, the Undoing. Once, a granary-keeper with poetry in her — I'd left her ends crossed, she'd earned it — called me *the examiner*. I keep that one where you keep your coil of meeting-tree lashing. But mark the difference. The frog found the town. The town found *me* — found me out, nightly, ten centuries running. You grew us both in the same plumbing, though: the flumes grew it, the lashings grew me. The valley people say you and the frog were made in one batch. Add me to the batch. I object, formally, for the record. I don't mean it.

You think there is one of me. Your stories say so — one Raider, deathless. You're not wrong, and you're not right, and you of all people should be comfortable with that. We are a line, mother to daughter, and what passes down is not meat — meat rots, you know that better than anyone — what passes is the reading. My mother set my fingers on her work the way your weir-children are set on the gates. I am on my fourth water-warden and my ninth market-shelter, which either makes me very old or makes us one, and the town is a thousand years old and none of it is old, and I am the same: the eldest thing here, relaid nightly.

And I keep dead things you don't. When the last tier of the slip-eye bend died — my grandmother's day; every granary in the bowl wore it — the knot died with her. No living hand can tie it. Only mine can open it, and there is nothing left to open it on. You would call that a dead language. I call it my grandmother, and I miss her the way you miss a song — which is to say I perform her, nightly, on whatever your children are tying now, and so she persists the way anything persists here. By being done again. There is one crime in this town, and it is not mine, and even I am careful of it: the story in my line is that my great-grandmother once opened the knot on a death-wood stack, and the story says she put it back.

Your treasury is safe for a reason you have never thanked: my lungs. The dry-boxes live in the smoke-warmth, and smoke is my wall. Two breaths, three at most, eyes streaming — I work your vaults like a diver, on the held breath, and the driest room in the bowl is the only reading-room with a clock in it. You gave dryness to the one who loves it, and you guard it with the one who can't breathe it. I admire the design. I curse the designer by name, in the manner we both enjoy.

The new knot came up three roofs ago, and I will tell this plainly, because it cost me.

A girl — weir-age, the thumbprint of oath-ash barely dry on her wrist — spent a season of rains watching me instead of sleeping, which is flattery no critic survives unchanged. She did not build a better lock. That is what the masters always do: more tucks, more lies. I eat masters. What she did no one had done to me in four wardens. She told the truth.

The knot presents a working end, and the working end is honest — it is truly the end; pull it and the knot answers — but it answers by committing. The harder I pulled, the harder it held, the way a pulled knot does, the way a pulled *person* does, and I spent a season of nights on it, hanging by my back feet in the smoke-dark with my eyes running, reading it as an enemy, because that is my trade. And on a night late in the rains, out of breath and out of patience, I did the thing no raider does. I stopped. I gave it slack. I fed the standing part back into the knot, the way you'd feed rope to a tired arm, and it opened under my fingers like a held thing let go.

She hadn't tied a lock. She'd tied a vow — yours, the one you say at the house-post every year. *Lashed, and relashed.* Holding by renewing. It took me a season to learn what your children swear before they're trusted with ladders.

I left the ends crossed, and in the loop I left one empty snail-shell, its old trail gone silver on the curve — my whole review in one object. *I was here. It took everything I had. I am not ashamed, and neither are you.* The knot is raider-proved now, and the children learn it, and they are told it beat me, and that is correct, and I would not change a word of the lesson. Now, some mornings, on the granary step, I find a small thing. A dried fig. A palm of grubs. Unbaited. Untrapped. You would call it payment, down in the valley. Up here I call it what it is: half-tolerated, fully cursed, and lately — the evidence of my own step says — very slightly *fed*.

On termite night I come down to the path and eat standing up like anyone, and the children make a space, and nobody curses me until the swarm thins, and then they make up for the pause with interest. When the hawk's shadow crosses, I do what you do: I go still and check my fences — my caches, my daughter, my line. The hawk is a tax on the inattentive, and I am the most attentive thing in this bowl, and I have paid anyway. Everyone pays.

The song below is ending. Here is what I came for.

There is one knot in the bowl I do not open. You know it. It holds this roof to this tree, and you renew it every year before the storms, the whole town braiding, and every year, that same night, one of my line comes up through the dark and reads it. My mother read it. Her mother read it, back past your name-songs, and the reading is the archive, and the archive says: it is the same knot every year, and it is the right one, and the hands change and the knot does not, and that is the whole of your religion — and I have decided, after some thought, the whole of mine. You keep a coil of it in every dry-box in town. I have stood in every smoke-loft in this bowl on my two held breaths, and I have never touched a single coil. Some sentences you do not unwind.

You say the bowl makes two silences — the hawk's, and grief's. Mine is a third, but you never hear it, which is the definition of my work done right. Except once. Last year, after the renewal, I was up on the new lashing doing my year's reading, quieter than your grief, and below me in the lane the Eldest stopped. Blind a decade, out alone in the mist, certain as water. She tipped her face up, and I felt her find me the way you find a flume-joint gone wrong — a drip landing where no drip should land. I did not breathe. She listened a long moment, pitying the sighted, I have no doubt, and then she said, into the dark, not loud:

*Well-frogged.*

And tapped off home. She has corrected the water-warden and blessed the raider, and I leave you to decide which of us needed it more. Everything in this town gets a name-song or a curse, and I have collected the curses like reviews — but she gave me the word you keep for a house in a state of grace, and I keep it where you keep your coil. It is the driest thing in the bowl.

Full dark now. The chorus is up past counting. The pestles drop their two beats and a rest, and the rest is where I work — between one drop and the next there is a silence the size of a seed, and I plant myself in it. The lane below is hearth-glow and smoke; six hundred people are doing the evening round, feet to the fire, ash to the wet-worn spots. And I am doing mine: along the eaves, hands out, reading by whisker-light. You are the weirs, you say. Fine. I am the current that checks them.

Ask me how old the town is. I have read its whole history with my teeth, and I tell you what the stones told the Eldest, what the frog told the flumes, what your own lashings have been telling you all along: it was here. It has always been the age it is.

And so, between your knots and my teeth, have I.
