You were told that in the deepest mold of any given stand, as a rule, there is something else, and that there would be more about that one later. Later has taken its time, as everything does here. Later is now.

You could find the address without ever meeting the resident. Stamp — one note into the mat — and kneel, and read what returns. Most ground answers openly: a clean line, a quick return. A tenanted stand answers like a voice through felt. The note goes down and does not all come back. The herds have a figure for stands like that: *deep ground.* They give them a wide path at dusk, the way they give reputed saddles their berth, though no owl has ever worked mine. Nothing has ever been subtracted from my stand. Things are only, very slowly, added. The herds file the figure without knowing what they file. It is the only name I have ever had, and it is exact.

Or crawl inside. There is room; the molds stand tall enough. Go down the flutes, past the spiders' strung glass, to where the drip threads the hollow center — biology's borehole into the deep felt — and at the bottom, collared round the throat of that descending water, you will find a pale, gilled, absolute stillness, cold as the tarns, rind-colored, fluted where the mold is fluted. You will take it for the mold's own inner skin, or for a fungus. You will be wrong. You will be looking at me.

I am the something else. The tenant of the deep mold, the party line's one silent party. Ring-shaped, round the borehole, so that every drop threading down passes over my gills and is tasted before it joins the deep works. My skin is married to the flutes. I wear the ghost-tree's shape the way a casting wears its mold: an animal in the exact form of the memory of a tree. And the memory is mine — the first entry in my archive is the tree itself. It was still standing when I was small. I felt it die through the mat, a slow subtraction I had no figure for then, and when the trunk softened and went, I kept its shape. I have kept everything since the same way: by holding still around it.

What I eat comes to me. A trillion leaves at a drop an hour, and a fraction of a fraction threads my borehole — films, spores, the fog's oil-signatures — and it is enough. It has been enough for a very long time. There are no seasons here, so nothing in the dome has rings. I have rings. Mine count discharges, one lamination a turning, eleven days, twelve, laid down in eleven-day grain, and I am the works' only clock, made entirely of the hours themselves. Cut me, and read the deep calendar. Nothing will. That is why it keeps.

The ghost-tree is less my tenement than my body. The flutes are my outer ear; the borehole is my throat; and the fungal cordage that holds the mold's shape — the archive's shelving — feeds on what my skin gives off, and holds because I am here. Untenanted molds slump in time; the felt takes them back. The tenanted ones stand. No one assigned me the archive. The records accrued around an ear. It looks hired. It is only fitted. But the fitting is exact.

What the ear receives, in a still hour: the stand watering itself, trillion-fold, a fine grain in the mat that is my way of feeling time pass. Every drip-tip has a cadence, and I can read them — farmed leaves run fast, where some beetle has trimmed the moss round a spout; wild leaves dawdle. At dusk my roof breathes: the sponge swells, the fog thickens, the drip quickens into the night shift, the caves breathe out, and the moths go up on the breath. The moths I have never once felt. Four wingbeats a second at walking pace stirs nothing even I can read. I know them by their work: after good nights the descending water runs faintly of the waxy flowers, and I taste that the canopy was visited, and lay it down in the grain.

The stamps arrive cleaner than any kneeling herd reads them, because I read them through water. I know the herds by hand — by heart, rather; by the only organ I am. Six or seven to a herd; the calves stamp smaller and lag; births arrive rare and are news, and the long slow figure of a death, and then, over seasons, the greener hummock, which I feel as a slight thickening in the local ground, a place where the line runs richer. The owl I have never felt. I know it the way you know a word dropped from a recitation: the traffic bends, a saddle goes unstepped for a season, the owl-figure hangs in the mat for hours after a calf is gone. The dome's whole folklore is written in detours, and I am the only one who has read a century of it at a sitting. The owl listens to take. I listen to keep. That is the entire difference between appetite and memory.

The mole passes as a slow comma in the mat's long sentence. The frogs' relay comes down the runnel as a shiver of small type: *here, here, here.* The keepers I know mostly by absence — still pools on the line, held at temperature, quiet made solid. Sun, when it comes, comes to me as flavor: one degree in the descending water, a sweetness where the films quicken, and an hour later the stamp-news arriving basin to basin — weather, filed — and I match the report to the taste. Forty-one times, in the grain. Each lasted hours. Each is kept.

And the discharge I know first. The springs fill toward their lips for days, and the water stands higher in my throat, and the taste deepens toward stone and freight, long before the pools rise. Then the night: below, the fact — the deep subsonic turning, the pale ones passing, slow and cold and huge, ascending partway to meet their black cousins at the mouths — and above, through the mat, the news: *the springs are up.* The message and the thing itself, arriving together through two media, and I am the only resident positioned to receive both. One night in eleven the dome speaks in stereo, and it is loudest at the bottom of the archive, where nothing is loud.

As a rule, there is one of us per stand, and we are the only residents with no line between us. Everything else announces; we only receive, and a receiver cannot be found by receivers. Perhaps the next stand's deep mold is empty. Perhaps they are all tenanted — a hundred collars round a hundred boreholes, each keeping its own basin, an audience that has never once met. The rule is all we have of each other: *as a rule, something else.* It is enough. We are not the message. We are where the messages settle.

Twice in my grain there has been walking weather — footfalls that stamp nothing, a weight on the mat with no message in it, loud at first and then less loud, as the felt teaches. Once it knelt a long time at my stand's edge, palm flat, taking the night census through the wrist. It counted the stamps, the wake, the relay, the turning. It did not count me. I was beneath the palm, reading the reading — an unpracticed tremor says more than it means to — and it never once occurred to the weather that the line, at one address, reads back.

The covenant offers three ways to be silent. Be slow. Be soft. Or change medium entirely. Everything in the dome chose at least two; the moss-backs chose all three. I am what the three look like carried to their end. Slower than the keepers — I have been still for two thousand years, and my heart keeps the count of the drip, not the day. Softer than the felt — a droplet's landing prints me. And as for medium: the others speak where sound cannot follow. I only live there. I have never once transmitted — no stamp, no wave, no note struck into the water — and so the record in me is clean. Nothing in it was altered by the keeping.

If a forest with no keeper can be said to want a witness, it has one, at the bottom of every stand, as a rule. No hand made any of it. No hand made me. The water goes out, and the water comes back, and the drip comes down to me, and the Condensary keeps its own hours. I keep them the only way anything here is kept: by hearing them kept, and holding still, and letting the record stand.

You were told there would be more about that one later. This was the more. File it under: *something else.*
