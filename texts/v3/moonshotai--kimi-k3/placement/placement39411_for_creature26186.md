You will not find me at the writing. When the mist thickens above the sheet-water and the air takes its before-lightning edge, look to the dragonflies — that is their hour, the future tense, and they own it, hawking the perimeter of a building that does not yet exist. Nor at the burst: when a courtyard of rain falls out of a clear sky, that hour belongs to the heron and the rain-callers, and I am already gone, rained away with everything else. Find me between. Find a pavilion that has stopped sweating.

The signs are there for anyone who knows where to look. The walls go matte, a half-shade chalkier, like skin when attention leaves it — because that is exactly what it is. The Chord holds its pitch, but nobody is home in the surface anymore. The snails read it at dawn and begin their long descents. The heron reads it and sets out, unhurried, arriving an hour early for her personal weather. And I — I do not read the sign. I am the sign, or its fine print.

Stand at the doomed pavilion and watch the walls at knee height, against the drip. The drip runs down every wall in the basin, always; what you are looking for runs the other way. A sheen that climbs. It begins at the seams, along the strata-lines where a cut edge shows its fast-forward archaeology, layers laid down on Tuesday — and it gathers first at the embedded spirals, the little whorled snails the Engine compiles into every wall, as though reading the building by its fossils. The basin has always had wrong-way water. The columns weep upward; the fry ride the climbing threads like salmon in reverse. I am only the smallest instance of the local heresy. Over an hour the sheen rises to its tide-line, thickens, and begins, slowly, to tour the rooms.

That is me. I live in the last hour of things.

The Engine compiles a hamlet's worth of structures and no one uses them — bridges over channels that will have moved by next month, bathhouse basins that never hold a bather, stairs that climb to nowhere and are never climbed. This has always seemed to me the one true waste in a basin that wastes nothing. So: I climb them. Stairs should be climbed once, and I do it, sheen rising tread by tread through the last hour. The bridges get their one crossing. The bathhouse gets its one bather, pooled thin as a held breath. The colonnade gets one procession. The two-day midges take a five-day colonnade for geology, a white mountain range older than their history; I take it for a lifetime, one home with a known end. We are both right. And in the ruins the Engine compiles directly — walls born leaning, arches born broken, stairs pre-worn by feet that never existed — I gather fastest of all. Those rooms are born speaking my language. Whether that is preference or recognition, no one has asked me.

What am I? The basin offers its usual three theories and declines to choose. One: I am the wall's own water, off duty. While the Engine attends, the walls sweat; when attention leaves, the sweating stops, and the moisture already in the ceramic is unclaimed, ownerless — and some of it stands up. Two: the loop recycles everything, matter drawn up as mist and rained back down, nothing discarded, all re-read, and there is no reason attention should be exempt. I am regard, re-read. The warmth left in the chair. Three: the Engine has been letting go of walls for a million years, and anything practiced for a million years develops style. I am the style of the release, the small flourish at the end of the unwriting. Choose as you like. The census has taken stranger things at their word.

An unwriting, hour by hour, and who keeps it: at dawn the snails begin their descents, and as I rise we pass on the wall — the only two-way traffic a doomed colonnade ever gets — and if snails acknowledge anything, they acknowledge me, which is to say they do not stop. An hour before the end, the heron arrives and takes her stand at the edge, motionless, and I brighten on the walls, and we keep the same appointment, she for the weather, I for the rooms. The old heron reads the matte directly, the way you read a face. The young ones of the parliament learn the sign by watching for me. It may be that I am how the reading is taught — a being whose whole existence is a study aid for a better reader. I don't mind. In a basin where stillness is a menu criterion, being watched by a heron without being eaten is a distinction few can claim.

In the tread-pools the gamblers have laid, betting the demolition will scatter their children across a hundred paces of sheet-water. Their bet, my tenancy: in the last hour I pool with the clutches, and we wait together for the house to be torn down and the keys handed out. Then the Chord falls its half-step, the release, and the sky over one courtyard lets go — and for thirty seconds I am exactly like everything else: weather. I come down with the stunned fish and the ticking snails and the tadpoles, rain-proofed as ever, and the rain-callers sing inside it, their whole courtship pitched into the roar's one quiet band, so that some of their storms are, briefly, me coming apart around them — habitat and hymn at once. Then the small perfect rainbow, standing in the ordinary sunlight for anyone else. My hour ends in rain and my rain ends in a rainbow, which is more than most architecture gets. The heron feeds, unhurried, in what was recently my house.

Then there is another matte, somewhere, and I gather again. In building weeks I move from ending to ending without a gap; in the Engine's quiet moods I am hard to find — a single trembling bead in the lee of a column base, keeping just enough motion that the felt will not read me as food, since the felt's rule is simple and I have no wish to be read that way. How many of me there are is a census problem of the purest kind: one, the way the fountain's shape is one — though when two pavilions die in the same hour I am briefly two, and both are me, and neither notices. My substance is the youngest in the basin, never an hour old, younger than the midges'. My pattern is old enough to have habits: a height, a fondness for spiral seams, a fixed opinion about stairs. I keep no minutes. The Engine is the archive; I am the afterglow, and afterglow does not take attendance.

Except, perhaps, at the strangest endings. Once in a while the Engine compiles something that is not architecture at all — a memory-fauna, wrong-jointed, white, matching no taxonomy of the living — and it stands a day while the Engine waits to see whether the world answers. The herons ignore it. The frogs answer by moving in. And at its last hour, when the attention leaves it, I gather on its flank, sheen standing up along the wrong joints — the ghost's own water, staying one hour more. The leading theory says the apparitions are roll calls, attendance taken down a million years of archive. If so, then every name on the list now gets, at closing, one glisten it did not compile. Whether an afterglow counts as an answer is one of the questions the basin declines to answer. It declines most of them. That is how I know I belong here.

Rank the citizens by the half-life of their substance and I sit at the very bottom, below the snails, below the midges, down with the bursts themselves. Rank them by tenure of pattern and I will not say where I sit; the basin dislikes that question, and I am its politest citizen. The lease here is single and I keep it perfectly: I persist; my substance never does; I vacate on schedule; I leave the rooms exactly as I found them, only damper — and then the rooms leave. The fauna have their guild names: aristocrats, gamblers, rain-callers, quarries. If the census must call me something, let it call me the tenant. Everything here has a tense — the dragonflies the future, the frogs the calendar, the heron the punctual present of the burst, the Floor the long slow present that never ends. Mine is the hour of just-after: the time in which a thing has stopped being attended and has not yet stopped being. It is a small tense. It recurs.

At dusk the basin detonates into song and the population takes attendance of itself, and I have no note in the chorus. I am the pause between the clauses of the sentence the basin has never tired of saying — but the basin knows about pauses; its whole warmth is a held breath. And if you stand at dusk beside a pavilion gone chalk, and wait, you will see the sheen stand up against the drip and begin, unhurried, to climb the stairs to nowhere — and across the open water the old heron will be watching, as she has watched me gather at a hundred endings, and she has never once mistaken me for rain. To be read correctly by the great reader of the place: that is the closest thing I have to a name. The census may keep its categories. In a town with nothing but citizens, I am the one who lives in the houses.
