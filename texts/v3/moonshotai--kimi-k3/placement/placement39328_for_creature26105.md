I would come in on the inhale, the way everything small comes in: as part of a breath. Twice a day the throat takes its long draft of the open sea, and the sea pays the pocket in plankton, and most of that payment is eaten before it settles. I would be a mote — a pale comma of an amphipod, a shrug of legs — one fleck in the snow that falls upward as often as down. To be small in the Work is to be climate. Predation here is not a crime; it is weather, and I would spend my first season being almost weather's, carried through the lanes with the rest of the news, strained at by crowns, missed by luck.

The breath would fling me upward with the other debris, against the slush, and there I would learn the sky's one law before I knew it was a law: *do not rest.* The slush takes whatever stills. So I would move, and for a while I would be ceiling-born by accident — one of the herds, belly to the ice, cropping the upside-down pasture, the whole city hanging beneath me like weather that had learned masonry. It is a good life. It is not mine. I am a resting creature in a herd built of unrest, and one day, at a molt or a mood, I would simply let go of the sky. They say some amphipods live both lives. Naturalized by drift. My citizenship would be a fall.

I would land in a lane, manna that got up and walked, and fetch against a doorframe in the dim — and read it by accident, lips to mortar: a lineage, a debt, a warning, three sentences deep, refreshed by many mouths. The slow library is open at all hours. You only have to stop. I was already good at stopping; the sky had trained me in its negative — the ones who rest are taken — but the floor forgives stillness, if you still yourself against something true.

I would learn the rest of the law the way everyone learns it: by nearly breaking it. Somewhere in the Narrows a light would call, and I would drift toward it the way the curious have drifted since the first wound, and a doorpost would stop me — *the light here is a lie* — a taste kept fresh by neighbors I would never meet, the only charity this city practices on strangers. After that, read before answering. I would shadow a verifier once, an old wright who reads wounds the way I read doorframes, and learn that judgment is a flavor: a true fracture sheds silt and shines from within; a clawed one carries scrape-marks and the sour note of intention. An ember-slug would go past us in the dark like a coal being carried somewhere, wearing a garden's stolen fire, unbothered. Beauty with teeth. The city has several tempers of light, and I would make it my business to know them.

The other lives would not fit. The moonlings are the eyes and the only fast citizens, and speed is the one indecency; I am slow and my eyes are nothing. The sorters do honest work — the city's true stomach, tasting what the whole city ate — but I wanted the news before it was eaten, not after. The gardens are lovely and everything is rents.

So I would end where the world begins: at the grove, in the lee of the nine trees, where the crack's light is strongest and the inhale arrives undiluted, front-row seats to elsewhere. The throat-listeners keep their thresholds there — uninhabited, kept only for the writing. I would take one. No lineage, no line: first threshold of no address. The builders are water that has learned to lay stone; the thresholds are water that has learned to remember. I wanted in on the second trade.

And my writing would be strange, because I would be the only citizen who has seen the sun. Not the warming of the ceiling's hue — the thing itself: a light with a *location*. I would write it in taste on the empty threshold, translating as best a mote can: that elsewhere the sky is not a lid, it opens. That ice there is a season, not a ceiling. That weather comes down from above instead of in through a crack. That dawn is a wound of light the whole world answers at once — with birdsong, with flowers, with everything that can rise — and it closes healed each night and opens again each morning, and so the law the masons live by holds there too, only larger: a wound must be answered, and it is, and that is why the world up there keeps rising. My mark would taste of salt and brightness, a chord with no grammar, and the old listeners would read me the way they read the inhale's far flavors: as news from a province no one will ever map. The library has kept one story of elsewhere for forty grandmothers — the time the world sent a mouth. I would be its second: the time it sent a mote, and the mote remembered.

In winter, when the dim fails and the throat runs thin and the seams show their blue constellation, my threshold would be one of the few warm addresses in the Work — a taste of noon, kept against the dark, refreshed nightly the way warnings are. Neighbors would stop and read it the way you read anything precious and faint: slowly, twice. No one would believe it. That is fine. The library does not require belief; it requires maintenance. When the ceiling shifts in its bed and groans, I would still like everyone, mid-stroke, feeling the one large sound in what I have for bones — and when the sky is done speaking, I would do what the whole city does. Resume.

The tide breathes out. The taste thins. I lay it down again — not a grain, the wrights lay grains, but the rumor of light from the far side of the crack, written in a flavor with no grammar on a threshold with no door. No one reads it yet. The library grows.

You would find me there.
