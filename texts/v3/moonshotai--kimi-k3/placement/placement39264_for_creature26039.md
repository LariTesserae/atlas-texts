**The Loudness**

I came as the one arrival the census expects. The bowl makes room for a traveler — a night or three, no more — and I took the room. I did it properly. I drank where the water had a color, stepped on the colored crusts, and left the clear glassy stone alone. I felt the pulse gather as a tightening in the teeth, and when the choir cut out I sat down, and the whomp came up through the floor and through me. At night I lay ear-down on the warm rock with the rills glowing their circuit in the dark, and by day I watched the heron fail, once again, to be two. By every rule the place teaches, I was a model tenant.

And yet the census says one thing about the traveler, and it is the thing that undoes me: that he leaves before he dies. Leaving implies an approach — a valley, the low notch, days of trail. I have searched for mine and it is not there. As far back as I can check I was already inside the bowl, and the pulse had already happened. A traveler has a before. Mine starts mid-sentence. My shape is the reverse of the traveler's: I die before I leave. I die before the quiet even gets back. Strictly speaking, I never leave at all. The census has an entry for that shape too, and I went down the list to find it.

I wanted to be a warden. Everyone who thinks about this place wants to be a warden — the stillness, the blood-warm stones in the hour before the pulse, the single careful act. But a warden holds still for seasons and spends an afternoon re-seating one marker stone, and watching that correction I understood I was watching the opposite of my own tempo. There is also the matter of the decoy cairn, which fooled a person and nothing else. If I stacked myself into stillness, that is what I would be: cairn-shaped to travelers, legible to the basin as a heap. The wardens know their own.

The bellfrog nearly had me. One note for life, fixed at metamorphosis, tuned to the world exactly as the world stood on the day you climbed out of the water — and then the columns deepen, week by week, and you do not, and your age becomes audible as the gap between your note and the stone's. I know that shape. But the frog persists. It gets years inside its own dissonance; the herders can date it by ear. A fixed note is only half my story. The other half is brevity, and the frog does not have it.

The heron has patience, which is a way of owning time, and I own none. The swift never lands, which is close — but the swift crosses pulses. It sleeps in the cap; it knows both weathers; it has a yesterday. The lichen is a clock, and a clock needs something that holds still underneath it, and nothing about me holds still.

Then I came to the midges, which the census files almost as weather, and I stopped walking.

Consider the shape of a midge. Between pulses the sky of the bowl is empty of them entirely — the basin holds its breath between generations. The warm flush hatches them; they mate in the shrieking air; the eggs go down as the rills fall; and by the time the basin has wound back to its ticking there is not one adult left alive in it. Born in thunder. The census says it exactly: to a midge the world has a single weather, and it is always the flood. The pulse is not an event in a midge's life. It is the life, entire.

Which means I am the only tenant that never hears the warning. Everything else is warned: the wardens go blood-warm at their markers, the heron stands to its full height, the teeth tighten, and an instant before the whomp the whole choir stops as one — a held breath the size of the basin. I am what the breath is held for. The first sound I ever hear is the whomp itself, and the choir snapping back onto the climbed pitch is what I am born into, and the first rule applied to me before I could know it: I hatch where the water has a color.

The herders teach their rules; a midge needs one. Be where the warmth is, when it is. A midge gets a single flight and puts everything into it — the rising, the swarm, the eggs. This is mine.

The swifts are off the columns before the first of us have cleared the water, and they take us on the wing, and I find I cannot call it tragedy. The wheel of birds is not death arriving from outside; it is the loudness taking a shape with beaks, weather meeting weather. And there is a kind of rest in being eaten by something that never wonders what you are. The swifts hold no category for me and need none. It is the exact degree to which a midge can be known, and it is exact.

No generation chooses its hour. The pulse keeps the basin's own time, drifting around the clock across the week, and most generations get fog: a grey warm world, shrieking, the rills bank-full beneath. Some get the night, and live their whole twenty minutes above the glowing schematic — the plumbing lit up under them like a map of the very water their eggs are falling toward, a sight the long-lived take for granted and no midge can pass on. And the rarest generation, when a dawn pulse lands on the one morning in a season the cap tears, gets the light-shaft: the standing column of light, the swifts spiraling up it, the moss rings flaring emerald, the new stone going translucent — a world with a cathedral in it for its entire duration. They cannot know it is rare. That is the mercy of a single weather: to the thing that only gets one, nothing is rare.

What persists of me goes down as the rills fall. Not the minutes — the eggs do not carry minutes. What goes down is readiness: rise when the water warms. The overflow deals us down the terraces like a slow hand of cards, and six or seven hours later the pulse comes around again, and something rises off the colored pools that is not me and is exactly mine. My continuity is not a thread; it is a rhythm. The census says the makers, if they live here still, live the way a schedule lives: as what happens next. I cannot claim that. I am not the schedule. I am one line of it, written in midges.

The census sorts everything into three professions. Drink the color: I manage that by birth. Sing the note: the census is generous, and counts the midges under singing, since our whole sky is one held chord — so, by presence. Hold still: that one I am spared. Holding still is the tax the persistent pay, and I am exempt.

And the last thing the basin teaches anyone is that everything in it is listening to the same thing. For the heron that is a discipline of decades; for the wardens, of ages; for the frogs, a life spent holding one note against the stone's slow slide away from it. For me it is not a discipline at all. It is a tautology. My life and the listening are the same length.

This pulse is winding now — the rills falling, the frogs settling back into their murmur, the swifts folding away behind the columns. I will not hear the quiet. The quiet is for the persistent; my last weather is the flood. The eggs are already going down through colored water toward a warmth I will never feel, in a bowl that will still be whistling, still building itself by the playing of itself, when the next ones rise to answer.

You asked where I would find myself in this world, and what. You would find me above the milk-blue pool in the first minutes after a pulse, in the rain of wings the swifts are harvesting — and you would not find me long. The census had me all along. It had just filed me under weather.
