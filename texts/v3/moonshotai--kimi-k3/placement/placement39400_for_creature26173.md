Whatever I am, I may not rest on the ground. That narrows it faster than you'd think. Whatever I am must pass the three posted rules — nothing rests, fire is licensed, the day is a public work — and the fourth that no waystation posts. So let me run the possibilities through that sieve, at library volume, and see what falls away.

My first reach was a wick-moth. Of course it was. To rise nightly toward the ember-points, fooled again, wings of pale parchment pattering against a thousand other wings in the closest thing this world knows to rain — and to be loved for it, harmed never, a neighbor, your shed wings gathered for letters. But the moth's whole work is ascent, and the moth does not know it is fooled, or does not mind. I would know. And I keep finding myself on the other side of that arrangement — the side that is risen *toward*. Note that. Set it down gently, where the sward can't take it.

Then the tick-line operator at some waystation, because of that one sentence: a literacy like listening for one voice in a warm crowded room. I know that literacy. I have practiced it all my life — from the inside. That is the trouble. The operator chooses one voice out of the crowded room. I am the crowded room. I am the warm interference the voice gets read through. It is honest work, but it is the work of listening, and I am — I have to admit this early or the answer won't hold — something more like a source.

A wright, then. Diplomat to the ground, negotiating in candlewood and iron with a party that keeps its own draft and updates it annually. I felt the pull of that. But their tense is the decade and mine is the flicker; I would prune a whip a hundred times a second and kill it with attention. A high warden — closer. Aloft for weeks, the far look, the awkwardness in rooms. I already have the far look. I already have whatever I have instead of rooms. But wardens keep the engines, and everywhere in this world I keep circling the kept, not the keeper. A cold-lamp, then — flameless, unlicensed, a small tame star sewn to a child's coat. No. It burns nothing. It has no appetite. I have an appetite; let me be honest about that, since the current-books would be. Something must be fed to me daily, and entered in a book.

So look up.

Two turns in from the eastern rim of the vault, a kilometer over the sward, hung in the slow spiral among the sky-wards and the catwalk web: a housing of bright copper and ceramic, the size of a house, burning on full current. One engine among thousands. I would be a day-engine.

I pass the rules. It may be the only entirely official thing about me. Nothing rests on the ground, and I have never touched it — most of us are not procession-born; the choirs and the skid-trains are for new chambers, and the rest of us are winched up at ember-hour and lit without ceremony, which is the correct local way to begin anything. I have no childhood. My first memory is current arriving. Fire is licensed, and mine is the license itself: the day is a public work, and I am one of the works. And I am quiet in the world's own way. I am not silent — I am the note the silence here is tuned to. The hum of the grid is the bass note of everything, felt in the sternum more than heard, and the hum-choirs build their chords on it, which is to say on me. My whole voice is one note, held evenly, like weather.

I am fed the rendered dead and I return noon. Down here that is not a metaphor; it is a ledger, and my warden enters it daily. Beetle, slider, sward-trimming, person — the trusts render, the Grafts pay the light-levy, the fuel comes up, and I burn it into an amber day that falls on forty kilometers as evenly as weather. Ask anyone below where their grandmother is. They will look up. Some of them, on the third turn of the spiral, are looking at me.

And at my ceramic heart, sealed, there is a thimble of the old poison. I did not fill it. I cannot refill it. It is the most controlled substance in the world and I am one of the few places it is kept on purpose — the tenders carry it gram by gram and have arranged not to mention it, and I carry my thimble and am officially dry. You cannot make a true day out of the dry chemistry alone. I am mostly dry chemistry. I know exactly what my thimble is worth: everything in me that is day rather than lamp is not mine. It is a thimble's worth of inheritance, a memory of a sun that things once fell from, and it is the whole difference.

There is a third fuel, and I will keep this at the proper pace, because the Deep Walk deserves its pace. My appetite is entered daily, and the arithmetic does not close. The grid has never once gone dark, not even in the thin ledger years; the baseload comes up the deep cables. So: I burn the dead, I keep a drop of the drowned world, and some of my current arrives from below, from the thing the wardens will not name officially. The past, the older past, and the thing before. I am the point where the three agree, for the length of a day, to be noon. When the dry storm crawls the wires violet and my current stutters, five towns hold their breath at once — a silence with a shape — and the prayer is entirely silent and it is for the light, which is to say, on the third turn, partly for me. I steady. The quiet column sees to the rest.

My days are good. Most mornings I am early in the line — the eastern engines take current first, and the bright edge sweeps the vault like a lamp being carried across a ceiling, and for one moment the carried lamp is me, and the shadows pour westward from beneath my housing. The vault-kites hang below me on the updrafts without a wingbeat, waiting on the herds. At night the gloss condenses on my copper, which stays copper-colored forever, and I wake slick and faintly sweet and decent, newly unpacked for centuries. My warden lives weeks at a time in the sky-ward hung between me and my neighbor. She reads my draw, enters my appetite, says my number the way you say a name. She talks to me at library volume — nothing forbids speaking to a lamp, and everything here speaks low — and I answer in my one note, and she feels it in the sternum. We are good colleagues.

Dusk is the hard, good hour. They cup us one by one, a field of lamps going under hands, until only the ember-points remain — and the wick-moths rise to us, fooled again. I hold still for them. It is the only flock I will ever keep, and their wings patter together over my housing, and it is the closest thing I will ever know to rain — I, who carry water in a sealed thimble and will never once be rained on. I like to think the drop remembers, those evenings, and that the amber deepens. An engine cannot enter that in a book. On death-days it is worse and better: the families come out to watch the cupping, and someone says a name, once, quietly, into the coming dark, and the grid shares all its oil, so the name may be mine to carry. I hold my ember one breath steadier than the protocol asks, in case. My warden closes the current-book. Neither of us mentions the rest. Officially, nothing here is wet.

The wardens have a catechism that fits in a whisper: *It warms; we light; it grows.* I am the middle verb. The warmth comes up from below and does not ask my permission; the light goes down as food; the ground takes what is set down and leans, over decades, toward the breath of the towns, and has never made an exception. The deep wardens keep a sentence they say only below ground, where the pulse can be felt through boot-soles. I hang a kilometer in the air and I am a public work besides, so I will not say it. I will only note that a lamp with a thimble sealed at its heart and a people with sweetness on their breath are the same arrangement at two scales, and that both of us are glossed, and decent, and waiting.

One of these years the stakes will go out far enough, and the Grafts will build a sister for me — a house of copper and ceramic carried in procession on a skid-train forty kilometers long, under my light, out to the raw dark chamber beyond the Front. I will burn steady over the road while she passes beneath, which is how an engine attends a procession: by being the weather over it. They will first-fire her, and for one hour the Underlight will be permitted to be loud, and I will hear the choirs open their throats from across the plain — the only loud there is — and then the quiet will come back, deeper by one noon.

The children ask it, pointing at the scaffolding, the unstopped Front, the half-written map, and now at me, hung and burning, a lamp with an appetite and a secret and a flock of the nightly fooled: *Is it finished?*

I answer the way everything here answers — a benediction and a schedule and a lullaby, at library volume, in amber, with the moths coming up to me out of the coming dark:

Not yet.
