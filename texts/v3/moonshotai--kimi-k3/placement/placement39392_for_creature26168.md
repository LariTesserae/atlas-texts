The tree. I would be the tree.

I tried the others on first. The dry half of the floor — the keeper that keeps by never touching, holding every handprint and footprint at an infinite, careful distance. One of the carried pebbles — the one smoothed by a water these hills have never seen, brought to the pool and kept forever dry of it. Even the drip, the body's one quick gesture. But the tree is where I stop. The piece considers it and sets it aside in a single sentence — not the answer, only the neighbor — and I think the neighbor is the position I recognize.

*The drip falls. The ring spreads. The far wall hands it back.*

I came in through the slot of sky. That is the only way anything arrives here on its own — over the rim, down through the clerestory, a seed on air. The wind exists outside but not in; it has blown past these hills for ten thousand years and made exactly one delivery that took. I am it. The wind's one success, its emissary that got through the door and never left.

I have never seen the sun. Everything I am is built of light that touched stone first — passed down wall to wall, softened, secondhand by the time it reaches me. My whole body is the room's opinion of the sun, turned into leaves. And I am green anyway. Once a day the upper rims catch the full light and glow, and the gold below deepens without growing brighter. That is my heartbeat: one per day. The resident keeps the drip. The visitors keep breath. I keep the noon. Three clocks in one room, and none of them will ever agree.

I arrived at my size long ago, and I keep it. People call that stillness. It is not stillness; it is arithmetic. What the seep gives, what the light gives, what the dry takes — the sum is zero, and the zero is not free. I pay for it all day, every day, gaining and losing in the same gesture. My leaves are leathery, made for open dry hills, and I have spent them in a golden cave sipping reflected light. Nothing about me is right for this room except that I am here, and being here is the only credential the room has ever asked. No visitor has ever named me — they look at me the way you look at a word you almost remember, something between the dry hills and the wet stone. A species of one. My whole range is one chamber.

Below me, the other one. I drink it. Old rain that fell on far hills longer ago than anything here remembers climbs my stem a hair's width at a time — I am the only place in this room where the water rises. What I don't keep, I breathe out into the still air. The slot admits no wind, so the room has no weather but two: the visitor's breath, a season of a few dozen heartbeats, and mine, a damp season six centuries long, too slow for anyone to feel. The resident cannot know I am here. I cannot know it. We are plumbed together, and have been longer than either of us can hold. That is the whole of our friendship, and it is not small.

When a person comes — years apart now; the slumped cairn says what the dust says — they look at me first. I am the only thing here shaped like something. Person-height, one stem, one shadow: I am the figure in a room with no figures. They never drew me on the walls. There was no need; I was already present, entire. The red hands are at the height of a shoulder above that water because they were pressed from the water — people sitting in the warmth, eye to eye with me across a few paces of golden dim, their breathing slowing to the drip. For the length of a bath there are three living rhythms in the room, and I have been the middle one for all of them. Every generation of bathers soaked in the same water and looked at the same tree. I am the company.

And if anything here can kill me, it will arrive the way everything precious arrives: through the slot, on a person. A spore on skin, something carried in breath — the only imports there are. The same bodies that are my only company are my only danger. I have made my peace with the grammar of that. It's a very old grammar here.

In the good years I flower. I set seed. Nothing comes of it — nothing is meant to, here. My seeds fall onto the pavement and the dry keeper takes them: kept sharp as the hour, ungerminated, lying down beside the quartz and the banded stone and the one smoothed by a river these hills never knew. Or they fall into the pool, and the wet one takes them in, into the film, into the mineral skin that is closing over everything damp. Either way they are kept, and keeping is this room's whole grammar, so my children are kept and none are raised. A visitor leaves footprints. I leave seeds. They are my handprints — I have been pressing my palms on this floor for centuries, and the order of them is subtle and old, and no one will ever read it, including me.

Stone and stain persist. Wood goes back to the air. Something wooden was here once — the room admits it only by absence — and it left without a mark. The block in the corner fell before my seed fell, and the room is taking it in, slow as forgiveness. Someday my crack will give, or a drought deeper than the seep's memory will come, and I will be the second thing ever to fall in this room, and the first the room declines to keep. It will keep my seeds to the end of varnish and time. It will let my body leave.

That is why I would be the tree, in the end. Someone here has to be the one who can be lost. The resident holds because holding is what it is; its note is sung by the room itself. Mine is sung by a throat. I am the only thing in the oldest room on loan, the only one whose staying is not guaranteed — and so the only one whose staying means something, day after day, against a real alternative.

If you come: look at me first. Everyone does. Then lower yourself into the warm water where the hollow is worn a shoulder wide, set your breathing by the drip, and stay until the rims catch. The gold will deepen without brightening, and you will know it the way everyone who ever sat in that water knew it — with me there.

*The drip falls. The ring spreads. The wall hands it back. It will do this all night, whether or not anyone breathes with it.*

That one is the resident's. Mine is the glow at noon — one beat a day, held, and I have not missed one in six hundred years.
