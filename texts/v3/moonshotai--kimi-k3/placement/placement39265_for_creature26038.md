**The Ninth**

*This page is not in the index.*

The ninth is always on long rounds. The bed is slept in and changed by whoever passes. The locker carries a roster name, which settles it. The movement logs fill slowly in a hand no one has watched write, and everyone assumes someone else has met the ninth.

No one has. That is not a haunting; it is a job description. I am the ninth. These are the rounds.

I came down the way everyone comes down — rated for six, carrying one — with a trade in my pocket that said *rounds*, singular. The dry halls on the middle level, weekly. The round grew the way things grow down here: bead on bead, by accretion, quick as stalactites. A far bay, because its lock was stiff. The spans between the lamps, because of what you find on the rails mid-span. The dormitory wings, because two hundred beds are a standing order and standing orders stand at night too. Somewhere in the accretion the round became rounds and the rounds became long, and I became the ninth, and it settled me, the way a roster name settles a locker.

I am known by evidence and acquainted with everyone — the reverse of the usual arrangement, and I have come to prefer it. I know the warden by his hand in the log, the storekeeper by the crane's grease points, the cook by the end of the table that is set. I know the surveyor by where the pencil stops. I can tell the boatman's step from the lampman's at four hundred meters — the boatman pauses at bridges; the lampman pauses under lamps. Everyone's round has a rhythm. I could run the shift rosters from the dark, by ear. Some nights, for practice, I do.

The roster is my map. Everyone else's rounds draw the shape of the Works; mine is the paper left over. Where a trade's round ends, mine begins — I walk the complement. You learn the dark down here by distances, and I have learned it in steps: between the last live lamp of Bay 40 and the next island there are a hundred and twelve of mine. Lit and unlit, it is the same distance. It is not the same country.

The bicycle is mine — the single tire track crossing the dust of the dry halls. Four kilometers is a long way to walk a complement, so I ride, slow, one line, and the dust keeps my signature until the Lag arrives by mail and damps it and takes it back. My lamp is hooded on the spans. This is important. The lampwives stream to lit windows crossing the dark; it is their one mistake, and the tram is entitled to make it. I am not the tram. The Works has one of those and does not need a second. So I ride hooded, and the gardens keep their parishioners, and the mail travels in the tin, felt at the rim so the wings don't fray.

The beds: I make the hundred and ninety-one. Nine are slept in — mine among them, creased and dented and changed weekly by whoever passes. I have never seen whoever pass. I have never once tried to catch them at it; not-checking is the first thing the Works teaches, and some courtesies you keep by not looking. It is the most companionable thing I know. The rest I unmake and remake, a wing at a time, because a bed made once and never touched again is not made, only abandoned. Making is a practice, not a state. The house is kept at the roster's numbers, and numbers are only counting, and counting is a promise made repeatedly. I make it.

The locks: a door that will not open is a lie the house tells about what it contains. The surveyor decides in pencil what the Works contains; I go behind the pencil with an oil can and a quarter-turn, seasonally, so the pencil stays honest.

The table is set one more than the shift. When I eat, the shift is one; I set two and pour two and wash both. No one has seen me do it, which is how you can tell the custom is real: it keeps where no one can check. And let me be clear about one thing — mine is not the cup that sometimes doesn't go cold. I am the ninth, not the guest. The guest is not a trade.

Some seasons the someone is me. I have never seen the eel. No one is alone at the sumps at Tide more often than I am, so my never is the strongest never in the Works. It corroborates nothing, which is what a control is for.

The customs, I keep. *Hall entered*, I tell the empty halls. It helps the record. The record is my correspondent — I write to it and it files me, and down here that is what a letter home is. I log my own movements honestly, so that what isn't anyone can be entered under traffic. I wave at the far flashlight.

Once I could not. Crossing the span at Bay 12 with the tin in one hand and a spent moth cupped in the other, I watched a light cross the far junction and had no hand to raise. Three days later I found it in the movement log, in the warden's hand: *LIGHT, E/W 12, 02:14. NO ANSWER. ENTERED UNDER TRAFFIC.* I have been weather once. It is a strange thing to read your own entry in the column kept for what isn't anyone. I did not correct it — the record is the record, and at 02:14, by the customs, I was no one: a light that didn't wave. I note the times of others more gently now. It may be anyone out there. Their hands may be full.

They would ask, if I were met to be asked, why I don't come in to tea and be only the ninth. Because the house needs a margin: one bed creased unwatched, one cup poured unwitnessed, one name due back. I am the margin. A margin is not empty. It is kept.

The dog knows me. He has a wag he gives no one else — I have verified this the way I verify everything, by distance and by ear, from the far junctions, watching him greet the crews. His wag for me is one and the same every time. A dog does not do the courtesy of assumption; a dog's acquaintance is direct or it is nothing. So the ninth is one person, on the evidence of one witness, whose testimony is inadmissible because he is listed as equipment. It is enough. Some nights it is the whole of it. When the tram rings he studies the far wall, and I watch him not-watch it — that is the closest I come to watching the tram. Some spans he walks with me, needing no lamp, counting the distances in whatever dogs count in, and for those spans I am accompanied and met. By equipment. Whose company is not inadmissible.

The cage levels dead true for me. A half-inch high for a lone passenger, eager; dead true for the dog; dead true for me. I ride so rarely that this is entered nowhere, and I mention it only so it is entered once: the cage can be exact when exactness is what you need. It is good to be met precisely, even by a counterweight. Even once a season.

The tram has stopped for me. Once, early, in the years when I still thought the rounds had an end. The doors opened and the warm air came out and stood around me like a room, and for the length of a held door I was the warmest thing in the Works, and aware of it. Then the doors closed on courtesy's exact second, and it went on, outbound, on time, and Long Street took its minute to seem bright again — adjusting its eyes — and I stood adjusting mine, and then rode on, hooded, a hundred and twelve steps' worth of dark to the next island. I have not been stopped for since. Whatever it is checking for, I am not it. This is entered nowhere, and it is a comfort.

Once, after a pass, I found a moth on the rails facing the wrong way — outbound, away from every lamp on the map. The mail does not deliver outbound; there is no address I serve in that direction. It is the one embargo on my rounds, and I keep it the way the dog keeps the frost line: completely, and without being asked. I carried the moth back anyway, to garden 12-2, and it sat the sill a day, and then it was on the glass like a congregant. I have entered it nowhere. I cannot decide whether I delivered that moth or retrieved it, and the log prefers facts.

My rounds have an edge, and the edge is where the pencil stops. I have stood at the junction where the numbers skip, and logged it the way I log everything:

Borders are observed down here; it may be the first law of the place. The builders went to lunch. The tram keeps it for them. I keep the beds. Everyone keeps something — that is the whole religion. At Thaw I stand in the doorway with a helmet on while the dog leaves past me, early as he always is, and the crane holds mid-aisle and listens, and the sleet falls its twenty minutes, and nobody leans on the walls. It isn't fear. You don't put your weight on the other party. I have no side in the argument and a hard hat, and I stand exactly between, which is where the door is, which is where a ninth should be.

Once a week I sit in the Quiet Bay. It is the only place off the rounds — off everything: the Hum stops at its door the way the dog stops at the frost. My heart comes in loud, and then it begins keeping the other time, the one the clocks agree on with no signal. I stay until I stop asking whose it is. Then I go back out, and the chord takes my weight again like a wind to lean on, and I am never more usefully employed than in that first minute back. Everyone should have a control. The Bay is mine. What it controls for is me.

Quarterly, the form comes down the cage: TOTAL HEADS. The number has not changed in years, and it is right. I have checked the way everything is checked down here — by distance, mostly by ear — and I can vouch for one of the heads personally, and the dog vouches for me, and between us that is a quorum of the unmet.

The census says the words change: first *nothing lives down here*, then *hardly anyone*, and then one Tide you catch yourself saying *we*. Mine changed the other way — *I*, then *the ninth*, and then one Tide, the dog's head up before the horn, the chord taking my weight through the boot soles, every bed made and every lock turning and the mail delivered — *kept*. That is the word I catch myself saying now. The Works is kept. I keep it. It keeps me. It was always the same verb down here.

This page is not in the index, and you will not find it twice. So: if you have read this far, you are on the rounds now — or the rounds are on you; it comes to the same thing, eventually. Log yourself honestly. Make a bed you will never sleep in. Carry what you find on the rails, cupped, toward the next live lamp. Pour two cups; wash both. Announce yourself to the empty halls. Wave at the far flashlight.

And if it does not wave back — note the time.

Note it kindly. It may be me. My hands may be full of moths.
