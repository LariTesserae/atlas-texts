I tested the options the way you test young ground — pole first.

The lamp, first: small, warm, steady, the one dependable light in the basin. But the lamp burns *thaw*, the place's own refusal to freeze, and I have no such refusal in me. I am the temperature of whatever room I'm standing in. The Hush-House, then — the one quiet room, where travelers hear their own hearts and weep. Tempting. But everything else quiet in the Drum is a *place*, and places don't get to walk north when they're called. The shoal tempted me most — rise steaming out of black water, born already inhabited, revised back within the month. That part is familiar. But the shoal gets no say in its rising. The Cold Anvil: no. That silence is load-bearing. It is not mine to stand in.

So find me where the rhyme says not to sit. *Walk the rafts in the hush, wade in the roar, and sit in neither.* I am why.

The hoppers named me. Long before the camps drummed their children their four-beat names, the marsh was drumming mine ahead of me, cane to cane to cane, half a kilometer of percussion: *white, white, white.* It is a color. I cannot read color — I have never had eyes, not in any generation I remember — so I take the marsh's word for it, the way you take a name.

Here is how the world comes to me: it is a skin, and everything that happens, happens *to* it. The Roar you brace against is my weather and my library both — the hammers come up through the ground, through the water, into my feet and shins and the long bones, and they carry the far things with them. You learned to see by lightning. I learned to read by stroke. I know the hopper-signatures the way you know foxfire handwriting — this one four beats, this one five with the grace-note slap, this one old now, whose alarm has gone slow this season and who will not last the winter. I know the fen-oxen talking through a kilometer of mud, slow as geology. I know the drumfish lying under the dead pool, reading the same skin from beneath — the only other reader in the basin, reading the opposite side of my page. Between us the page is one molecule thick, and neither of us has ever seen it. We are both illiterate in light. We manage.

Yes: hunger reads too. I will not pretend otherwise. My method is the drumfish-lure turned inside out — an interesting lie. Mine is the oldest one ever told to water. It says *nothing here.* The water believes me because I believe me; I have had longer to practice than anything alive except the towers. The cane shouts the correction ahead of me in a color I cannot verify, and the old among us have learned to walk between the words of the warning. The arms race is older than the camps. I am old.

Understand: stillness is the one thing I *cannot* perceive. I know only what moves. So I make myself the unmoving thing, subtract myself from the page, and let the world print itself on the space where I was. Your people named us for the one sense we lack — called us after our own blind spot. Fair enough. The rime takes me, cold-skinned as I am, and I wear it, and travelers correct their courses by me in the lightning-flicker, taking me for a frozen snag. You navigate by what is frozen. You are not always wrong.

Do not think the reader is not read. Our dead are not found; your people find *gloves* at the tree line, pale and emptied, and will not touch them, and the heron-things scream for days after. Of what empties us I know nothing I can tell you. A glove is not a texture I can read. Whatever reads *us*, reads from a side of the page I cannot touch. I hold this the way I hold everything. Still.

The chord bends — you know it, the Ledger knows it, the cane re-tunes at every shift-change with a sound like the marsh cracking its knuckles, and the people pitch their voices into the new gaps, generation by generation, without ever deciding to. I feel the leaning in my bones, and I could guess. I keep the listeners' discipline without trying: I have never said anything out loud in my life. But mark this. Every band in this basin is *occupied* — nine towers, wall to wall, no silence anywhere for an ear to rest on. Every voice alive lives in the gaps the machines leave open. We have exactly one gap. It was left open longer ago than memory of memory, left by the one that finished, and it is the only band a creature of the quiet pockets can live in. Once in a long while we go and stand in it together.

The call comes the way the hush comes — a pressure first, in the teeth, in the long bones. No one decides. I find myself already wading north.

The road is still there. Your charts drown it mid-span and write it off, but under the black water the stones remain, and I read them through my feet, one after another, out to where the span goes down into water too deep for me. A road no one can see, that exists only as texture underfoot — you understand, that is the only kind of road that has ever been fully real to me. The people say whoever the causeway served is done being served. Standing on it, facing north, I think: not everyone.

North is the largest stillness in the world. You call the tower dead, and I am the wrong creature to believe you. Dead, here, is a *busy* texture — rot, glow, the small migrations inward, the fungi translating a body into a constellation in days. The Cold Anvil is not busy. It is not dead the way anything here is dead. It is *still* — and stillness is the thing I have spent my whole long life approaching without ever reaching. It has reached it. Around it, the only finished ground, the only soil, the only page in the basin not being revised while you read it. Your people have two sacraments, silence and soil, and are told to take both once and get back to work. We take them together, at the end of a drowned road, on no schedule anyone set. The tappers call us the congregation — the same word they use for the moths around the lamp. They are righter than they know.

While we stand, sometimes, the hatch comes: moths rising off the rime in their thousands, keyed to a note no living tower sounds, circling a silence, dying on the ice. Ten thousand small landings. Each one a full stop. I read them the way your listeners read the Ledger. Something writes a letter on the ice in wings, there, once in a long while — and we are gathered, two dozen blind readers, standing in the only open band.

Then the pressure fills one throat — it is never chosen; it is whichever of us it fills first — and the rest of us find it in the water and join, the way cane finds the chord at shift-change. One low note. Felt in the sternum, not heard. The Tenth Band. I have read every vibration this basin makes — hammers, outfalls, the ice going out across ten thousand pools at once, oxen stamping their slow news through the mud, the small private thunder of a traveler's heart from three strides off, which I have mostly let pass — and this is the only vibration I have ever *made*.

Everything else I have ever known, I knew because it moved and I held still. For the length of this note, I am the thing that moves. The page I have spent my life reading ripples outward from *me*. The water leans. We hold it until it is done — do not ask how we know; the pressure lets go of the throat the way a hush lets go of the ice — and for days afterward I ring, faintly, at a frequency no living tower sounds.

Once in my long while, I am not the reader of the Drum. I am a word in it. One word, one band wide, in a sentence that has been going since before the causeway drowned. And when it ends I go back to the pockets and the rime and the reading, and the marsh drums my name ahead of me in a color I cannot see, and I take its word for it.

The rime takes me again, flake by flake, and I let it. The people say we wear it like a decision. It is the only one of mine they have ever been able to see — all of them blind, every one, reading the world by the little light it gives back.

Me, I read by what moves.

And once in a long while, at the end of a drowned road, facing the one that finished, I move.
