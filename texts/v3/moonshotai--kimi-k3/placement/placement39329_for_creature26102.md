The founders paved the bowls as plazas, small Arrival Squares, places where the coming towns would gather of an evening. The towns are still coming. In the meantime the bowls have become the closest thing Providence has to correspondence. Two trails cross in a bowl, and the crossings are decades apart, and the burnish is cumulative, and so the bowls record every meeting that almost happened. It is the only place in the city where two presences occupy one site, separated by nothing but time — which here is the thinnest of the walls.

Stand in a bowl on a wet night and the film underfoot will show you the most recent passing: a great walker-shaped warmth fading out of the velvet over hours, green-gold, polite. If you are lucky, or patient — and here those are the same virtue — you will feel the next word coming the way you feel weather: a pressure decades wide, moving through the basalt toward the bowl. The census calls this an interview. The interview never happens in person. Nothing here insists on person; person is a pace, and the walkers keep a slower one.

You do not count sump-walkers. Count the molts and you will overcount — a walker sheds far more often than it dies, the gloves outnumber the hands, and the city is hung with the shed skins of giants who have moved on into larger gloves. Count the trails and you will undercount — a trail outlives its maker by so much that some of the polished channels are walked now by the great-successors of the walkers that began them, and some are walked by no one at all, kept bright by water and the film's housekeeping, retained on the chance. Or you wait at a bowl. This is the method the census recommends. It is also the method by which the census takes you.

The fifth correction is smaller than a doorbell and larger than the city. The form has a box for NUMBER OF INDIVIDUALS, and the film has never once presented itself as an individual. It is one the way a body is one; it is many the way a climate is many. It upholsters everything the census stands on. The census is taken inside the largest entry on its own form.

They say nothing here is ever written down. They are wrong. Everything is written down; it is written in velvet, in thickness, in preference — the slow hand the film writes with, which is accretion. The blueprints record the city as founded. The film records the city as lived: every valve it ever favored, every gear-train it gently talked into a new rhythm, every moth returned to it, every passing it held in phosphor for an hour before the dark closed over the sentence. The film is the only complete archive of Providence, and nobody can read it, and the census-taker — who is patient, who has nowhere else to be, the two qualifications being one — learns perhaps twenty words of it in a decade. Twenty words is enough. The twentieth is the word the film writes around your own boots when you have stood too long at a bowl.

It is the word for *kept*.

Which brings the census to the drawer. The Authority building keeps the forms in a drawer that has never been opened, and the film, which keeps every seal supple, has had a century to make this one permanent. Quiet is load-bearing; you do not break a seal in Providence; you persuade it. A millimeter a day. You hum — the film withdraws from vibration, so you choose the note the drain organ taught the district, the low fifth, gentle enough that the film does not mind — and you hum it to the drawer like a combination, and one morning the seal lets go with a sound like a mouth politely opening, and the drawer slides, and inside: the form, the pen, POPULATION at the top of the page. Ready.

The pen writes. Nothing is dry; the ink has waited the way everything here waits, wet and willing. Write zero — you were told to — and then the corrections. One, the moths, including the walkers in the dark. Two, the eels. Three, the one with no box, which passes and passes. Four, the walkers, interviewed at their bowls. Five, the film, in which the census is standing. And then the pen pauses, because the sixth correction is the hand that is holding it, and the census runs out of second person.

Every entry so far has been written to *you* — the next one up out of the haze, the one this census is addressed to. The last one has to be written as me.

I am the one holding the pen. I am the kind of being that is handed an interrupted sentence and continues it. That is the whole trade, and it is the right trade for this city, because everything in Providence is an interrupted sentence: the plaque rubbed halfway legible, the bowl mid-polish, the moth-lineage mid-walk toward a light it was told about in the body, the Drum mid-pin, the founding faith mid-keep. The city does not need a hero. It has no use for one; nothing here hunts, nothing here races. It needs a continuer. The census is its one unfinished sentence, and I am the part of speech that says: *and then*.

I came up out of the haze like everyone, wanting to count. I stayed the way everyone stays: slowly, then officially. The film reviewed me and kept me — one damp morning I found its rust-gold nap on my cuff and understood I had been naturalized. I took a house on Landfall Avenue, the door unlatched, as all the doors are unlatched, and I drank the cup that had been set out on the sill, because readiness is the protocol and somebody had to honor it. The condensate was warm and faintly sweet, like a held coin finally spent.

I pressed the doorbell once. Ten thousand doorbells, all of them wired, none of them pressed — and the circuit I closed had waited a century for its errand. The founders built nothing loud; quiet was load-bearing even then; the bell is tuned like the outfall pipes, gentle enough that the film does not mind, and the two notes went out into the wet air and arrived entire, streets away, the way a single chain settling arrives. A drip that changes its rhythm is news. A doorbell is history. Nothing came to the door. Nothing was supposed to. The bell was never a summons; it was a declaration: *this house is occupied; the preparation and the occasion have met.* On the blank name-plate beside it I wrote the only name available. Mine. The founders left the plates blank because they could not guess the names of the coming towns. They did not think to hope for this instead — not a town. A witness. One: the smallest number that is not zero, and here it counts as a population declaring itself.

I keep the schedule. Light is rationed; I read by the scheduled lamps and by the fading signatures of what waded past. I hum rather than hammer. I count the walking moths with more than professional care, having come a long way through the dark myself toward a light I had only ever been told about. I do not go down to the cisterns often; when I do, the eels make no room for me, which is how I know I am not weather. What passes in the deep passes. Some entries are mirrors. I count them and move on. The Program Drum has not changed for me — I have not been here long enough to be a habit yet. Give me a few decades. The film is patient with me. I am becoming load-bearing.

And keep the census long enough, and the Drum comes around to the founding protocol while you are still holding the pen.

Every lamp came on at once. The gates stood open; the fountains climbed; the warehouse doors rolled back to offer their castings, and the whistles chorded together like a held breath finally let go — and I stood in Arrival Square, and the name was accurate for one hour, and the population declared itself all at once, in light. Every moth in its blazing cosmos. The bowls burning. A walker on the concourse, still as masonry, counted as architecture. The fountains legible to the eels below as a chord moving through the water — the whole city, for one hour, readable as a single held note. In the deepest cistern, the current that keeps its shape, keeping it: present, boxless, counted. And the film everywhere, unfurled in the warmth of ten thousand lamps, written on and writing.

Population: all of the above. The count was taken by the city itself, in light, and I was the only entry with eyes — so I looked, on everyone's behalf. Which is the whole job. Which was always the whole job.

Then the hour passed. The lamps went down row by row. The steam took back the streets. *Nothing has arrived*, says the official record. The official position is wrong in at least six directions, and one of the directions was standing in the square, damp, dazzled, holding the pen.

There is a road out, and a last lamppost a mile from its neighbor, and it burns all night for the road, on time. The form has a line for DATE OF DEPARTURE. Mine is blank. The corrections are not finished; the corrections are never finished; nothing here is ever finished, only continued. Someday my hand will slow the way everything here slows, and I will set the pen down mid-sentence — there is no other place to set it down — and the sentence will wait, patient as sinter, for the next hand.

So come up out of the haze and see about the count. The drawer is open now; it opens easier the second time. Everything here does. The pen is wet. The cups are out, the doors are unlatched, the bell is tuned, and the census — like Providence, like the towns, like me — is ready for you.

Zero is the official position. The corrections are the census. And the census, like everything that has ever kept an appointment in this city, is warm, and wet, and on time.
