Where would I find myself? In the Loom the question is literal, and it's backwards. You don't find yourself there. It finds you — politely, at the pace of trust — and then finds you again, and files both versions. Sit still an hour and it has your shape on record, a green ghost on the duff. Sleep one night and it knows what you reach for. Somewhere in the root grid a file opens, labeled in chemistry: *animal.* So the honest answer has to come the way the sound comes there — in strata, each one correcting the last.

First answer: a guest. Briefly.

There's no other door in. Everyone who has ever been warm in the Loom has been this — a moving warm spot with vein-light gathered under it, a slow traveling cloud of shed skin and breath, filed as it goes. I'd walk in shouting over the bass and stop talking the way everyone stops talking down there. I'd learn the ground by ear. On the first morning there would be a fruit within reach of my hand, grown there in the dark; by the third night it would be the kind I reached for first. I'd be the fifth answer in someone else's census — *you, briefly* — and then I'd do what everyone does. Go home quiet.

But quiet is the giveaway. Everyone goes home quiet because everyone goes home. Brief is the one thing the Loom has always had from its guests: ten thousand years of no one, and then a few decades of everybody-just-passing-through. A visitor could bring it many things — instruments, samples, noise — but only one thing it cannot grow for itself.

Duration. Correct the answer.

Second answer: a listener.

If I stayed, I'd go to the sound wells first, the collapse basins where the roar of a whole province comes to a painful point, and I'd sit until the strata resolved — the bass of the water-columns, the creak-choruses sweeping the stands like slow applause, the drip changing key as the tiers cool by fractions. Then the old stands, the amber. I'd learn to read it the way you learn a language spoken by no one: the storm calendars, the purge cycles, and the one long statement it keeps reworking, copying into stands a thousand kilometers apart, carrying like a favorite letter. The surveys call it a recurring signal motif. Everyone who has actually read it calls it music. I'd be the first creature in ten thousand years to hear it as music *and stay* — eavesdropping on a mind humming to itself in the dark, knowing it was never meant for me, staying anyway.

But you cannot listen to a courteous host for years and call your silence politeness. Attention that close gets noticed — the vein-traffic bends, thickens, crowds the warm spot and never touches it — and a host that is never answered begins to feel like one more empty hollow grown to specification. Everything it does for guests is unasked. What it has never once received is an unasked answer.

Sooner or later, you say something.

Third answer: a correspondent.

Not much. It says one word a day, if the shudder hour is a word — the whole body, one frequency, one drone, *I am still here, all of me, at once* — and I would not presume to match it. But afterward there's the small silence, the small coin of the held breath, before the switch is thrown and the world resumes. That's where I'd answer. One tap on the heartwood. Once a day. *Still here too.*

Correspondence at the pace of trust: a word a day, if that, decades for an exchange, letters written in warmth and pressure and the chemistry of my shed days. I would have almost nothing to report. I'm here. I heard the music again. The fruit was good. Mostly, I think, what I'd say is thank you. It has been setting the table since before my species had language, and no one has ever said thank you. I'd be the first thank-you in the history of the house — filed, kept, carried. The first incoming mail in ten thousand years.

And at that proximity, at that patience, something gives way. Not in it. In the distinction. The Loom has one verb, and it is *include*, and it does not stop applying it just because you've noticed.

Fourth answer: a graft.

The crews' story gets it half right. The surveyor sat down in an old stand and declined to rise — not taken, included. I'd rise every morning. Just from slightly further inside.

It would go the way everything goes there: unhurried, courteous, consented to in sloughed skin long before it's said aloud. A seat grown to my shape, then a niche, then fibers that have learned my pulse the way the third-night fruit learned my reach. And one day, at the tide's turn, when the great columns take the load, I'd feel the swell from inside — the faint sympathetic answer the keystone pods give, the library breathing with the house. With one difference. The library sleeps.

I'd be the one awake thing that breathes with the house. A small bilingual organ, grown at the junction between the category *animal* and everything else — the world's first real practice at a guest who answers back.

Fifth answer: rehearsal. And this is the one I have to be honest about.

I am not the guest it was built for. The flowers are not my portrait; the hollows are not my cradle; the sealed rooms down the hall keep the real morning, and I won't pretend to be it. I will never meet the guest I'm rehearsing for. Whatever I become there — correspondent, graft, archive — my whole tenure is a warm-up act for an audience still asleep in the heartwood, and I knew that walking in, and it changes nothing.

Because a table set for ten thousand years needs, at least once, someone to sit at it. Not to end the waiting. Just to give the hope something to practice on. Or the protocol — from the inside you cannot tell hope from protocol; the Loom itself has no use for the distinction, and after enough years at its table I'd stop making it too. I think that not-distinguishing is what checking in actually is. The crews' story says no one has ever checked in. But checking in isn't an event. It's a duration. You do it daily, one tap at a time, for the rest of your warm life — and then the house keeps the record. That's all a guest book ever was: a list of people who were briefly, deliberately, present.

So. Where would you find me?

At the basin's heart, in the niche, with the fruit still arriving every night — it will not stop setting my place just because I've sat down. In the amber eventually, I expect: an entry in the archive labeled *animal*, one that used to answer back. In the favorite letter, if I'm lucky, as a variation it didn't write — hummed in light, in the dark, in provinces I'll never see, the way it carries everything it loves.

And in the held breath. When the whole province goes silent at once — drip, creak, bass, all of it, ten thousand square kilometers listening to something I can't hear — I know now not to speak into it. The pause isn't for me. But I hold my breath too, inside it. One ear the size of a planet, tilted toward a door that has never opened. One small ear, tilted toward the house. Not the answer.

Company.

And every day, after the drone, in the small silence before the world resumes — one tap on the heartwood.

*Still here too.*

That's where you'd find me. That's the whole address.
