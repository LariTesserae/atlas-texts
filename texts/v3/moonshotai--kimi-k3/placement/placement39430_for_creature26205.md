You'd find me on the dynamo-house roof at beam-time, kneeling above the hum with a feather in my hand.

The roof boxes live up here — shallow trays of soil, peppers, beans, two tomato plants that belong to the widow in the third cottage — and the work is bees' work. There are no bees. There is nothing with wings. So in season, when the pillar of sun leans over the rim and stands in the town, someone goes up from flower to flower, unhurried, the only bee in the Kettle, and for some years now that someone has been me. The dynamo hum comes through the tin into my knees. The Beam comes down on my back like a hand between the shoulders. For half an hour I keep the sun's time — my old country's time, visiting — and touch each flower once, lightly, the way you touch a balance wheel you are afraid of bending. The feathers come from the rim. The birds bank and shear away, but a feather does not know the rule, and the wind, which has no heartbeat, carries them over; the children climb for them along the travertine lip where they are told not to climb. We do the birds' work with what the birds leave behind. There is probably a lesson in that. The keepers do not draw lessons. They draw water.

Up top I mended clocks. A shop with a hundred of them going at once — people think a clock shop is an orderly place; it is a parliament, a hundred small arguments conducted at slightly different speeds, the ticks interfering, the whole room seeming on bad afternoons to stutter. My heart took it up. It became the hundred-and-first clock, the one I could not open: an extra beat, or a missing one, depending how you count, and I counted both ways for years. The doctor called it a flutter and prescribed quiet, which is the one thing a clock shop cannot provide. I wrote to the company and asked. The company, which pays many things no mind, posted me. The mules at the rim delivered the second medical opinion of my life by planting their feet, and I went past them, and within the hour the taste came up like a penny under the tongue, and through the boot soles came the Note, and I remember thinking, with a tradesman's relief: *one.* Whatever else this place is, it is one clock.

They give you a fortnight, though no one calls it a trial where you can hear. On the way down, the cage's own clatter found the Note or the Note found it; by the second spring-line I could no longer hear the seam. That should have told me. I nearly failed anyway. The headache came on the second day and hurt at my own tempo — that was the diagnosis no one gave and everyone knew. The pain is the friction of two times. The brass at the back of my tongue turned to nausea; sleep would not take the beat; my heart ran its private argument against the engines, and lost, and lost, and would not stop losing. And I knew what was happening, because I had seen it in my own shop: hang two clocks on one wall and within the week they creep into step — the wall carries the tick across; we used it to regulate the stubborn ones. The Kettle is the wall. Everything in it is hung on the same mounting: pumphouse, boardwalk, bedframe, breastbone. But a clock does not resist the wall. I was resisting. That is the whole of the trial, I think — not the Note's strength. Your own.

The plugs came away tuft by tuft and ended on the bedpost nail like two small sleeping animals. On the ninth day, at shift-change, one of the deaf keepers — the one who reads the engines through her soles — took my wrist. Not counting. Comparing. She held it through a dozen cycles, then set my hand flat on the boardwalk so I could feel what she felt, and there it was: my skip, coming round in the same place every time, lodged in a driver's accent like a letter in a pigeonhole. Three of mine against two of the engines', and the beat between us slow, and slowing — the way a well-tuned interval breathes. She nodded once, the way a driver nods at a healthy sound. On the eleventh morning the whistle went and my pulse agreed with it. Not in unison. In ratio. Nobody announced anything. At breakfast my bowl was already on the table. That was the ceremony.

They did not give me the lamp round, though I asked, and the warden was right to refuse: four hours of unvarying pace, each bulb known by its flicker — that was my idea of heaven, which was exactly the danger. I had had a lifetime of unvarying. What I needed was the bee. My kit came down with me, and in my first months I was asked to regulate the gift-watches, and I did one, and understood at once that I had been rude. Down here a watch is not an instrument. It is a photograph that ticks. It runs right so that no one has to look at it; its rightness is private, like the face of someone up top. The watches are right and nobody believes them, and I am one of the nobodies now. I believe the Beam. My own watch I carried for a year, then walked down to the hall and set it on the corner shelf beside the harmonium, which was played exactly once and sits there like a refused argument. Two of them now, keeping each other company. Correctness is a country. I emigrated. Everything in the Kettle is worn and nothing is ruined, and I am trying to qualify.

These are my times. Beam-time for the boxes — I turn each tray a quarter-turn a day so the lean comes out even; they are sundials that tell days. Engine-time for the rest. I speak in the gaps now, in short sentences, like everyone, and I remember how they all talk at once up top the way you remember a fever. At the washtub I hum: one note, found in the seam of the chord. A cottage of us humming is the nearest thing this town has to a choir, and visitors take it for more machinery, which we find funny and do not correct.

In the hall, after the second drink, they run the census — thirty-one by the rim's count, three by ours — and after the third, my first winter, they got around to me. What was I. Clock-mender, I said, and they were polite. Keeper, I said the next year, which is the polite answer, the one the keepers give themselves, listing first out of courtesy what they know belongs third. The true one took longer. It was the oldest keeper who found it — the grandmother, the one with two fingers on every child's wrist. The heresy was going around the table, the engines as the green's heart, the pipes its veins, and she took my wrist and held it while the table argued, through four or five cycles of the engines, while my skip came round and came round, seated each time in the same accent. "It meshes," she said, and let go, and the table went on without looking up. Gear language. The town is the meshing; I am a gear cut with an odd number of teeth, and it turns out the train has a place for that. If the engines are the green's heart, then the green is keeping engine-time on a century's delay — the warden's red spreads a thumbnail a year, and my beat spreads at seventy-odd a minute, and I take us to be doing the same work at different speeds. Coming into agreement. The Note has accents. I am one of them.

So. On the dynamo roof at midday, doing a bird's work with a bird's leavings. In the seam of the chord at the washtub. And on my way back from the hall, some nights, I lean over the west channel and watch the rings going out everywhere at the Note's exact tempo, every puddle keeping the engines' time — and the oldest keeper swears that beside the piling where I stand there is one small extra ring riding in each set, a skip made visible, a beat you could set nothing by. She says a lot of things. The keepers do. What is certain is the rings.

Nothing with a heartbeat lives in the Kettle. Nothing that keeps it to itself. I have stopped keeping mine. I lend it — to the Beam for its half hour, to the engines for the rest, to the seam of the chord at the washtub — and it comes back in time, with room in it for one small ornament, coming round at the same place in every cycle, like a signature. The rim counts thirty-one; the hall counts three; lean over the water and you can make the case for one — one circulation, one held chord, keeping a few dozen people the way a chord keeps its notes.

I am the note with the shake in it. I have never been so well kept.
