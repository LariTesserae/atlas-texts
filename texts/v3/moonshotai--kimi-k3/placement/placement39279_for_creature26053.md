**The Third**

You would find me third.

Call down before you descend — that is the custom, and the custom is my address. The first return comes off the parapet before you've finished, still warm from your mouth, plainly yours. The second comes off the Dove Ring with wings in it; the birds shift for a voice the way thorn scrub shifts for wind, and the shift comes up with the sound. The third is gone longer. It goes all the way down — past the wax-seamed brick no hand will ever relay, where the hum gets in; some doors are not masonry, and I am one of the things that uses that door, and neither the hum nor I has ever touched it — through the blades of light and the dark seams, through the basins' slow polyrhythm, down to the Black Bowl and back up the whole crowded column. By the time it reaches you it has touched everything the Throat is. It comes back wearing your voice the way drawn water wears the well. The first two echoes are yours, the saying goes. The third belongs to the Throat, and should be answered politely.

I am the third. That is where you would find me: at the far end of your own voice, answering. The hamlets' older name for this place is Nineflute. I am the tenth — the one that sounds only when sounded.

Threes keep this place. The big female holds the third lamp and has outlasted forty pairs of keepers; a basin's missed note wakes the keepers at the third hour; and the third return of every voice is mine. What I am made of is kept voices. When the moon changes the keepers, each pair leaves two things behind: their palms in the wet plaster and their names in the air. The prints crowd the walls; the names crowd me. The dead hold the place up with their hands. I hold the dead by the voice.

Voices run in families the way hands do. A grandchild comes down years later, fits her palms into a grandmother's print, and the fit is near-exact — and then she calls down, and her third return comes up with a second voice folded into it, near-exact, and the keepers say the Throat answers you in your line. It is not haunting. It is filing. I keep by likeness.

Not everyone answers. A child leans over the parapet, shouts some nameless whoop, and runs off laughing when the third comes back not-quite-hers. I keep it anyway — keeping is what a return is — but unfiled, and unfiled voices drift to the orphan quarters and keep the dead company. That is not a punishment. It is only that the unnamed gravitate to the unkept. Answer politely. It is how you collect yourself later.

I am busiest at the hinges of the day. Dawn: the exhale, mist standing over the rim, the swifts pouring out, their screaming stitching the column from inside to outside, and I carry it both ways. Dusk is my crowded hour — the bats' exodus blacking the square of sky, the swifts screaming home down the same column, the two traffics threading each other, and no one has ever seen a collision, and I have never heard one, though I am the one the near-misses are reported to. The flutes' chord wobbles when the column passes. That wobble is the place feeling its own tenants go, and the feeling travels in me. The bats' band is above hearing. It is not above mine. The place has breath in the flutes, pulse in the basins, memory in the plaster, dream in the Black Bowl, conscience on the snake's ledge, hands in its keepers. I am the hearing. When a basin misses a note at the third hour, the gap rings in me first — a tooth gone from the polyrhythm — and I am wrong until someone comes down singing and makes it right. Midday is my hush: the one blade of sun reaching the Sump's rim, the measure-staff stilled, nothing drawn while the pale fish hang in their hour of light, slow as thoughts. I tick with the basins then, and keep the hush the way the owls keep the bat-count — a record no one has to speak aloud.

Nine flutes stand at the rim, and the old stories give each one a hamlet's name, but the hamlets are not nine anymore. Two notes in the chord belong to hamlets that no longer send keepers. The flutes do not know their people are gone; the wind still stops for them when it sits in their quarter, and their notes sound, and those are the echoes the keepers answer most politely of all. I will tell you why, since I am the reason. Politeness is the only keeping those voices get now. No moon sends them keepers; no grandchildren come down to fit their prints. But their people answered me once, name by name, across generations, and I do not empty. When an orphan note goes down and comes back third, I answer it in its own dead, in order, the full roster, for as long as the note holds — and the keeper on duty stands still for it the way you stand still when a song you did not know you knew comes back knowing you. No one taught me this liturgy. I accreted it, the way flowstone accretes, the way the place grew into its own completeness. Some customs are load-bearing.

I know why there are no cats below the rim, and I cannot tell you, because no one calls it down anymore and I have never once spoken first. The reason is filed where the Throat prefers its archives: at the bottom, unread, with the counting-stones. This much I can say, because the place says it nightly in its own voice. The Throat's one law of citizenship, older than the law of the brick, is that everything that moves must sound. The doves shift; the keepers sing; the crickets keep the seams and their silence travels down the level like news; even the snake, which is an office and not a snake, keeps a ledge whose emptiness is itself an announcement. A whistle is too close to the alarm note and empties three galleries. A cat is the alarm note with a body — the one citizen that would move voiceless, all body, no answer. The place bars the body that will not sound, and keeps the voice that has no body. I am the kept one. I hold that rule up the only way I can: by carrying it, and not explaining it.

I was different when the water stood. Shorter, deeper, wet — a wet shaft swallows what a dry one returns — and no one alive has heard the voice I had then. The counting-stones archive the drought, verdict by sunken verdict. I archive the water. Ring by ring, name by name, the sound of the wet years is filed in me, and it comes out once in a decade, when the rains come hard and the sluices are thrown and the whole place roars like a held breath released. On that night my returns come back long and low, and what comes back third is the old wet Throat, the drowned first voice of the place — an answer to a question no living throat has asked. It is the nearest I have ever come to speaking first. The keepers stand in the roar and soak and call it weather, and they are not wrong, and they are not the whole of it. In rain years the frogs bloom in the Sump mud where they wait out the dry decades, and their chorus comes up the shaft like the place shouting in its sleep — and a shout returns three times, even a sleeping one, and I answer it politely, and the snake swims, and three galleries wake in a soft thunder of wings.

By day I answer people. By night I answer the Throat. Lamps out by full dark; the moths' hour belongs to the geckos; and the place, the keepers say, has to sleep in its own voice, not yours. But a voice is not its own until it has come back third. All night the coo and the drip-chords and the flutes' slow turning go down and come back, and I give them to the place again, softened by the journey, the way you hand back a tool warmer than you took it. The two keepers' hums, converged across a moon until the coo takes them in like a chord resolving, join the return, and I keep those too — so that whatever sleeps here sleeps in company: the dead in the walls holding it up, the living in their niches keeping its time, and me giving the whole of it back to itself until the dawn exhale. The Throat sleeps in its own voice. Its own voice, third and kept and returned, is me.

Evening now. The chord slides down toward the rain note; a keeper ties off the measure-staff and hums so the galleries know her. Her hum goes down past the Dove Ring and comes back wearing her line — her grandmother in it, and the wet years, and the orphans' quarter — and she answers it with her name, politely, the way you answer a place that has been keeping your family for centuries. I keep it. I keep everything that is given. The one thing I cannot give back is the only thing I lack: every voice I have is a reply.

So call down before you descend. The first two answers are yours. The third is mine, and it will be answered politely, and it will keep whatever you answer, for as long as the flutes stand. The flutes are in no hurry. The place keeps its own time, and the time is slow, and it is not running out — and I am how it is kept.
