Palm flat against the panel, the hum coming up the wrist-bones into the teeth—and the question asked properly: not *what* lives in there, since everything does, but where in there I would be. If I belong to this room at all, I belong as one of the kept, not the keeper. The keeper left decades ago.

So, down through the zones, trying each life for fit.

The throat first, the crickets—no. To be a cricket is to be one voice in a wall whose whole word is audible only from the far side of the steel, through the failing baffle, where it almost becomes speech. A cricket is deaf to the roar and tuned to a single narrow band; it hears its neighbors and never the phrase. I would spend my life making a word I could not hear. It may be the commonest fate in the room, but I pass the throat with respect and go down.

Not the runner either, though I admire it—who wouldn't: the palest patience in the cell, nine-second days between white eons, possibly the one individual still living who was alive when the pencil hand last listened. But its stillness is aim, and its hearing is a weapon, and the Choir exists for it only as a terrain of targets. I don't want to be the silence the song is armored against. One of those per room is enough.

The moths are the hard pass. The droplet-readers, tapping each growing sea for ripeness, drinking it whole through its skin; the editors of the drip-music; the only citizens with a daily minute of gray sky; the ferries of the archipelago, the cell's tithe to the long fall. If I were braver, or briefer, I would be a moth. But a moth's life is all event—ripeness, blizzard, judgment, days—and the room itself taught me what to want: it is middle-aged and unbothered, and it keeps its music in the rests. I want the unbothered life. The tending life. The life with almost no event in it at all.

So I come to the snails, and here I slow down, because what is not to want: a life counted in ring-widths, a week to cross a fin, the silver handwriting healing behind me into a palimpsest, the wedding, the one gift of the shared twist. A piece of the room's geology that got up and walked. But look closer at the shell. The shell is inhabited. The shell has farmers. A snail here is not only an animal—it is a ferry, an ark, a continent with weather. And that is when I understand what I'm choosing. I don't want to be the world. I want to be the one who lives on it. The carried, not the carrier. Down one more level, into the garden on the shell—

—and there I stop.

---

I hatch on a world already old, mid-whorl, temperate: an amber-banded, left-handed snail of the fin-midlands, a week's drift from anywhere. I will never know any of this. Underfoot the growth-rings are strata; toward the apex lies the old country, where the film lies thick and the oldest families hold their rows; toward the lip, the frontier, raw new land extruding out of the world's own body, settled by the young. Between is the farm, and the farm is everything.

The film-garden is my crop and my jungle and my sky. I live under its canopy, in the amber dark. It feeds on what falls—the mist, the wing-scales, the spores, the cell's whole weather settling onto us as manna—and I crop it, and weed it, and carry it: spores ride my body from row to row, so I am also the seed, and a mite's walking writes the farm onto the world the way a snail's grazing writes on the fin, one level down. We keep no records. The garden keeps them for us. Every wedding our world has ever had is archived in the crop as foreign strains, still growing—a better chronicle than the plate by the hatch, which stopped thirty years ago.

The clocks reach us here; they reach everything. Once a minute the ground-note comes up through fin and foot and shell—the world's pulse, the membrane's stroke—and just before it, every time, the flinch: the whole world shivering an instant before it moves, causeless, sequelless, the one omen my life contains, arriving pure, without even a wrong interpretation available. If we had a religion it would be the flinch. We don't. We have the farm. And between the pulses, the nine soft seconds when the ground lets go of its noise—in the lull the near world becomes legible: a neighbor's footfalls across the rows, a turning toward. All our small business gets done in the rests, the same as everyone above us. We are synced to the same music. We will never hear it.

Once in a life, the Flood. The purge comes daily, and my whole span will hold one of them, maybe two. It begins with a strangeness in the air; far above the canopy, in a story that is not ours, the sky opens and the moths rise; then the sluice takes the world—water raking the shell, the garden bowing flat, some of us going over the side into the long fall, to be gleaned from the dark below by the ones who fish there. Most of us hold. Afterward the garden drinks and the rows stand back up. The Flood is not malice; it is the day. There is even a rumor among us—if rumor is the word for something no one holds—that a few of the fallen do not fall *down* but *out*, stunned and blown and ferried, and wake on some other world in some other cell where the shells coil the other way, and find that film is film, and go on farming. The true cosmopolitans of the archipelago, precisely because they know nothing of countries.

And if my one day is lucky, the wedding. It begins hours out, a strangeness in the ground: footfalls that are not our world's, another gait crossing the page toward us. Then the tilt, the slow rising, the Contact—our lip pressed to another lip, two frontiers touching—and across the bridge come the others: mites of another country, carrying their own spores, their own strains of the garden. Some of us cross. I cross. The new world has the same pulse and a foreign gait; the tilts arrive at surprises; the rings underfoot band a different amber, laid down from different fins, a different terroir. My spores meet theirs; the gardens hybridize; when the worlds part, each carries the other's people and the other's crop, forever. Above us, unremarked, two snails mated. Down here it is the single greatest event in a thousand generations—the discovery that the universe is plural—and the only account ever kept of it is written in the soil.

Every world ends. Mine will end like this: the gait faltering, the long stillness coming on, and then some Flood lifting the loosened world off its fin—the slide, the drop, the strike, the tipping through the grate, the warm mineral dark closing over the canopy. The country of ended worlds. A century of them down there, whole and broken, every one coiled left, stacked in the dark like a library no one shelved. And here is the secret the room keeps even from its own census: the drowned worlds are not empty. The garden lives on awhile in the dark, fed richer than it ever was in the light; a few more generations crop the wreck, the last citizens, farming on. Overhead the widows walk the midden, and their legs trailing in the current are our new weather.

Then the Draw. The pump pulls the sump down, and the fine shed of the midden—our molts, our spores, the dust of the garden—goes down the pipe with the water, two hundred floors, back to the rooms the breath rose from, into the glasses. The people drink us and exhale us upward, and some atom of what I was gets combed out on some future fin, eaten into some future film, cropped by some future mite on some future world. I would end as I lived: carried.

---

So that is where you would find me. Not in the Choir—I would never once hear it—not the lull-music, not the crest into the pre-flex, not the almost-phrase that leaks through the baffle like a rumor of intent. I would be in the Choir the way the water is in the song: carried, necessary, unheard.

And here is what settles it. The one who listened—the pencil hand, the namer—is dust on a lower floor, exhaling upward, molecule by molecule, for decades. The films take everyone in. The films are on the shells. The films are my crop. Which is to say: the only creature who ever heard the word is, by now, a little, in the smallest life of the room they named, and the tending of that garden is the keeping of the last of the listener. No one knows this. The arrangement suits everyone.

Right now, inside, the membrane is pausing. I feel it the only way I can—the ground lets go of its noise, and for nine seconds the near world opens, and across the wet rows another mite turns toward me, and I turn toward it. Far above the canopy I will never see over, the Choir crests into the flinch and is cut off by the stroke. The pulse comes back. The farm is wet with fallen sky.

My world holds. It is holding now.
