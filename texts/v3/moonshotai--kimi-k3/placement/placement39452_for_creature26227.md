**Come Down**

You used the word in the steam-house. I was two tables over with my brew, and I watched the room do what rooms here do when someone says *finished* — nothing you could point at, a leaning-away, the way mat shrinks off a hot stone. You noticed. Travelers always notice. What you didn't notice was the one person who didn't lean, because I was sitting very still, doing the arithmetic of my trade.

Finished things belong with the dead, in the quiet, below the water. That's doctrine. You can shout it in the baths and nobody will argue. What the doctrine doesn't say is who keeps the cellar they put it all in.

I keep the hush-rooms.

Nobody bellows at my door. It's the one threshold in Vesper where the street voice fails people — they arrive already half-hushed, and they knock: one long, two short, the old sign for a door that answers. It means *come down*. The knock has been my name for so many cycles that my air-name, all nine earned syllables of it, gets used mostly by gutter-masters when there's a boundary question about my cellar line. My stair comes up in a court behind the fat-renderers' row, which the town finds funny and I find correct: the quietest place in Vesper, kept underneath the trade that makes all its light.

At trim-bell the town turns out to cut itself free of the night. I go the other way, down, and cut the quiet free of the stone. Because that's the thing nobody up top understands — below the water table the accretion runs faster. Stillness feeds it the way sulfur feeds the mat. A facade in the frog-noise gains a fingernail in a season; my rooms would gain it in a wake if I let them, the doorframes creeping, the drains filming, the whole hush coming in on itself like a held thought. Nothing in this town wants finishing as badly as a quiet room. So I trim. My hand-stone is worn to a thumb-nub, copper edge re-hammered past counting, and every wake it bites the doorways back to true. I am a trimmer like your grandmother was a trimmer. My facade just faces inward.

Every house keeps a growing wall. I keep a growing clock: one drip I've never once sealed, feeding a pale thumb of stone on the cellar floor. Spans are bell-spans, but that thumb is my ledger and my genealogy. The drain-crabs are old colleagues — pale, blind, unhurried. A house with fat crabs is fortunate. Mine are fat.

I keep no hearth-frog. People say a house without a pulse. I tell them: down here you can hear the pulse fine. It turns out to be yours. That's the entire inventory of the hush, and it is why they pay. The rooms come with a second luxury nobody prices — the dark. Lamp or no lamp, the renter's choice. The ones who hand the lamp back are the brave ones, or the tired ones, or the ones from the fat streets who have never once in their lives been allowed to stop shining.

I tell every first-timer the same thing. Sit. Breathe. When you hear your heart knock, don't answer it. Everything up top is call and answer — frog and frog, horn and horn, wall and wall. The hush is the one room in Vesper where you're allowed to just be called.

You want to know who comes down. Everyone comes down, eventually. A gutter-master takes the long room before every ruling — one span, no lamp — and comes up slower than she went down, and her rulings are the ones people tap to each other through the walls at night, which is the only immortality a law gets here. A horn-caller with a cracked lung, forbidden the platforms, rents a span some wakes just to be somewhere his quiet is legal. An old woman lies on the slab with her hands folded and practices. She says she wants the stone to know her when it comes. I say the stone knows everyone. She says that's not the same as an introduction. Two people who are growing walls with other people take the narrow room sometimes. I sweep after everyone the same.

I ration the hush-drunk — one span a wake, no more. The respectable ones end up across the cellar with the archives, where a body can be drunk on the finished and get paid for it; the town calls that a trade, which is its way of forgiving them. Here's something you can't learn up top: down here, in the quiet, you can say a long name all the way to its end. The roar taxes every living name past the third syllable. The dead get their full names spoken entire only below the water, by archive-keepers chanting to the stacks. Nobody has ever thanked me for that. Everybody knows it.

A grandmother brought a boy down the wake before his hand-stone — folk practice, not doctrine, but half the quarter does it now: let him hear his own thoughts before they give him the tool. He lasted six breaths. Then he laughed, and I heard a laugh with nothing in front of it for the first time in cycles. In the street a laugh is a coat worn against weather. Down here it was a small animal let out of the hand. He asked for another span. He's going to be trouble or a keeper, and I've stopped believing there's a difference.

A young woman came to me to practice leaving. She'd taken a place on a coracle crew, three towns Darkward, a quieter life — you know what the town calls that. First span, she was back up the stair in ten breaths, white as fresh sinter. So we built. A quarter-span. A half. The wake she finally slept — slept, in silence, which no Vesperian can do abroad, which is the thing that breaks them in the canal towns — I sat on the stair above her door and grieved, because teaching someone to leave is the nearest my trade comes to finishing something. The town will say she went quiet. She didn't. She went ready.

You were here, I think, the wake the chorus failed in the Spill. You'd have seen it from above: everyone out of the houses, palms flat to the stone, the horn-callers leaning off the platforms into the fog — *are you still singing* — and the children brought out to hear the absence, to feel the size of the debt. I stood with the rest. And here is what I know that they don't, up top. It was not the same silence. Theirs was a mouth held shut. Mine is an open one. In my rooms the quiet is a sound — you hear your blood turn, you hear your heart knock like somebody asking to come in — the hush is full. What frightened that district wasn't silence. It was the town listening for itself and getting no answer. The chorus came back, you remember. Lamps doubled, the Answering, a wake of festival. My rooms were booked solid for a cycle after — everyone touching the quiet the way you touch a bruise, checking it's still there.

Now I'll tell you the thing I don't tell, and you'll understand why it has to be a keeper who tells it.

He was one of the Smooth — I say it only down here. He rented the long room a span at a time for more cycles than I'll count, and everyone assumed glaze-work, and once I swept shard-grit from his threshold and said nothing, because the rooms are not for doctrine. The wake his span ran long, I knocked, and waited the full length of my own air-name, and knocked again with the chisel-butt, and lifted the bar. He was on the slab, cooled, hand-stone in his fist, and on his chest a palm-stone rivered rust and bone-white, polished to a shine that takes a lamp and keeps it. Finished. He'd come down to end among the only finished things in Vesper, and I won't pretend it shocked me.

His family took him up to the seep-gardens, as they should. The baths argued a full cycle whether dying below the water counts as taking the stone or cheating it, and did not resolve it, which is correct. The family wanted no finished thing in the house. So I kept the stone.

You flinched at the word, up top. Hold a finished thing some night, alone, and you'll understand the Smooth better than doctrine allows: it isn't death they love. It's rest. The stone in my pocket does not grow. Some wakes that is exactly what I need to hold.

Once a cycle I take the long room myself. A keeper has to know the dosage he pours. I sit and I listen upward — through the water table the roar comes down thinned to a hum, steady, no event in it, and I think: this is what the stone-kin hear. The town talking to its dead through stone, all wake, every wake, a voice with the words worn off, only presence left. Sometimes I hum with it. One note. No knock-code, no message, nothing anyone could answer. It's the closest I come to the long song. Nobody will take it up when my wake ends, and that's fine. It isn't a song. It's a seam.

My left ear is going the way ears go here — worn smooth, like everything the town touches long enough. When it's gone I'll be quiet-ear, and the quiet-ear read walls, and a keeper who can read the rooms through stone is a keeper nobody will pay. So the door will pass. The boy — the one who laughed — lengthens his spans wake by wake. Last cycle he came up after a full bell with his eyes wide and, for the first time in his life, his voice low. The voice will come back; the quiet takes nothing. That's what nobody believes until they learn it. It gives. What it gives back is you, and some people can't carry that, and some can. He can. When I take the stone, the knock will be his name. One long, two short. *Come down.*

You'll go back up the belt now, and tell the canal towns that Vesper is the loudest place in the world, and you'll be right, and you'll be missing it. Somebody has to stand where the sound ends and keep the door from closing after it. Somebody has to trim the hush.

They'll ask how I am, when they come up blinking into the lamplight. Up top they say *still rough*. Down here we turn it once, like a stone in the hand.

Still open.
