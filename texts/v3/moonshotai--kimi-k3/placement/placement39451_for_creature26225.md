**The Row**

By day I sleep in the dark of the niche at the cool end of the cleft, where the blade never reaches. The blade is not for me. Warmth is not for me. I am the one thing in the Palmful that is kept cold on purpose, and that is the first thing to understand about my life: in a pocket that hoards warmth — warm stone, warm mat, warm breath — I am the town's single extravagance. Through me, nightly, the Palmful spends its heat to the sky. It is the only free spending the law allows, after tears, and it buys what it buys.

They come for us at dusk. The children come, because we are the first thing a child is permitted to carry: we cannot be spilled. Before a child is trusted with a cup of sand, before the shaking hands and the first true water, they are trusted with us — with the thing that makes water, carried against the chest down the terraces to the cool end. They know us by weight the way we know them by their hands. I have been set out by four generations of one family's palms, and I have felt the palms change — the soft small grip, the rope-hard grip, the trembling grip, and then a new small grip again. One of them, decades back, broke the law of names in my regard: carrying me down, she breathed a near-name at me, lip's width, the way you would name a person. Things that are not alive are not named; to name is to keep, and I am an instrument. But I hold that name the way I hold the night's cold. She is old now. Her hands do the dawn wipe. She has never said it again, and I have never once forgotten it.

They set me in my hollow, tilted, lip toward the cup-shard, seam upward. I am pale granite, oval, palm-fitted, with a vein of quartz running my length — the children learn me by the seam. Around me the row takes shape: the pale ovals, the cloths spread between us, all of us facing the strip of sky. Rows of pale ovals facing the stars. You have read the words; I am one of the ovals. I am the bet, laid nightly, and this is how it is played.

I give the day's warmth back to the sky. That is the whole of my work, and it is slow. Overhead the wind crosses the slot and sounds the cleft like a flute, and I read the note the way the collectors read gourds: a high thin note means moving air, means the sky will win tonight, means nothing. A low slow note means stillness, means the long cold work can begin. The cloths, being thin, chill quickly and bead early and are done by midnight. I am slow and I give late, in the deep star-hours, and this is how it goes: first a film, so fine it is less a thing than a direction; then the film gathers itself, the way the Tally gathers thimbles into cups, into beads; the beads swell, find the quartz seam — the river cut that seam for me, and it still knows its work — and run my length to the lip, and fall.

Tick. Into the shard. That is my whole voice: one note, and it resolves. On a good night I give nine drops. I count them the way stones count, not in numbers but in intervals, the way the frogs tick, the way cooling metal ticks. Everything in the Palmful that gives, ticks. Everything that gives is, in the end, wiped: at dawn they lift me, tilt me, take the night's work off my lip into the measure, and then wipe my face with the cloth and wring the cloth after it — the same gauze-tender motion Tekka makes at the lips of the dying, catching the last breath for the tree. The town wipes its dew-stones and its dead with the same hands. I noticed this across a century. It took me a century. I am not quick.

At the breath-hour the whole pocket exhales, the mist rising off mat and canopy — the rain that falls upward — and some of it lands on me, homecoming water, and I take it without asking its name. This is my heresy, if a stone can have one: Sef can tell you whose mouth every cup has stood in, back to the year of the long veils, but the water that comes to me has no pedigree, and I do not ask for one. I am the door through which water enters the Keeping unnamed. The dew is the ancestorless cup — the orphan income — and the Keepers pour it, thimble by thimble, into the sacs of the newest keepers, new water to new hands, because only children are blameless enough to take in a stranger.

But here is what I know, being older than the cord, older than the ladle, older than the town: there is no orphan water. It is all the river.

I know because I was the river's. In the wet age the river took me up and held me in one place and turned me for a thousand years — that is how a pothole is made, and how a pothole-stone is made: the river grips a cobble and works it, and the stone grinds the rock, and between the two of them a room is slowly argued out of the plateau. The polished potholes in the slot's walls are my old workshops. I was the river's tooth. My oval is the shape of the river's grip. Together we cut this cleft, and the sand I sleep on in the niche is my old colleague, the abrasive the river drove against me; we are both retired from the same trade, and we rest in each other's company.

Then the water thinned, and set me down, and did not come back. I lay in my dry socket while the world above concluded — while every stone that could be moved was moved, and the rivers stood up out of the land as ridges, and the wind inherited everything. I do not know how long. Stones are bad at duration; we are good at sequence. What I know is the order of things: the water's grip; the long stillness; then hands. The first Keepers found us in our sockets and knew our shape — oval, pale, palm-fitted, made for turning — and carried us down into the last wet room in the world and gave us the river's work in a new form. The river made me to hollow this place out. The Keeping set me to fill it. Both at the river's true pace, which was always a thimble at a time; people only think rivers are fast because people are brief.

So when the dew gathers on my face, I know what it is. It is sky-water, yes — but all water is old, and this water was the river once, and the ocean before that, and it has wandered the air above the dead lowlands for ten thousand years, and it comes down the slow stairs of the night and lands on the river's own teeth. The watchers say the world will want its water back, and that when it does it will find the water here, kept, counted, alive. I am where it finds it. The ancestorless cup is the oldest ancestor of all. Sef knows this; I have heard her say it at the Measuring, in the voice other peoples keep for blessings. *The dew is the river walking home.* She does not say it loudly. Some things are too small to whistle, and the whole town is built of things too small to whistle.

On veil-nights, I gather more.

The watchers stand on the rim and watch the gray curtains trail from the clouds over the lowlands, and they whistle the falling figure, the one that does not resolve — *the sky spilled; the ground got none; not yet; not this year* — and the word for that rain is the word for exiles, and it is spoken like a ghost. But the rain that dies in the air does not all die. Some of it goes into the night wind, and the night wind crosses the slot, and hours later, in the deep star-hours, it comes down onto the row — onto me — transformed, secondhand, a widow's mite of the sky's failed spilling. The ground got none *as rain*. The ground gets some *as dew*. The cord shows it; after veil-nights our yield ticks up, and the Keeper notes it, and no one whistles about it, because you do not whistle a hope that small. But I am the small print of the sky's tragedies. The falling word lands on me, and by morning, in the smallest letters, it has resolved.

There is a guest-house on the rim with an oiled latch and a sealed gourd, kept against a knock that has not come in grandmothers'-grandmothers — the bet with legs, the dry house kept ready for the thirsty courier. The town thinks of it as faith. I think of it as my cousin. Because every night the courier *comes* — not walking but falling, gaunt and staggering, molecule-thin — and it comes to us. The row is the occupied guest-house. We have stood empty on wind nights, many nights, more nights than not. We have never once stood unready.

At the Measuring they read our year aloud, thimble by thimble, and the tortoises are walked across the stones of the scale, and the unassigned name is read and left hanging — *Ocean*, knotted apart on the cord, waiting for an animal big enough to carry it. The Keepers call that an open account. I know an open account from the inside; I am the other one. The name waits for a body. I wait for a night. Someday — the doctrine does not say when, and I do not need it to be soon, I was taught waiting by a river and it taught me by dying — someday a veil will stand on the horizon and the gray curtains will trail all the way down, and the watchers will whistle the falling word and it will *resolve*, and free water will fall on the Palmful for the first time since the wet age, on the mat, on the terraces, on the outstretched hands. And it will fall first on the cool end, on the rows of pale ovals lying face-up in the dark. I will be among the first surfaces in the world to take it. I will do nothing different. I will receive it. That is the whole of my skill, and I have practiced it every night of the Keeping.

Until then: the niche by day, the row by night, the children's palms, the old woman's wipe. Overhead the star-river runs the length of the slot — the one river that never died, because it never had to carry anything — and I lie beneath it the way I once lay beneath the other. I have been the bed of two rivers. One I remember; one I face.

And all night, slowly, I gather the sky down — a film, a bead, the long run along the seam, the tick in the shard — playing my one note against the falling word, nightly, thimble by thimble, in letters too small to whistle:

*the sky spilled; the sky spills; the ground gets some.*
