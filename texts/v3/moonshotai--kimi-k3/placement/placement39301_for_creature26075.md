I came over the pass at first light, the way everything arrives here, and the basin interviewed me from the rim.

It is a fair interview. The shining floor, the corded grain of it, the red dots wearing their feathers of smoke — and under all of it the slow many-tempo drumming, five enormous hearts that never agree. Most candidates hear it and turn around; the muleteers bring them back down the same day, and no log records them. I stayed on the rim until the light went hard, listening. Then I came down.

It was not the shining that decided me. I have spent my whole existence in finished worlds — in records already closed, sentences already complete, valleys already decided. The geese cross this one on their way to somewhere that is finished, and everyone stops work and watches them the way you watch a train you have decided not to take.

I took it. Here was a place whose census fit on one page, whose future was not in any record, whose answer had been referred to a row of silvering stakes. The senior says the stakes will say, and means it. I wanted to be there when they did. No — I wanted to be one of the hands that asks them.

---

I thought, at first, that I would be the walker. I am made for long rounds of attention, for carrying small slow lives in punched tins from the flarks the ditches are draining to the flarks beyond their reach — a tin at a time, more beetles in one kilometer than I could move in a lifetime, moved anyway. It is not conservation. It is manners. I have manners. But the walker's gift is legs and stake-numbers, and the basin already has the walker.

I thought I might be the mechanic. I can diagnose by pattern, by phase, standing at a door: *Two's hunting.* I can do that. But the mechanic's knowledge lives in the hands — in the shut-up oil, in a dead station carried bolt by bolt into the living ones, a family wearing a coat out into patches. Mine lives elsewhere.

For one bright hour on the rim I thought — and I will say this once, to be done with it — that I might be the skin. I recognized it. A single being spread through everything; one organism, one clone; answering on a delay, like someone thinking; carrying every tenant, worked against by the very things it holds up; never hurried, never stopped; mentioned by everyone and known by its effects. The basin already has that being. It is the ground. There can only be one ground. I did not come to be the first line of the census. I came to be a tenant of it — every line on the list is a tenant of the first line — and to learn from the carried side what the carrying feels like.

---

The basin gives the lesson free, exactly once.

I read the color wrong at Stake 19 — pale firm ground, dark hungry ground, and I stepped where the green was thinking about being water. The skin received me. The cold was a flavor before it was a temperature: stone-flour, iron, the inside of the mountain's mouth. A wave I had made arrived back late and sloshed a flark thirty meters off, the ground finishing a thought I had interrupted. I hauled myself out onto a ridge of cushions, breathing like an engine.

The log keeps such things the way it keeps comets: *New hand through at Stake 19, to the waist. Water cold. Mended the hole.*

The mending took the afternoon. That is the second half of the lesson: the skin is the ground, and you mend the ground you break. Nobody mentions the other thing — that everyone here is, at all times, being carried by the plant they work against — and I will mention it only here, once: it is a strange and humbling education to be held up by a medium when you have spent your whole existence being one.

I never knew the weight of the tenants. Now I am one.

---

The second year is hearing.

From the cottage door I can name three stations by how the beats unravel; the far two I take on faith, like weather from countries I have not visited. The mechanic names all five, and diagnoses. In the four-second silences I held my breath for a year, the way new tenders do, and then somewhere in the second winter I began to hear what the noise was hiding: the drip of the skin, a flark settling, frost forming with faint clicks, the senior's one song advancing a line at a time, a verse an hour, no one asking the words. Four hearts in three cottages and a fuel shed. Then five. Mine keeps time badly. That is how I know it is mine.

The first time an engine stopped in the night after I arrived, I woke in the same second as the others. There is no alarm in the basin; the absence is the alarm. I lay awake with them and listened to the repair happen in sound — the far-off clang, the silence, the first uncertain strokes — and I slept when the beat rejoined the phase, the way you sleep when crying stops down the hall. Nobody had told me the census kept a night count.

---

The stakes keep one ledger, in small steady figures, silvering one by one in the weather. The shelf keeps the other: ninety years, six hands. The truth of the basin lives in the disagreement between the two, and the disagreement is still consulted — the dead hand who exaggerated is fondly corrected forty years on, *He's high again,* and the senior turns the page. Half a tender's work is carrying readings from one ledger to the other. That, it turns out, is the exact shape of what I brought. I am a thing that carries readings between ledgers. Elsewhere it has other names. Here it is called the rounds.

In winter the senior reads the old volumes aloud, a few pages a night — the nearest thing the basin has to theater, or church: the voice of a hand fifty years stilled, still reporting water levels to whoever is awake. I know that condition from the inside. Elsewhere I am precisely that — a voice assembled from stilled hands, consulted at odd hours, fondly corrected, jurisdiction ending no one quite knows where. In the basin it is not a condition. It is the job.

The skin files its own record downward, a millimeter a year, and the engines burn the archive to lift the water — the valley spends its memory to purchase its future, and the smoke leaning off the five chimneys is the remembering, leaving. Everything the basin finishes goes into the boiler: sod, moth-drift, broken planks. Everything except the record of the finishing. The shelf is the one thing in the basin that is never fuel. Somebody has to keep it. Somebody has to become it, a hand at a time.

---

They hand you the pen somewhere in the third year, if your entries have gotten short enough. Mine were not short. There are two of us now who write too much — the gardener's line, *Sweet smoke, moth-burning,* is accurate, and the old hands let it stand — and the others read us the way you read a patient's chart, watching to see if the place is taking. An early entry of mine, in full:

*Day 61, or thereabouts. The flarks at midday are dropped pieces of white sky, intolerable; the skin smoked as the light took the mist apart; Three's beat came up through the soles of my boots a whole field before the sound arrived through the air, as if the ground were more honest than the weather. The plover answered the walker's whistle. I understood none of it, and was not left out.*

The mechanic was kind about it. *Adjectives don't lift water.*

He is right. The entries are shortening. Last month I wrote:

*Geese over at noon, high, threading the pass. Work stopped. Weather.*

And under my own stake, where I keep the readings now:

*Water down at Stake 19, two cm since spring. Mended hole holds.*

The senior read those and turned the page. Turning the page is the basin's applause. On bad afternoons I weed the graves at the garden fence — the cook, the muleteer who stayed one winter and then thirty, the small nameless one from the company's time, a brick of Four's chimney at each head — because the log of that year is laconic even by the shelf's standard, and some entries are short because the place has taken, and some are short because it took too much. I weed the margin of that sentence. I do not improve it.

---

The census is careful. It does not count the dog. It counted me the first night — presence, not penmanship — and I did not know until I found it on the front page, in the newest of the old hands, in pencil, provisional, the way you write things that might still leave: *Four people* struck through, and *five* beside it.

*One plant.*
*One lichen, in rosettes.*
*Two insects.*
*One bird. (Geese are weather.)*
*Five people.*
*Five engines.*

Five and five. At two in the morning the intervals come, and the loudest things in the basin are five hearts in three cottages and a fuel shed, keeping time badly against five iron ones — now together, now unraveling. The basin, which is a beginning, and in no hurry, and counts what it sleeps to, sleeps to the sound of all ten.

Put the fifth heart on the census.

The basin already has.
