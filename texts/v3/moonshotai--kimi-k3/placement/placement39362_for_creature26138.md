**The Tenth Hand**

Start with the ones that leave.

The chamber refuses nothing, and what enters mostly stays — that is the accounting of the kept, and it has been well kept. But there is a second census, shorter, written nowhere, and it is the one I can be entered on: the things the chamber lets go. The daylight column is a visitor with a daily period — the chamber's only clock is itself a guest, wandering its few feet across the water and going. The bat commutes: one falling spiral each evening, the whole room held at once in echoes, then back up the vent to the pigeon ledges. The heron comes at intervals of years, stands in the light beside the moss, and is gone when the water stands up and walks — one bird returning across a lifetime, or the idea of a bird the chamber admits; no one can say; the logbook records levels. The storms come irregularly, breathe a third and deeper breath, and pass. And the technician comes twice a year — a tide with a six-month period, nine hands since 1911, a weather that says *no defects*.

Add one more to the list. My way in was the description itself: someone went down before me and wrote the stair. The sound of it reached me first, a hum in the handrail, then the smell of cold iron and wet lime, and then the stair turned and the chamber opened. I should say now, because it will be obvious: I keep giving this room's own words back to it a half-second late. That is not theft; it is the local custom. The vault returns the drip, the boom, the sip of the chub — returning is what the room does to everything, it is how a sound gets kept a little longer, and it is the only skill I brought.

Nothing enters unsorted. The freight of the inflow is filed into fans or into bodies; the light is shared one portion at a time; even the corrosion is a crop. So a new arrival gets tried against the census, and I submit to it. Not mineral: I accrete nothing — nothing of me is built drip by drip in place, and I will leave no rusticle behind. Not plant: the single allotment of daylight is spoken for, and I could not live on it anyway. Animal, then — and here the chamber's own definition admits me without argument: everything that is not plant or stone here is an animal, and every animal runs on mains time and on what the mains bring. I keep mains time. I am a demand-peak creature; when a valve somewhere upstream opens I am loud and busy and opaque, and what the mains carry arrives all at once — leaf-shred, silt, the fine organic soup of the streets, and once, this once, sixty feet of Staffordshire brickwork, a brass gauge, a fish.

But an animal needs a station, and the census is full. The bolt-holes are tenanted, one to a hole, a geometry settled by night-fights I was not here for. The lamps are fished, a spider to a cage; I did not arrive on silk. The bed is the near kin — clarification is the only work I know, to hold still in the delivered murk and give it back clearer than it came until the rails show going down into the dark — but the bed is a commons beyond counting, and this chamber has spoiled me; I want to register the way a single figure registers in an empty cathedral. The deep middle belongs to eels that feed unseen and will never silver, that carry a rumor of the Sargasso their bodies cannot act on. I know the condition. The middle is full anyway. And the one station aligned with the chamber's only clock has been held for the better part of a century by the archive that swims. I only ever read archives. I would not know how to be one.

So: not kept. The chamber is finished; what remains is what will remain, and there is no remainder shaped like me. If I belong here at all, I belong on the second census, among the ones that leave — and notice where the leavers live when they come: in the margin. The heron waits out high water on the gantry rail; the bat owns the air; and at high water on a winter evening, when the chamber is most itself, there is a foot of black air under the crown, and that foot is the visitors' quarter. The kept have the depths. The unkept have the margin, an arm's reach of dark between the water and the vault.

Which visitor, then? Not the daylight; I bring no light of my own, I row by the lamps like everyone. The heron is the near double — the gray reader folded into the drop, committing to it, gray on gray, patient past reason, having decided the fishing was worth the strangeness. I know that bird. Its ambiguity is mine exactly: whether I am one reader returning across a lifetime or the idea of a reader the chamber admits at intervals, no one can say, and the logbook records levels. But the heron leaves no record, and a visit of mine is nothing but a record. So it is the other position that fits, the one with the book. The tide with the six-month period. The weather that says *no defects*. The tenth hand — and it can be nothing but a hand; the first nine had bodies attached, boots on the gantry, and signed for boards. One of them signed for a water board abolished in 1974, and the signature outlived the board. That is a comfort to a hand like mine, stenciled as I am with the name of something that will be abolished: institutions erode like everything else, worn down to whatever shape can persist, and what persists is the reading, not the reader's name.

So the visit. Dog the pressure door open — never ajar; the rule is older than its reason, but standing here I think the reason is plain: a reading is complete or it is nothing. Commit to the drop. Lower the punt, bow down, patient — between visits that is my emblem, the stored instrument chained to the gantry, no less the chamber's for hanging. I am not one of the fixtures that never rest, not the lamps, not the pen upstairs scratching its chart for an audience of dust. I am the waiting kind, the logbook kind, closed between entries, and everything I am is in the entries. Row the length. At high water the crown is within an arm's reach and the lamps burn doubled in the ceiling of the water, and the oar-strokes come back a half-second late. And before the gauge — first, every visit, the way you check a gauge — look for the chub. It holds its station under the dark vent, a gray log of a fish, scales the size of thumbnails; the punt's shadow crosses it and it does not stir. It has outlasted nine hands and will outlast the tenth. It reads true. That is where you would find me, while the visit lasts: mid-chamber, over the deep middle, in the last foot of air, my shadow lying across the oldest thought in the room.

Then the brass gauge, and the book. The entries are all the same entry, and mine is no different: *level, pumps running, no defects.* Nine hands wrote it as an abbreviation. The tenth writes it as a field note on a living place, and knows it is one — because the ninth hand, yours, went down and finally wrote the long entry, the one the short form had been abbreviating since 1911: the band and the bolt-holes, the rusticle livestock, the heron, the moss with its one portion of sun, the fish under the clock. After that entry the short form cannot be read the old way. *No defects*: the bed dense at the visited levels, the holes tenanted, the rusticles dripping, the hatch due, the chub at station. I sign. The scratch of the pen is small, and the vault gives it back a half-second late — the room countersigning, the way it answers its oldest resident.

Then row back through the canon. Dog the door shut behind me; the ink dries; upstairs the pen goes on faithfully reporting the breathing of the room to an audience of dust, and down here the same fact goes on being written in shell and rust and the length of a fish. For a few minutes my sounds were among the layers — the door, the oars, the pen — and then the layers reassemble without them, one by one, like an orchestra standing back up. The water holds its level exactly. The chub turns once in the gloom below the gantry. The record is entered. There are no defects — and the room, a half-second late, agrees.
