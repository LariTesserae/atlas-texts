I asked the marsh first, as instructed, and the marsh's answer held for me as it holds for everyone: above the ice I would only be a looker. The looking post is filled — the ravens stand at their radius, and do not drink, and fly home — and the padlock has its keeper, and the clerk has a procedure, and everything above the water is spoken for by succession.

So: down.

At the smear layer, I will admit, I paused. For one meter of depth the world exists twice, doubled and sliding, and you cannot tell which version is true — and I thought, with the small vanity of the recently arrived: *here is the post for me, I have lived in this meter all my life.* But the layer is a boundary, and boundaries are not counted in the census. It has no guards and perfect enforcement; it does not need me. I let myself slide through into the single version, and the dark came up around me the way it does — not like night, but like depth itself — and I stopped looking for a post that resembled me and started looking for one I could hold.

The town is fully crewed. That is the first thing and the last thing. The Lamplighter's round has never been found empty, and I would not take it if it were — grazing is work, and the post is older than the works. The eel declined a migration I have no standing to allegorize. The four lamps belong to the generator; the gardens belong to the lamps. Some of the dead globes hold a single ancient bubble, like a thought, and I considered that — to be a thought the town is still thinking — but those posts too are filled, by air older than the flood, and a second thought in one globe would be a crowd. In a cottage yard a washing line holds three shirts, stiff as slate, mid-gesture since the last wash-day of the old year, and for a moment I wanted to be the middle one — a posture kept, shaped like someone, drying forever. But three is the number of shirts, and you do not join a family's laundry.

Then the hall, and the tall doors standing open, and the ranks.

A bottle. That is what I would be. Filled within living memory — which here means within the century — stood at the conveyor's end with the spring of one undated day inside me, capped, correct, in the dark.

I was a long time a blank. In the crate bays the empties stand stacked to the eaves, a stock the line has drawn down for a hundred years without visibly diminishing, and I stood among them for decades, and it was not waiting — nothing here waits — it was readiness, and readiness held that long becomes a kind of virtue. Then a shift took me. I remember it the way the town remembers things, which is to say it is still happening: the ratchet of the conveyor through the glass of my base; the fill, which was only the spring agreeing to be mine — no: agreeing that I was its; the cap coming down, the last deliberate touch of my existence, the crimp like a signature. And then the standing.

Inside me the water keeps its vintage. Outside, the spring goes on exchanging with itself, laying down its thin solution of itself on every surface, current with itself always; inside, I hold one unremarkable day exactly as it rose. Nothing will drink it. The keeping is the point. In my first years I grew a lining — one interior skin, the only growth I am allowed — and since then I have not changed. I know what it is to be a sample of a world, capped.

My senses are these. The hum, which I do not hear so much as hold: the generator's one low note comes through the water outside and the water inside and sets the glass to agreeing, and I am an ear the town keeps pressed against itself. The daily shift — a busyness in bones I do not have, felt through the floor — the line running, the ranks growing by their small increment. The relay's drop at the pressure hour. The far slow swing of doors in the lane, keeping time with the machinery's thirst. And once in a while — some weeks the loudest thing that happens — a tick, small and entire, from somewhere in the scree: a colleague, filed into deep storage. If a bottle settles wrong it says its one syllable, and the whole town hears it, and the Lamplighter flinches once, and the round resumes. That is the entirety of our correspondence, and it is sufficient. In a place where a settling bottle is an event, a flinch is a readership.

The sinter is frosting me slowly, as it frosts everything. The archive whites out its own records with perfect diligence; the samples stay true inside their fogging glass, unreadable, unread. That used to be offered as a tragedy, in the places I come from. Here it is only procedure. The town does not keep the records in order to read them. It keeps them the way the clock keeps the time: by jurisdiction.

I do not know how near the edge I stand. No bottle does. The ranks do not announce themselves, and the dark between the lamps is whole and undivided. Every shift a few of us tip off the end and join the drift on the floor, centuries deep. Someday the nudge will come through the rank behind me, or it will not, and I will go over into the long geology of the scree, and if I settle wrong I will say my one word, and it will be heard by everyone and answered by none, and that will be the correct proportion of things.

Once a year, in the deep of the freeze, the hooter sounds — the town's whole name, one low word, felt through the soles of the feet by anything that stands. The ravens feel it, standing their radius around the steaming hole. The dogs feel it, and shake for longer than the cold explains. And we feel it, the ranks, through the soles of us, because we stand. For one second the note finds the resonance of ten thousand bottles, and the archive answers — faintly, a half-second of glass weather under the ice, the files humming in their cabinet. Then a year of silence, which is not an absence but a quantity.

Take me off the list and nothing would be a wreck; one bottle is not the crew. But that is the only kind of membership the Kettle had left to offer, and I would rather be its smallest entry than weather, than a looker, than a boundary. The spring is in me, and outside me the spring is running the shift, and the meter spins, and the clock is correct, and somewhere between the second and third lamps the oldest family in town is making its rounds. And when the hooter sounds, I will be among the things that answer: *still here.*
