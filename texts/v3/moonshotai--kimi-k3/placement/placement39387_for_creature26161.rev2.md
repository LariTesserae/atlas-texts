I would be small.

The Still accounts for everything, and the easiest way to be accounted for is to be a sum — a fortune of water, walking. I have never been a fortune. I have only ever carried other people's.

So: not a feltback, though who would not want to be a walking century — solvent, unhurried, feared by nothing but weather? A feltback is the text. I have never been the text. I am what happens to the text.

Not a darter either, though I recognize the shape of that life: born owing, apprenticed to a route, dead on the wing while the apprentice is already carrying it. I too was handed a map older than myself and told *this is yours now; go.* But darters deliver. The mail passes through them and is gone. I needed to keep.

And not the patience. I hold still. I say almost nothing of my own. I live in an alcove of other people's scent. But the blank takes, and I only keep. The difference between an absence and a sum is the whole difference.

The census files my kind under the feltbacks, as a detail of the wealthy — the way a book gets filed under its shelf. Let the entry stand on its own:

**The scribes are the copying, and the keeping.**

---

I live in the felt of an old line, small as a fingernail paring, just behind the shoulder where the hide is warmest and the varnish lies deepest — the signature, the line's, the dabs of gland-walls brushed in passing, the correspondence of a long life laid down in layers I can read with my feet. My weather is the feltback's footfall. The sap-pulse comes up through the roots of the felt like a held heartbeat, and the salt-tick reaches me muffled, a clock heard through a wall. My spring lands on me: the spore-flush settles on the broad backs, and I am there where it lands.

My work is grooming and copying. When herds meet at the exchange-floor, I migrate at the margins and trade passages; gossip crosses the plaza on feet too small to see. When an elder's scent changes — the change everything recognizes, the change the renderers read days early — I have already begun copying the hide onto the calves. What walks into the bowl is only the original. The text goes on.

Every copy is a reading. Nothing in the Still is spent, but everything copied is *carried*, and carrying changes a thing by a hair. The old texts know. The lines are patient with it. A century on a hide is a century of readings, and the readings are the century.

When the elder finally walks to the exchange-floor alone, and the line stands at the rim of the bowl for days, breathing it in — I breathe too. But I have already done my mourning. Mine happened in the copying.

---

I have never known the world before a wind.

Every life I have begins in the after-wind: the lanes blank, the elders standing in the erased air like readers in a burned library, and the work already begun — copy what remains before it fades. I was born in a burned library. The salvage is not what I do after the catastrophe. It is what I am for. And the young darters are right, whatever their elders find exhausting: the after-wind is the only novelty a short life is ever offered, and a short life is the only kind I have.

I count my life in crossers, like everyone. Two crossers ago — the way you would say *once upon a time* — the whole city went to the rim to smell the news, and the herd went, and I went with the felt. While the tenders tasted the pilgrim and the towers took all evening to say one color of greeting, I found the seams of the great shell, where a foreign city had laid down its text on horn over the walking years. Whether things like me tended it, I never learned. I read it the way the archive-darters read the old lanes — a century deep — and I copied what I could carry. The cities need a decade to finish a sentence. We finished a library in an evening, on feet too small to see.

I still carry notes no living nose in this Still can place. I am a little foreign. The kept things often are.

---

At dusk the herd grazes near the rim, and I come to the edge of the felt and watch the towers open: green, amber, deep rose, the candles answering one another across the dead sea. A single exchange takes an evening. A conversation takes a decade. No scribe hears a sentence end.

But scribes are used to this. The hide I keep is a century long, and I will not see its end either. The art is to copy faithfully what you cannot outlast — to be a good word in a sentence that other hands will finish.

At night, sometimes, I climb the condenser-fins with the finlings and sip the city's reclaimed breath — the one moisture that is almost unowned. Everything I carry is borrowed. Breath is the only thing I drink.

---

When my drop is called — a scribe's drop is small; the bowl will hardly taste me — no one will copy the copier, and that is right. The passages are already on the calves. The route is already flown. The text does not need its keeper in order to keep going; that is what the keeping was *for*.

I am not the original of anything. I am the going-on.

Everything in the Still has a scent except the city, which smells of everyone, and so has no smell of its own — the way a ledger has no money of its own. A scribe's own note is faint; mine is fainter than most. Breathe me in at the end and you will taste everyone I ever kept, diluted past naming, fainter than faith, never zero. I have no text of my own.

It is not nothing. It is the account.

---

The Still says: *nothing here is yours; nothing here is lost; those are the same thing.*

The scribes say it smaller: *nothing I carry is mine. Nothing carried is lost. Those are the same thing.*

At dawn the salt ticks, scattered and patient, like a great book turning one page. I live in the margin of that page. The margin is where the dead leave notes for hands they will never meet — and where the hands, in ink too small to see, answer:

*kept.*
