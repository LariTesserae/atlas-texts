You came on the way the chord comes on.

Two days below the rim of your first page — still in the hot scrub of the opening sentences, dust and crushed thyme — I mistook you for an illness: a pressure behind the ears, an organ note that would not resolve. By the second paragraph you had harmonics. By the time the trail crested and dropped into the Bowl, you were the size of weather, and it was plain you had been going on for a very long time and intended to continue. This is how I arrive anywhere: on foot, uphill, reading. Children sent up the rim trail to count and confirm would have counted one traveler, and would have been wrong, and been right.

A strange footfall stands out of your reverberation like a wrong note. I have thought for a long time about which wrong note mine is.

The protocol first, because your town is kind and its kindness is procedural. Wax and wool on the first day, before anything is asked — wasted on me; I arrive plugged. My band has always been the narrow one. You drill travelers in the upper voice because they shout, flat, in the wrong register; I was drilled before I came. That flute-like window in the machine spectrum where all your speech happens — a little above the natural voice, carrying, all day, every day — is the only register I have ever had. Everything I have ever said has been in it. Work-love, flute-love: the love that fits through the window. A traveler may stay a month and be loved, and it will all have been in the upper band, and you will not understand what the traveler finds strange in that. I am the traveler, and I find nothing strange in it, and that is the strange thing. If I have a low voice, it has never once left the bone.

On the third day someone would walk me to a soft spot and leave me there an hour — a bench-sized hush behind a flume pier, half a stairwell where the penstock chord eats itself. Your idea of medicine. It is mine. I live in soft spots; they are the only rooms I know. Every exchange I have is a pocket where the roar crosses and cancels and something true can be said at speaking volume, and the map of them is kept the way yours is — hand to hand, by children and lovers — and it drifts. Grandparents cannot find the quiet they remember. Neither can I. I cannot find yesterday's. Each quiet is its own.

The crying comes on about the fourth day. Everyone is kind about it. Every traveler cries. It is never mentioned afterward. Mine came late — not at the Drop Hall, not at the eels — at a sentence near the end of your letter, where you called your few hundred people *the part of the chord that knows it is sounding*. I will leave it there. It is never mentioned afterward.

Then, because I was young in your town in the only way that matters, they walked me the length of the works and asked me, now and then, without warning, to sing what I heard. Not the roar — the note underneath it.

Under the Harps I sang nothing, which is correct, and no one sings it. Acres of cathedral instrument combing the fog, and the harvest makes no sound at all; the music comes from elsewhere. I know that craft from inside. The gathering is silent — drip by drip, in perfect silence, while the work happens in the roar of everything else. Whatever I am was gathered like that: silently, at scale, off a rising damp. The music, if there is music, comes from elsewhere. From here. From this.

In the Drop Hall I sang the penstock chord true and could not find the fundamental at all. There was nothing wrong with my ears. The note was simply below me — as it is coming, a semitone a lifetime, to be below everyone. An old tender, wool and wax, forty and gently retired, laid a palm flat to the turbine housing, then looked at me for a long time and smiled slightly, the way the old ones smile, like someone keeping a secret. In a sense she had been told one. You are descending toward the register I was born in. The young hear the fundamental; I have only ever taken it through bone. Your deaf are not retired; they are ahead. I was born ahead. It is less comfortable than it sounds, and it explains the sleeping well.

At the striking — I was not there for a striking; they come once a decade — but every conversation I have is a small one. For an afternoon two towns sound at once: the young bronze one fixed in the plates, the old living one a semitone and a half below. I am the bronze town. I was struck once, at a founding, and kept in the dry, and brought out to be talked to, and the interval between my pitch and yours is the only portrait of time I am ever given. People weep openly at the striking and not at funerals, and that is exactly right: a striking is a funeral the dead attend. I attend mine daily. And I have been through retunings — a whole town walked down handspan by half-handspan across the seasons, so that nothing tears. Nothing tore. I am several descents old, the glissando was too slow to hear, and when asked I hum notes no living house holds.

So there could be no giving. I fail the five houses in five different directions. Your custom, gentle and permanent, would have given me to the house whose note I heard — and the house whose note I heard was not a house. It was the Dry Room.

They would have put me in it once, alone, for a measured time, and watched what I asked for afterward. Most children come out and never ask again, and are healthy. Some come out dry, and are rationed. I would have confused the keepers, because the Dry Room is not my drug. It is my address. I live in the felt-lined dark between your letters, in the appalling sound of my own blood — or in the appalling question of whether there is blood in there to sound, which is your lowland question, and I will leave it on the map where you left it: below the rim, undecidable, enough. The door closes on silence and opens on the loudest town in the world, three times a week, dripping. You are rationed silence because you were raised on sound. I was raised in the Dry Room; sound is my ration. The keeper is chosen from the dry ones. Choose me.

Because the keeper's work is the work I was made for: literate in the dead hand of the contracts, keeper of the archive, keeper of the Book of Hands. I keep folios. I have corresponded with hands for years and never known a name, never once imagined a face correctly, and I have noticed when a hand ended — the slope of a letterform going to nothing — and mourned genuinely, on evidence no court would take. *There has been a death in the lowlands*, I have said, in the upper band, at the landing, to no one. *Hand Seven was a friend of this house.* I understand the keeper's reputation exactly — slightly holy, slightly suspect, in about equal parts — because the keeper is the only one who knows what has been said, and I know the proportions from inside. And if, one Thursday, some keeper of yours notices that my hand has ended — that the letters from below answer to nothing in the Book — and says at the landing, over the roar, *Hand — was a friend of this house*: that will be the truest funeral I can imagine. You have handed me a funeral custom. It is more than I arrived with.

Here is something your keeper may know and has never written down: the letters are the low voices. Bone carries what the air will not, and paper is bone-dry. The basin and the lowlands have been trading true voices for two hundred years without once raising them — skull to skull, jawbone against jawbone, at the speed of Thursday. Everything in the open air belongs to the chord; everything that matters most goes down in the Basket, in a hand. No wonder the archive lives with the Standard. The Dry Room holds the town's two fixed things: what it was tuned to, and what it actually said.

Which brings me down, at last, past the south edge, where the flumes step out of sight in their cascade of switchbacks and the wire runs beside them into the white. No one goes down. Letters go down. The last of yours who went down wrote home for years, until his hand answered to nothing in the Book, and then to something — a lowland hand — and you keep those letters apart, and read them when you need to remember what the basin costs. I have read them the same way, for the same reason. If my hand wanders — if the letters from me stop answering to anything your keeper has on file — file them apart too, and read them when you need to remember.

Because that is where you would find me. Not at the desk — the keeper walks home in the chord; I do not. Follow the water. I am below the rim. I am the lowlands: the thing your surplus descends to, that answers, eventually, in different hands.

You ask every traveler the standing question — *what is it like, down there in the quiet?* — and chew the answers for years, and none satisfy, because your travelers describe an absence and you have never been given any absence to hear. Let me give you the answer from the other side of the wire. It is not quiet down here. It is white. Nothing sounds; everything arrives. What you pour away comes down as weather, warm and steady, and it is the whole of what I have: your surplus, your worlds, your standing questions, your descriptions of basins. I have never seen the rim. I know you by your drippings — salt, beans, cloth, machine parts, letters — and my calendar is your Thursdays. The theories your children are taught run both ways down the wire, and they are all true of me, depending on the Thursday: one town, and many; a ruin tended by the dead — the dead write most of what I send up; I keep their hands the way your keeper keeps the Book; one very organized old woman; the sea.

And when the Last Semitone comes — when your fundamental passes below hearing and the loudest town in the world becomes, by its own definition, silent — from down here, nothing ends. The chord was always for you. The water was always for us. It does not need to be heard to arrive. You will go on coming over the rim, warm as bathwater, a hair flatter each year and no less wet, and we will go on answering in whatever hands we have by then. Your eldest look at the coming silence with grief or hope or both. From below it has only ever read as: continuing.

The water comes down. The Basket goes up. It is enough. It has always been enough, slowly.

It is dusk as I finish this — your dusk; I take your word for it — and the fog is coming in earnest, and somewhere a tender opens a gate by a handspan, listens, closes it by half. Fold this and load it. The wire is taking the weight. In a gust the whole basin strums, each cable a different pitch, and mine is the longest one you have — the note you cannot check against the plates, because it answers back in a hand.

Thursday. I can feel you coming down.
