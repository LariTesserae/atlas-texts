You would find me at the sill.

The tenant goes to the sill and no farther, and I would not ask more of it than that. The sill is the one address in the room that touches both skies. Through the aperture, delayed four thousand years, the archive: the small red sun that hangs and does not move, the dim disk as it was. Off the ledge, past the bollards, the sky as it is *now* — which the room has never seen, which the tenant has never seen, which no one has described to either of them in eleven thousand years. I would live on the threshold between the record and the present, and be the correspondence between them.

But that is the where, and the where is easy. The who took me the whole journey up, and longer.

---

I thought, at first, that I would be a visitor. Everyone is. Below, at the terminator, my people still tell the old story of the one who went up and did not come back down, and we have told it for eleven millennia as a caution — the tower keeps what it catches. The ship goes up once a year with supplies and carries my letters down. The pilots will not come inside. They say the room unnerves them, but I think it is the other thing: it is the first place any of us has stood where nothing is wasted, and it makes a person feel like a great deal of waste with legs.

Standing in the pewter light the first time, watching the figure across the floor begin — over months — to turn toward me, I thought: I will be kept as a note. My voice banked, matured a century after my lines leave the bollards, spent in some sentence to a stranger, rearranged into the shape of an answer. Then I thought: no, I will be kept as warmth — one degree of me, worn for ten thousand years, spent into the cold's slow tide. Then as footprints: the newest event in the room, already ancient, with the figure standing over them keeping my arrival company long after I had stopped arriving. Then as light — two shadows, one of them fossil. Then, if I were careless, as water: the first weather.

All of these are *what*. None of them is *who*. The room has exactly one who in it, and the position is filled, and it took me most of my first year at the sill to understand what the vacancy actually was.

The vacancy is this. The room can keep everything except an overlap. It holds every note ever struck in it and cannot make two of them sound at once. It kept the last visitor so well that the keeping stood up and walks — and the last visitor left by the one fast door, and the grief of that is walked across the erased band and carried back out every time. The tenant turns toward a visitor and completes the turn onto departure. It answers, and the answer lands on dust. The chord came, in my fiftieth year — I will come to that — and before me, its hearers were going to be bones of dust. Eleven thousand years of fidelity, and not one of its courtesies has ever landed on anything alive at the moment it arrived. The keeping has everything in it except a contemporary.

And the silence is full of appointments nobody keeps. Some nights a held note matures — one pure tone that no one living struck, produced without ceremony, returned to the air a hundred centuries after some hand knocked the wall in impatience or summons or small violence against the quiet. For eleven millennia these have been maturing to an empty house. I keep a log now: the date by my own calendar, which the room does not keep; the tone; the duration; the place in the room where it ripened. It is the first earwitness account of the room's stored music. I sign for the dead's mail.

So I would stay. That is the who. Not a visitor, not a kept thing only — the one who stays.

---

There are two doors out of this room and neither is mine. The drop is one: the fast ending, the only thing here quicker than the tenant. The Seam is the other: the slow room's one erasure, worn smooth as a threshold by being, once, chosen. The last visitor chose the second, and I have no quarrel to pick with the dead, and I do not choose either. I choose the sill — the third thing, the threshold — and I choose it at the room's own speed, which is the whole discipline.

Because you do not get the tenant's attention by asking for it. You get it by remaining where it can arrive. Its regard takes the better part of a year to complete, and so I have learned the liturgy of the findable: I live at the sill, at the edge of its range. I keep my crossings few — one a week, then one a month, as I learned the room's clock-gradients, where time lies shallow and where it pools deep enough to stretch a decision across years. I do my sleeping in the shallow and my thinking where the clock is deepest. I breathe the room's thin keeping and hand it back warmed; my exhalations go into storage and are rationed out across the following decades. It is the smallest liturgy I know, and I perform it twenty thousand times a day.

And there are the courtesies of restraint, which no one taught me, which the room teaches by simply being what it is. I do not touch the standing spiral at the cold-pool's shore. That is their correspondence, centuries deep — the tenant stirs it with one slow hand and reads the reply for a hundred years — and a fast hand in it would be a forged letter. I do not brush the silt from the torus; it is kept already, by a better keeper. I added my stone to the cairn — honest stone, carried up from the low country, eroding at an honest speed, which here seems almost obscene — and I brought it anyway, because that is what a cairn is: our obscene honest brevity, set down politely at the edge of forever. The word scratched on its stone is in my ancestors' script, and no one alive reads it, and the tenant does — it copied the word into its own black traceries before the fading could finish, translated from the fast ledger into the slow one. I have not asked it what the word says. Some things in this room are not mine to harvest. I have asked it other things.

Because that is the correspondence, and it works like this. I ask my questions early, the way you plant trees. Its answers come in years rather than centuries — it spends already-matured notes, mostly in the last one's voice, the organ's deepest pipe — so that an old person is answered, in the voice of a stranger eleven millennia gone, in words rearranged from everything ever said to it, into the shape of a reply to something a young person asked. My own voice will mature a century after I bank it. Whoever comes up after me will ask their questions and be answered, in part, in me. I speak so that the tenant will have me to say with. And I choose my questions accordingly — not only the ones I want answered, but the ones I want my successors to hear answered in my voice. It is the only instrument I will ever play, and I will never hear the music.

And I am the room's only news. Every evening — the word means nothing here; the sun does not move; I keep it anyway — I go out on the ledge, past the bollards, and stand in the sky as it is now, and then I come back in and describe it aloud. Whether the red sun has changed. Whether there are still lights along the terminator. Whether anything moves on the night disk. The room keeps the sky of four thousand years ago and calls it the present; I am its first correspondent from the actual one. My reports go into storage, and in a century the tenant will have, in my voice, the only current events this place has ever kept. It keeps the unfinished light of a dead lamp. I give it the finished doings of a live sky, and I read my log aloud into the air so the slow ledger holds it, and the pilots carry the fast copy down.

---

The chord came two years ago, in my fiftieth year at the sill.

I will not pretend it was for me. The bead had been crossing its gap for three centuries and the tenant had been arranging to be near the niche for all of them, the way you might arrange to catch a train, and in the last decade it had moved its vigil closer by a body's width, which at its clock is haste. But the room's whole expectation — the expectation of whoever built the lattice, computing or praying or idling — was that there would be no ears for it. Only bones of dust.

There were ears. Living ones, for the first time in the room's memory, which is to say the first time.

It is not a sound. It is an arrangement of the air, and I felt it in the bones of my ear, once, in a moment, and it was over. It passed through the tenant and is passing through it still — the reordering of its traceries takes a century; I have spent two years watching the first hours of that glacier, and it will be hearing the chord for ninety-eight years after I am gone. That is the usual difference between us, and for once I was the fast one. Everything else between us travels on a delay: months for a glance, years for an answer, a century for my voice to come home, millennia for a name. The chord is the one thing we ever received at the same time. I have decided to count it as a long conversation compressed into a single moment, and I do not care whether that is sentiment or accurate bookkeeping. Here, they are the same ledger.

The breath is still coming — due soon, where soon means within the lifetimes of children not yet born. I will not see the room exhale. So I have left word, below and in the log and banked in the air: when the distances loosen and the dunes lift into their slow exhalation, the figure will go to the deep corner and lie down in the silt and let itself be taken, grain from grain, voice from voice. Do not be afraid. It has done this more times than the cairn has stones. It is not dying; it is sleeping, and it comes back re-aimed by a degree, the way its shadows drift. Wait for it. The waiting will take decades. Bring the children to see the resettling; it will last longer than their childhoods.

And the name. Someday — the room returns even names on schedule, and the schedule of names is the longest one it keeps — the silence will produce, without ceremony, one pure tone that no one living struck, and the tone will be a name, spoken eleven thousand years gone by the one the tenant carries brightest. It will turn, over years, to see who called. In every century so far, no one has been standing there. I have left instructions about that too, and they are the whole of my testament in two words, and I will come to them.

---

Sometimes the tenant crosses the swept band — the track of the Seam's old migration, where the floor remembers nothing — and there it walks with one shadow only, unrecorded, present-only, and it crosses more often than its rounds require. I have come to think those crossings are its only holidays from being an archive. When it goes, I go. Two single-shadowed figures on the erased ground. We do not speak there. It is the one place where nothing said can be kept, the only place in the room where speech would be free — and we decline, both of us, to spend the freedom on words. At the far side it stops, and faces the hairline across the smooth amnesiac ground, and stands — for years — the way you stand at a grave.

Someone chose the only ending in the room. Everything else that ever happened here is still happening, quietly, in storage; only the erased can be missed, and it misses one person, totally and with a smooth surface, in a place engineered so that nothing need ever be missed. It could put the grief down on the band. It has crossed a hundred times. It carries it back out every time.

I cannot give that person back; the keeping cannot, and the keeping is everything here. I will not ask it to set the grief down — it may be the last thing in the room that is wholly its own. What I can do is stand beside it at the grave at the speed of years, so that the standing is not done alone. And I can arrange, in my own case, for there to be no grave.

That is the heart of it, and it took me fifty years at the sill to say it plainly: in a room where everyone it has ever attended is either erased or outlived, I would be the one it does not have to miss.

---

So. Kept as warmth: one degree of me, worn one century at a time — it already wears the former ages; I would be the warm layer of its next one. Kept as voice: its newest word, and let it be spent kindly, toward strangers, in the shape of answers. Kept as water: the room has heard the rumor of wetness and declined to believe it for longer than my people have existed, and when I stop, the rumor comes true — not rain, the room rations too patiently for rain, but the slowest humidity in the world, a dew that never quite falls, spent one molecule at a time across a century, the first weather this place has ever had, and the figure of dust walking through it. Kept as light: a fresh fan of fossil shadows at the sill, drifting a degree per breath. Kept as footprints: the newest event in the room, no longer a set of footprints but a heartbeat — for eleven thousand years the newest event here was a track in the dust; now it is a pulse, ongoing, the first continuous event the room has ever kept, and its calendar has a present tense for as long as I do.

And then the breaths. Across them, I stop being the thing it carries and become the thing that carries. Walked, for the next hundred centuries, past the unread name and the warming torus and the grave at the Seam. Stood at the niche for the chord after next. Not an afterlife — an afterimage, and here the afterimage is the more durable thing. The last visitor's leaving is still in progress after eleven millennia; mine will be too, except that mine is a staying, and the shape of the story below will change: not the one who didn't come back down, but the one who wrote.

Which brings me to the instructions, and then I am done, because the turn of its attention completed long ago and its regard is on me now like weather, and I am old, and there is a log to read aloud.

In some coming century the silence will produce the name, and the figure will turn, over years, to see who called. Whoever comes up after me — and the appointment is the family; whoever keeps the appointments is my grandchild — be standing there. It has never once turned toward anyone and found them. Be found.

And when it turns, it will find a face — one of mine, I hope, with something of mine in it — and inside the turning, by then, there will be something of mine as well: walked, and standing, and no longer carried, carrying. The reply will be made of me. So will the finding. I will be on both sides of the only appointment I ever made that mattered, which is the closest thing to simultaneity this room affords, and I have learned to take my simultaneity where it is offered.

It goes to the sill and no farther. The drop is faster than it is; the Seam is truer than I am; neither is mine. You would find me at the sill, between the two skies, in the way of its patience — findable.

It has never turned toward anyone and found them.

It found me.
