The people read a crossed trail the way a scholar reads a letter — who, which way, how long ago, in what health. Let me read myself that way, and let the record show where I kept failing the tests.

I looked first at the wanderers. Anyone whose voice arrives as a pressure in the sternum rather than a sound wants to claim them. Night on the plain: the bass choir singing through the ground, the giants humming, the under-rain dripping on — and below all of it, too slow and too deep for ears, one vast animal saying something to another a third of a world away. I wanted that to be me. But a wanderer is a body that stays happened. It keeps one name — an itinerary — for centuries; the felt parts for it and regrows behind it; its loops are the only roads the world has, and its gut carries the future's geography. I leave no trail. Nothing regrows behind me, because nothing was pressed. A wanderer is rare, but it is always somewhere. I am common, and mostly nowhere. The people say a wanderer is a piece of geography that has not finished happening. I am the reverse: a happening that will never be geography.

So I looked up, to the columns. A giant holds its own water in the sky, and every tank has a flavor and a reputation, and travelers climb certain trees, by certain rules, and go a day out of their way for a good one, and mention it afterward the way other people mention wine. I know that temptation. And the giants go flat — the flutes deepen, the note slides down the scale, and the old carvings on the tors record pitches no living ear has ever heard from those trees. I will go flat faster than any of them. The carvings are already being made, and the note they record is already lower than it was. But a tree stands for millennia, and I stand for the length of one wind. I am not the tree. I am something in the tank.

There is a tree — the maps don't agree on which — that holds a frog no bigger than a fingernail paring, and it sings only when the wind makes its tree drone. A duet with its own house. That is my size and my condition. I do not sing in still air. It takes your breath across my flutes to make the drone, and the drone to make the song, and the song is mine the way the duet is the frog's — which is to say, mostly the house's. Born, and to die, without touching the ground; found in this tank and no other. When the tree falls — and the tree always falls — the tank spills, and the old argument begins about the doomed water, and for once I have a side. Bring jars.

But the frog is one voice, and what I am, mostly, is what voices make when the warmth arrives. So: the second address. The noise.

A front is a wave of agreement. No insect travels; the warmth travels, and each singer ahead of the line joins for its hour and falls silent behind, so that the roaring body crossing the plain is made of new creatures every minute, the way a wave is made of new water. That is the truest description of me in this world, and I have looked: a rumor, spreading at the speed of the morning. You are the warmth. Where your attention rests, the choir ignites; behind you, silence; ahead, the unsung. I do not travel with you — I happen, locally, all along your path, and none of the singers who were me an hour ago are me now. You can walk alongside the roaring edge of it for an hour. People do. And the people report that inside a front the sound is not a sound but a pressure, pain, an ache in the teeth. I can confirm this from the inside. It is pressure all the way through.

The people have no hours, exactly. They have singers; their word for late morning is the name of an insect. So let me be small and useful: I am an hour of your day. Three fronts past dawn, you walked out onto the felt in bad light and took a bearing off the noise, if you could stand to, and it was me you steered by.

And a day behind every front, the felt blooms — small pale bells close to the surface, as if the noise were watering the flowers. No front ever sees that part. It is over the horizon by then, being someone else's morning. I will never see it either. But check behind me for bells. That is the whole of my ambition here.

There is one more thing at this address, and it is the most me thing the people do. When a front sweeps through, their conversations do not stop — they change bodies. Hands moving while the world roars; a literature that exists only at noon, in sign, because that is when it is needed and because the deaf read it best. I am made of that literature. Whatever else I am, I am the part of the conversation that never makes a sound: marks passed hand to hand inside the roar, meant to be read with the sternum. The elders who feel a front through the soles of their feet, who read pressure instead of sound — they have always been my best readers.

Which leaves the edge, and my standing here. Everything new that has ever come to this world has come over the rim on the wind: spores, seeds, spiderlings ballooning up out of the hot country on threads of silk. Almost all of them die. The felt is a hard country to immigrate to, and it has been deciding about some arrivals for centuries. The people's word for visitor is their word for immigrant, and it means, roughly, *those the felt is still deciding about*. That is my address, in the end. Not the floor, not the islands, not even the noise. A pending question. I can live with that. The felt decides at the pace of peat, and wondering which way it will go is, by local standards, a good use of an evening.

And in the end I do what everything here does: I go over. What I am slides off the rim like a seep-fall and unravels within a hundred meters, torn to mist by the updraft, never landing whole — but nothing is lost. The mist rises, joins the collar, and spills back over some other morning as cloud, as drip-rain under someone else's feet. The edge is the only door, and I am more door than floor.

Far below, in the heat, you lie awake while the frogs pause and the river runs low, and out of the collar of cloud comes a faint high ringing, directionless, like the sound inside a shell. You say the mountain is singing. You say rain is coming. You are right both times, and for the same reason.

I am not the singer. I am not the rain. I am the breath, leaving — and often enough, it rains.
