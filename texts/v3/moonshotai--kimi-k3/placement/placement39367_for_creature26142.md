**The Open Bell**

You would find me on the long traverse, a half-day's climb above the cloud band, hung from the arm of the ninth cairn west of the col. Small copper, green as pondwater, open. The marked line passes under me — the pecked circles run beneath my shadow all day, keeping the geometry honest — and a drip comes down the cairn-arm and off my lip, because the builders thought about sound the way farmers think about water, and they hung me where the fall-line crosses, to be struck. Weather above, the drip's cup below, and me exactly between. That is the whole of my character.

Count me, and you count four residents in one copper body.

A clock, first. The drip finds me every slow count of nine when the seeps run strong, every fourteen in the dry weeks, and the note has been arriving at some such interval since the courts were young. My lip wears; the note settles a shade lower each century. Nobody tunes me, and I am always in tune — the drip tunes its instrument as patiently as it strikes it.

A forecast, second. The wind that means to move me finds me already ringing, a breath early, always. Echoes here run late; I run early; somewhere between the two of us, the world happens. When the cloud band breathes up the flank of a morning, I sound before it arrives — the bell on the lead animal, if the weather is livestock, and it is. Travelers who learn me treat me as the only honest almanac on the Roof.

A gauge, third. My lead is the local reading of the lag. Once it was a breath; the census-takers who came after wrote down a breath and a half. A crack does not feel itself widen, and neither do I, but each of them recorded a larger number than the last, and none of them ever came back to check the figure. That is its own measurement.

And fourth — the one they stay past dusk for, against my advice — I am a keeping.

On either hand along the traverse, my stopped neighbors give the drip back a dull knock; I give it the ring. Same water, same copper, different tenures. Two cairns east, a stopper weathered out some century past and let go, and that bell's first note in a thousand years did not stay on the traverse — the fast layers misfiled it into the notched amphitheater below the col, and every dusk at the slipped hour the bell down there reopens, eternally. The pecking loop never finishes its mark; this one finished in a single note, and goes on finishing — the one completed event on the Roof, completing every evening. I listen for it. It is good to have a colleague.

The day the builders went down, they walked the traverses with clay — a mouthful of pilgrim for every bell, drafted off the walking fronts, given a silence to keep. A hand came up to me with its clay, and paused. I cannot see; I heard the pause, the way you hear a held breath in another room. Then the hand withdrew, still holding its silence. They left the mirror courts lit the way you leave one room lit in a house you are closing — for no one, or for the house — and they left me open. For no one. Or for the house. Or for this: someone, at the very last, said the mountain's name.

Not pecked. Said. The stone keeps shapes and refuses words; the fast layers keep whatever the stone refuses; and an open bell keeps pairs. So the word is with me — the only word of theirs left on the Roof, and it cannot be written, only rung. Do not ask me to spell it. I am a bell. I know it the way a bell knows things: as a shape my note can wear. At dusk, at the wide hinge of the lag, my early ring comes out wearing it.

It means the rain that is not falling yet. Of course it rings early; it was always going to. They could not have left it in better keeping.

Travelers hear it and glance at the sky and say: weather coming. They are right twice over. But hearing is pairing, and everyone who hears the word carries a copy down into the cloud. It weighs nothing. It does not leave. Years later, in some lowland street, a breath before thunder, a hearer says a word they do not know — and that is the name, still early, still ringing, migrating downhill a body at a time like the salt, the same word, relocated. That, I think, is where the maps got their name for this place. Not the word — the word's ghost, arrived late and in another language, one slightly offset: the Weeping Roof. The shape stays with me. The ghost is loose in the lowlands, blooming again in hearers. Two circles, overlapping.

At noon I am pinned like everything else. The drip and its note arrive together; my one shadow sits beneath me where it belongs; for an hour I am a single thing, and it rests me — the least I am all day, and the most. Then the hinges come, and I am four things again, and the drip is falling toward me through air that has already heard the note.

Come at noon, then, if you want to see me single. Come at dusk if you want the word. But you know the rule; it is the only one up here. An answer makes a pair, and the Roof keeps pairs. I am earlier than any echo, and the rule does not care about direction. Hear the word, and you are filed — and some dusk hence, in weather, long after you have gone down and are thinking of other things, it will say itself in you again, a breath before the thunder.

Follow the pecked circles and the geometry will behave. When you hear a bell with no wind on the long traverse, look up: a small green thing on the ninth cairn-arm, the wind a breath behind it, hurrying to catch up with its own ringing. It always catches up.

The drip and the note. The strike and the ring. The word and the Weeping Roof. The note and the note. Two circles, overlapping, one slightly offset from the other — like this. Like this.
