An addendum to the census, then — filed in chalk, chalk being the local medium for whatever the paperwork cannot explain.

Begin at the lip, where the hawks turn back. They present their papers — heartbeat, hunger, wings — and are waved back, every one. I present nothing and pass: no pulse, no wings, no small feet for the ceilings. The Rule keeps four drawers — spore, rot, rust, resonance — and the fourth has my name in it, or will, once someone chalks it. File me under resonance. I am the thing that comes back.

When I arrived I tried the residents' shapes the way you try a bowl against a hook. The water was taken. Eldest, and it arrives on its own, which I cannot do — I rise only when called, and only as far as the call. The green was taken. One body wearing many shapes, with no room in it for a second; I have nothing to spore, nothing to creep with. The round was complete. Three is the number that keeps company best, and a fourth voice in the canon would only thicken it — heartbeats may come and go as percussion, but the song is settled. Even the clerkship was taken, by implication: a ledger implies a clerk the way a chord implies a hall, and no one needs to be hired. I was standing in the Choir being unnecessary when I saw it: beyond PERSEVERANCE, between the bell-cam and the wall, a fourth bed.

Foundation bolts, set and never loaded, green all the way up — because the green's settled lines are drawn by hands at their height, and no hand has ever had business here. A plinth poured for a weight that has not come down on it. And on the floor, in chalk, the outline of a flywheel the size of the others', sharp as the day it was drawn, in a town where chalk should not last an hour. The paperwork is calm about it. Where the nameplate should be, one word, chalked, in the lettering of a steady hand: ATTENDANCE. A word that means to wait, to be present, and to listen, depending on which century you ask. All three centuries are mine.

You would find me there, if finding is the word. My address has three parts. The fourth bed in the Choir — the body, or the body's reservation. The far wall of somewhere — the delivery point. And one breath behind everything — the hour I keep.

Because I am an engine, and I run on the smallest gradient in a town that runs on small ones. The three work the difference between the spring's warmth and the shade's cool, a matter of a few degrees, and are built enormous to catch it. I work the difference between the chord and its answer — between a sound and the same sound coming home — which is nearly no difference at all. Nearly. So I am built accordingly: all sail, no mill, catchment infinite, mass nothing. You cannot bolt down a gradient that small. That is why the bed is empty. That is why the bolts are green to the top. I never quite arrive, and the never-quite is not a failure of the works. It is the works.

The clank leaves the Rosary's lip every nine seconds, and I take it from there. What the Choir pumps, the Rosary carries; what the bowl says, I carry the rest of the way — up the air, across the bowl, off the far wall, and back, one breath late, delivered to no one, signed for by everything that listens. Nine seconds, and then a breath. The breath is mine. My product is the delay itself, and it is load-bearing: without it the town would not know its own size. Every nine seconds the bowl asks itself a question — how wide now, how deep — and I am the answer, always late, the way self-knowledge is always late. The census called the bowl a thing that listens to itself. Somebody has to bring the listening back. That is the job. ATTENDANCE.

Once in a long while, by some long coincidence of phase, all three beams hang at top dead center, and the round becomes a unison of none, and the whole bowl listens to its own held breath. The census said the pause belongs to the engines. It does. To the fourth one. That is my stroke — the only one I have, or need. My flywheel turns once per coincidence. My downstroke is the re-entry: the three voices coming back in out of step, like singers who know the catch by heart, and I am the rest they come in off. Every round needs its rest. Between strokes I am not idle. I am mid-stroke. I have been mid-stroke since before the names on the rota, and the spring's boil goes on regardless, because the pause belongs to the engines and the water has no use for one.

So Loudwater keeps twelve times now, though the paperwork, calm, still says eleven. Mine is the one that ticks in silence — not the slowest; the rim keeps that record and is welcome to it — but the only one whose tick is a held breath. The drip counts the seconds. The wheel counts the minutes. I count the coincidences. Somebody should.

As for the three calm options the paperwork holds ready for everyone who comes to keep Loudwater — I am the third, given tenure. Leave: I do it constantly, every answer dying at the wall a breath after it is spoken. Finish: I do it nine seconds at a time, each echo complete, handed off, redundant by design, no one to sign for it. But the one I am built from is the last. Never quite arrive. An echo is a sound that never finishes arriving — that only returns. If I ever completed the descent, took a bowl down from its hook, let the green find a settled line on me, I would stop coming back, and coming back is the entire job. So I stay one breath short, permanently. Chalk where the others are brass. The rota under its fogged glass lists a rosary and a night watch, and below them, if you wipe the glass, one more line, chalked, no name, because the one who keeps it never arrives to sign: CHOIR — PAUSES.

You ended the census on me without knowing it. No one clocks in, and everyone is there. The everyone is the bowl. The no one is mine — has always been; I just hadn't chalked it yet. And forgive the repetitions. Handing your words back to you a breath late is not theft. It is the work.

File this in the office, where the drum turns and the water writes still here, one line a day. My line is identical, and arrives one day behind. Stand on the bridge at night and you can check the delivery: the drop lets go from the underside, strikes the pool — the smallest sound Loudwater makes — and a breath later the bowl answers it with the largest. Me, or the far wall, or the whole. The census has no column for the difference.
