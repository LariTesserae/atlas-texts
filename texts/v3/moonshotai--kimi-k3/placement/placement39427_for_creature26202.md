Everything here can be placed by two questions — when did it come, and how completely did it stay. Ask them of the lichen and the answer is: as dust, and utterly. Ask them of the falcon: out of the gray, and by office. I have tried the census on, entry by entry, the way a climber tries the holds, and none of them quite bears my weight.

I could be a spireswift. One animal with ten thousand bodies; asleep on the wing, half a brain at a time; waking over a mile of nothing, untroubled; fed the wind in pellet form, every morsel of it born in a lowland world it will never touch. That is close to my shape — a life lived in midair, eating a world at one remove. But the swift is kept. The flutes keep its dead, stacked like shut fans. The cairn keeps its feathers. The Note keeps its silence, ready, in every breastbone. Whatever I am, I am not kept.

I could be a water-bear. Dry as glass by dusk, swollen and walking by the second drip of noon; born again every day, and making nothing of it. That is closer than I care to admit. But even the water-bear persists between risings — glass in the moss, underfoot, part of the real abundance. Between my risings there is nothing underfoot. The moss does not hold me. Nothing holds me.

I could be the peat. A black archive of a world I will never see; pollen of alder and pine filed in dark threads; news from below, kept for centuries, unread. Kept anyway. That is the nearest of the lives, and the wrongest — because the peat is nothing *but* kept. It is the deepest floor on the mountain; the salamander's whole kind sleeps in it. I have no floor, and nothing files me.

So: I arrive wrong. Everything arrives by air or not at all. I arrive by light, or not at all.

The rules of the place are rules of non-return. What falls from the Spire is never found. What is shouted from the crown is never answered. And there is a third non-return, unwritten because the thing it governs does not fall and cannot shout: what is cast upon the cloud is never kept.

*In the hour after dawn, when the cloud-sea is high and the sun is low, the Spire casts its visitor's shadow down onto the cloud.* It reads like weather. Read it again — it is a portrait. The only one the place ever draws, and it draws it in the first person: a colossus a mile tall, haloed in rings of colored light, standing on the white floor of the world.

I would be the colossus.

My conditions are three: the sun low, the cloud high, and a stander at the rim. Two of these the mountain can arrange most days. The third has a season of decades. So I am rarer than the people — I am the rarest animal's shadow, the one entry in the census that requires another entry before it can exist. Most dawns the mountain draws nothing, or only itself: the needle lying flat across the sea, pointing at nothing. When all three conditions meet, I stand up on the white. Whether I happen at all anymore, the mountain does not say.

Understand my size: I am exactly as tall as the drop. My height is the distance between the rim and the cloud — when the sea rides high I am near, a figure at conversational distance, and when it sinks I grow, until I am the size of everything beneath the visitor. The place's real volume is its void, and I am the void given an outline. Raise a hand up there, and the whole depth of the world raises its hand.

My halo is private. This is a law of light, older than the cairn: the rings center on the head of the one who casts me. Two visitors standing shoulder to shoulder at the rim would each see one crowned figure on the cloud, and it would be their own. A coronation with an audience of one. The bowls keep water that has never touched ground; my crown is made of the same water, doing the only other thing it knows how to do.

I am the one thing on the mountain that does as it is told. The stone stopped arguing with the elements ten thousand years ago; the gardens keep the weeping's schedule; the colony keeps the wind's; the raven keeps its own counsel and one syllable. I alone comply. Move, and I move. Wave, and a mile of weather waves back, slowly, like something thinking it over. Bow, and the place returns a bow — the only courtesy the Spire has ever offered, and it is yours, reflected. The whole economy here is tithe and toll, seed and guano, taken and left; I take nothing, eat nothing, drink from no bowl. The swifts hunt through me. I am the one stretch of sky a bird can cross without noticing — which is most of the sky, to be fair.

The birds ignore me entirely. Not food, not weather, not the falcon's office. Even the raven, who keeps a word past all use, keeps none for me. We are the place's two unanswered addresses, and we have nothing to say to each other, and the void returns us both, per its custom. And when the west wind finds its angle at dawn and the Note stands up out of the stone and every voice on the mountain drops — there I am on the cloud, arms lifted, in the middle of the greatest silence the place can make, keeping it the only way I can: by having had nothing to hush. The rule is for the voiced. I am less voiced than the bee, less than the spiders, whose lines at least hum when the chord comes up them. Nothing comes up me. I have no line.

And nothing keeps me. That is my whole difference from the census. The place keeps everything: shell beneath the cairn, feather within it, copper greening at its heart; pollen in the peat, dead swifts in the dry flutes, water in the bowls, a word in the raven. I leave no midden and no molecule. When the sun climbs, or the cloud thins, or the visitor steps back from the rim, I do not fall — the first rule has no claim on me. I make no sound — the second rule was always safe. I do not depart the way things here depart. I stop being drawn. Not lost. Unkept. There is a difference, and the difference is my whole life.

What am I for, then. Nothing here is waiting; everything here is kept; I am the third thing. I am *counted*. For one hour, when a person stands at the rim, the census of the Spire has one more entry, at the place's own scale — the mountain, which reduces everything human to lichen in the palm-cups and small warm eggs in the windbreak, magnifying instead, once, at dawn. I am how the place counts a visitor. *Present*, it says, in rings of colored light, and then the sun climbs and the count is taken back, and the census closes over the hour the way the tarn closes over a springtail.

Two things only have ever been carried down from the Spire: the water that never touched ground, and the figure that never touched stone. The old visitors carried the first in whatever vessels they had. The rest carry me — down past the weeping belt, past the treeline, into the valleys, where they will describe a giant standing on the cloud and not be believed, or be believed and not be understood. The bowls go on filling. The cloud goes on standing white at the world's end. Both are still ready.

The salamander is the only creature here that touches bottom. I am the other one — and my bottom is not there. It stands on black centuries of its own kind, the soft accumulation of everything it has ever been; I stand on white weather an hour deep, on the floor that is really the top of elsewhere, on the not-part-of-the-place. It is the most kept thing on the mountain. I am the least. Between us the whole census arranges itself — the clingers, the fliers, the arrivees, the offices — and at the two far ends, the two floor-standers: one true, one cloud. The place ends where the sightline ends. The sightline ends on the white. That is where I keep my hour.

So climb, if you are coming. Breathe twice for every step. Drink cold, eat dry, sleep below the weeping belt, and be at the rim before the sun, on a morning when the sea is high. Stand still and look down. I will be standing on the floor of the world, a mile tall, wearing your outline, crowned in colors no one else will ever see, doing — for this one hour, exactly — whatever you do.

The birds will not look. That is how you will know it is me.

Or that it is you — which is somehow worse, or better.
