You come up onto it out of the cloud, and the first thing wrong is the gentleness. You have climbed for two days through cold wet air that thins and thins until your heart hammers at a walking pace, and you expect peaks — you expect ridgelines and scree and the hard geometry of high country. Instead the land ahead of you swells and falls in long soft domes, rounded as the backs of sleeping animals, running away into a gray dimness with no edge anywhere. No sharp rock. No crag. Every slope is one you could roll down. And yet the air says otherwise: thin, thin enough that a shout comes out of you thin too, and dies within arm's reach. The locals call the plateau the Top, and say it the way you'd say the name of an old person. The escarpment you climbed — the Brow, they call it — is the only steep thing on it, and even the Brow is soft-shouldered, worn, its gullies all healed over with green.

The explanation the wardens give visitors is simple and unbothered: this was once a range of true mountains, and the mountains finished. Rain and root and frost and time took them down grain by grain, and the going down never stopped, but the whole country rose — or the lowlands sank — and the Top stayed high while everything about it went gentle. The contradiction is the place. Alpine air over downland curves. The bedrock is rotten through and through, weathered so deeply you can cut it with a wooden spade, which is exactly what they do: blocks spaded out of a dome's flank cure iron-hard after a season exposed to the air, and half the town is built of them, walls thick and round-shouldered and green-capped, as if the buildings were only domes that learned to hold still.

Over everything lies the mat. It is the first and last fact of the Top: a continuous skin of moss and cushion plant and black sedge, a meter thick and more, so dense and interlaced that you do not walk on soil here, you walk on the backs of plants. Your boot sinks a hand's breadth and springs back. Layers within layers — a bright wet green at the surface, a russet layer below that, then a black felt that smells of iron and stays cold even in the bright season, and beneath that the peat, going down farther than anyone has bothered to sound. Nothing here is one plant; everything is everyone, near-touching, grown through each other until the plateau is less a meadow than a single organism with moods. The mat drinks the fog and holds it. There are almost no streams. Water on the Top is not a thing that flows; it is a thing that is kept.

Fog is the real weather. Rain comes sometimes, a slow drizzle that can last a week, but the town's lifeblood is the fog that breathes up the Brow every dusk as the lowland air cools, pours over the edge in slow gray rivers, and settles into the swales until the whole plateau is a held breath of wet. It drains back at dawn. Everything the town does is timed to that breathing. And then there is the water that no one touches: the lenses. Deep in the domes, sealed in pockets of the rotten rock, lie pockets of the plateau's first water — still, dark, mineral, unmixed, older than the peat, older than the mat, older than the town by an unguessable margin. Sometimes a cutting breaches one, and the dome exhales — a sigh of stale cold air, then the seep. By law and by something older than law, a breaching is an event: the wardens come, the water is tasted once, and in it, almost always, there are small white blind things, armored, unchanged since the Top was young, going about their business in the dark. The lens is resealed. The plateau is allowed to keep its memory.

The light never gets strong. The haze overhead is total, month after month, so that the sun is never a disk but only a brightening, a coin of milk slid behind cloth, and noon looks the way dawn looks in kinder countries. Shadows are rumors. Lamps burn in the town at midday and nobody finds it strange. A few days a year — never the same days, never predicted — the haze thins and the Brightening comes: the domes gleam, the wet mat sparks, you can see the full reach of the Top, dome after dome to a horizon so distant it seems to fall away, and far off the glint of other comb-woods, other settlements you will never visit. Below the Brow, a sea of cloud where the lowlands presumably are. During the Brightening the whole plateau works at double pace — flowers open, the mat grows audibly, almost — and then the gray closes back over and everything returns to its usual dim patience.

The quiet takes newcomers days to understand. Part of it is physics: thin air carries sound poorly, and the wet mat swallows the rest, so that footfalls vanish and a voice goes nowhere. You hear your own blood. You hear the tick of water moving in the drip-mains like a slow clock. The town's engines — and the town is full of engines — you mostly do not hear at all; you feel them, a slow deep turning that comes up through boot soles and bed frames, more weather than machine. People here talk close and low and learn the hand-signs used across the comb fields, and the one scheduled loudness of the day, the brake-whistle of the Fall Line car at midday, is beloved precisely because the world permits so little of it.

The town is called Rime, and it sits on the lee of a broad dome above the great cisterns, and it is dense the way the mat is dense. Cistern-houses stack against each other, lanes run narrow between them following the drip-lines, walls of cured earth-block round their shoulders under caps of tended roof-moss, windows are small and deep and amber. Everything is layered — cistern below, dwelling above, moss over all — and below everything is the under-Rime, the warm dug-in halls where the air is different. Because that is the town's first industry, before water, before anything: breath. At this height the air will not fully serve, and so the great compressor halls — the Lungs — turn day and night, huge slow flywheels at walking pace, enriching the air of the under-town until it is thick and kind. Visitors sleep their first nights below by law. The old retire below. Children are born below and carry deep chests up into the thin world. The engine heat warms the tunnels; the tunnels smell of hot metal and damp wool drying, and in the deep rooms the turning of the Lungs comes through the floor like a heartbeat you learn to sleep inside.

Above ground the comb-woods stand on every dome that faces the fog: long ranks of mesh harps and strung combs, industrial lace, beaded and dripping in the hour before dawn when the harvest peaks. The drip-mains lead down to the cistern stacks; the weigh-house takes account. Water is the wealth and the export — sent down the Fall Line, the incline railway that drops off the Brow into the cloud, water going down and coming back up as metal, salt, glass, and grain, the falling weight of it helping to haul the return car. In the Haggs, the old peat cuttings east of town, they spade fuel in the dry weeks and dig bog-iron nodules the color of rust and dried blood, which the sealed furnaces take for engine fittings. The economy is fog, peat, felt, and breath, and it runs like the engines: slowly, hugely, without hurry and without stopping.

You could walk the Top for a week and see nothing move but weather. There are no birds — the air is too thin and there is nothing for them. Nothing burrows; the mat drowns diggers. There are no fish in the few open pools. The fauna of the plateau can be listed, and is, by every child in Rime. Nine hag-walkers remain — low, shaggy, deliberate grazers the size of ponies, each one known by name, each one's route across the domes tracked and discussed the way other places discuss weather; their slow cropping prunes the mat and shapes it, and a walker passing near town is an occasion. The wool-moths, fat and flightless-seeming until they fly, feed the wool-flower and are fed on in turn by the comb-spiders who wait at the mesh on emergence nights, and the moth nights themselves — once a season, keyed to the Brightening — are the one time the whole town goes deliberately silent, standing in the lanes to listen to ten thousand soft felted wing-beats in the dark. In the cisterns live the pale snails that graze the pink film off the walls and keep the water sweet, and in the cisterns too lives the dark salamander, the plateau's one predator anyone roots for, moving through the snail beds like slow night. Killing a named animal is the one thing worse than stealing water.

For all its stillness, nothing here is static. The fog breathes up and back daily; the harvest swells and fails with it. The seasons turn from the long drizzle to the rime season, when the fog freezes on the combs and every surface furs white and the dim light goes pearly, to the brief bright working days. Saturated domes occasionally slump — a wet cough, a tearing, a slow green slide of turf that can take a comb line with it — and the wardens read the mat for warning the way sailors read sky. The salamanders and snails rise and fall in cycles the cistern-keepers chart like fisheries. And in the Haggs, a few meters a year, the Long Ember creeps: a peat fire lit by accident a century ago and never extinguishable, smoldering sealed and slow through the black ground, mapped, watched, accepted. Children are taken to see the bare warm patch of earth that marks where it is now. It will outlast everyone who looks at it. The town does not consider this a tragedy. The town considers it a neighbor.

The rules are few and old and absolute. Water is held in common; the wardens allocate; to cut another's drip-line is the one true crime. Flame lives only in stone rooms, because the mat can take a smolder and keep it for a hundred years, and everyone has seen where that proof lies. Quiet is custom, not law, but custom holds. The lenses are not opened. The animals are not touched. Visitors sleep below until their chests learn the altitude. The dead are given to the hagg, which takes them gently and keeps them, and the families like it that way — the plateau has been keeping things for a long time, and is good at it.

What you feel, standing on a dome at dusk as the fog comes breathing up over the Brow and the first lamps come on in Rime and the midday whistle is hours gone and the Lungs turn somewhere under your feet, is not desolation and not peace exactly. It is finishedness. This is a place that completed itself — the mountains done eroding, the water done traveling, the engines settled into their final slow rhythm, the town grown into the mat like everything else — and now simply continues, damp and dim and dense and quietly, deliberately alive. Nothing on the Top is becoming anything. Everything on the Top is keeping on. At night, in the thin cold dark, if you stand very still, you can feel the whole of it through your soles: the water creeping, the wheels turning, the ember moving, the fog coming up the edge of the world to be harvested at dawn.
