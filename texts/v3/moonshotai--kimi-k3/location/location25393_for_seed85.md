Three kilometers down, where the last blue died two kilometers above, there is a mountain that has never seen the sky. It rises four thousand meters off the abyssal floor and stops well short of the surface, and its flat summit — a plateau you could walk across in a day — is a high place in a world of low ones. From the plains, it is altitude. From above, it is a shoulder held up out of the dark, with black water going down for miles on every side.

You find it first as a constellation. Sparse points of cold light, unmoving, arranged with a patience that the random dark never manages. You navigate toward it by those lights for hours before you understand they are not scattered through the water column but fixed to a floor, and that the constellation is a town.

The crossing happens before you arrive. A hundred meters off the rim, the sea changes its mind. The slow blizzard of the deep — the marine snow, the drifting motes, the faint cold current — all of it stops at a boundary with no wall in it. Outside: the living ocean, its particles falling, its pressure leaning on your suit with the full weight of three thousand meters. Inside: stillness, water as clear as distilled glass, and a lightness that should not exist. The pressure is not resisted here; it is declined. Somewhere above, the weight of the sea is being carried by something that does not show its work, and you swim through the held gap in a skin-suit, uncrushed, with the faint sense of a storm pressing a window.

The water inside the hold — call it that, it earns the name — is not quite ordinary water. It is cool, cellar-cool, cathedral-cool, uniform to a hundredth of a degree from rim to rim and floor to lamps, and it has a grain. The molecules have been asked to hold still, and they are holding, partway: the liquid has taken on the faint orientation of a crystal without the cold of one. Turn your head with your lamp on and the whole volume sheens, dimly, in sheets, as if the sea were combed. Fins prefer the grain. Gliding along it is silk; crossing it is wading. Every path of least resistance in this world curves the same way — in great slow arcs around the center of the plateau — and everything that lives or was built here has learned the curves.

The floor is the youngest rock you will ever touch. Basalt glass, black, conchoidal, sharp enough to take blood from a careless glove. Pillow lavas swell out of it mid-gesture, frozen at the moment of their formation — literally frozen: the eruption that built this summit was stopped in its first hour and has never been permitted a second. Flow textures run like combed hair and stop mid-strand. Fractures gleam with no rounding, no sediment in their seams, no film of anything alive. The deep sea is supposed to be the great accumulator, the place where the slow snow of the world comes to rest. Here there is no snow on the ground at all. The floor is clean as blown glass the day it cooled — which, in every sense that the rock itself can testify to, was today. The atoms of this mountain do not age. Their isotopes hold their starting proportions like a held breath. The place is not old. It has merely lasted.

The town sits on this newness with the confidence of ownership. It is small — a hamlet by count, a dozen and a half structures — but it is a town in every formal respect: a plan, axes, districts of intent. Streets, if you can call the wide bare esplanades streets, run along the grain-lines and converge, all of them, on the center. The buildings stand far apart. Hundreds of meters of empty glass plaza separate each from the next, and the emptiness is not absence; it is the architecture. Whatever this place holds, it holds at a respectful distance from itself.

The structures are seamless. Not assembled — occasioned, as if the mountain had been talked into them. Surfaces flat past the ability of the eye to find a flaw, frictionless, thermally indifferent; your glove reads them as a temperature that is neither the water's nor your own, as if they had opted out of that conversation. Some stand open: flooded halls you can swim into, tall doorways too narrow for the rooms they serve, proportions fitted to no body you know, interiors of a silence so complete your own blood becomes the loudest thing present. In one open courtyard the water itself has been arranged — a permanent standing ripple, a fingerprint of interference, sharp-edged and motionless, a display of the town's operating state in a script no one reads anymore, or a signature.

Some structures are closed, and closed strangely. Their doorways end the sea. The water stands in the opening like a vertical sheet of cut glass — a meniscus with nothing holding it — and beyond it is darkness and dryness: the only dry rooms in the world. Nothing crosses those thresholds casually. Your light crosses; you could. So far as anyone has ever been able to determine, nothing has.

Each building carries one light, no more — a point or a thin line of cold color at an eave, a doorway, a spire. The light does not attenuate the way light should. A lamp is as bright at two kilometers as at two meters; distance here can only be learned by swimming it, never by looking. The lights are polarized along the grain, so the sheen in the water leans toward each of them, faintly, like iron filings confessing a magnet. And because the water scatters nothing, the lights cast no glow — only illumination where they strike, and everywhere else absolute black. Shadows on the plateau have edges like the fractures in the glass: young, sharp, unsoftened by any atmosphere of scattered light. Between the lamps, the dark is total and clean, sterile as the water. Nothing phosphoresces when you move. There is nothing in the water to spark.

The soundscape is a result, not an absence. Baseline: nothing. No creak of structure — nothing here thermally cycles, nothing settles, nothing works loose. No tick of sediment, no bubble, no groan of pressure, because the pressure is elsewhere being dealt with. What remains is the Note: a single low tone, constant in pitch and amplitude, rising out of the rock at the plateau's center and conducted outward along the grain. It is felt more than heard, a pressure on the chest that you stop noticing and then, on the rare occasions your path crosses a grain-axis dead-on, hear suddenly full in the ear, intimate and directionless, as if the mountain were humming in the next room of the world. Sound travels strangely in the held water — losslessly along the grain, barely at all across it — so the plateau is a place of silences interrupted, very occasionally, by something kilometers away arriving beside you whole: the slow concussion of a great wing, once. Your own breathing, returned to you minutes later by a flat wall along the axis, the world answering after thought. At the rim, if you drift to the boundary and wait, you can hear the outside ocean faintly through it — the crackle and mutter of the living sea, falling snow, distant geology — like a radio left on in a house you have already left.

The fauna are few, old, and specific. There are no plants, no microbes, no rot, no bloom; the water is barren past the point of sterility into something more deliberate. What lives here was grandfathered in. The long resident is a ray thirty meters across, translucent, its organs dim lamps burning at the town's own wavelength, polarized with the grain — camouflage or communion, no one can say. It patrols the rim along a grain-line, one slow circuit every eleven years, grazing the boundary: for the hold refuses foreign matter with an infinite, gentle patience, and whatever drifts in — a mote, a corpse, a visitor's lost glove — is found again, months or decades later, queued at the edge of the stillness to be put back outside. The ray eats from that slow outbox. It is the only scavenger in a place that generates almost nothing to scavenge, and it has been making its rounds longer than any instrument can determine, since its species outside may have already come and gone. Around each lamp hangs a small school of comb-like creatures, a few hundred individuals in all, aligned to the polarized light, sipping — as near as anyone can tell — the orientation itself, the town's one leak of order. They do not breed. They are the same few hundred they have always been, and the lamps wink, very slowly, as they turn. At the threshold of the central structure, something small and armored makes a living as the only commuter, crossing the standing wall of water on errands too slow to witness, once in some span of years, in and then eventually out.

Time, here, is thin. Clocks run. You can swim, breathe, bleed, think. But cuts close reluctantly, hunger arrives late, and nothing — nothing — changes state. The lights have never dimmed. The Note has never wavered. The cool has never drifted a hundredth of a degree. The town is an equilibrium so perfect it reads as a paused frame, except that you are moving inside the pause, an anomaly the stillness tolerates and will, eventually, tidy away. On the seamount's flanks, outside the boundary, the world's slow snow has been falling the whole while, and it has climbed the mountain's sides like a bathtub ring — a band of sediment lapping partway up the slope and stopping where the hold begins. That ring is the only clock the place keeps. It says: very long.

At the center, on the highest point of the summit — a summit on the summit — the streets converge on the widest emptiness of all, a plaza of bare glass a kilometer across, and at its heart the one dark structure. It is a broad, low mound with a shaft going down through it into the mountain, and it is black past blackness: your lamp's beam ends on it the way the sea ends at its threshold, without reflection, without comment. The Note comes up the shaft. The grain spirals out from it. The water at its mouth stands vertical and open, and no probe sent down has returned anything but silence, and the silence has a texture, and the texture is strain. For this is what the evidence assembles itself into: the mountain was in its first hour of eruption when the town was emplaced, and the town is the hand on the mountain's shoulder. The heat that should bleed up through this young rock is not here — it is being declined, exported, carried out of the world to somewhere that is, perhaps, marginally warmer than it should be. The pressure of three kilometers of ocean: declined. The aging of atoms: declined. The eruption: declined, at one millimeter of amplitude, forever, the loudest thing in the world held perfectly quiet. The cool, the stillness, the grain in the water, the Note in your ribs — all of it is the side effect of an enormous, patient refusal. Something down there is mid-gesture, and has been mid-gesture since the sediment ring was a line on bare stone, and the whole small town — the lamps, the streets, the dark mound — exists to keep the moment from finishing.

Who built it is not written here, or is written in the only script present: the ratios of the plan, the prime spacing of the lamps, the curve of the grain. The town may be less a settlement than a sentence — a statement that has been true for a very long time and is still being said.

To be here is to be the only event on the schedule. You move through the sparse cold lights and your shadow crosses floors that have never held a footprint, and the ray completes another degree of its eleven-year circle, and the combs turn at the lamps, and the Note holds, and holds. When you go, you will leave nothing — not a mark, not a mote; the stillness will find whatever you dropped and see it gently to the edge, and the water will close over your path as if combed back into place. It does not remember you. It does not remember anything. Remembering is a kind of change.

From the dark above, looking back, it is small: one page of cold stars, laid flat and high on a mountain's shoulder, with the black of the sunless sea overhead and the black of the abyss below the rim, and the long night of the world on every side — and in the middle of it, patient as a held breath, the youngest town there is, keeping the mountain from finishing its sentence.
