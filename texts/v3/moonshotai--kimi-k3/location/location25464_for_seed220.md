The warmth reaches you first. Long before the stair ends, the air in the shaft turns blood-warm, then bath-warm, and the rock of the walls begins to sweat. Then comes the sound — not heard at first but felt, a slow pressure arriving up through the soles of your boots on the spiral treads: one deep stroke every eleven seconds, patient as a pulse. By the time the hatch wheel turns, the humidity has beaded on your skin, and when the seal cracks, the sound takes you whole.

You step out onto a gallery, and the gallery hangs on the wall of the Vault.

It is not enormous, as deep places go. You could walk its perimeter in an hour. Five hundred paces end to end, a long nave of dark with a shallow crown above and a floor that bowls gently toward one end — an apse of black water down there, the Sump. There are side chambers, service tiers, a walkable boundary all the way around: a hamlet's footprint, a cathedral's volume. But the defining fact of the place is not its size. It is that there is almost nothing in it.

The Vault is a city with the furniture removed. The walls are dense with urbanism — stacked strata of construction, original lining encased in retrofit gantries encased in centuries of patient machine self-repair that has hardened into something between scar tissue and flowstone. Conduits bundle and dive. Junctions are gridded, addressed, labeled — every gantry root carries a resonant boss that rings a particular chord when brushed, because this city was built by listeners, and its street signs are sounds. But the volume itself, the great warm hollow the walls enclose, is void. Fewer than a dozen presences stand in it, minutes of roaring darkness between one and the next. A city of only infrastructure. A city that is mostly a shaped volume of loud warm dark.

The dark has texture, and it is not passive. Bring a lamp and watch its throw die within a few meters, eaten — every surface here drinks light, some old photoabsorptive skin still doing its job after all these ages, harvesting stray photons for a trickle of power or out of pure habit. Nothing in the Vault was ever meant to be seen. The native lights are few and small: deep red standby embers on housings, instrument beads, the faint green ghost that blooms in the floor-felt where it is bruised. Your own footprints glow briefly behind you as you walk, and fade. Within minutes there is no evidence you have ever been anywhere.

What there is, is sound.

The Bass Engine stands in a pit near the Sump, a piston the size of a tower, and its eleven-second stroke is the floor of the world, felt in the sternum more than heard. Along the eastern flank runs the Choir — a bank of hundreds of tuned vanes, and this is the mid-voice, a chord that never stops and never quite repeats, swelling and thinning across the hours in a slow tide of loudness, as if the Vault breathes. Above that, the high tick of thermal contraction, the seep-hiss, and — moving — the metronome of the Verger, the one walking presence, tall and jointed and unhurried, pinging softly as it paces its perpetual round of the perimeter. Its tick is the city's clock, and the echo it gets back is its map.

The echoes are the strange part. Clap once and the Vault returns your clap smoothed, completed, harmonized — answered. The cavern's shape is not accidental. Over millennia the antinodes of the standing waves have spalled and polished the rock where the pressure gathers, while mineral skin has grown undisturbed in the quiet nodes: the city has acoustically eroded itself into a perfect resonator, and the resonator, in turn, has tuned the song. There are pockets of relative quiet in mid-roar, places where you can stand while sound rages around you like the eye of a storm; there are lanes that rattle teeth. The felt grows best along the quiet lanes. Everything here has learned the geometry of the noise.

Water is not a river here. It is a cycle, and it has a liturgy.

The Sump is the only open water — a black lens at the apse end, warm, still, deep. From it the air takes its humidity; the air gives it up at the Crown, the ceiling, where ranks of condenser vanes run cold against all that warmth, gathering droplets on a constellation of drip-points. Those points are mirrored on the floor below: rings of rimstone, little calcareous dams built grain by grain, a star-map of the ceiling written in stone on the ground. Between rain and rain, the place is only damp — weeping seams, glistening films, droplets fattening overhead with astronomical patience.

Then the hour comes.

The Choir thins. Over perhaps ten minutes the great chord sinks toward silence — the only silence the Vault ever permits — and in that held breath you can suddenly hear everything small: the tick of the walls thinking their slow thermal thoughts, the seep of water, the Verger standing still somewhere in the dark. Then the Crown lets go, all of it, at once: a warm soft rain falling from the entire ceiling for an hour, hissing on the warm floor, steaming faintly where it strikes near the Bass Engine, sheet-flowing down the bowl to the Sump. It is the strangest gentle weather — rain indoors, rain on schedule, rain that does, by any instrument you might carry, slightly more work than the heat that drives it accounts for.

During the rain hour, the gaskets come out.

They are the fauna here, or most of it — perhaps forty of them, soft grey toroids from hand-span to arm-span, descendants, by every sign, of engineered living seals: maintenance organisms, once. Now they graze the felt in slow loops along the quiet lanes, flexing a resonance bladder to chirp back at the Verger's tick — a soft blip from the dark, then another, the true population of the city announcing itself at roughly one soul per ten thousand cubic meters. They still exude sealant. It is a minor hazard: stand in one place long enough and something will begin, gently, to fasten your boot to the floor. The Verger will pry you free eventually, without comment, as it pries them off door seams and valve faces, fixtures that have not needed sealing for a thousand years.

And there is one eel.

It lives in the Sump, white, eyeless, as long as the Sump is wide, and it is, as far as anything can be, the original — one more living tool, except singular, except immortal, except that it has become the water it was built to tend. It rises at the rain hour to take any gasket that skates too close to the black edge, and when it rises, the Choir shifts key. The whole city registers the event. There is one eel. There has always been one eel. An instrument by the entrance hatch still counts the rains, in a base no one reads anymore, and the number is large, and the eel predates the first digit.

The warmth is everywhere but not even: furnace-breath near the Bass Engine, a degree cooler in the upper galleries where faint thermals lean past, coolest — comparatively — at the Crown, which drinks the heat and sends it somewhere the surviving plans do not say. There is no dust. Nothing can stay dry enough to be dust. The air tastes of limestone, hot metal, and the faint musk of the felt — that charcoal-grey vegetation, chemosynthetic veils no thicker than cloth, rooted along the warm wet seams and drip-rings, pale tufts at the vent collars. Sparse. You can walk the full perimeter and count the patches on your fingers twice over. It is the base of everything that lives here, and it may also be the city's filter organ; after this long, the distinction between agriculture and anatomy has stopped meaning anything.

At the far end of the nave stands the Plug — a transit gate, sealed, dormant but not dead, humming one low standby note. Once each cycle it tests its bolts: a series of heavy, deliberate clunks that are part of the liturgy, like a door being checked in a house everyone has left. Strike it and the echo comes back doubled — once off the Plug's face, and once, much later, off something far behind it. There is a larger dark back there. Other vaults, perhaps, on a network gone quiet. This one sings alone.

Whatever it was for — refuge, pump station, monastery, seed — the Vault has outlasted the purpose and kept the practice. That is its state now, and it is a settled state: outflows match inflows, the felt grows at the rate it is grazed, deposition matches wear, the rock has shaped itself to the song and the song has tuned itself to the rock. The machines are old in a way that has stopped degrading and started *being* — self-annealing metals, walls that compute slowly with heat and seepage for thoughts, engineering that no longer matches any catalog and no longer needs to.

The rules here are simple, and the place enforces them gently. Bring no bright light; it will be drunk. Speak if you like; you will be answered, improved. Keep moving, or be sealed. Mind the antinodes when the tide swells. When the Choir thins, rain is coming — find a drip-ring, stand in it, and look up into the dark you cannot see.

After the rain, the Choir re-swells over an hour, the tide coming in. The Verger resumes its round, tick and echo, tick and echo. A gasket chirps from the felt beds. Your glowing footprints have already faded behind you; the mineral rings have taken their thin new layer; the Sump has closed over the eel like an eye. The Vault folds your visit the way it folds everything — into the sound, which does not stop, which has not stopped, which is the one thing the emptiness is full of.
