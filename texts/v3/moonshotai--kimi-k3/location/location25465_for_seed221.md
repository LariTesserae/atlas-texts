**The Curing Loft**

High in the works — high enough that the wind never stops its low moan against the outer plates — there is a room the builders walked out of on the last day of construction, and the last day has not ended. It is a small room, a maintenance void really, a pocket left between the crown of the compressor stack and the tower's cold skin, and it has been being finished ever since. Everything in it is made. There is no stone, no soil, nothing older than the pour. And the pour is still wet.

That is the first strangeness: the concrete has never cured. The walls are formwork and the grey, soft, sweating mixture behind it, held forever at the consistency of the afternoon it was placed — because the cold arrests the chemistry, and the chemistry's faint heat feeds the cold engine, and the engine's vibration keeps the mix agitated and workable, and so the loop closes and the walls remain exactly as unborn as the day they were poured. The plywood forms were never struck. You can read the grain of the shuttering pressed into the surface like a fossil that hasn't decided to happen. Tie-wires still bristle, clipped but untwisted. A chalk line hangs taut across one corner, snapped never, its blue dust still waiting to fall.

The cold is total and the wet is total, and they coexist because the water here is not innocent. What skins the floor and weeps down the plates is a brackish condensate — machine water, salted with the tower's chemistry, its freezing point pressed below the room's deep chill — and what still might freeze is kept restless by the sound. Nothing in this room is still enough to freeze. The water lies in a shallow, opalescent sheet over the floor, trembling, slightly viscous with moth-scale, and it climbs a finger's width up the downhill wall, because down here is not quite down.

The tower leans. Not much — a few degrees, constant, the wind of altitude pressing it into a permanent bow — but inside the loft the lean is law. A plumb bob on a string hangs at its slant and has never hung otherwise; the drips fall at the same small angle off true, every one, forever; the waterline stains run parallel to the bob's string. The moths know no other gravity. To live here is to stand very slightly crooked and never know it.

And the sound. The room is not a place that has a sound; it is a sound that has a room. The compressor's bass is the root of it, a note felt in the teeth and in the soft floor through the soles of the boots. Over it, the hiss of the arc — a welding arc stands at an open seam on the stack casing, a small blue permanent lightning, struck on the last day and never withdrawn, the bead forever almost laid, the seam forever almost welded. Over that, the percussion: the condensate drips, each one phase-locked to the whole, falling at the slant into resonance trays that someone tuned, once, as a joke or a diagnostic, so the room plays its own water — a slow, exact, unvarying drumline. And through and under all of it, the shimmer of the moths.

The moths are the overwhelming fact. Thumbprint-sized, slate-pale, furred like the lagging wool they nest in — perhaps partly made of it, by now; the distinction was given up long ago. They do not rest, because stillness is fatal here: they shiver, every one of them, constantly, vibrating their flight muscles to hold their small warmth against the cold, tens of thousands of them, and the sum of their shivering is the high luminous hiss that rides the chord. The walls are upholstered with them. The true texture of every surface is moth. They cluster thickest over the engine casing in a breathing gray blanket, a living cozy that drinks the machine's waste heat and gives back, with their shed scales, the fine dry dust that lubricates its bearings. Where the sound field cancels, they hang in the air itself: standing swarms, lens-shaped, motionless as ornaments, each one a clot of furious wings holding a shape that has not changed in unguessable time. The mist hangs with them, in the same places, in horizontal bands at chest height and head height — sheets of suspended droplets pinned by the chord, so the room has weather, indoor weather, always the same weather, strata of wet and dry you would pass through one after another if anyone ever walked here.

On the cold outer plates the sound writes itself in ice. Where the vibration stills, frost grows: pale ferns of rime in bands and islands. Where it doubles, the steel weeps bare. Frost and weep alternate across the wall in a fixed zebra — the chord's fingerprint, the room's single note made visible. An acoustician could read the pitch off the plating. The moths graze the weep-lines, drinking, and along the weep-lines and in the lamplight grows the only vegetation this place permits: a thin grey-green film, an alkaline garden fed by lime sweated from the forever-curing concrete, threadlike in the best-lit corners, sparse and patient, growing exactly as fast as it is eaten. The moths eat the film. The film eats the dead moths. The loop has no slack in it.

The water skin on the floor is not quite water either. It seethes, gently, at the limit of sight: the fry — translucent larvae, thumbprint-small, born larvae and dead larvae, the adults of a species that does not exist in this world. Nothing here finishes becoming. At the drip-points on the ceiling, the lime-rich condensate has begun to build the very first rings of stalactites — one molecule's worth of intention, a halo, a start — and has begun them eternally. A cave's infancy, held in utero, in a cave that was manufactured.

The light is dim and never moves. Pilot lamps burn low and orange behind glass hazed to a fixed opacity with scale-dust; none has ever failed and none has ever been changed. The arc is the brightest thing, a blue point no larger than a tear, and it has stood so long that the moths orbit it in slow, shivering rings, warming themselves on a weld that will never close. Every shadow in the room is painted still. Nothing flickers. Nothing has ever flickered.

There are human traces, all of them dated to the last day, which is every day. A logbook open on a shelf of formwork, its pages damp, its pencil at rest, its entries identical: *steady*. A mug on the compressor housing, placed, by chance or design, at a dead spot in the sound field, so the tea inside froze long ago — froze mid-tremor, and holds on its surface the fossilized ripple of the chord, the note's waveform preserved in brown ice. A second engine stands in the corner half-assembled under canvas and cosmoline, parts stacked in order of use, waiting for a crew that is, in some sense, still just about to arrive.

In the center of the floor, the grate. Everything the room cannot use goes there: the frass, the shed scales, the small pale dead, the drips that miss the trays. Through the grate the tower opens downward into a depth of humming dark, and it is always gently snowing there — a fine, slow, endless fall, drifting down the throat of the works at the slant of the leaning gravity, falling farther than hearing. No landing has ever been heard. The cold up-draft that rises in exchange smells of iron and distance, and it is how you know you are high: not by any view — there is no window, the pocket does not disclose itself — but by the depth of the hum below and the thinness of the air and the ears that popped once and stayed popped.

The rules of the world are few and absolute. The last day never ends. Down leans slightly and no one may notice. Stillness is death; everything alive here works without rest to remain exactly as it is. The chord governs — frost, mist, swarm, drip and drum all answer to the one held note, and the note is deafening, and because it never changes it is also the room's only silence: the moths have never heard anything else, and so, in the only sense that matters, neither has the room. Nothing is wasted, nothing accumulates, nothing completes.

Nothing here has ever happened. Everything here is about to. The arc hisses at the open seam, the moths turn in their rings, the concrete sweats against its forms, and the curing loft — hidden, high, brimming, brand-new and ancient beyond reckoning — remains forever on the verge of the first morning after construction.
