The basin sits near the top of a long forested shoulder, where the mountain has almost finished being a mountain and has begun, instead, to be weather. You climb to it through cool upland forest — wind-combed, lichen-bearded, the trees hunched and narrowing as you rise — and the whole way up, the wind takes sounds apart. Birdsong arrives shredded. Your own steps are stolen off the ground. Then, at a certain rise where the slope should continue and doesn't, you come over a low rim of mossy boulders and pale stone, and the world inverts.

The wind is outside now. You are inside.

Inside is a green room open to the sky, maybe sixty paces across, bounded on every side but one: the boulder wall you crossed, a seep-face of dark rock along the uphill flank, a sloping apron of cream-and-rust flowstone on the downhill flank, and a single notch where the water leaves. Through that notch, the only view: the long drop of the forested mountainside, and far below, a valley with a river in it like a hair laid on cloth. The elevation is in that gap. Everything else in the bowl faces inward.

The first thing that meets you is the warmth. It has no drama to it — no steam, no reek of sulfur — it is simply that the air in the bowl is at blood temperature and the air outside is not. The second thing is the sound.

The water arrives from three directions at once, and each direction has its own voice. From below: the spring, welling up through the bed of the pool at the bowl's center — rising so evenly that the surface does not break, does not even ripple, only dims now and then as the sand beneath breathes in slow cones, exhaling and settling. The pool is a mirror that occasionally thinks about moving. From across: the seep-face, a wall that weeps along its whole length, water coming out of the mountain sideways through moss a hand's thickness deep, gathering on a ledge, and falling as drips — dozens of drips, each with its own patient interval. And from above: the air itself, which is at dew point, always, so that every leaf and frond and stone is slowly condensing the atmosphere onto itself and raining it off its tips. It rains here without rain. Nothing in this bowl has ever been dry.

The drips are the music. Each one falls a different height onto a different thing, and so each has its own note: into the pool, a soft dark gulp; onto the moss, nothing at all; into the small stone cups the slowest drips have carved for themselves over centuries, a bright wooden *tok* that carries like a struck instrument. The intervals are long — a breath, two breaths, half a minute for the high ones — but the wall is wide and the sources many, and the bowl does something to the sound. The flowstone apron curves like the back of an instrument, the seep-face is hard and concave, the moss floor swallows footfalls whole. The result is a room with two acoustics: your body moves in silence, and the water plays in cathedral. A single drip falls, and you hear it three times — the fall, the return off the apron a second later, and then, improbably, another drip somewhere across the bowl that seems to answer it. Listen long enough and the whole basin phases in and out of slow chords, coincidences of water that almost resolve into rhythm and then drift apart again. It is the loudest quiet place you will ever stand in. The mountain outside is a hush of torn wind; the bowl is a held breath that keeps choosing, gently, to speak.

The green is total and the green things are few. That is the strange economy of the place. Every surface is furred, filmed, cushioned — moss over everything, ferns unscrolling from the seep-face, the flowstone patched with algae in rust and emerald — and yet the living presences stand apart from one another with room around each, like guests at a small gathering who have arranged themselves courteously. Three tree ferns, spaced across the bowl, each one tall enough to comb the passing mist and drip from every frond-tip, each standing in a ring of its own slow private rain. One broadleaf tree near the notch, leaning outward toward the drop, as if eavesdropping on the valley. A handful of boulders, canted, warm to the touch on the pool side and moss-furred on the far side — because the bowl breathes, a nearly still exhalation of warm air rising off the water and sliding up and out, and the moss has learned which way the breath blows. Many of the plants here belong a thousand meters lower, in the warm wet lowland forest. They should not be able to live at this height. The spring has made the bowl a thermal island, a pocket of the lowlands lifted up and set on the mountain's shoulder, and the plants have found it the way seeds find everything — by accident, across centuries — and stayed.

Underfoot, away from the flowstone, the floor is moss over a deep soft accumulation of everything the bowl has ever dropped, held together and slowed by the wet. Fallen fronds that drift into the apron's shallow water do not rot; the warm water coats them, grain by grain, in pale stone, and they go down into the growing floor of the place as exact mineral replicas of themselves. The apron is partly made of fossilized litter. The bowl is recording itself in stone, a page a decade, and has been at it long enough that the whole downhill flank is its archive — terraced micro-rims and coin-sized basins descending in a staircase to the notch, each little pool overtopping into the next, which is the source of the one continuous voice in the bowl, the small bright speech of the outlet stair beneath the slow percussion of the wall.

Nothing here hurries. The water that enters is the water that leaves; the pool holds its level through the year, rising a finger's width in the misting season when the weeping wall speaks along its whole length, falling back in the clearer months until the wall narrows its speech to a few persistent veins and the drip-rhythms slow like a heart resting. The flowstone grows a fingernail's thickness a year. The notch deepens a hair's width a century. Events are so rare they would be dated if anyone were here to date them: the year a boulder let go of the rim — no boulder has let go of the rim. The bowl is in the middle of its life, in balance, and its long future is legible to anyone patient enough to read the floor: it is filling with its own patience, moss-peat and mineral and coated leaves, and in some deep unhurried millennium it will be a meadow-hollow, then a knoll, the warm spring still rising through it, unbothered, addressed to nobody.

The animals are few enough that you can sit for an hour and watch a single snail be the only thing that moves. The snails are large and unhurried and graze the moss in silver handwriting that dries to nothing by the next condensation. Under the seep-face there is a salamander the color of the wet wall, which you will not see. At dawn a bird comes down out of the wind-torn forest to bathe at the pool's lip, and its bathing is an event, and its few notes go around the bowl like something being passed hand to hand. And there is a frog — one species, perhaps one individual, no one could say — that calls a few times an hour, a single round note, and the bowl takes that note and multiplies it and gives it back so enlarged and prolonged that the frog, wherever it is, must live inside a version of its own voice far grander than itself. It is the loudest animal on the mountain. It is three grams of animal, somewhere in the moss, being played by a room.

A person with stone in their hands would find the place unnervingly prepared. The rimstones of the little basins ring when struck, each a different tone. Hollows in the flowstone hold water at exactly the shape of cupped palms. Rounded stones in the pool's shallows, turned for centuries in the spring's slow sand-stirring, fit the grip as if measured. The terraced apron descends like laid masonry; the ledge below the seep-face runs level as a workbench; the whole bowl is tuned like something intended. Nothing here was made. There is no artifact, no mark, no hand's work of any kind — and yet the place has the quality of a workshop kept swept and ready for a craftsperson who has not yet existed, all the tools present as potentials, all the patience already installed.

By midday the sun clears the rim and stands over the bowl, and for a few hours the place is in full normal daylight: the weeping wall glittering along its whole length, the pool showing its one deep blue-green eye where the water wells up, the tree ferns dripping in the sun with no cloud overhead. Then the rim's shadow begins its slow advance across the floor, and the warmth of the spring asserts itself against the cooling air, and mist begins to form and unform along the lip of the bowl where the mountain's evening slides over. In and out, the mist, at the boundary — the place breathing its warmth up into the cool, making a small local weather of the disagreement.

When you leave, you leave through the notch or over the boulder rim, and the threshold is exact: one step, and the resonance stops like a door closing. The wind takes you back. Behind you, below the rim, invisible now, the drips continue their slow phasing, the snail continues its sentence, the pool rises without rippling, the stone grows. Perhaps the frog calls. Perhaps the bowl holds the note a moment and gives it back, late and enlarged, to no one — an answer to a question asked in an empty, green, patient room near the top of the world.
