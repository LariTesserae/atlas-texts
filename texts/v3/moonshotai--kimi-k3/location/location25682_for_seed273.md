From the basin floor, in the hour before the heat haze thickens, the Condensary reads first as geography — a paleness stacked against the sky, tier over tier, climbing out of the warm murk into hard clarity. Only after a while does the eye admit what it is seeing: every line is laid. Every edge is intended. The terraces step upward for as far as the haze lets you follow, and at the crest, where the air thins and the light turns metallic, fields of silver vanes catch the first sun before the basin has even brightened. On very clear days you can make out two others at the limit of sight — sibling works, faint as watermarks on the horizon — which is how you understand the scale of the thing: it is not a building. It is a region, poured and bolted and whitewashed into place, and whatever mountain may stand inside it as armature has not been touched in living memory. There is no natural surface anywhere on it. There is only the made.

You approach across the Apron — a plain of outwash concrete, consolidated slag, and brick rubble rolled flat by centuries of its own weight — and the first water you meet is a seam of moss in a joint between slabs, fed by a seep that has been dripping since before anyone's grandmother. This is the pattern of the whole place: water in pockets, in seams, in the dark hearts of walls, while the bright outer skin dries in the sun. The materials announce themselves slowly. Mass concrete gone soft-edged as old soap on the stairs. Iron patinated to a stable plum-brown that no longer flakes. Copper gone green, tile gone chalky, timber gone silver and iron-hard. Handrails polished by ten thousand hands. Nothing looks new, and everything works — that is the equilibrium the Condensary has settled into, repair and decay matched so evenly that the place has stopped aging in any way you could mark in a lifetime.

Water is the doctrine here, and the doctrine is condensation. There are no rivers. What the Condensary drinks, it drinks at night: the great vane fields and the strung dew-harps at the crest cool after dark, and the warm air rising off the basin gives up its moisture onto them, bead by bead, running down the wires to the drip-tips, into gutters, down through cistern doors painted with their names and gauge marks. Below a certain altitude — the tenders call it the Dew Line — the nights stay too warm to wring the air, and the lower tiers live on what is pumped back up to them and on the seep of what leaks down. A few times a year comes warm rain, and that is a feast: overflow channels opened on purpose, cisterns topped, dust washed off a thousand gardens, and a frog chorus so loud you shout to be heard over it. Otherwise, nothing leaves. Water arrives as vapor and leaves as vapor, and in between it is owned, owed, rationed by gravity. The great pump in the deep halls takes its stroke every four minutes, a bass concussion you feel in your teeth, lifting the night's harvest back up to where it is needed. Water always moves down here, and is always owed upward.

The climb takes a day and a half by stair, four hours by the Slow Cars — the funiculars you board with lunch — and the zones unspool like climates. The skirts are hottest, haziest, densest: compost vaults and workshops, hens underfoot, dovecotes in the eaves of every landing, the smell of hot oil and bread and wet concrete layered together. The middle tiers are the town proper — named landings, a market, bells marking shifts and weather, cables laced so thickly with wisteria and grape that you cannot tell which is carrying load and which is carrying fruit. Here are the Warm Benches, masonry seats built over the ventilation flues, always warm, occupied in shifts by old people and lizards who regard each other with the same patience. Higher, the air thins and brightens and the gardens give way to the harp fields — a forest of strung wire and finned silver vanes, dry and ticking in the noon glare, the brightest and driest place on the whole structure, and the wettest after dark. And inside everything, behind the terraces, are the vaults: the Cistern Choir, cool and dark and enormously loud, where the tenders move drip-pans to keep the falling water in chord, where pale fish cruise the still water of the grand cistern, and where the drone of the great engine lives in your ribcage whether you want it there or not.

The ecology follows the drip-lines the way vegetation elsewhere follows rivers. Where water drips: lichen, then moss, then ferns hanging in curtains, then vine, then fig — figs root in cistern silt and slowly split the paving, and are allowed, because the fruit is worth the repointing. Where the sun owns the face: lichen fields in orange and grey-green, then grass, then thyme and herbs baking aromatic on the hot ledges. And the soil itself is made — compost from the deep vaults, guano from the dovecotes, silt washed out of the gutters, ground slag for body. Soil is not found here; it is issued. As for animals, the place is a reef that happens to be an engine. Swifts nest by the thousand in the drain-holes and hold their screaming parties along the faces at dusk. Kestrels hang all day in the Plume, the standing warm updraft the structure makes of its own waste heat. Geckos work the warm walls at night, goats work the ledges by day, tree frogs hold title to every cistern, dragonflies patrol the seep-basins, bees both kept and feral argue over the seep-flowers, and cicadas — at noon, the cicadas are a wall.

Because it is loud. Loud the way a market is loud, and slow the way a cathedral is slow. Before dawn there is only the drip-solo in the vaults and the deep note of the engine. Dawn brings the birds, a glitter of small fast sounds. Midmorning, the cable-song of a passing lift, horn calls traded across two kilometers of face — long, melancholy blasts like the structure talking in its sleep — goat bells, the clatter of the Bread Landing. Noon is the cicada wall over the engine drone, punctuated every four minutes by the pump's great stroke. Dusk ticks: a thousand tons of metal cooling. Night is nearly quiet, except in the seventh year, the rest year, when the engine is let down to idle for its deep maintenance, and the tenders say the silence itches, that the whole population sleeps badly until the note returns.

The light is a managed resource, like the water. High thin air, hard shadows, whitewash renewed each year until the south faces are painful to look at. Apprentice mirror-tenders keep the heliostats aimed, lending sunlight down into the shaded gardens, and the glints double as signals — a grammar of flashes spelling weather and news across the tiers. The air itself breathes on schedule: upslope wind by day as the structure heats, downslope by night, and the harp fields and vanes are tuned to that breathing, strung and angled to it. It is warm — warmer than it has any right to be at this height, held so by sun-trap terraces, by heat-sink masonry, by the machine-warmth bleeding through the floors — and the whole place smells, in layers, of wet concrete, ozone, jasmine, hot metal, and bread.

The tenders' rules are few and absolute. Dew that falls on you is yours; water that falls past you belongs to the cistern. Trace a vine to its root before you cut it. Rust that holds is left alone; rust that fails is fought to the death. Light may be borrowed but never wasted. And the engine does not stop. What the engine was originally commissioned to do — beyond the pumping, beyond the dynamos humming behind their copper doors — no one now remembers; the ledger of what it was built to earn is lost, and only the manual of its tending remains, and that is apparently sufficient. Decisions here are made on the scale of decades. A vine takes ten years to train. A rust-patch is a generational opponent. Children learn the ledges before they learn letters. No one builds anything new; the knowledge of making has narrowed, over the centuries, into the knowledge of keeping.

Stand at the crest at dawn and you can watch the place do the thing it is for. The harp fields, dripping all night in near-unison, go silver with a million beads at first light — for an hour the whole structure wears its water like jewelry — and then the sun strengthens, the breeze turns upslope, the beads dry, the first horn calls rise from the tiers below, and the great pump, four minutes on, four minutes on, keeps its patient count. Everything fast here is small: swifts, bees, lizards, children. Everything large is slow: the water, the warmth, the rust, the engine, the keeping. The Condensary drinks at night and sweats by day, and holds.
