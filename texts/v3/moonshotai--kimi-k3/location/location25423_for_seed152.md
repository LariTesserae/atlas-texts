From the plateau, it is nothing.

The country up there is dry and enormous — a long low basin floor stretching to hazy ridges, grass the color of straw, heat lifting off the stone in visible threads. You could walk it for days and find only space. But near the bottom of a shallow fold, where the land dips toward its own lowest point, the green changes. A single seam of deeper color runs along a crack in the limestone, and at dusk the swallows give it away: hundreds of them stitching the air above one spot, then dropping, one by one, straight into the ground.

The crack is barely wide enough to fall through. Below it, the hillside opens.

A cave roof came down here not long ago — not long in the stone's reckoning, a few centuries perhaps — and left a room half-open to the sky. A slot of daylight above, a slant of collapsed blocks below, and at the back, under the remaining roof, a grotto that never sees the sun directly. The descent takes you over the breakdown, and the first thing you notice is the temperature: the air turns warm and close, ten degrees kinder than the plateau wind, and damp against the face. The hillside is breathing on you. The second thing you notice is the quiet. Sound does not carry in. The wind crosses the slot overhead and stays outside, a rumor. Down here, you can hear water arrive one drop at a time.

The light is dim but not dead. For most of the day the chamber glows indirectly — a warm, gray-gold gloom bounced down off pale walls. Then, for an hour or two, the slot above admits a single shaft that crosses the room like a slow hand on a dial, rakes the back wall, slides across the pools, and is gone. Everything in the place is arranged around that hour.

And everything in the place is new.

This is the strangest fact of the sink, and it takes a moment to understand. Ordinary landscapes are being worn away; this one is being built. The water that seeps from the back of the grotto is warm and freighted with dissolved limestone, and the moment it surfaces and breathes, it begins to set. Stone precipitates out of it the way frost precipitates out of cold air. Where the water threads down the collapsed blocks, it lays a rind of cream-colored calcite over them, a glaze that thickens year by year, sealing the rubble into a single growing mass. Where the flow slows, it builds dams — rimstone, the geologists would call it — low curled walls with edges as crisp as the lip of new pottery, each one impounding a pool the size of a bathtub, or a dinner plate, or a cup. The pools brim. Each spills into the next by a single shining thread.

Nothing here is rounded. Nothing is weathered. The fallen blocks are still sharp as the day they dropped. The rims are still sharp. There is no soil to speak of except what the birds have carried in. The whole chamber smells of wet lime — the smell of stone being born, which is not a smell of decay at all, but of something clean and faintly sweet, like rain on hot concrete a moment before the concrete was ever poured. Time runs the other way here. Every year the sink is more itself.

The water budget is modest — a seep, not a spring, a thread not a stream. Most of the chamber is merely damp: filmed stone, glistening moss, air you could drink. The standing water gathers in the terraced pools and in a few deep pockets among the blocks, and then escapes through a crack at the far end and is gone. But the sink makes more weather than it receives. At night the warm wet air rises and meets the cold overhang of the grotto roof, and beads there, and at first light it falls back down — a slow interior rain, a few hundred drops a minute, ticking onto the same districts every dawn. This is the loudest regular event in the place. The dawn rain and the evening swallows. Between them, mostly, silence.

The green lives where the wet lives, and it lives in islands. Along the seep-lines the stone wears velvet — moss so dense it holds water like a held breath. In the drip zone beneath the overhang, maidenhair ferns stand in spaced clumps, each one its own small fountain of black stems, with bare pale stone between them and the next. Rust-orange and emerald ribbons stain the rims of the pools: the microbial mats, the algae and bacteria that do the actual masonry, laying down calcite grain by grain, building the dams they live on, a town planned at the scale of slime. And in the one place where the daily shaft of sunlight comes to rest longest, a single fig tree stands rooted in the rubble — bird-sown, decades old, its canopy pressed up into the light like a green roof over the middle of the room. Beneath it, a circle of leaf-dapple. Its shadow is the clock of the lower town.

Because it is a town. Stand on the last of the dry blocks and the reading is unavoidable. The terraces descend in long curves like streets contouring a hillside. The pools are plazas, each with its rimmed promenade. The fig is the square. The seep-lines are the water mains; the overhang's drip-line, an aqueduct that runs on a nightly schedule. Under the back roof, densest of all, hangs the residential quarter: several hundred mud nests, gourd-shaped, packed shoulder to shoulder against the stone — the swallows' tenements, plastered up mouthful by mouthful from mud mined at the outflow, old nests layered under new ones, rebuilt, subdivided, inherited. Construction upon construction. Nothing here was ever drawn on a plan, and yet the place has districts, a waterfront, a dense core, a green center, and — at the back, in darkness, where the water has not yet reached — an unbuilt quarter: bare collapsed blocks lying in shadow, sharp, pale, waiting. Half the room is empty. The emptiness is part of it.

The tenants are abundant, and they are all small, and they all work quietly. The swallows are the visible citizenry, out at dawn, back at dusk in a raining of wings. Bats own the night shift, pouring out of the deep cracks to work the air above the pools. Frogs the size of a thumbnail sit the rims like night watchmen and emit, very occasionally, one note each, unanswered. Dragonfly nymphs stalk the pool floors; the adults hunt the shaft of light when it comes. Water boatmen row the still water. In the outflow thread, caddisfly larvae build themselves cases from sand grains and shell fragments — masonry at the millimeter scale, the stonemasons living in the gutter, indistinguishable from the street they repave. A snake keeps a ledge near the swallow quarter and eats well and slowly. Once in a while, down the slot from the dry world outside, a heron drops in on stilts and stands in a pool with outrageous patience, and the whole town holds still around it, and then it goes, taking one frog and leaving tracks in the soft mud of the rim.

And here is the thing about those tracks: they are still there. They are under a glaze now, sealed beneath a season's deposit of new stone, but they are there, filed. This is what it means that nothing erodes. The sink does not forget. Every disturbance is archived rather than erased — a heron's footprints, a swallow's feather caught in a rim and entombed, the silt signature of every flood year laid between denser layers of drought-year calcite. Cut the mound open and it would read like annals. The town keeps its records in its own substance, and its substance grows.

It grows in pulses. Most days, construction proceeds at the pace of a rim thickening by a film — slow, silent, continuous. But when rain finally comes to the plateau, maybe a few times a year, maybe once in three years, the sink drinks. Water finds the old cave passages and flushes them; the seep becomes a voice; the pools overtop every rim at once and run milky with sediment, and in those nights the terraces accrete measurably, the rims rising like bread. Sometimes a dam breaches and a district rearranges itself in an hour — demolition, the one loud thing that ever happens here — and then the water subsides and the sealing begins again, and the new configuration is glazed, archived, incorporated. The town demolishes rarely and rebuilds always. Across a human lifetime a dam grows high enough to overtop, and a pool is born where there was a wall, and an old pool silts quietly into a moss garden, and the districts shift their uses without consulting anyone, because no one is in charge. The water decides. The mats interpret. The swallows rezone.

The rules of the place are few and they are strict:

Everything arrives from elsewhere. The water from deep stone, the light through the slot, the seeds on feathers, the mud in beaks, the warmth from the sun-struck rock of the collapse. Nothing originates here except stone.

Nothing wears away. There is no erosion in the sink; there is only accretion. What is made, stays. What happens, is kept.

Construction is silent and constant. Demolition is loud and rare.

And the last rule is for visitors, if any come — and almost none do, though the herders on the plateau have a name for the place, something that means the warm mouth, and they say their goats will not descend. The rule is this: walk the dry stone. The living crust is soft, and a footprint does not fade here. It is preserved. You would not be leaving a mark so much as filing a document, entering yourself into the archive of a town that is not yours, that predates your tools, that has no use for them, that is building itself by breath and drip and wingbeat in the dark — slowly, warmly, in near-perfect silence, and is nowhere near finished.
