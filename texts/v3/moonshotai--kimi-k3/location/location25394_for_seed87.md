The whole of it would fit beneath the floor of a single room, and no one standing above it would know.

On a polar coast where the sea ice fastens itself to the shore and stays, there is a shallow pocket — a kettle the glacier gouged and abandoned, flooded and then lidded. Rafting floes piled over it and froze into a ceiling a body's thickness deep, and snow drifted on top of that, and the snow swallowed the last of the sound. Below the lid: a bowl of raw stone, dim green water, and a city. The only way in or out is a crack in the rim of the bowl, wide as a fist, through which the tide breathes twice a day. The citizens call nothing by name, because names are sounds and sounds are not made here. If it were named, it would be called the Work, because that is what it is and what it does.

Everything in the pocket is water in one of its tempers: the lid is water standing still, the world is water moving slowly, the builders are water that has learned to lay stone.

The light is the color of deep bottle-glass, sourceless, as though the water itself were faintly lit rather than lit from anywhere. No one here has ever seen the sun. They know it only as a warming of the ceiling's hue, a seasonal brightening of the dim. In winter the dim fails almost entirely, and then the city shows its seams — faint blue threads of light in the mortar, which I will explain, because they are also the city's wounds.

The cold is a held note. The water stands just this side of ice, kept liquid by its salt, and at the ceiling where the two waters meet there is a skin of slush that crusts over anything that rests too long against it. Down at the floor it is a fraction warmer — measurably, to those who live by fractions — because ten thousand small metabolisms leak their heat into the lanes. The districts have microclimates. A tongue's-width of warmth divides the inner city from the wilderness at the bowl's rim.

The floor is young. The glacier left only centuries ago and the basin has not yet learned to be soft: boulders sit where they were dropped, angular, unapologetic, still sharp at the edges; gravel lies unrounded; the silt plain between the stones is thin as a first coat of paint. Nothing is weathered. Nothing is finished. This is true of the geology and it is true of the city, and the city would have it no other way.

The builders are wrights — worms no longer than a fingernail paring, each crowned with a fan of feeding feathers, each with a gland at its collar that weeps cement. They build the only way anything can be built here: with the body. A wright takes a mouthful of silt, grades the grains with its jaws, rejects what is wrong, and lays what is right against the lip of its tube with a dab of cement. Then the next grain. Then the next. Nothing in this world has ever been picked up to be used. Stones are moved, never wielded. The only engine is a muscle; the only glue, a gland; the only crane, a mouthful.

And from grains, a city. The tubes fuse where they touch, and where they fuse they are reinforced, and where they are reinforced more tubes climb, and so the Work rises from the floor in mounds and towers and arches, a forearm to an arm's length high, covering a footprint a person could walk around in half a minute. To its citizens it is a metropolis. It has districts.

The Foundation is the oldest mound, half-buried now in its own descendants, its ground floors long since filled in and forgotten — except they are not forgotten, they are rented, because the buried tubes of the dead are the city's basements, and a whole under-class of tenants lives in the layered archaeology. The dead here are load-bearing. Every citizen is born on the roof of its grandmother's tube, and the grandmother's grandmother is a wall, and the wall's mother is a foundation stone. An address is a lineage: third roof of the fourth line, where the mortar tastes of rust.

The Bright Lattice is the new quarter, and it gleams — literally. Fresh fractures and young cement host a dim luminous bacteria, and where the Work is broken or still curing, it glows a faint blue. The light is a message: here I am hurt, here I am unfinished. Repair crews find their assignments by navigating toward the glow, and so the Lattice, being newest, is the brightest district, a scaffold lit by its own becoming. There is a law here, though no one wrote it: a wound must be answered. No one walls over a light. The light is answered or the city falls.

The light is also lied to. Somewhere in the Narrows — the over-built quarter where the tubes crowd thickest and the tenants poorest — there lives a small scraping thing that has learned to claw the mortar and wait. The false-lighter makes a wound it has no intention of answering, and when the curious come drifting toward the glow, it eats them. The city has no defense against this except a taste left on the doorposts: a chemical warning, refreshed by neighbors, that reads *the light here is a lie*. For the city is literate, though it has no sounds and almost no eyes. Messages are written in taste on the thresholds — lineage marks, debts, warnings, courtships. A fish reads a doorframe with its lips. The water of the pocket is a slow library, and every current carries the news.

The streets are water. The wrights orient their towers against the flow, and ten thousand pumping mouths draw the pocket's water through the lanes in slow, predictable rivers: feeding lanes that bring the plankton in from the throat, cleaning lanes that carry the waste away to the Middens, where boroughs of small sorting things pick it to nothing. You do not walk to work in this city. You lean into the correct river and are delivered. Traffic is plankton. The rush hours are tidal — twice a day the throat draws in a long breath of cold, rich, open-sea water, and twice a day exhales, and the whole city leans into the inhale like a field leaning into wind.

About the throat: there is a grove beside it, the only trees in the world. A handful of tall blades of cold-water algae stand there where the light through the crack is strongest, and they are famous in the way that the only grove in a world is famous. Vegetation is sparse everywhere and singular: the nine trees; two boulders near the rim wearing crusts of red algae like rust-roses; the green patina-fuzz that softens only the oldest districts, so that in the Work the new is pale and the ancient is green. And the meadows — but the meadows are on the ceiling.

The underside of the ice grows a brown-green film of ice-algae, an upside-down pasture, and it is grazed by herds that never touch the floor in their lives. The ceiling-born — small amphipods, certain flatworms, one pale fish that cruises belly-to-sky — live their whole span inverted, walking on the sky, cropping it. For them the law of the world is different, and the law is acknowledged: the ceiling is not a floor, except for those born to it. In spring the meadows bloom and the light coming through them goes brown, and the herds rise thick as dust, and the floor-city below looks up at weather made of grazers.

The other citizens crowd everything. Density is the condition of the Work: tubes on tubes, every surface tenanted, eggs glued into every seam, anemones planted like gardens across the rooftops of the Garden district — bright, slow flowers that are animals, because in this world the fauna does much of the flora's work. Moonlings — small pale fish with coin-wide eyes — den in the galleries and pay their rent in guard-duty, snapping up the mites that overgraze the wright crowns. Ember-slugs, nudibranchs the color of banked coals, creep the lanes as the only brightness in the dim, beautiful and predatory. And over it all, at every hour, the motes: amphipods in their thousands, the crowd, the snow that falls upward as often as down, the recyclers, the audience, the traffic of flesh that fills the water between the towers the way pigeons fill a plaza.

In summer the throat admits the bells — drifting medusae that pulse slowly through the lanes, trailing fine nets, feeding on the mote-snow, harmless to anything with a shell. The city does not mind them. They are weather that is also wildlife, and when one brushes a tower, the tower's wrights simply withdraw their crowns and wait for it to pass, the way a street pauses for a slow parade.

Winter is the other master. In the dark season the ceiling thickens and rejects its salt, and the brine it squeezes out is so cold and so heavy that it sinks — and as it sinks it freezes the water around itself into a descending sheath: a finger of ice growing down from the sky. Where a finger touches the floor, the cold pools and crawls, and everything it touches freezes mid-gesture. The city calls this nothing, because it calls nothing anything, but it plans for it. The wrights have learned to read the dimples of the ceiling, the places where the sky gathers to drip, and beneath those dimples they raise the Cold-Anchors: tall, slender, uninhabited towers, built in the open, offered to the fingers. Sacrificial architecture. A district of empty lightning rods standing in the wilderness between the Foundation and the rim, some of them sheathed in memorial ice with the frozen dead of other winters still inside, come spring, when the ice releases and leaves its bare salted scar.

And there is the Slow Star. It comes over the rim in the lean season, a starfish the size of a spread hand — a giant in a pocket world — and lays itself over the tubes and tastes the mortar for weakness and patiently, patiently, forces the wrights out. A siege of the Star can last weeks. The city answers the only way it answers anything: it builds. Masons wall up galleries behind the Star's advance, abandon a lane, raise a bulwark, and wait, because the Star is slow and the Work is slower but more patient. Sometimes a brinicle does the city's work for it, and the Star's stiffened body becomes a landmark, a plaza of five arms, until the motes take it apart.

Over everything, the law of quiet. The snow and the ice swallow sound, and the water holds what little is made, and the masonry — dense, fused, continuous — carries every vibration like a sounding board. A shout, if anyone here could shout, would tell every predator in the pocket exactly where you are. So the citizens move slowly and touch gently, and the loudest sounds in the world are these: the tick of a grain being laid, the whisper of ten thousand feeding fans, the soft collapse, somewhere, of a tower losing its argument with time. And once in a while the ceiling shifts in its bed and groans — the only large sound there is, felt in the bones more than heard — and when the sky speaks, the whole city pauses, crowns withdrawn, rivers faltering, until it is done.

The Work will never be finished. The ice scours it, the fingers pierce it, the Star besieges it, and its own growth outruns its repairs — and this is not its tragedy but its nature. The wrights do not imagine completion. A finished city would be a dead one; here, to be under construction is to be alive, and every district is always becoming the next one, the Lattice hardening into the Foundation, the Foundation sinking into basements, the basements filling with tenants who read the old mortar with their lips and taste their ancestors' handwriting.

Above, on the ice, the wind crosses the drift and makes, by this world's standards, an enormous noise that no one below will ever hear. The whole of it — the grove, the anchors, the gardens, the library of tastes, the siege, the herds walking the sky — lies under one footprint of snow.

Down in the green dim, a wright lays a single grain against the lip of its tube, and the grain clicks into place, and no one hears it, and the city grows.
