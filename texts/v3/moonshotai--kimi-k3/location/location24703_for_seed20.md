The sea is warm here, warm as a held hand, and dark all the way down. Almost all the way down. In one place the dark is interrupted: a slope, a shoulder, a rim, black on black, rising out of the deep until it nearly reaches the last thin blue that filters from the world above. A mountain. A new one. It has just arrived, and nothing about it has had time to soften. Every edge is a first edge. The basalt is still glassy with its own newness, sharp enough to take skin, and in the freshest cracks a dull orange seam-glow persists — no flame, only a remembered heat, like coals under a door.

The crater at its summit is small. You could swim the rim in minutes, and from the rim take in the whole of it: a bowl of fresh black glass, mostly empty, holding a few things the way a room holds furniture. Outside the rim the slope lets go of everything and falls into night. Out there, down is the only direction, and it is total — the one honest direction the place keeps. Inside the rim, down is negotiated.

What the crater holds is countable. A colonnade: a half-circle of hexagonal basalt columns, jointed as the lava cooled, standing like the pipes of an organ the size of a chapel. Like an organ, it is for sound. The columns ring, each at its own pitch, when the current worries loose stones through them — the young mountain is tuned, and it plays itself. A stair: eleven fracture-ledges descending the inner wall, level as laid flagstones. No one laid them. The rock cools in right angles here, and the result is a stair the way a crystal is a tower — by habit, not intent. Three mineral chimneys, white, hollow, ribbed, standing where warm water rises; two more lie fallen, coming apart into pale rubble that the crater is slowly taking back. And on the shore of the lake, a scatter of obsidian beads, knuckle-sized, glass tears spilled from the mountain's making. When the lake breathes, the beads chime.

Because in the crater's heart, the sea gives way to a second water.

The lake is denser than the ocean that contains it, pearl-green, warm as blood, and it has a surface — a true one, with a shore of crusted salt and weather of its own. Its face dimples with slow rises from below, like rain in reverse. You can swim down to it and land on it; the surface takes your weight like a floor that dents. If you put your face through from above, you do not see the lake bed. You see the crater from beneath, rim and colonnade hanging overhead, as if the lake were the sky of a smaller, hotter world. Sometimes your own face arrives on that surface a breath after you do. Near the lake, down points at the lake, from every side; a stone dropped above it falls, and a stone released beside it falls sideways, and both are correct. If you swim here, your wake stays standing behind you for minutes, a glass sculpture of you having passed. The water keeps shapes the way warm wax keeps them.

And the sound. There is no quiet version of this place. The mountain is cooling, and cooling basalt cracks, and water does not muffle sound — it delivers it, whole, to the skeleton. The carillon of contraction rings through the colonnade and the stair and the chimneys, gunshot-sharp, bell-deep, arriving through the skull, the teeth, the long bones of the arms if you touch the rock. You do not hear it so much as get included in it. When the vents breathe out, the lake's bass joins, a note too low to hear that you nonetheless hear, everywhere in the body at once. Occasionally the sound arrives before its cause: a crack rings out across the bowl, and a breath later the rock obliges it. The mountain answers questions it has not yet been asked.

Then, on no schedule, the lake turns over. The surface sinks at one shore and rises at the other, the whole body of it rolling like something in sleep — and the noise stops. All of it, at once. This is the only silence here, and it is total, and in it the white chimneys grow. You can watch it happen: a hand's width of new mineral in the span of a held breath, spires rising while nothing rings. The noise unmakes. The silence builds. Then the lake finishes its roll, the beads chime on the shore, the carillon resumes, and the shoreline is elsewhere than it was.

Life is sparse, and mostly waits. Bacterial veils drape the warm seeps, white as ash, orange as rust. Near the rim, where the last blue reaches, five tussocks of pale grass stand apart from one another, blades translucent, fed half by that thin light and half by the mountain's warmth. White eyeless crabs work the veils, farming the bacteria in the fur of their own claws; some live upside down on the underside of the lake's sky, grazing the salt film there, walking a ceiling that is also a surface. An eel lives in the colonnade and is the only creature here that has ever been seen twice. And once in a while — it may be punctual in a way nothing else here is — a great bell jelly comes over the rim, slow as weather, carrying light stolen from the mats in its tissues, arriving like a lamp carried into a chapel.

Between these few things: black glass, bare and gleaming, a great deal of it. Most of the crater is nothing, and the nothing is well-kept.

Give the place an hour and it will spend it. A chimney gains a hand's width and loses it. A colonnade of slowed water stands in the current for nine minutes — pillars of still water in moving water, a building made of refusal — and then the sea forgets it. The lake inhales, its surface doming above the shore like a held breath, and exhales, and the beads ring, and somewhere below the rim a new crack announces itself before it happens.

The whole crater is a bell that has not finished being cast. It rings while it cools. It is young, it is loud, and it is not done deciding what it is — watch, and you can catch it deciding.
