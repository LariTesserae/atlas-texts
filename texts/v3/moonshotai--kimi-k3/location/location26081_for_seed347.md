The plateau gives almost no warning. It is a cold, wind-combed grassland under a wide gray-blue sky, the kind of country where sound travels for miles because there is nothing to stop it — and then the earth simply opens. A crack, eighty meters deep, running crooked for kilometers across the plain like a seam giving way. This is the Sunken Stair, and everything about it is recent. The walls are limestone, pale and angular, their fracture-edges still sharp enough to catch light like broken crockery. Not long ago — a few thousand years, a breath — this valley was a cave. A river ran through a hollow mountain, the roof thinned, and the roof came down in a chain of collapses, leaving a gorge floored with colossal squared-off blocks, raw talus, and one surviving span where the old ceiling still holds: a natural bridge, mid-valley, a hill with a river inside it. You can walk across it and feel, faintly, that you are standing on the one piece of roof that refused to fall.

Drop your eyes from the rim and the floor resolves into water. Not one stream but a staircase of it — pond upon pond, stepping down the length of the gorge, each held by a curved wall, each wall somehow both deliberate and wild. Springs weep from every bedding plane in the fresh rock, so the cliffs themselves are wet, streaked black-green, dribbling into the valley from a hundred seams. The collapse freed the water, and the water has been busy ever since.

Two kinds of builders share the Stair, and neither has ever held a tool. The first is the stone itself, or rather the moss that makes stone: in the upper reach, where the spring water arrives richest in lime, the stream steps down over travertine barrages — dams grown by mosses and algae, precipitating crust over crust, pale as new bone, soft to the thumb, hardening slowly into rock. These tufa walls are the oldest architecture in the valley and they are still under construction, still swelling by fractions of a millimeter a year, rimming each pool with a lip that overflows in a hundred silver threads. Below them begin the stickworks. Beavers — dozens of clans, uncounted generations — have dammed the gorge's middle length into a terraced chain of long ponds, weaving willow and alder and mud into walls that run fifty, a hundred, two hundred meters, bowed upstream against the weight they hold. Where a tufa barrage already stood, the beavers built on it, and where old beaver dams drowned out, new ones rise on the silted backs of the old, so that the valley floor is stratified like a tell: stone-dam, stick-dam, drowned meadow, stick-dam again.

There is no empty surface here. That is the first thing the body learns, climbing down: nothing is bare, nothing is spare. Every boulder wears moss to its waterline; every ledge holds a fern, a dipper's moss-ball nest, a heron's platform of sticks; every slack corner of every pond is scribbled with pondweed. The dams themselves double as streets — wolves and otters and lynx walk them to cross the water, their paws packing the crest — and the beavers have cut canals back from the ponds into the willow carr, radiating channels like roads into a managed woodland, so they can float branches home. The slopes above are a coppice, gnawed and resprouting, gnawed and resprouting, tended by teeth for centuries. Lodges stand shoulder to shoulder in the deeper ponds, heaped domes of stick and mud, some two meters tall and multi-chambered, some abandoned and gone green, colonized by mink below and goldeneye ducks above, moss gardening their roofs. Down in the streambed, at the smallest scale, the construction continues: caddisfly larvae crawl the gravel in cases of sand grains and shell shards, tiny masons mortaring pebbles, the city repeating itself at thumbnail size.

The light is ordinary daylight, but the gorge edits it. Sun reaches the floor as slabs, a few hours at midday, sliding down the west wall and up the east; the rest is a long, cool, green-reflected dimness, the ponds holding the sky like dark glass. Cold is constant. The spring water enters at the temperature of deep ground and the air follows it; breath fogs at dawn even in summer, mist lies on the ponds when the air is colder than the water, and by autumn every dam crest is furred with hoarfrost at first light. Snow comes early and stays late in the shadows. In full winter the ponds skin over entirely and the city goes quiet and subterranean — beavers in their lodges, dippers walking the streambed under the ice, trout hanging motionless in water so clear they look held rather than swimming.

And it is quiet. The walls swallow sound. The loudest events of an ordinary day are a beaver's tail-slap — a single crack, shocking, then rings spreading — or the thin silver thread of a dipper's song, or a heron's wings dragging air. Otherwise: seep, trickle, the tick of a settling stone, a frog, once. The valley smells of wet limestone and mud and bruised willow, and faintly, near the dams, of castoreum — the musky, almost sweet scent the beavers heap on little mud mounds at the water's edge, boundary stones of smell, marking which clan holds which reach. There are jurisdictions here, written in scent and renewed nightly.

Everything is in motion under the stillness. At dusk the ponds begin to stir: wakes cross them in V's, a lodge's underwater doorway exhales bubbles, branches move against the current. Otters work the pools for trout and sculpin, porpoising, rolling. A moose stands chest-deep in a backwater, hauling up pondweed by the mouthful, water sheeting off its dewlaps. Mergansers run the riffles. A lynx waits on a dam crest at the willow edge, patient as a post, for a hare. Now and then the whole system lurches: a spring spate comes down off the plateau, a dam lets go in the night, a pond drops a meter in an hour, mud flats gleam — and within days the breach is woven shut again, tighter than before. Repairs are not events here; they are the baseline condition, like breathing. Even the cliffs are still arriving: last winter a flake the size of a house came off the east wall, and its scar is white and unweathered, its blocks barely napped with moss.

At the lower end, the valley exhales. The last ponds fray into fen — quaking mats of sedge and horsetail, groundwater at ankle depth, no footing you can trust — and then the gathered river slides into a blue slot in the limestone and goes under. The sound goes with it, which feels like a rule of the place: water leaves by the dark. It will reappear days later, kilometers off, boiling up out of a spring in some other valley, cold and clear and already rich with the lime it will lay down somewhere else.

Nothing here was planned and nothing is accidental. The rules are simple and total: water finds the low way; everything built is rebuilt; every surface is claimed; the quiet is kept. The beavers do not know they live in a city. The moss does not know it is making stone. The gorge does not know it used to be a cave. But stand on a dam crest in the blue hour, breath smoking, a wake crossing the pond toward you through the mist, and it is very hard not to feel that you have come down into a town — an old, cold, saturated, patient town — that was founded when the roof fell in, and that intends, dam by dam, terrace by terrace, to fill the crack back up.
