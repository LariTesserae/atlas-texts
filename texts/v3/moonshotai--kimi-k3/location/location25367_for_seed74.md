From the rim of the basin, the place looks like a puddle that forgot to evaporate. The land around it is dry gravel and pale scrub, sloping gently down for an hour's walk into a broad shallow bowl — and there, at the bottom, sits a pool of grey-green maybe four hundred paces across, with two thin stacks rising from it and a thread of steam that barely moves in the cool air. The green is not water and not exactly field. It is the floor of the town: one continuous cushion of plant, dense as felt, mounded and seamed, filling the whole basin to a hard line at the old shoreline. Locals call the basin the Catch, for what it catches — cold air, dew, the occasional rain — and they call the floor itself the fleece.

The fleece is ankle-deep to knee-deep, springy underfoot, so tight-knit that nothing grows through it and almost nothing grows on it except itself. It is grey in dry weeks, olive after rain, black-green and swollen wherever moisture gathers. Botanists from downhill have argued for a century about whether it is one plant or ten thousand fused; cuttings taken from anywhere in the Catch root identically, and the town has its own opinion, which is that the question is rude. What is certain is that the fleece is old beyond the town's oldest records, that it grows at the pace of fingernails, and that it holds the whole settlement the way soil holds other towns — underfoot, under the foundations, under everything.

Walking in the Catch is strange at first because it is silent. The fleece eats sound the way fresh snow does, only permanently. Footsteps make no noise. A bell struck at the Works comes out oddly short, its tail clipped. Voices carry a little way and then stop, as if the air were padded. You learn to speak at the volume of the place, which is low. What you do hear, you hear clearly, and most of what you hear is water: the tick of condensate in the dew-frames at dawn, a drip counting into a cistern, the muffled breathing of the boiler, felt through the soles as much as heard. Water is the rarest thing here and therefore the loudest.

The town's true architecture is plumbing. Iron dew-frames stand in scattered fields across the fleece — angled panes of sheet glass and ribbed fins of blackened metal that cool in the night and gather dew, which runs down into troughs, which run to cisterns, which are gauged every morning and chalked on a slate outside the engine-house for anyone to read. Rain comes a few times a season and is the great event: the whole basin darkens within minutes, releases a green medicinal scent, and for two or three days the shallow hollows of the fleece stand with water — the drown-hollows, brief private wetlands where the town's few animals appear and its people come to look at reflections. Then the sun returns, the hollows dry, and the place goes back to its long patient gleaning of the air. There is one rule older than the Works, and it is that no one drills the fleece. Its deep interior holds damp accumulated over centuries, and that water is its own. The town may have what falls and what condenses. It may not mine the bed.

The fleece is also the fuel. It is resinous — that is why almost nothing eats it — and resin burns. By license and by measurement, the cutters take sections from the licensed margins, cake-thick slabs that dry for a year in the long sheds before they smolder in the boiler. The engine-house is small, brick gone soft-edged with age, its machinery slow: a boiler, a pump, a line shaft, a little dynamo that runs a handful of bulbs. Beside it the retort renders resin into lamp oil and pipe-sealant, and the stacks breathe out a steam that smells of the plant itself, so the whole town carries a faint cool scent of green smoke. Ash goes back to the cut sites. The cut sites regrow over decades. A person sees two, maybe three major cuttings in a lifetime, and so a cutting is half work and half ceremony, and the overseers who set the take are the town's nearest thing to priests, though they would say they are only accountants.

Everything here runs on that accounting, and everything is a negotiation with the floor. The fleece creeps. Left alone, it swallows. Around every house, every cistern, every pipe-junction there is a swept margin of bare gravel a hand's width wide, and keeping the margin is daily work, unglamorous and absolute. A shed abandoned in the last century is now a green hummock with a chimney poking out of it like a periscope; the town calls it the drowned house and points children at it. The negotiation goes both ways. The town prunes the bed back from its doorsteps, and the bed presses gently against the town — shifting pipes over years, tilting fence posts, recording everything. For the fleece remembers weight. A habitual path becomes a pale pressed lane that lasts for seasons. A cart left standing a month leaves a dent for a year. The floor is a slow palimpsest, and the townspeople read it without meaning to: who has been walking where, at what hour, toward whose door. Lovers learn to vary their routes. It is no use. The floor gossips.

The buildings are few and far between — perhaps thirty structures scattered across the green, low brick houses on stone footings, their roofs humped with the same plant rooted over felt, so that from the rim the settlement looks less built than grown in bumps. Some forty keepers live here: gaugers, engine-keepers, the cooper, the warden of the margins, the one who chalks the water slate. Houses cluster, subtly, near the Works' waste heat, because the Catch is cold. Cool air drains down the basin slopes all night and pools in it; you can walk out at midnight and feel the invisible river of cold around your ankles flowing toward the lowest point, your breath fogging at knee height. Frost comes some nights and is noted. The fleece itself, strangely, is warm — compost warmth from its dense interior — and sitting on it on a cold evening is like sitting on the back of some great sleeping animal, a comparison visitors make exactly once, quietly.

Animals are rare enough to be known. There is the ring-moth, a grey night-flyer whose caterpillars graze the fleece in widening circles, leaving rings that read like growth charts across the town; the rings are tolerated because grazed fleece is easier to cut when its license comes. There are the sealed snails, which no one sees for months and which appear by the hundred in the first hour of rain, feed, mate, and glue themselves shut against stones before the hollows dry. And there is the cistern-heron, or rather the heron, singular — a gaunt bird that stalks the water tanks with the air of an inspector, tolerated, half considered an omen, full considered a colleague. That is nearly the whole census. The resin that makes the fleece burn makes it inedible, and so the Catch has no grazers, no flocks, no swarms — except once in a generation, when the fleece flowers. The whole basin blooms at once, small gold flowers in such density that the Catch becomes a bowl of gilt, smelling of honey and creosote, and the moths come in clouds, and for one week the quiet town is loud with wings. Then the seeds fall in drifts like ash, nothing eats them, the condensers are swept, and the town begins counting years again: before the bloom, after the bloom. The elders remember two.

The deep time is everywhere if you look. Pale gravel benches ring the basin at heights no flood explains — old shorelines. The Catch was a lake once. There is a boathouse on the fleece, kept painted, used to store pipe fittings, a day's walk from any water a boat could float in; nobody finds this funny anymore, which is its own kind of joke. The Works is on its sixth boiler; each lasts about a human life. The lanes of pressed fleece shift decade by decade as habits change, but the buildings have not moved in memory, and the morning ritual has not changed: the gauger walks out at first light, reads the cisterns, and chalks the numbers on the slate, and the dew-frames tick as they dry, and the boiler breathes, and the heron lifts its head.

Nothing here is becoming anything else. That is the thing a visitor feels longest — not the silence, not the strange floor, but the settledness. The town is not growing and not dying. It takes what regrows, drinks what falls, holds its margins, and waits for rain, and when rain comes it is the loudest and best day of the season, and everyone goes out to stand in it, on the warm green floor, in the cool air, in the bottom of the Catch, where the water briefly is.
