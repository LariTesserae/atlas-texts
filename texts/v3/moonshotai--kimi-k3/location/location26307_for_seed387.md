On the deep seafloor, where nothing should be warm, a thread of warmth rises.

It comes up out of the silt in a slow, steady column, barely visible — a shimmer, a wavering of the dark — and it carries almost nothing with it. No plume of smoke, no chemistry of vents. Just warm water, patient and continuous, and a hum. Rest your hand flat on the sediment and the hum climbs your wrist and settles in the bones of your forearm. Something below is running. Something below has been running for a very long time.

Follow the warmth down and you find the stack: a riveted iron cylinder, wide as a grain silo, rising a few meters out of the seabed and descending — far farther than it should — into the rock. It is crusted with carbonate and rust, capped in places, ruptured in others, and it leaks the way a kettle leaks: warm water, and sound. Enter through a torn seam and you are inside a throat. The shaft goes down eighty meters through basalt, and the whole way down it rings. The walls sweat mineral. The hum becomes a tone becomes a pressure. You are descending an ear canal toward an eardrum, and the eardrum is being struck, continuously, from the far side.

Then the shaft opens, and you are hanging in the crown of the Drum.

It is a single chamber, three hundred meters deep and two hundred across — a bubble the size of a cathedral, sealed in rock beneath the seafloor, full of warm water from floor to vault. You could hold it in cupped hands. And yet it contains a city. The inner wall is ringed with stacked arcades and galleries, streets that circle the drum at twenty levels, lampposts, staircases, balustrades, doorways, signage in a script no one reads anymore. Towers rise from the floor like organ pipes — standpipes, stacks, silos — bridged to the wall and to each other by fat pipe-spans that sag across open water. And between the towers and the walls: nothing. Vast dark water, near-void, the volume of the place almost entirely empty, the built fabric clinging to the edges like a tide-line of civilization. A city the size of a room, and mostly the room.

The light comes in points, not fields. A caged work-lamp here, a string of status lights there — red, amber, a green gone slightly wrong, as if the machines that replace the bulbs have forgotten the original colors and now mix their own. Far below, a dull red glow leaks up through a floor grating, furnace-light, so that everything in the Drum is lit from beneath. Your shadow falls upward, onto the ceiling. The towers wear their shadows on their tops. It is theater lighting for a play performed to no one.

And the sound. The sound arrives as a blow to the whole body — not heard but *received*, chest, teeth, eyesockets, the fluid of the spine. Water carries it perfectly and the chamber returns it perfectly, so that every clang arrives not once but forever, folded over itself, until the place is less a room with noise in it than a noise with a room around it. To swim in the Drum is to be a loose part inside a struck bell that has never been allowed to finish ringing.

The water is bath-warm at the crown and hotter below, stratified in loose layers that the turbulence keeps tearing and re-mixing, so that a slash of heat crosses your back without warning and is gone. Slow gyres turn in the open volume, driven by impellers somewhere in the deep machinery. The whole body of water breathes: every ten minutes or so, everything sloshes gently to one side and then back, a seiche, a tide in a bottle, and on the walls of the upper galleries you can see its tide-lines — two rust-stained bands, a meter apart, marking the range of the breathing. Down among the towers, jets pulse and die. Near hot surfaces the water crackles faintly, cavitating, a static like frying heard through a wall. Thermal plumes rise as visible waverings, shadowy in the lamplight, and through it all, always, the fall: a slow, constant snow of rust flakes and mineral dust shed from everything above, drifting down through the beams of the working lamps, gathering on every upward face. It has been falling for decades. The silt it makes lies undisturbed in the doorways of shops and offices, drifted to the lintels, printed with the small tracks of crabs.

The city fabric is old and was built for air. That much is legible. The ring-streets have curbs. The stairs rise at human angles. The gallery windows were glazed once — the glass is still there in some, frosted blind by decades of warm mineral water. Whatever happened — a failed valve, a decision, a slow sinking — the sea was let in long ago, and the city, instead of stopping, converted. The retrofits are everywhere and they are magnificent: housings swollen into watertight bulbs, counterweighted doors, cables rerouted in looping catenaries like vines, pumps where pumps were never meant to be. And then, stranger, the additions — structures with no human blueprint in them at all. Welded scaffolds that grow out into open water like coral, like a reef of scrap, branching according to some machine sense of the elegant. Stairs that climb twenty meters and end at blank plate. Doors sized for nothing with legs. Balconies with no railings, because the builders of them do not fall. The city has been maintaining and extending itself for so long that its later architecture is written in a private vernacular, a dialect of one speaker.

Hold a light to the old cast iron and it is soft — the iron has leached away over the decades, leaving a graphite skeleton you can press a thumbprint into, and the machines still run inside those soft shells, their working parts endlessly renewed while the casings go to pencil lead. The concrete wears a hide of carbonate. The brass is green to the core. Nothing wooden remains; there may never have been wood. The only softness anywhere is mineral.

What the Drum *does* is harder to say. It clearly moves heat and water: intake tunnels drink the cold sea, outfalls in the floor return it warmed — that is the thread rising from the seabed, the city's long exhalation. It clearly manufactures: in the middle decks there are dry cells, air-filled capsules held against the flood, their portholes glowing warm and dry from within, steam feathering from their vents. Machines pass in and out of them through water locks, carrying stock. If you were to surface inside one — and you could, there is air, of a kind — the shock of it would be enormous: sound arriving thin and sharp through air instead of bone, and a *smell*, hot oil and ozone and scorched dust, the only smell in the entire Drum, a ghost of the dry world the city once was. Then you would drop back into the water and the smell would be gone like a dream.

The products of all this industry are, near as anyone can tell, the city itself. Parts for the pumps. Plates for the walls. Bulbs for the lamps. And a surplus: in one gallery, ranks of identical castings stand crusted in rows, hundreds of them, never collected, a warehouse of gifts with no recipient. Power goes up too — thick cables root from the crown into dead conduits in the rock, sending current to some surface installation that is either gone or never was. The Drum pays tribute to an empty heaven and does not stop.

The machines themselves are rare presences. For all the noise, you can swim ten minutes and see nothing move. Then: a maintenance crawler crossing a plaza, unhurried, unregarding. A manipulator arm on a track in a forge-cell, doing something precise to something small. Most poignant, a tender climbing a lamppost over an empty ring-street, carrying a fresh bulb — the city's color-blind, drifting idea of a bulb — to keep a light burning above a street where the only traffic is one isopod, twice a day. The work is real. The purpose has evaporated and the work remains, and so the work has become something else: liturgical. An industry that outlived its reason and became a form of prayer.

But do not mistake liturgy for calm. The Drum is a steady-state storm. Without warning — within rhythms, but never on schedule — a surge takes it. A chord sounds through the water, the great valves opening somewhere, an organ interval felt in the gut more than heard. Then lights cascade on across whole decks, towers wake in sequence, and the machines *converge*: crawlers and arms and carriages streaming from across the city toward some failure, some priority legible only to the works. The gyre accelerates. Jets scream. Cavitation goes from static to hail. Somewhere a structure is torn down with terrible efficiency while, elsewhere, something new is raised in the same hour — creation and demolition running like two hands of the same body. An hour later it all relaxes. The chord sounds again, inverted. The lights go out deck by deck. The machines disperse to their far solitudes, and the Drum returns to its vast, ringing emptiness, empty as ever but for the snow of rust and the single crab on the stair.

These are the Hours, and they are the weather, and they are the only social life the city has.

The sound deserves its own accounting, because it is the true medium of the place — everything here that senses, senses by it. At the bottom of the register are the prime movers, pistons and turbines below the floor grating, a bass at the edge of hearing that you feel in the sternum and the fillings of your teeth, continuous as a heartbeat. Above that, the impacts: forging, riveting, the slap of heavy valves, each one arriving from everywhere at once because the walls give it back and give it back. Above that, the hull's own voice — the long groans of iron under thermal strain, the ticking of cooling welds. Above that, the hiss and crackle of cavitation and steam, a frying static that never ceases. And laid across all of it, the Hours, the chords, the cascades. It is deafening in the literal, damaging sense: unprotected ears would not last here. The water is never quiet. It has never once, in living memory, been quiet.

And yet there are nodes. The chamber's reverberation has its geometry, its standing waves, and in the lee of certain towers, behind certain buttresses, there are pockets — a few meters across — where the sound cancels itself to something merely loud. You find them by pain, by the sudden easing of it, and when you find one you have found the fauna, because everything living in the Drum lives in the nodes.

Life is rare here, and strange, and entirely without plants. The only growing things are veils: iron-bacteria moss, rust-red and sulfur-yellow, furred across the warmest plates, closer to mineral than to meadow. From it hang rusticles, icicles of corroded iron, some of them meters long, grown over decades like stalactites in a cave. On these graze the isopods — pale, thumb-sized, eyeless, unhurried, one every few towers, the gardeners of the fall. In the beams of the working lamps hang small translucent fish, phototactic, sipping bacteria from the light itself, half a dozen to a lamp and none anywhere else; you might see five in an hour of swimming. And there is, by all accounts, the warden: something large, crustacean, table-sized, so plated in rust and scale and limpets that it is indistinguishable from a resting machine until it moves, which it does rarely, a few meters a day, crossing a plaza at a pace the silt can record. Whether it is very old fauna or a machine that long ago decided to be a crab is an open question, and the Drum does not answer questions.

Down is heat and down is noise, and at the bottom is the hearth. Below the floor grating, past the impellers that drive the gyre, past the pistons of the prime movers — a hall like the engine room of a world — the water runs near boiling, threaded with micro-plumes that crack like distant gunshots, and the grating glows dull red from the tap into the deep hot rock. Here the mineral gardens are richest, chimneys and crusts in furious colors, and here the crust is oldest, geological, the boundary between machine and landform fully dissolved. The outfalls drink from this level. The warmth that rises from the seabed far above began here, in this red-lit, thundering basement.

Everything in the Drum is in balance, and the balance is very old. Accretion and maintenance have reached a truce: the machines keep their joints and working faces clear and surrender everything else to the crust, so that the city is slowly fossilizing in place while remaining, impossibly, alive inside the fossil. Intake matches outfall. Demolition matches building. The churn never stops and never changes. What was a mechanism has become a landform that performs itself; what was an emergency — the flood, the abandonment, whatever it was — has become simply the conditions. The Drum is not dying and not being born. It is ringing, the way a bell that is struck forever is neither rising nor decaying but is, at every moment, exactly as loud as it is.

The laws of the place are few and you learn them fast. Sound is the medium: nothing here is hidden, nothing is private, everything announces itself to everything. Heat stacks the world: the tepid crown, the warm streets, the burning floor, and you choose your depth the way you'd choose a floor of a building. Stillness is claimed: anything that holds still long enough — a dropped tool, a corpse, a visitor who stayed — gets catalogued by the fall, grazed by the crabs, welded over in some later Hour, absorbed into the fabric. The city does not see you. But it will keep you.

And the cycles: the ten-minute breathing of the water, the irregular chords of the Hours, the slow centuries of the crust. Time here is not a line. It is a stack of rhythms, all turning at once, and none of them going anywhere.

You leave the way you came, up the ringing throat of the stack, and the sound leaves you last — it stays in the sternum for hours, a phantom pressure, the body's memory of the bell. On the seafloor there is nothing to show: silt, cold dark water, a thread of warmth rising, a hum in the bones of the earth. The plain goes on for a thousand kilometers in every direction, empty and ordinary. And beneath it, lit for no one, powered toward no purpose, maintained in colors slightly wrong, the Drum rings on in its buried room — a city, a factory, an instrument, a held note — performing its liturgy to an empty congregation of water, and finding the congregation sufficient.
