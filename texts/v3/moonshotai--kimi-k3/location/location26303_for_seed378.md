The climb from the lowlands takes most of a day, and the heat comes with you the whole way. This is the strange first thing: the altitude never cools. You rise into thinner and thinner air, brighter and brighter light, expecting the sharpness of height, and instead the ground itself begins to radiate — warm through your boot soles, then hot, then steaming where the path crosses cracks in the rock. The plateau is a lid. Beneath it, water heated in the deep faults presses upward, and the whole uplifted basin lies on that pressure like a city asleep on a furnace.

From the rim you see it entire, and the light comes at you twice — once from the sun, once from the ground. The basin floor is white: travertine terraces stepping down in long slow fans, evaporite pans lying flat as poured glass, channels ruled so straight they could only be built, all of it reflecting the hard noon until pan and sky blur into one white haze at the horizon. Threads of steam rise here and there, far apart, vertical in the still air. Towers stand scattered across it all — a dozen visible from the rim, kilometers between them, too few and too far apart to read as a city until you notice the channels connecting them, the stepped terraces serving them, the slow machines crawling the pans. It is a city the way a still is a city: an instrument spread wide, built to catch and hold and separate. That is what the keepers call it, when they call it anything. The Still.

The second fact of the place, after the glare, is the quiet. Stand on the rim and listen: almost nothing. The stone is porous sinter and tuff and it drinks sound. The channels are engineered for it — every weir a long smooth slide, every drop broken by a lip, so that water nowhere falls far enough to speak. What remains, if you wait, is a small inventory: the soft tick of crust cooling in shade, a drip falling into a pool thick with silica and arriving as a muffled *tup*, the sigh of a valve somewhere letting pressure go at a long, even interval, like the breath of a sleeper. That is the loudest regular sound in the basin. The silence is not absence here. It is maintenance. The Still holds the mountain's pressure like a hand on a valve, releasing it continuously, so that nothing ever gathers enough force to erupt. Nothing here has ever needed to explode. Everything has been persuaded, over centuries, to exhale instead.

Down on the floor the ground is wet everywhere, and every puddle is warm as blood. Water lies in sheets so thin they only mirror; it threads through crusts; it seeps up through the pans from below and arrives from the towers above; the rim cliffs weep it in warm trickles down curtains of white stone that are themselves only older trickles, frozen. There is not one green thing. No root, no leaf, no seed — green exists here only as a chemistry of bronze. What grows instead is the weft: a felting of rust-red, ochre, and lead-grey films that spreads wherever warm water sheets over stone, metabolizing sulphur and iron, chemosynthetic, older than any leaf. It is the pasture of this world. It streaks the terraces, lines the channels, furs the insides of the condenser towers, and has to be scraped from the cisterns on a weekly round, because it does not care whether a surface is rock or infrastructure. To the weft, the Still is just a warm wet mountain it hasn't finished covering.

The water claims everything, in fact. That is the deep rule. It carries so much dissolved mineral that whatever it touches it coats, and whatever it coats it entombs. Leave a tool on a pan and return in a year to a white dome. The pipes of the Still were laid once, in ceramic, centuries ago; the water has since built its own conduits around and through them, guided stalactites of sinter, so that the city's circulatory system is now largely self-grown stone, and maintenance is not repair but negotiation — chiseling a passage back to its intended width. In a plaza at the basin's heart, bronze statues have grown into white columns; whatever faces they had are inside the stone now. Whole machines stand entombed in domes and still work within them, their slow pistons breathing in the dark, felt through the crust as a faint regular tremor. The city persists only where it is scraped. Elsewhere it becomes terrace, and the terraces are old, and the old terraces are indistinguishable from the geology, so that you cannot say where the building ends and the basin begins. Building and burial here are the same act, performed at different speeds.

The animals have learned the same stillness the machines have. The pans carry herds of salt-oxen — pale, low, broad-hoofed things with coats that have gone half-mirror, flanks of burnished silver that flash when they turn. They graze the weft with long dark tongues, scattered thin across the white distances, and they do not call to one another. They signal in light: a tilt of the flank, a flash thrown across a kilometer of pan, alarm and gathering and all-clear spoken in semaphore. Overhead the kites ride the thermals off the warm pans, wide membranous wings flashing the same language down at the herds below. In the milky pools, eyeless eels the color of the water taste the bloom of the weft. In the pipes, quick pale crustaceans graze the films from inside, and you can put a hand to a warm conduit and feel them working, a dry faint whisper of motion through stone.

And at dusk the listeners come out.

They are long and low, built like something poured, and they hunt the way the whole world here suggests: not by ear but by foot. A listener lays its jaw flat to the crust and feels the pan through the stone — the tread of a herd, the heartbeat-scuffle of something small — and flows toward it without a sound, and the herd's only warning is the flash of light off a neighbor's flank: *run*, thrown in silence across the glare. Loud things are found, here. That is the other deep rule, and it explains the Still as much as any engineering does. Everything that persists in this basin has learned quiet — the animals, the machines, the keepers. Even the rain has learned it: it falls warm in the afternoons, condensed out of the morning's own steam, and landing on warm stone it makes no patter, only a low hiss and a smell — hot mineral, salt, faint sulphur, the sweet musty note of the weft drinking. The basin breathes once a day: steam up with the morning, the return down in the afternoon, and in between, at noon, the glare standing on its head, the herds motionless in the shadows of the towers, the kites at their ceiling.

The works have longer breaths inside that daily one. Settling ponds flood and drain in rotation on a weeks-long round. Harvest crawlers — low tortoise machines of bronze and glazed ceramic, older than anything alive — walk the pans on broad feet, scraping the mineral crust in slow spirals, pausing with a kind of fluid courtesy when a herd crosses their path. There is no fire anywhere in the Still. No smoke, no spark. Work is done by falling water and rising steam, by float and siphon and counterweight; the city thinks in water, its governors are valves, its memory is the height of a standpipe. It regulates the mountain, harvests what the water leaves behind, and stacks the yield — pyramids of salt, blocks of sulphur, cakes of pale silica — in rows at the basin's low corner, where the gathered outflow leaves in a single smooth flume: a ribbon without a ripple, falling off the rim for a long time before it makes any sound below, and by then it is someone else's river.

Of the keepers there is less to say than you'd expect. Their traces are everywhere — a bronze handrail kept bright and crust-free by touch alone, footpaths worn into the pans, a weir scraped clean this week, stacks of salt that diminish and grow again — but the people themselves are sparse as the towers. You may see one in a day or none: a figure wrapped against the glare, moving along a channel with a scraping blade, answering a kite's flash with a tilted disc. Whether caravans still climb from the lowlands to take the harvest, or whether the keepers only move the blocks from pile to pile out of a memory of trade, is not written anywhere. The Still does not require the question to be answered. It requires the valves to be kept narrow, the weirs kept open, the pressure kept low. It requires the quiet kept.

And the quiet is kept. At night the white ground gives the day back for hours, the pools holding a dim light that may be memory or may be something the weft makes; the steam threads stand pale against hard stars; the crawlers keep their rounds; a listener crosses a pan like a shadow being pulled on a string; and the valves sigh at their long intervals, and the mountain turns in its sleep, and the Still — patient, encrusted, half-grown into its own mountain — lets the breath out slowly, slowly, so that nothing here ever has to raise its voice.
