The light arrives before anything else — or rather, the insufficiency of it. A small sun, dull as a banked coal, hangs low and moves slowly, throwing a bronze half-light across a plateau that runs to every horizon at once. The air is too thin and too cold to blur anything, so the dimness is clearly not in the atmosphere; it is upstream, in the star itself. Visibility is enormous. You can see a hundred kilometers of nothing between somethings, and the somethings are visible for days before you reach them.

The silence takes longer to notice, because it masquerades as an absence rather than a condition. Air this thin murders sound politely, an arm's length from its making. A dropped stone ticks and is done. A shout falls at your feet. Your own breathing is the loudest sustained event you will produce here, and your footfalls squeak in the way snow only squeaks when it is very, very cold. But the silence has pressure behind it. It feels occupied. It is.

The ground is old and finished. Nothing jagged anywhere — ranges worn to sleeping shoulders, valleys broad and patient, every slope at its angle of repose, frost-shattered scree that has had eons to settle and has settled. The plain sorts itself, slower than glaciers, into nets and polygons of stone, cell by cell, patterned ground stretching to the horizon like tiling no one laid. There are dunes of gray ash and clinker, wind-worked into ripples. And there are the grooves: channels worn a body-depth into bare rock, polished smooth, arcing across the plateau for thousands of kilometers, converging, diverging, some half-filled with sorted stones where they have been abandoned for ten thousand years. Nothing here was built. There is not a shard, not a cut block, not a straight line that intention drew. And yet the land is inscribed, and the inscription is a map of thirst, and it was written by walking.

The herds walk it still.

You will see one on the horizon for the better part of a week before it resolves: bodies like hillside boilers carried on striding iron legs, slow as weather, eyeless, navigating by the groove underfoot and the faint warmth of the sun and the vibrations that come up through the stone. Their mouths are grates. They take in the frost-shattered scree — the lichen-crusted rock that is the only pasture this world grows — and somewhere deep inside them a mill turns, and you feel the grinding through the soles of your boots long after the thin air has refused to carry it to your ears. Along their spines, vents open in slow sequence, and the breath goes up in columns that catch the low sun and burn brighter than anything else in the landscape. Each herd travels beneath its own weather: exhaled moisture condensing overhead into a private cloud street, snowing back down as dry spicules. Most of the snow in this world has been breathed at least once. The water cycle here is largely a respiratory system.

Because the cold is total — the cold is the oldest thing here and the most patient — dampness is not a condition of the land but a possession of its animals. The world is dry; its creatures are wet. Moisture lives as rime in shadowed hollows, as frost-feathers growing on hulls and subliming away in the long day, as drip near glowing seams, as dark tarns that exist only where heat is kept. At night — and night lasts for days — the herds stand with their seams glowing dull ember, constellations of low fires strewn across the dark, the only warm light that has ever existed here.

Where the herds are mobile furnaces, the stacks are rooted ones. Sessile, fluted columns planted over deep heat, spaced hundreds of kilometers apart, each one breathing out a soft chuff every few minutes — and that is the fastest regular rhythm in this world, that slow exhalation, like a heartbeat through a wall. Everything else is slower. Each stack keeps a halo of melt around itself: a ring of dark water rimmed with frost-flowers, and beyond the water a band of moss and black cushion-growth, an oasis measured in tens of meters on a plateau measured in continents. The grooves run from stack to stack. The herds come down their own history to drink, and these are the only crowds the world assembles — a dozen hillside bodies around a tarn, venting gently, exchanging the small seam-fauna that live in their joints the way islands exchange birds.

The vegetation is a rumor the warm places tell. Rust-colored lichen spreading a fingernail deep across a province of stone. Moss packed into hull seams, living on the gradient between furnace and frost. Everything that grows here grows on an edge — the boundary between kept warmth and ambient cold — and everything grows so slowly that a meadow can be older than the grooves that cross it.

The smaller fauna fill the margins the way water fills a footprint. In the grooves, rollers: disc-bodied, rimmed scavengers that travel the polished tracks the herds have cut, canting to steer, gathering in the wake of a passing furnace to glean spilled fines and clinker. Ash-mites winter for years in the dunes. Whole meiofaunas inhabit a single seam. There are not many animals here, and the animals are almost everything — the biomass of a continent concentrated into a few vast slow bodies and the thin rust that feeds them, until the distinction between landscape and fauna stops holding.

It stops holding entirely at the end of a life. A furnace does not die so much as go out, over decades, cooling by degrees while the world reorganizes around it: the seam-fauna emigrating, the rollers gathering and then dispersing, moss colonizing the warm hull, frost feathering the cold one, sorted stones creeping over it year by year until it is a mound, and then a hill, and then a ridge. The plateau is dotted with these. Some of the older ranges, rounded and settled and utterly geological, may be older herds. Erosion treats dead engines and bedrock identically, with the same patience, and after enough time there is no difference to treat.

The day here lasts weeks. Noon goes on for days, the dim sun leaning on the horizon in no hurry, and the twilight is a long blue gradient rather than an event. Migration is measured in centuries. The grooves record older routes the way stone records older rivers. Ice margins advance a fingernail a year and retreat on the same schedule. Nothing is becoming anything else. This is the finished state of the world — not a world winding down, not a world building toward, but a world that found its form long ago and keeps it the way the herds keep their grooves: by walking it, slowly, forever.

Stand in it once. Feel the squeak of your own steps, the loudness of your breath, your heartbeat suddenly available to you in the hush. Then feel, below all of that, through boot and bone and the fillings of your teeth, the slow throb of milling from a herd still beyond the horizon, and the fainter answer of ice in some hollow, relaying a frequency you were not built to hear. The plateau is not silent. It is silent only in your register. Underneath the silence the engines turn, breathing out the weather, and the weather falls on the stone, and the stone is ground, and the grinding is older than the grooves and slower than the cold, and it does not pause for the dark.
