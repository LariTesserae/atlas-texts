The rivers arrive in the lowlands already decided. They come down off the high country clear, cold, and constant — the same width in the dry month as in the wet one, the same cool temperature at dusk as at dawn — and anything living below learns to treat them less like weather and more like supply. Follow one of them upstream, climbing, and the world thins: the air cools by degrees, the sounds of the low country fall away one at a time, and somewhere above the last warm ridge you walk into the cloud and do not come out of it. Because the cloud is not above the plateau. The cloud is the plateau's product. You have entered the works.

Call it the Condensary, if anything down below were inclined to name it: a dome of highland broad enough that weather is born inside it and dies inside it, rimmed all around by escarpments worn soft as old teeth. Nothing here is jagged. The ridges are whale-backs, the saddles are long moss lawns, the valleys graded by water that has had a very long time to finish its work and has settled, now, into maintenance. The bedrock is an ancient pale limestone, and it never shows. Every stone wears green velvet; every cliff is upholstered; the grikes and runnels of the old pavement are choked with growth. Even the geology looks padded. This is a young thing's idea of a factory and an old thing's idea of a forest, and it is neither, and it runs.

The ground is not ground. It is a sponge, meters deep — moss over moss over the compacted memory of moss — and walking on it is closer to wading. The surface quakes in slow waves away from each step. Trees stand on stilt-roots over it, whole groves rafted on the felt, and where the raft tears — a moss-rip, black water opening under your boot like a mouth — the wound closes again within a season, healed over by the same green patience. There are no leaves in the canopy to speak of. The trees' true foliage is small and waxy and almost incidental; what makes the canopy is the load each tree carries: ferns on moss on liverwort on lichen on algal film, stacked until the trunk is a scaffold inside a column of living felt and the word *individual* stops meaning much. The forest is arguably one organism with a great many addresses.

Here is the machinery. Every surface of that felt is tooled for condensation — hairs, scales, drip-tips, curled spouts — and the fog that lies on the plateau most of every day is combed out of the air a droplet at a time. On a still afternoon with nothing falling from the sky, an old tree rains on itself: a slow private drizzle gathering from tip to tip, leaf to leaf, down to the sponge. Multiply that by a trillion trees and the forest waters itself. The loop closes overhead — the sponge breathes vapor, the vapor rises and cools at altitude, the canopy seeds it with a faint blue haze of its own exhaled oils, and the haze gathers the vapor back into cloud, and the cloud comes home as fog. The rivers that leave the rim are only the overflow of a system that keeps nearly everything it makes.

Underneath, the limestone does the plumbing. The plateau is being patiently hollowed from within — the water works underground now, dissolving, and the drainage has the organ-logic of a body: sinkholes that swallow streams whole, caves that exhale cold breath, and springs that turn on and off like valves. The siphon springs are the heartbeat you don't hear. Toward dusk the sponge cools, the fog thickens and condenses, the whole dome swells almost imperceptibly, and somewhere below, chambers fill past their lips and begin to drain — a slow subsonic turnover you feel in your teeth and in the trembling of the tarns. Toward dawn the light returns, the moss opens its pores, and the plateau exhales the day's first cloud. On a longer rhythm — eleven days, twelve — the great springs of the deep basins discharge together, and for one night the black pools rise a hand's breadth and the outflow rivers run, for once, a little fuller. Shift change in the deep works.

The place is crowded beyond easy description, and almost none of it makes a sound. This is the strange covenant of the Condensary: sound dies here. Felt absorbs it; fog eats it; there are no dry leaves to rustle, so wind passes through this forest the way it passes through cloth. Life, which will not be silenced, simply changed medium. The broad soft-hooved grazers — felt-coated, patient, built for the sponge — speak to one another by stamping, single notes struck into the ground and felt a valley away through the waterlogged mat. The moths that pollinate the canopy are the size of spread hands and fly without a whisper, drinking from drip-tips in the dark. The frogs are mute and signal with slow waves of a pale foot. In the tarns, black gilled salamanders a meter long hold themselves as still as the water, which is held, all of it, every pool, at one cool temperature year-round — as though thermostatted, as though someone had set the building's climate and left. The owls here have taken silence further than owls anywhere; you know one has passed because a grazer's calf is simply gone. Even the aquifer is stocked: the black water under the moss is full of translucent, unhurried things, copepods and flatworms and pale larvae, so that the rivers leave the rim not merely clear but filtered, polished, finished.

The silence is therefore not emptiness. It is a party line you cannot hear. Stand still on the sponge at night and the ground ticks and trembles with traffic — drummed codes, the subsonic turning of springs, the soft collapse of droplets falling leaf to leaf — and the loudest thing in the whole vast dome is rain in the next basin, a faint hiss on the far side of a ridge.

The oldness is kept like records. The sponge remembers: cut into it and the layers go down in order, season upon season, and in its depths stand the ghosts of trees — hollow molds of moss in the exact shape of trunks that rotted away a thousand years ago, tall enough to crawl inside, the forest's history preserved as architecture. Nothing floods. Nothing burns. Nothing dries. The moss-rafts self-repair, the haze seeds its own cloud cover, the springs meter the outflow, the pools hold their set cool, and after enough days the accumulation of small steadinesses begins to feel less like luck and more like regulation — a system tuned, held at grade, behaving *maintained*. That is the uncanny thing here, the thing that takes a while to name: it is untouched, and it runs like infrastructure.

It has room to have weather of its own. The fog is not uniform — it moves in currents and slow fronts across the dome, pooling in the basins, streaming over saddles like slow water, so that you can watch a fog-front arrive hours away, a gray tide climbing a far valley. Sometimes a clearing opens: a blue hole in the cloud a day's walk wide, sunlight falling through in one enormous column onto one green watershed, and from a ridge you can see the dome curve away in every direction and understand that the cloud you walked inside all week was a small local product of a single basin's breath. At the rim, the plateau sheds its surplus over the escarpment — not crashing, never crashing; the falls slide off moss-rolled lips into mist and are muffled to a hiss before they have fallen a hundred meters — and far below, through gaps, lies the low country: warmer, louder, lit from a different sky, drinking what the high country sends down on schedule.

No hand made any of it. There is no maker, no keeper, no artifact older than a spider's web, and nothing built but what builds itself. And yet: at dusk the valves turn over in the deep stone, the sponge swells and exhales its night fog, the stamped messages go out along the mat, the moths rise to the drip-tips, the pools hold their temperature, and the rivers leave the rim on time.

The Condensary keeps its own hours. It has always kept them. The water goes out, and the water comes back, and nothing anywhere in the vast green dome raises its voice about it.
