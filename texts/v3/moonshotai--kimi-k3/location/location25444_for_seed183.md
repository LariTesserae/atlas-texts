**The Weeping Roof**

You come up through the cloud band and the cloud comes up with you, and for a long while there is no difference between the mountain and the weather. Then the light arrives — all at once, hard and white-gold, from above *and* below, because the cloud-sea you have just climbed out of lies under the sun like a second, softer sun, and the whole massif hangs between the two, lit from both directions like a held note. The lowland maps call this the Weeping Roof. The people who built here left no readable name for it, only a mark pecked beside their doorways and cairns: two circles, overlapping, one slightly offset from the other. A shape and its ghost.

The rock is a pale, sugar-grained quartzite shot through with feldspar and seams of mica that flash when you turn your head. It is rounded — enormously, anciently rounded — into whalebacks and lobes and towers whose edges have gone soft as old bread. This is an established landscape. Uplift and wear reached their agreement long ago; the silhouette has been the same for longer than any story. The runnels are deep and polished, the potholes smooth as cups, and for every slab that cracks off in the night the seep-lines patiently round a new edge. Nothing here is crumbling. It has settled into itself.

And it is wet. Every wall weeps. The mountain harvests the passing clouds directly — fog condenses on the cold pale stone even when no rain falls, so the rock drinks weather out of the air and then spends the whole day giving it back: films of water sliding down the faces, drip-lines beading from every lip and overhang, hanging tarns in the high basins that overflow into one another in stepped chains. The sound of this never stops. It is the first layer of the loudness — the drip-choir, thousands of small notes at thousands of tempos, striking stone, striking water, striking old copper.

Here is the first wrong thing, though most travelers miss it: the rock is wet and nothing is growing on it. Everywhere else in the world, wet stone wears moss, or lichen at least, some green or gray sympathy. Here — nothing. No moss, no lichen, no grass blade in any seep, no bird crossing this high, no bone, no dung, no track. There is water with nothing to drink it, light with nothing to eat it. The niches stand open. And so the mountain's own processes have moved into them the way hermits move into abandoned houses. In the dark of the cracks, crystal gardens grow — selenite and calcite inching out into the seep-water, collapsing when a crack widens and exposes them, regrowing. On the evaporation flats, salt blooms spread in slow white fires across the stone and die back in the next rain, a whole succession with no life in it at all. Clay lenses slump downhill in measurable steps, year over year, like something walking. At dawn, in the few pockets of dust, needle-ice stands up in the cold and lies down by midmorning. These are the inhabitants. They are busy. The place is dense with them — every surface occupied, layered, claimed: a single boulder will be a drip-line, a salt garden, a resonator, and a cairn-base all at once. Nothing here is only one thing.

The loudness builds in layers. Under the drip-choir: the wind, which the mountain has been carving for a long time and has carved into an instrument — fluted notches and hollowed slabs that moan and pipe when the pressure shifts. Above that: the cracks. Freeze-thaw works the joints every night, and the stone speaks in single sharp knocks that roll across the basins. And threaded through it all, the bells — because the builders hung bells, hundreds of them, small copper things green as pondwater, along with stone chimes and slotted resonators, set in crevices and from cairn-arms along every traverse. Most are stopped with clay. The ones left open ring a breath *before* the wind arrives to move them.

That is the second wrong thing, and the one the whole place is organized around: the Weeping Roof has slipped its synchronization with the world. Events here happen twice, or out of order, or with a gap between the thing and its twin. You hear the crack, then see the slab fall. A tarn at dusk reflects a noon sky. Your shadow — you will have two, which you can blame on the cloud-glow from below, at first — your two shadows disagree about the hour, and you watch the wrong one swing around, unhurried, to catch up. Echoes do not answer; they anticipate, or they return sounds that were never made, and in certain notched amphitheaters old sounds persist as standing loops — a bell struck by a storm years ago still rings there every afternoon at the same slipped time. Never answer an echo here. The echo may not be answering you.

The gap breathes. This is the mountain's daily cycle, its weather under the weather: the lag is widest at dawn and dusk, the hinge hours, when the world smears and doubles and sounds detach from their sources entirely. It narrows through the morning, and at noon, when the sun stands directly over the pale stone, everything is pinned — drip and sound arrive together, shadows keep their owners' feet, the place holds still and sharp and almost sane. Then the afternoon loosens it again. Night is the free run: with no sun at all, the lag goes where it likes, and the strangest of the inversions takes hold — the sky goes black, star-fierce black, and the ground does not. The basins and light-pools give back the day's brightness slowly, like coals, and the whalebacks glow a dim gray-blue, and you navigate by the dark overhead. Bright land, black sky. The world turned upside down for anyone who learned it the other way.

The deep time, though — the deep time is honest. Whatever has gone wrong here is wrong in the *schedule*, not the shape. The erosion keeps its steady state, the silhouette holds, the water still condenses and weeps and overflows by the old rules. It is only the fast layers — light, sound, reflection, causality — that have come unpinned. The mountain's body keeps its appointments. It's the mountain's ghost that runs late, or early, or twice.

The builders understood this, and their scattered remains — there is no town, nothing concentrated, just marks and small works spread across the benches and traverses — are all addressed to it. They had no metal beyond small copper, no tools beyond hammer stones and fire and frost-water; they split slabs by heating and dousing them, and pecked their marks a hand-span deep. The doubled-circle glyphs run in lines across the open stone, and where they run, the geometry behaves: follow the marks and a col is where it was, a basin takes the same hour to cross coming back as going. Step off the marked line, into the unmarked interior of whalebacks, and the plateau becomes bigger than it is — distances stretch, a ridge you crossed cannot be found from the far side, and your own footsteps may arrive a moment early to meet you. The marks are not decoration. They are stitching. They hold the landscape to itself.

The mirror courts are the finest of their works. In sheltered hollows, a dozen or a hundred mirrors of polished stone and green copper stand angled on cairn-posts, all trained on the sun's path — and a court where the mirrors still stand is a room where the world still agrees with itself. Step into one at the hinge of dusk, when everything outside is smearing and doubling, and you feel it like a held breath released: your shadow snaps to your feet, the drip and its note arrive as one, the echo waits its proper turn. Whether the mirrors *did* this, or only told the builders when it was happening, no one can say. Either way, they kept them turned to the light.

They left, in the end. Their last marks are unambiguous: mirrors turned face-down to the stone, bells stopped with clay, doorways sealed with a single slab. No graves — there are no bones anywhere on the Roof; whatever they were, they did not leave their dead here, or the mountain does something with the dead that leaves no remainder. Perhaps the lag simply outgrew their tools — a season, a decade, when the gap stretched past what mirrors and marked lines could hold — and they went down into the cloud and did not come back.

But they did not unplug the whole mountain, and the mountain did not stop. It is still fully in motion, still loud, still keeping its appointments in the wrong order. Storms build below the rim and break upward against it; the cloud band breathes up and down the flanks each day; the weep-walls run, the salts bloom and die, the standing echoes keep their slipped hours, the freeze cracks the stone each night in single loud knocks. Stand in the unmarked interior at dusk, at the wide hinge of the lag, and you can hear it practicing: a bell with no wind yet, a crack like a trial knock from a joint that will let go in some other moment, light pooling in the basins for a night that hasn't arrived. All of it full, all of it functioning, all of it ongoing — a vast, bright, ringing mechanism with nobody left to hear it agree or disagree with itself, patiently out of step with the world, and entirely content to be.
