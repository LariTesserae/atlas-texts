From the lowlands — the dry lowlands, the true desert that rings it — the plateau looks like a green weather system that decided to settle. It rises maybe half a day's climb above the stone, a table the size of a small continent, and it wears a ring of cloud around its shoulders that is not weather arriving but weather *leaving*: the land breathing out. Everything beyond that rim is dry, has been dry for longer than memory. Everything on top is the Keep.

The Keep is one plant. Or it is a parliament of plants so old and so intergrown that the distinction stopped mattering before any eye arrived to make it. There is no soil up here. Soil was a phase the world went through. What you walk on is a felted living crust — springy, fibrous, warm — root over root over ancestor, miles deep in the old basins, thin as a drumhead on the swells. It covers everything. It is the terrain, the architecture, the furniture, the larder, the reservoir. When something dies, the Keep takes it back within a season, which is why there is almost no history up here, almost nothing built that stays built, only faint marks: a well-head of woven stalk, a line of rattle-poles leaning, half-digested.

The water is inside it. That's the first rule to understand, because the air up here is dry enough to crack lips, rain comes perhaps a dozen times a year and each rain gets a name, and yet the whole continent is a cistern. The Keep closed its water cycle an age ago and never reopened it. At dawn it raises cooling-vanes — broad, pale fins of leaf — and dew gathers on them in the thin high air and runs down into bladders. At dusk it exhales, and the exhale fogs back down as a fine local rain, mostly onto itself. Under the crust, in basins the old rivers carved when the world was younger and wetter and open, the water sits in cistern-caverns, some the size of cathedrals, some lidded, some open to the sky as ponds — the only open water on the plateau, scattered days apart, pockets in the dryness, each one rimmed with a different lip of bloom. And deeper still, the rivers still run. They never stopped. They just went under, into the dark, and you can hear them through the wells if you put your ear to a shaft: a bass rushing, the bloodstream of the place, under everything, always.

You hear a great deal up here. The Keep is the loudest place in the world, and this is not incidental. It is structural.

The floor of the sound is the needle-singers — thumb-sized lives in their uncountable trillions, densest in the flute-forests where the Keep grows hollow organ-pipe stalks thirty feet tall. Their drone is a continuous silver pressure that peaks at noon past the threshold of pain, and after a few days you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heart, and then you spend the rest of your time up here half-listening for it without knowing you are, because of what it means when it falters. Above the drone ride the treaders. A thunder of treaders — that is what a herd is called, and it is exact — is a few hundred animals the size of hills, broad-padded, patient, walking the felt-flats in files a mile long, and their footfalls are not just noise. They are the hydrology. The great pads work the crust like bellows, driving water through the mat's channels the way a heart drives blood; when a treader stops to drink at a cistern-mouth, the long draw of it primes the whole local system. The herds migrate in vast loops keyed to the bloom, and where they pass, the wells run sweet. Where they linger too long, the mat stagnates and the cisterns sour.

Something keeps them moving. That is the other sound, or rather the other *absence*.

The people up here call it the Quiet, and the name is also the description. It is a predator — pale, fringed, soft as ash, big enough to take a treader at the flank — and it hunts by listening for holes in the song. Silence is what it eats. Everything on the Keep knows this the way everything everywhere knows gravity, and so the world screams. The drone, the dusk-chord, the foghorn calls of the thunders, the shrilling of the flute-forests — all of it is camouflage, a murmuration of noise, a continent roaring so that no single life can be located within it. When the needle-singers falter in some direction, when a shape of stillness opens in the wall of sound and moves, everything with legs either freezes or drums. The people carry frame-drums for exactly this, and they can drum false-panic well enough to steer a thunder, which is the closest thing up here to herding. Fear, on the Keep, is a public utility. The Quiet culls the treaders, the treaders flee, the fleeing pumps the water. The whole green world runs on terror as much as on light, and the old people say so plainly: if the Quiet ever died out, the water would stop.

The people themselves are sparse — the Keep is sparse, for all its totality. One living carpet, and then enormous distances between presences: a cistern-camp here, three days of humming empty felt before the next. Their technology is what the plant provides: thorn and bone and fiber and resin, woven huts grown from living stalk that must be pruned every few days or the walls close up and digest the furniture, well-heads with bone ladders going down into the cold dark, long lines for the blind white things of the under-rivers. Metal is sky-iron, meteoric, found out on the flats where falls can be watched at night and walked to by day, and a sky-iron blade is an heirloom with a genealogy. Their maps are songs. In a land with no landmarks but sameness, where dusk fog can erase the world in minutes, a route is a sequence of pitches and rhythms you perform as you walk; to forget the tune is to be lost, and children are taught the roads before they are taught anything else. At every camp at dusk, when the exhale lays its dense warm breath-layer over the ground — a duct, a sound-conducting stratum in which a shout can travel a hundred miles — the horns begin, relay to relay, news crossing the whole vastness on the world's own breath. Births, deaths, a thunder's heading, a storm sighted at the Edge. The dusk-mail. Primitive hands, and physics doing the rest.

The scale of it does strange things to time. The bloom arrives from the east each year as a wave hundreds of miles long and walks westward at the pace of a slow traveller, so that the season is a place as much as a date — you can live behind it, walk with it for weeks inside it, or wait for it. You hear it coming before you see it: seed-pods detonating like distant artillery along the front, the needle-singers changing key, and at night during the bloom's passage the pollen-haze catches the light of the Keep's own faint luminescence and the dark turns green-gold, a second dawn that lasts till the true one. Storms are the other calendar. Out of the dry sky beyond the Edge they come up the scarp a dozen times a year, and the whole continent tilts its vanes to them and strips them in a day, and the people run to open the cistern-mouths, and the event gets a name, and years are remembered by their storms the way other folk remember kings.

There are older rhythms underneath. On the swells where the crust runs thin, the old rock comes near the surface — the bonelands, dark wind-polished stone from before the green — and there the Keep does something it does nowhere else: it retreats at noon, the mat creeping back from the scorching rock, and flows over it again at dusk. A daily tide of green over stone, the one place you can stand and watch the plant *move*, the one place you can see the world's old body and understand that all of this — every stalk, every song — is a skin over something ancient and eroded and patient. And in the collapse-shafts, where the crust has slumped into a cistern-cavern, you can climb down into the Undergreen: the pressed ancestor-layers, warm with their own slow fermentation, stratified like pages, a hundred thousand years of blooms stacked in the dark. It is the only archive the Keep has, the only place anything is remembered. The people give their dead to it. What the mat eats, it keeps.

The drifters come up from the Undergreen too, in a sense — gas-bladder bodies the size of sails, hatched from blisters in the warm deep layers, rising on the hot dusk air to trail tendrils down into the cisterns and steal. The people harpoon them with thorn for their water-skins, and forgive them, because the drifters carry spores between the provinces and keep the far-flung Keep one body. Everything up here is like that: theft and gift indistinguishable, predator and plumbing the same system viewed from two sides.

The light, at least, is honest — a white-gold noon, a green-gold dusk through the exhale-haze, nights of violent stars once the breath-layer settles. Nothing hides up here except everything, inside the noise.

So the rules, as the camps teach them:

Sing while you travel. Drum what you cannot sing. Drink where the mat allows, drum the old foot-rhythms at a lidded cistern and it will open to you, and pour a measure back, because the Keep keeps accounts. Give your dead to the deep. Learn the roads as songs. When the dusk-chord sounds, listen for the horns.

And when the song falters — when a hole opens in the great roaring and begins, softly, to move — be still, be loud, or be gone below, and wait for the Quiet to pass, and for the world to remember how to scream.
