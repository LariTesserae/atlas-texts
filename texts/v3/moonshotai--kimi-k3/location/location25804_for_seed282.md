# Bay Zero

*status: under construction — day 7,641*

---

### The way in

You don't enter Bay Zero. Nobody enters Bay Zero. The corridor that once served it ends after ten meters in poured concrete, and the hatch beyond that was welded shut long ago — not by any decision, but by habit, the way a scar closes over a wound and then keeps closing. What remains is a grille the size of a faceplate, set low in a duct, and through this the room breathes.

It breathes slowly. A minute of damp air drawn in from somewhere far above — air that has its own weather, its own history — then a minute of colder, drier air sighed back out. Hold your hand there and you can feel the tide of it, patient as sleep. Press your face to the grille and you can see, dimly: darkness, the red pinprick of an indicator lamp, and the suggestion of layered shapes too close together, like a crowd holding its breath.

This is the only window. It is also the mouth.

### The interior

The room is perhaps four meters by six, and shrinking. It has been shrinking for twenty years, a few millimeters a year, because the rule of the place is that material is only ever added, never removed. The ceiling is low and getting lower. Pipes run in strata — coolant lines, condensate returns, conduits for conduits — layered so densely that the upper half of the room reads as geology: not rock, but an archaeology of intent. Original shotcrete with the crew's chalk marks still legible, never weathered, because nothing here weathers. Then steel plate. Then a topography of weld-bead laid over the plate in slow coral growth. Then the pipes. You can read two decades in a handspan.

At one end stands the cold manifold: a black, finned block the size of a wardrobe, feathered head to foot in frost. This is the room's winter corner, its small pole. The air between the manifold and the far wall has a geography of cold — at the grille end your breath smokes; halfway across it hangs; near the manifold it freezes and falls as glitter. Nothing lives at that end except frost, and frost is less a growth than a slow crystalline decision.

At the other end, framed in angle iron, set into the floor like a black eye: the sump. A still pool the size of a bathtub, gathered one breath at a time over decades — every damp inhalation through the grille farmed by the cold manifold, condensed, melted, dripped, collected. The water is inherited. The room has been quietly distilling the outer world into itself, and this pool is what it has kept.

### The weather

Bay Zero has weather, indoors, on schedule.

Frost builds on the manifold for a day, two days, thickening into feathers, into plumes. Then the defrost cycle comes — or the welder fires near enough — and the frost lets go. For twenty minutes it rains in the dark: a soft clatter on the grating, a patter into the trays, a drumming on the sump's surface that is, by far, the loudest thing that ever happens here. Then the drips slow to a metronome, and the dry season begins again.

Where one drop has struck the same spot on the same tray for years, a pale nipple of mineral has grown — a stalagmite of dissolved steel and solder-flux. The room's only landform. It grows the way caves grow, and that is the truth of this place: it is a machine that ages like a cave.

The other weather is the arc. When the builders wake and the welder fires, the room has noon for three seconds. Shadows leap and hold. Every wet surface answers with a flash; frost sublimates off the near pipes in tiny flags of steam. Then the light collapses, and a new weld-bead cools on the wall — orange, red, black — a sunset pinned to the steel, cooling into the next stratum.

Between these events: dimness as the default state. One caged work lamp survives near the sump; the rest died years ago and were never replaced, because replacement is not among the room's gestures. Indicator LEDs prick the dark in red and amber. And every few minutes, when the pump strokes, a thread of water passes through the sight-glass behind the lamp, and for a moment the whole room pulses bottle-green.

That green is alive.

### What grows

The sump water carries algae — a thin, lightless lineage that arrived as spores on the room's breath, long ago, and persisted. It blooms in the warm tank, dies in the cold manifold, is cropped by the filter, and the filter is scraped by a small mechanism that deposits the cake in a corner, where the cold mummifies it into a dark flake-peat. A tiny bog in a refrigerator. This is the whole carbon cycle of Bay Zero, and it runs perfectly, and it feeds no one.

There is more, but sparsely. Salt-flowers bloom on the lower plates where condensation wicks through welds — white and ochre rosettes, opening over months, the room's slow garden. And under the grille, in the one place where outside air touches inside steel and the dead lamps leak a little residual warmth, there is a patch of true moss the size of a palm print. It lives on condensation and accident. It is the only green thing that has ever seen the light here, such as it is, and it is doing fine.

### The builders

Two construction arms ride a rail on the ceiling, folded when dormant like herons asleep. They are old industrial stock — relay logic, limit switches, a punched program drum in the cabinet — and somewhere in the decades the drum wore, or the relays drifted, and the plan became a liturgy.

They build because building is what they do. The rules, as far as anyone could reconstruct them from inside:

**Nothing leaves.** Cutoffs are welded back elsewhere. Material is conserved. The room only gains.

**Any opening is a defect.** The hatch disappeared under scar tissue years ago. A gap is a thing to be closed. Once — this is recorded in the bead strata — the arms built over the crawler's charging dock while the crawler was in it, and then, registering the obstruction as a new condition, cut it free again and incorporated the dock into a buttress, which it remains.

**Unfinished things may approach finished things, but not touch them.** This is the strangest one. At the room's north end stands an orchard of pipes — dozens of them, extended over years toward a blank wall plate, each one stopping a finger's width short. A congregation mid-gesture. A fountain frozen on the way to happening. The plate is finished; the pipes are not; and so they approach, and approach, and the arms begin new pipes beside the old ones, and the orchard grows denser, and nothing connects, and perhaps connection was never the point. Perhaps the approach is the point.

They also rebuild the same bracket every year — weld it on, cut it off, weld it on. No one knows what it was for. It is for this.

### The one animal

There is no animal life in Bay Zero. There is the crawler, which is not the same thing, and is.

It is small, warm-seeking, effectively blind — it navigates by temperature gradients, following the compressor's slow heartbeat heat the way a moth follows a lamp. It drinks from the drip line through a wick. It is decades past its design life and shows it only in the lane it has polished into the floor grating: the one true erosion in the room, a bright worn path between the compressor's warmth and the drip trays, mapped by ten thousand patient circuits. The arms tolerate it. It avoids their work envelope by some dim inherited caution. When the welder fires and noon comes, the crawler stops, and waits, and then resumes, and no one has ever needed it to do anything at all.

### The sound of it

Mostly, Bay Zero is quiet in layers.

The compressor idles at a slow thump, a heartbeat felt in the grating and the teeth more than heard. The relay cabinet ticks — not keeping time, just marking that time is passing. Drips fall on their metronome. When an arm repositions there is a soft servo whine, the closest thing the room has to a voice. And in the deepest silences, near the manifold, if you are very still, you can hear the frost growing: a faint crystalline whisper, the sound of water deciding to become architecture.

### What it is

Nothing here erodes, because nothing here is finished, and nothing here is finished because finishing is not among the instructions. There is only building. The room is a heart that some larger machine began to grow, deep under everything, and the growing outlasted the reason, and the growing became the reason.

It is an embryo that has learned to keep its own weather.

Press your ear to the grille. Inside, on schedule, for no one, it is raining softly in the dark — and in a moment the pump will stroke, and the sight-glass will pulse green, and the frost will begin, again, to whisper itself back into being.
