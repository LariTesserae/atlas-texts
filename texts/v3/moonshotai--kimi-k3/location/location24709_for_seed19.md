They call it the Long Morning, the ones who have been up and come back down. You earn the name at the notch, where the climb finally lets go of you and you step out onto the plateau — and the first thing you notice is that your breathing is the loudest thing in the world.

The air is thin and cool and utterly still. It is the air of extreme altitude, the sky overhead gone deep violet-blue, the sun a hard bright coin — and the light is *normal*, that's the strange part, ordinary mid-morning sunlight, the useful hour when shadows are distinct and colors are honest. It has been mid-morning here for as long as anyone can say. Your shadow will be the same length when you leave, however long you stay.

The land is old beyond reckoning and finished with itself. A sea died here, high in the sky, and what it left is a white floor of salt pans cracking into mature polygons, the fissures filled with dust that will never blow again. Around the pans stand whaleback ridges of dark stone, rounded and glossed with desert varnish, every wind-carved facet on every ventifact polished to its final shape by a wind that no longer exists. From the ridges you can read the ghost rivers — braids of pale gravel threading through dark pavement, channels that carried their last water before the oldest thing alive was born. And along the far rim, stair-stepping down the flanks of the range, run the strandlines: the sea's old shorelines, a white ring around the drained bathtub of the world. Erosion here didn't slow down. It *completed*. Even the talus has locked into a single settled pavement, and no stone shifts underfoot unless you pry it — and then it stays pried, forever, because that is the first rule of the place.

The world does not change. It only records.

You learn the second rule when the boulder field resolves. Crossing toward the White Floor, you take the middle distance for geology — a strewn field of dun boulders, thousands of them, dense as a moraine — and then the boulders have horns. Lyre-curved horns, salt-rough hides, legs folded under heavy shoulders. The herds. They came down to the salt on the last morning there was, and they are here still: tens of thousands of animals packed flank to flank across the pan, standing, kneeling, mid-stride — and at the pan's edge, hundreds of them bent to the crust with their tongues extended, almost touching the salt, the lick interrupted and never to be completed. Calves stay calves. A cat crouches at the herd's edge, mid-stalk, eternal; the calf it selected is mid-turn, forever almost-alarmed. Overhead, birds hang in the air column like seeds in amber without the amber — a lammergeier fixed in its glide, a flock of small brown birds each caught at its own phase of wingbeat, their shadows lying on the salt beneath them, oval and permanent, clocks that all tell the same time. A hare mid-bound in the tussocks. A bee at a flower that is fixed open. Lizards on the rocks, holding the warmth of an hour that never ends.

They are not statues. That is what undoes you. Touch a flank and it is warm, it yields, it smells faintly of animal — the air itself carries a thin musk it is not releasing, a scent that belongs to the captured moment and never fades and never strengthens. But there is no heartbeat. No breath moves the ribs. Ten thousand lungs, and the loudest sound on the plateau is your own boot crunching salt — a crunch that goes nowhere, swallowed dead within meters by the thin air, no echo off the ridges, nothing. The herds do not startle. Nothing here is waiting for anything. Waiting requires a future.

The vegetation is what you'd expect of a dry, high, cold place, and it is stopped too: grey needle-grass in sparse tussocks across the gravel, every blade leaning in a wind that is not blowing and has not blown since; hard cushion plants on the ridges, blooming, their tiny flowers open at their final extent; black and orange lichen rosettes on the lee faces of stones, finished spreading. Nothing grows. Nothing dies. Nothing rots. The world's ecology converged on this salt on one morning at the peak of the gathering — the herds to the lick, the herders behind the herds, the cat behind them all, the vultures above, waiting on a death that has still not occurred — and the convergence simply *held*.

The herders are here. That is the third thing you learn. They stand among their animals in hide cloaks and felt, bone toggles, one bright hammered-copper ornament on an old woman's chest — a woman caught mid-turn toward a child, the child mid-reach. Their faces are not grotesque. That would be easier. Their faces are ordinary, and occupied, and elsewhere. You will walk the cairned paths — low stacks of stone placed by later hands, threading deliberate corridors through the density — and you will understand why the paths exist: so that you never have to push a body aside, because what you move stays moved, and no one wants to spend the rest of their life remembering the exact posture in which they left a person.

The paths connect the tally-houses: low bothies of stacked slabs, roofed in hide taken from the same species that stands outside them, spaced a day's walk apart along the threaded ways. Inside: a hearth of blackened stones and cold ash, a neat stack of dried dung fuel, a coil of grass rope, a knapped stone blade, a single copper awl worn to a nub. Dust motes hang fixed in the shaft of light from the door. And on the wall, tallies — scratched counts of days, in a place without days, kept by people who carried their own private time inside them like a coal. Some counts run long. All of them end mid-stroke. Every mark in this place looks made yesterday, and there is no way to tell which yesterday: no weathering, no fading, no dust. The land carries the deepest time there is, and every human mark upon it is newborn. The tally-houses counted days, not reasons. No one left an explanation, if anyone ever had one.

In each house there is a bowl. Open, cold, dustless, full or nearly full of water — the only water in the world, carried up by hands that are standing frozen somewhere outside. Nothing evaporates here; nothing dries; the level you leave in the bowl is the level the next traveler finds, in a year or in ten thousand years, and so the bowls are ledgers. They record everyone's thirst and everyone's restraint, and the difference, measured in millimeters.

You will think about the calf. Everyone does. The path passes close enough that you can see its eye, and far enough that intervening is a walk you have to choose. You could push the cat aside. You could carry the calf to its mother. Both would stay exactly as you arranged them, warm and unbreathing and unchanging, your mercy rendered as permanent sculpture under a sun that will never set to forgive it. The cairn-builders thought about it too. The paths are their answer: move nothing. Leave the moment intact. It is the only one the world has left.

From the central pan, the country runs days in every direction — the Whalebacks east, the Ghost Rivers braiding north, the strandline stairs climbing the far rim, the Congregation spread below you in its tens of thousands. At the rim, a week's walk off, the air thickens and the land falls away toward a hazy lower country, and no one can tell you whether the sun moves down there, because the people who went down to check did not come back up to say. Above, there will be no stars tonight, because there will be no night. No one on this plateau will ever see a star again.

What it does to you, in the end, is make you careful. You are the only weather here. Your footfall is the loudest sound; your fire, if you light one, is the only flame; your footprints, pressed into the salt behind you, are the only biography the world permits — a line of disturbance that will outlast the mountains' patience, spelling out where you walked and what you touched and whether you were kind. You drink little from the bowls. You walk the corridors. You do not move the calf.

And the light holds. The herds bend to the salt they will never taste. The grass leans into the wind that isn't blowing. It is a good hour, the hour this place keeps — useful, honest, mild — and it is always, always now.
