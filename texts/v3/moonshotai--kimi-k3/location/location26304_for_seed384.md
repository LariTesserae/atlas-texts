The country around it is flat and cold and gives nothing away. A marsh of black water and sedge, birch scrub no taller than a fence, a sky that spends most of the year the color of old tin. In the middle of this flatness is a pond the shepherds call the Kettle — maybe two hundred meters across, round as a coin, frozen from early autumn to late spring. The ice on it is not like other ice. It is very dark and very smooth, and it does not creak the way pond ice should.

On the shore there is a small concrete hut with a transformer inside that hums through the ground more than through the air. A cable runs from it down the bank and into the water and does not come out. The gate to the hut has a padlock that is newer than everything else here — the only bright, recent object in the whole marsh. Someone still pays for the electricity. Somewhere, in an office that has changed names four times, a meter reading is still being filed.

And out in the Kettle, about a third of the way from the northern bank, a riveted iron stack stands a meter above the waterline, no wider than a man's shoulders. It does not smoke. It breathes. Every few minutes it exhales — a slow, warm, oil-smelling breath — and then draws in again, patient as a sleeper. In the deepest winter, when the rest of the Kettle is a single black pane, the ice around the stack never closes. There is a circle of open water there, steaming faintly, big enough to swim in.

That is the door.

---

Going down through the water column is like falling in slow motion through a building whose floors have been removed. The first meters, in summer, are tea-colored and almost warm. Then you pass through the smear layer — a visible, oily boundary where cold water meets colder — and for that one meter of depth the world below exists twice, doubled and sliding, and you cannot tell which version is true. Below the layer, the water is three degrees and clear as glass, clear in the way only cold, spring-fed, lifeless water is clear, and the dark comes up around you not like night but like depth itself.

Below you, arranged with unmistakable intent, are a few amber points of light. Not scattered. *Placed.* One at a junction. One over a doorway. One at the far end of a long open space. They are very far apart, and the darkness between them is whole and undivided.

You can follow the stack down. It continues into the black, rivet seam after rivet seam, cold through the glove, a conduit of cables lashed to its flank with clamps every meter. It goes down thirty meters and ends in a roof.

---

The town is small — you could swim its perimeter in ten minutes — and it is dense the way old industrial hamlets are dense, everything built within a shout of the hooter. Two lanes cross at a lamplit junction. Six workers' cottages stand in a row, shoulder to shoulder. There is a yard, a weighbridge, a loading dock, a rail spur with a single sheeted wagon still sitting on it, and at the bottom of the long lane the bottling hall, the biggest building, its tall doors standing open. The lanes are narrow enough that your shoulders nearly brush both walls, and they are roofed — not by the sky, which is thirty meters of water away — but by pipework. Water mains, air lines, steam pipes gone cold, cable trays, all of it crossing from gable to gable in bundles, layered and re-layered, furred now with a grey mineral rind, so that swimming down a lane is like swimming through the inside of a diagram.

Nothing here is ruined. That is the first strange thing, and it takes a while to understand. The water that drowned this place a century and more ago is hard, lime-heavy spring water, and it does not erode. It *deposits*. Everything sharp has been rounded not by wearing away but by being coated — dipped again and again in a thin solution of itself until brick carries a sinter skin, iron grows rusticles like frozen wax-drips, and a washing line between two cottages still holds three shirts, stiff as slate, swaying only when the current shifts. They have been drying for a hundred years. Nothing rots here. Time moves in one direction only, and that direction is *onward onto* — layer upon layer, the town slowly becoming a stalactite of itself. Whatever was going to fall fell long ago and is cemented where it landed. Whatever stands, stands.

The long lane is a sluice. A slow, constant current walks down it — drawn by the hall's intakes, bound for an outfall grille at the pit's far end — and it has kept its own street swept for a century. The cobbles in the current's path are clean; silt lies only in the lees of doorsteps, fine as ash, in dunes the size of a hand. The doors on the current side all stand open, and they move — a slow swing out, a rest, a slow swing back — keeping time with the faint pulse of the machinery's thirst. The whole town breathes like this, at the scale of hours, and after a while you stop noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heart.

---

Of the streetlamps, most are dead — intact globes full of dark water, some holding a single ancient bubble of gas like a thought. Four still burn. Amber, hooded, steady, drawing their power from the cable off the marsh, and each one keeps a garden. Around every burning lamp, on wall and cobble and lamppost base, grows a perfectly circular lawn of pale algae, cropped by light to the exact radius of usefulness — the only green circles in the town, glowing faintly, belonging entirely to the lamps. Apart from these, vegetation is a rumor: a black-green felt of moss on the shaded brick, five drowned orchard trees in a cottage yard with their fruit still on the branches, turned to stone, faintly green where copper nails bled into them a century ago.

Under the junction lamp hangs the resident. A trout, white as paper, slow as a clock-hand, thick as your forearm — whether one fish or a succession of fish occupying the same post, no one can say. It has rounds. Lamp to lamp, a circuit that takes most of a day, and it is always somewhere on its circuit, hanging in the amber light as if waiting for a tram that is, on the whole, likely to come.

---

The hall is the heart, and the heart is a spring. In the floor of the hall, under an iron grate, is the old well shaft from which the water rises — endlessly, steadily, a half-degree warmer than everything around it, so that a faint shimmer stands above the grate like heat off a road, and you feel the upwelling on your cheek as a thin cool push. For fifty years the town bottled this spring. The spring has now had the town for more than a hundred, and it seems to find it useful.

The rising water turns two horizontal turbines. The turbines turn a line shaft. The line shaft turns a generator, whose hum you do not hear so much as receive in the sternum, a single low note with no edges. Needle gauges on a brass-bound panel tremble at their readings. A glass-domed utility meter spins, patiently, for the office on the far shore of nowhere. And on the wall, lit by its own small lamp, the master clock keeps the correct time.

Once a day — when the pressure behind the panel reaches its mark — a relay drops, and the bottling line runs a shift. There is no crash of machinery; sound here is muffled to a felt vibration in the water, a busyness in the bones. But the line runs. Bottles march from the crate bays — thousands upon thousands of empties, stacked to the eaves, a stock the line has been drawing down for a century without visibly diminishing — they march, and they are filled with the water they are standing in, and they are capped, and they are stood in ranks at the conveyor's end. The ranks are full. Every shift, a few bottles tip off the end and join the drift of their predecessors on the floor: a slow glass scree, centuries deep, every bottle sealed around a sample of the thing it lies in.

Sometimes one of them settles wrong, and ticks against another. It is, some weeks, the loudest thing that happens in the town. Sound travels far in this water — the silence is not a failure of transmission but a scarcity of events — and when the glass ticks, the whole town hears it. The white fish flinches, once, and resumes its round.

---

The compressor house still feeds air to the old tool lines, and by fault or by fortune — the distinction has dissolved — this means that several of the buildings hold their breath. Find the floor hatch in the watchman's cottage. Swim up the stairwell. The water stops at chest height in the upstairs room, and you surface in black air.

The air is cold and dense and it smells of machine oil, ozone, old paper, and the ghost of wet wool. It is breathable; the compressor tops it up, and it leaks out slowly through the window joints, so it is always a century stale and always fresh enough. A small brass electric lamp burns in a wall bracket. A coat hangs dry on its peg. A ledger lies open on the desk, the ink sharp, the last entry mid-line. On the wall is a telephone. If you lift it, there is a hum. The line is live. Somewhere, an exchange answers with silence.

The water level in the room rises and falls a hand's width over the hours — the room's small tide, matching the pressure-breathing of the whole basin — and on the wallpaper there is a dark tidemark stain, and the water still touches it exactly. It has not gained or lost in decades. Sometimes the room sighs a bubble out under its eaves, and a burst of silver goes climbing up the lane, and the cottages along it, each in turn, let out their own small breath, as if the street were passing a message upward.

In here, a drip from the ceiling lands like a tap on a tabletop. Your own breathing — you are on a rebreather, out of courtesy to the quiet, recycling your breath the way everything here recycles everything — is the loudest sound in a hundred meters. From the lane outside, the pay office can be seen as a single lit window, thick glass glowing amber into the black water: an aquarium reversed, light on the inside, the fish passing by without looking in.

---

The place runs at many paces at once, all of them settled, all of them reconciled with each other long ago. By the minute: the stack breathes, the rooms tide, the doors swing. By the day: the line runs its shift, the clock stays right, the fish completes its round. By the season: the ice roof closes and the darkness becomes total except for the four lamps; pressure builds under the sealed basin all winter; and once a year, in the deep of the freeze, the relief valve on the old hooter line lifts, and the shift whistle sounds — one low note, muffled by water and ice, lasting several seconds, felt through the soles of the feet by anything standing on the pond above. Then a year of silence. In the brief summer the ice thins and breaks, and for a week or two a grey noon reaches all the way down, and the moss felt greens almost visibly, and once — it is remembered the way a town remembers things, which is to say it is still happening — a duck came down the light in a panic of bubbles, tore the length of the long lane, and was gone. By the century: the rind thickens. The town sets. The tidemark does not move.

The boundary is exact. Swim past the outfall grille — its bars polished by a hundred years of current, the eel that lives behind it sliding once, slowly, through its dark — and there is a last lamppost, and after it the town simply stops, and the water continues into the empty black corners of the pit, and that is all. There is nothing else. There does not need to be.

---

Going up, you follow the stack, and its warm exhaled breath touches your cheek through the water, and you come up inside the black circle of open water in the ice, under a sky the color of old tin. The hut hums. The padlock shines. The marsh says nothing.

Below you, the meter spins, the clock is correct, the fish is mid-round between the second and third lamps, and at the pressure hour the line will run its shift again, filling bottles with the water they stand in, standing them in ranks for no one, correctly, in the dark.

The town drowned a long time ago. It has not stopped. Nothing here is waiting — waiting implies an end. This is a shift. The shift is long. And once a year, the hooter sounds, and everything is as it should be.
