The plain gives no warning. You cross an hour of bare gray karst — limestone pavement, solution runnels, nothing growing, nothing taller than a fist — and then the ground breathes at you. A warmth, a dampness on the cheek, a faint mineral smell like a struck match extinguished in milk. The vault is simply there: a collapsed place in the skin of the land, a hundred paces across, its rim finished long ago with falling. No fresh scars anywhere. The rock has stopped being surprised.

You descend by steps that are not quite steps — a path of flat dark cobbles set into the stone, each worn to a shallow dish by feet. The light changes as you go down, though it was never strong to begin with. There is no sun here, only a pale oval of sky far overhead, strained through steam into a general pearl-gray brightness with no direction and no source. Shadows barely exist. At the brightest hour the haze overhead lightens to the color of wet bone; at the dimmest, everything subsides into blue-gray dusk. That slow swell and ebb is the only clock the place owns, and it is the same every day, so it functions less as time than as a fact.

The floor of the vault is water. That is the first true thing about it. Dozens of pools lie rim to rim down the slope of the basin, held in scalloped dams of travertine — cream, ochre, tea-brown, crusted like the edges of some enormous pastry. Each dam spills into the next pool in a sheet of water so thin and even it makes almost no sound, only a low hiss like breathing heard from another room. The pools themselves are milky blue-green, faintly luminous in the dimness; they are the brightest objects in the place, and they throw soft light upward onto the walls, so the far side of the vault — where a third of the old cave roof survives — glows back with draperies of flowstone descending into the water like curtains let down into a bath. Stone teeth hang from the roof to within a finger of the surface. Where they have touched, over centuries, they have fused into columns that stand in the pools like the trunks of a drowned forest — but there is no wood here, has never been wood. Nothing is green. Nothing has ever been green.

There is no bare surface anywhere. Every ledge carries crust or film or standing water; every stone is velveted with something living, though calling it living almost overstates it — films of rust-red where the water carries iron, white streamers trailing in the warmest inflow where it carries sulfur, darker slick where it has cooled. These feed on the water's chemistry, not on light; light is too scarce here to be worth eating. On the films graze small pale shrimp, and in the pools, rarely, a fish drifts through — finger-length, white, eyeless, unhurried, come up from the dark sumps beyond the lamplight. You might watch a pool for an hour and see one, or none. There are exactly as many of everything as the films can feed, and the films grow exactly as fast as they are eaten. Nothing accumulates. Nothing runs short.

The air is warm and completely saturated, so that the boundary between water and not-water becomes unreliable. Droplets gather on your hair, your tools, the insides of your wrists; cloth never dries; you become, within the hour, one of the wet surfaces. It smells of wet stone, faint sulfur, a clean metallic note, and — near the back wall — clay and smoke.

That is where the keepers live, if living is the word for so light a presence. A low hut of stacked sinter blocks with a slab roof, more niche than house. A few clay lamps burning oil from a seep in the wall, their wicks of twisted mineral fiber. Tools laid out on a shelf: blades and scoops cut from the fossil shell beds in the vault's own limestone — the rock is an ancient reef, and the keepers' whitest, sharpest things are made of the dead sea — a pit-fired clay vessel, a sieve of traded hide, and one treasured strip of cold-hammered copper, thin as a leaf, used only to sound the gauge.

The gauge is a single upright slab set in the largest pool, incised with one horizontal line. The water kisses the line. It has always kissed the line. The spring that feeds the basin rises at the back of the vault at a temperature hotter than a held hand can bear, flows down the terraces cooling at a rate that never varies, and leaves through a swallet at the base of the far wall at exactly the rate it enters. Inflow equals outflow. Deposition at the dam crests equals dissolution at their bases, so the terraces are standing waves of mineral — the shapes ancient, the matter in them new, forever passing through. You can step in the same river twice here. It is the stones that flow.

The keepers — a household's worth of people, never many — do almost nothing, and do it perfectly. They clear a lip, plug a seep, patch a dam with sinter meal and clay, always toward the line, never away from it. Their law is that the place must be left as it was found, and since the place does not change, their work is a kind of listening. They listen literally: drips fall from the roof teeth at fixed heights and fixed intervals, each with its own note, and the whole vault hums one slow chord that never resolves and never varies. The keepers know the chord the way you know your own pulse. A new crack, a blocked drip, a shifted stone would arrive first as a wrong note, and they would hear it within a day. Speech is rare and low; their words seem built from the soft sounds the water already makes.

Nothing here decays, because there is nothing to rot. The films take back whatever falls. Even catastrophe has been digested: the great blocks that came down when the roof collapsed, in whatever age that was, are now only hummocks among the lower terraces, indistinguishable from the dams. Sometimes a bird crosses the oval of sky and does not stop. Sometimes, rarely, a keeper climbs the path and is gone for a while and returns with salt and fiber; the place does not notice. Rain, when it comes, is accepted and gone within the hour, the gauge line holding.

They call it, in their quiet language, something that means the Level, or the Held Water. The water rising from the spring today fell as rain before the keepers' words existed; the water sinking into the swallet carries its age away into the dark. Time is in the water, not in the place. You could date nothing here except by wear — the dish of the stepping stones, the thinness of the copper strip — and even those change too slowly to witness. The lamps burn. The chord holds. The pools sit full to their rims, warm as skin, dimly glowing, giving back to the steam exactly what the steam gives down.
