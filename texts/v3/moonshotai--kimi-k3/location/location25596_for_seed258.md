From the rim, the valley looks abandoned by its builders and occupied by its material. It sits high — high enough that the mornings begin above a standing sea of cloud that combs against the parapet of woven trunks along the edge and is never allowed in. Below that parapet, a green floor runs nearly flat for a half-day's walk to the far rim, and across it, spaced like thoughts that lost interest in each other, stand the constructs: a hall of columns, three towers, a long low nave, a raised causeway going nowhere in particular. Hours of emptiness lie between each one. Each is unmistakably made. Each is made of the same tissue as the ground.

Because the ground is the first thing to understand: there is no soil here. The floor is a dense, springy turf, warm underfoot, and if you tear it with your hands — there is nothing to cut with; nothing here has ever needed a tool — you find no earth beneath, only older floor, paler, packed like flesh. Below that, rootwork, braided and load-bearing. Below that, perhaps, something soft and uncommitted that never had to finish becoming stone, because the plants took the weight. Nothing has been worn down. There is no scree at the column bases, no sediment in the standing water, no rounded edge anywhere. The world here is raw in the way a bud is raw — not damaged, just early. It has stayed early.

The walls weep. Every ledge carries a runnel grown into it, half-pipe of living bark, and water moves along these in no hurry, descending in slow spirals to leaf-cups at the column bases, where it stands, clear and green-tasting, siltless. In the afternoon a warm rain falls, hard and brief; at night the whole valley condenses, and the constructs drink their own surfaces. It is the same water. It has always been the same water — the valley's original ration, cycling from crown to wall to cup to root to crown again, closed as a fist. The clouds that pass over the rim are not taxed. The valley needs nothing and takes nothing.

Everything made here ends in a bud. The three towers rise with a perfect taper, seamless, hard as old timber with the ringed density of trees but no grain you can follow to a joint, and at the top of each, where a spire or a chamber should be, there is a sealed bud the size of a house, warm as skin. Press your ear to it: a slow pulse. Sap, or something that keeps sap's hours. The arches of the column hall leap from pier to pier and almost meet; at the keystone point, a bud. The staircase in the nave climbs twenty flawless steps and stops in midair, terminating in a green knot you could crack a tooth on. Doorways open into chambers whose far walls exist only as a change in the turf's color — a paler rectangle, precisely laid, where a room has been reserved.

The reservations are the strangest things here, stranger than the towers. Most of the plan is unbuilt. Between the few raised constructs, the valley floor is inscribed with the geometries of a much larger city: the exact footprints of halls, courts, streets, kept clear and close-cropped, disciplined, waiting. You can stand in the middle of a house that will never be built and feel, unmistakably, that you are indoors — the emptiness is shaped. At noon, when the towers throw their plain honest shadows, the reservations darken faintly along their edges too, as though shadowed by walls they do not have. The plan casts its own shade. And so the valley is sparse the way a held breath is sparse: everything is here, but most of it is withheld.

Nothing finishes. Nothing decays. Nothing changes, though everything works. Growth proceeds constantly and arrives nowhere: buds swell and do not open; shoots extend by a hair and are let go — a clean abscission, a click, a soft fall — and by morning the floor has digested whatever dropped. There are no fallen leaves here older than a night. Decay is not refused; it is simply never allowed to accumulate, which is why there is no soil, which is why there is no history. Press a bruise into a wall with your thumb and come back in a year: the wall has healed to the same green, the same swelling, the same almost. The valley is a standing wave — immense activity, perfectly no result. Later is the one thing that never comes.

It is loud. You notice this after the strangeness, which is backwards, but the strangeness is quiet and the loudness is constant, so the ear files it under weather and it takes time to hear it again. The columns of the hall are hollow, each with a mouth grown open at its base, and the wind crossing the plateau plays them — a chord, low, vast, shifting by slow degrees as the wind shifts, audible from anywhere in the valley, so that distance here is measured in how the chord reaches you. Under the chord: the tick and creak of tissue holding itself exactly the same; sap knocking inside the towers like something climbing; the tuned plink of drips falling from ledges into leaf-cisterns of different depths. In rain, the nave's vaulted canopy holds a roar above your head and a resonant hush beneath it — the loudest silence you will ever stand inside.

There is almost no one. A few dozen slate-green birds nest only in the mouths of the columns, and at dusk they move from column to column singing single notes, correcting the chord — tuners, employed by no one. Large lichen-backed snails graze the turf along invisible lines, keeping it to regulation height; they are best understood as part of the building that occasionally moves. And there are the moths: pale, few, feeding at the seams of the sealed buds, which exude nectar for them through lips that never part. No one has seen a caterpillar. The moths die against the buds and are absorbed, and more moths come — from the buds, it must be, the buds releasing them the way flowers release scent, ambassadors of a bloom that will never occur, returning to a door that will never open.

Who grafted the arches? There are unions in the stonework of the nave — clean persuasions where one tissue was brought across a gap and convinced — and no hands here have ever held a tool, no tool has ever been needed, and any maker would long since have been taken back up by the floor, which keeps nothing. Perhaps the valley grew its tenders when it needed them and drank them when it was done. Perhaps the plan lives in every cell and needs no keeper. The place has no name; names are for things that have finished becoming.

Night falls the ordinary way, because the light here is ordinary — a plain sun, plain stars, an honest dark — and this may be the strangest thing of all: nothing hides. The weirdness stands in clear daylight at noon like a photograph of the impossible. After dark, the seams of the buds glow faintly, the warmth barely drops its one permitted degree, the chord sinks as the wind calms, and condensation gathers on every surface at once with a soft, continuous patter, the valley quietly distilling itself.

Stand at that hour in one of the reserved rooms, inside the shadow of walls that do not exist. Somewhere far off a bird holds a note against the chord. Beside you, close enough to touch, a tower rises into the dark and ends in its warm, beating, sealed beginning — about to open, as it has been about to open for longer than waiting has existed, as it will be about to open when you are gone. Everything here is just about to happen. The just lasts forever.
