The plain above is so low and level it seems poured rather than formed — a warm slab of country running flat to every horizon, dressed in scrub that keeps its distance from itself, each bush alone in its own allotment of space, the ground between them bare pale gravel and dust. Nothing drains anywhere here, because nothing flows. The one accident in all that levelness is a dimple, a shallow pressed place, as if the plain had been thumbed once and never quite forgot it. At the dimple's bottom, a slab has slumped against another slab, and under them is a slot. The collapse that made this happened so long ago that the rubble has forgotten it was ever an event. Every stone is at rest. Every angle has slumped into repose. It is the least dramatic opening in the world, and it is the whole of the door.

Inside the slot, the light fails fast. A talus slope goes down between the slabs — stones that finished falling before there was anyone to hear them — and at the second bend a grey persists, a thin, diluted grey that noon and midnight cannot settle between them, because the turns of the passage strip the day of its hours and deliver only its average. This is the last the sky ever sends. Beyond the second bend, the dark does not deepen gradually. It simply begins, the way a material begins — less an absence than a substance the place is made of.

The grey zone keeps the only garden. Tufts of a thread-fine grass grow there in pockets of blown dust, etiolated, colorless, pale as boiled roots, each clump a hand apart from the next with nothing between. A film of pallid algae dulls the one wall the grey can reach. And that is all, because nothing has ever come to eat any of it — no beetle, no cricket gone white and blind, no bat, no bird sheltering from the plain's heat. The grass is never grazed, and it barely rots: the dead blades stand beside the living, desiccated and indistinguishable, death here being mostly a way of remaining. Nothing has ever been hungry in this place.

The passage turns once more and opens into the room.

It is small — forty paces across, with a ceiling low enough that your hair would brush it, and a low crawl at the far side leading to a single cell no bigger than a closet. The whole province of this place, from the dimple to the back wall, would fit inside a village green. But the room has the composure of something far larger. It was made by water, long ago, and water left no forwarding address. What it built while it lived here still stands: a handful of columns, each one alone, like thoughts the room had once and chose not to repeat; a flowstone apron against the wall like cloth caught mid-fold and cooled into permanence; a satin crust over everything, filmed now with dust. The drip that built the tallest column stopped ten thousand years ago. The column has not noticed. It is finished, and finishing was the whole of its work.

At the room's low point is a pan, the size of a courtyard table, where the last shallow pool stood. What remains of that pool is its autobiography in salt: a crust of pale polygons, plates rimmed with sharp upthrust ridges, thick enough to take the shape of a hand. At the pan's edge, gypsum has flowered into petals and blades so delicate a touch would powder them. They would crumble at a touch; they have never been touched. These are the same fact.

In the alcove off the north wall, the room does something stranger, though there is nothing strange in it. Nodules of dark chert stud the wall, and heat and salt have worked them loose over the ages — the stone spalls, and where it spalls it flakes. On the floor beneath lies a scatter of palm-sized flakes, each with an edge that could open a hand, and beside the scatter a block of dense stone, flat-topped, waist-high, exactly where a worker would want it. The mind refuses the scene and then refuses its refusal: it is, in every particular, a knapping floor. But the dust over the flakes is unbroken, continuous with the dust of the whole room, and the dust of the whole room has never been broken. No one struck these edges. Heat was the hammer, salt in the cracks was the wedge, and time was the hand. This is the only technology the place has ever known — the earliest crafts there are, wedging and flaking and etching, practiced without a practitioner, ownerless, pre-metal, patient as stone.

The room's only residents hang from the ceiling. Somewhere above, on the plain, a scrub tree lives its bright life in the sun, and generations ago one of its roots found a hairline crack and followed it down. Now a dozen pale roots enter through the ceiling — thicker than a wrist where they emerge, hair-fine at their tips — and descend through air that has never once moved enough to sway them. Some have reached the floor and spread there in slow stars, drinking nothing, persisting. One hangs with its tip a finger's width above the salt pan and has held that pose for a hundred years. They are not idle. Their mild acids etch the stone line by line; their growth pries grains loose; they are tools the tree does not know it is using, in a room it does not know it is touching. Near them the air carries the place's only perfume — a ghost of resin, sun-chemistry conducted downward into the dark.

The dust on the floor is deep and flour-fine, a snowfall that has never been walked in. No foot has ever pressed it — no animal's, no one's. If you crossed the room you would write the first sentence ever written here, and the room would keep it forever, because nothing in this place erases: no wind, no water, no creature, no rot. The room keeps everything that happens to it. That is why so little has.

The air sits within a degree of your own blood. After an hour you could not find the boundary of your skin by temperature. It is perfectly still — the only weather in the room is the small plume of warmth rising off your body — and it is thirsty, borrowing moisture from your lungs and giving nothing back. It smells of dust and salt and that faint resin. Sound behaves strangely: the dust and the soft crust drink it, and the room returns no echo. Clap, and the room files it away. The silence has mass, the kind that rings in the ears until you understand the ringing is your own. Once in a season, once in a decade, somewhere in the crust a crystal outgrows its neighbors and lets go — a tick, like a knuckle. Then the silence resumes. The tick changes nothing. That is the point of it.

Understand the rules, because the room has so few. The temperature does not vary by a degree across the year or across the floor. The dark is not a phase the room passes through, like weather; it is a property, like hardness. Light, the one thing that could alter this place, has never entered it — which means color has never occurred here. The columns are not white, not amber, not grey. They are unwitnessed all the way through. Nothing arrives. Nothing leaves. The salt creeps and the roots grow at rates indistinguishable from rest, and everything in the room sits exactly where its forces balance: the flakes, the petals, the hanging root, the unbroken dust. Time passes here, but it does not accumulate the way it does in ruins. The room is not old. It is finished.

No one has ever stood in it. If you were the first, you could carry a light down and be the first light — and the columns would take their colors like a held breath released — and when you left, the room would keep your footprints and your warmth, and within an hour the warmth would have equalized into the general warmth, and only the footprints would remain, kept beside nothing else, forever. But you have not come. No one has.

The room is not waiting. Waiting implies a door intended for someone. It is not asleep; sleep ends. It simply continues — at the temperature of a held hand, in a dark that is not the absence of anything, in the middle of its finished work, while above it the plain shimmers, the scrub stands apart from itself, and one unremarkable tree touches, without knowing it, the dark.
