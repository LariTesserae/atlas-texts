The rim is raw. The stone up there was broken recently — by what, nothing says — and it is still sharp-edged, still almost soft, the way new things are before the world has had time to argue with them. Beyond the rim there is mist, and the mist is not weather. It is the edge of what has been gotten around to.

The place sits below the rim in a bowl, a sump, the one low place in a world with no other geography to be low against. Everything drains here. Everything collects here. Over the single gap in the rim where the path comes down, a lintel of living bark carries raised script, legible more to fingers than to eyes — because it is dark, it is always dark — and it reads: WORKS No. 1. Whether that is a name, a boast, or a care instruction has not been decided.

Descend, and you descend through districts laid in rings, seven of them, an hour's slow walk from rim to center. The ground gives underfoot like felt because it is felt — root-felt, leaf-mold packed by generations of passage into something that behaves like pavement and heals like skin. The walls are grafted limbs fused at the seams. The gutters are petioles grown hollow. The doors are valves of leaf that open when the air-pressure shifts, which it does, slowly, on a long tide: the whole bowl inhales and exhales on a cycle of perhaps twenty minutes, and the doors flap softly with its breath. It is one plant, arguably — or a parliament of thousands grafted into such continuity that the votes stopped mattering long ago. Total. There is no surface here that is not alive. The rock only shows at wounds, and at wounds it bleeds wet gravel.

What strikes you next is the emptiness. The Works builds volume, not furniture. It grows halls and leaves them empty — a nave at the center you could call cathedral-scaled if cathedrals were this patient, corridors that open into chambers where nothing happens except, on a schedule, a single drop of water falling from the dark into a black pool, timed like a slow clock. The fauna are abundant in kind and almost absent in number: a rich bestiary of one-offs. Each species here is a first edition. You can stand in a hall grown for a crowd and share it with one moth.

Water is not free here; it is farmed. The canopy — total, closed, sky-replacing — is planted in drip-tips and guttered veins, hydrophobic velvets that comb moisture out of the breath of the place and aim it, leaf by leaf, into the mouths of gourds. On a schedule the Works itself keeps, the canopy precipitates: an interior rain, a fine timed drizzle that the shifts are planned around. The rain runs down into curb-side pitchers, and the pitchers are the storm drains, and the drains eat. Step over them. At the bottom of the bowl, terraced cisterns step down to the Well, where the condensation of the entire canopy finally gathers, cool, black, mirror-still. The cisterns are also the power station. They hold schools of electric fish, and their slow collective discharge is the grid. You don't hear it. You feel it in your molars and through the soles of your feet, a pulse the lamps answer to.

Because it is dark — genuinely dark, darkness as a medium rather than an absence — light here is not illumination. Light is language. The Works grows lamp-galls, cold blue-green, and rations them; they ignite with a soft click and spell slow sentences across the walls: routing, hazard, shift-change, the hour of the next rain. Streets are marked by scent-posts. The citizens navigate by whisker, memory, and the pulse in the floor. Sight is a specialist's sense here.

It is cool. Breath smokes. Chemistry dawdles. Which is why the Vat Quarter — two rings in — is the heart of the settlement in the way a hearth is a heart: the fermentation vats run warm as compost, and it is the only warmth in the world. At shift-change the citizens gather there. Beetles huddle at the vat-walls with their feet on the warm rivets of fused bark. Moths shiver on the vents, working their flight muscles up to temperature before their courier runs. The air smells of sap and petrichor and bread-adjacent fermentation and, faintly, of ammonia from the bat roosts under the rim.

The bats are the only ones who leave. At rim-song hour they slip through the mist-gap and fly out into the not-yet — not into another place; there is no other place — and they call, and the mist is the only thing that answers. They are surveying. They measure the size of the world by its echo, and the echo comes back a little sooner every night. The Works listens for this through its roots, which is the reason for the quiet. Silence here is not etiquette; it is occupational safety. The whole settlement keeps its voice down so the roots can hear the rim answering. The permitted sounds are small ones: the drip-clock, the vat-hum, the rasp of pruning-shears, wing-brush, the click of a lamp igniting.

The ants do the pruning, and the pruning is governance. Nothing here is finished, and everything is subject to editing. The ants move through the architecture in slow red committees and snip away whatever has drifted off-template — a wall bulging wrong, a gutter dreaming of being a vine. The snails follow behind them, laying trails of conductive slime that dry into circuitry; the city is wired in snail. The beetles turn the valves. The moths pollinate the dark-blooming flowers and carry seed and message between districts. Salamanders keep the cisterns, slow and black-spotted, plumbers by temperament. Small creatures ride the sap-tubes from ring to ring, the transit system being vascular.

And at the Lines — the third ring, the one that smells sweetest and wrongest — the Works makes its citizens. This is the industrial heart: scaled production of living things. Out of the seed-vats, on a night shift, come the new: a beetle variant with wider valve-cups, a moth with a longer proboscis and opinions about it, something with six legs and no precedents. Each a prototype, most a run of one. They come out wet, and they come out remembering. This is the part no one has explained: the new ones wake with memories of places that were never grown. A moth fresh from the vat remembers a meadow in sunlight — sunlight, which has never existed here — and some remember hands. Gentle hands, five-fingered, a rumor the seeds keep telling. The flawed ones, the ones that come out wrong, mostly know it, and walk themselves ringward to the Compost, which is not a graveyard but a feedstock reserve; nothing is wasted, and death is logistics. The ones that don't know it meet the Inspector — a giant salamander, unhurried, blotched, old by the standards of a place where nothing is old — who patrols the Lines and the cisterns and culls what fails. It is not cruel. It is the schedule. Predation here is quality control, and the ecology runs hot: pollination, pruning, predation, compost, reprint, all turning over each other in the cool dark.

Age is a direction. It points inward. The center is the oldest part of the world; the rings get younger as you climb, until at the rim the ground is still wet from being printed — the Printfields extrude terrain there like paste, raw embryonic hills, rivers that stop mid-course because the rest of the river hasn't been grown yet — and beyond the rim is the not-yet. Not a hidden future. An absent one. You cannot leave, not because anything stops you but because there is nowhere to go; you can put your hand into the mist and feel nothing resist, not even time. Ahead of you at the rim, there is no ahead.

The seasons run on an eleven-day cycle — bud, leaf, flower, fruit, drop — the whole canopy doing a year in less than a fortnight, to keep the templates exercised.

Everything alive here is the plant, in the end. The fauna are its seeds that chose legs. The garden walks.

And the whole of it — the seven rings, the vats, the nave, the Well, the bestiary of first editions — is itself a seed. That is what "emerging" means here, what the raw rim and the soft stone mean: this hamlet is the germ of a much larger world, still being drafted. Everything is preliminary. Even you, if you stay long enough to be pruned into place.

Stand in the Hollow Core at printing hour. The fish-pulse comes up through the floor. A lamp-gall clicks on and spells one slow word across the dark, and the word is almost always the same: CONTINUE. Above, faintly, the bats return through the mist-gap with damp fur and a measurement. Tonight the echo came back a half-breath sooner than last night, and the Works notes this the way a seed notes water — not with hope, exactly, but with growth.

It is not waiting for anything. Waiting implies a future. The future is what it is busy making.
