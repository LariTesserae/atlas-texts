The first thing wrong is the light.

You have come down four hundred meters of shaft — through raw young rock, through the collar works and the airlocks with their gaskets of waxed felt — and it is noon. Not lamplight, not the glow of fungus or crystal: noon. A broad, sourceless, amber-tinged day lies across a plain that runs forty kilometers to a far rim of unlit stone, and overhead, a kilometer up at its crown, the vault of the cavern arches like a sky that has been roofed. Studded across that vault, in a slow spiral from rim to rim, are the day-engines — thousands of them, housings of bright copper and ceramic, each the size of a house. They are burning on full current now, and their combined light falls on the plain below as evenly as weather.

The plain is the sward, and the sward is alive in a way that takes time to understand. It is not grass over soil. There is no soil. It is vegetation all the way down — a single colonial tissue, wax-bladed, springy, dark green-black with an oily iridescence where the light strikes it low, felted and matted and intergrown to depths no survey has finished measuring. It is the ground. It is also, by every account the wrights will give you, one organism: the whole forty kilometers of it, a single plant that has filled the chamber the way water would fill a basin, if there were water. There is not.

That is the second thing wrong, and the deeper one. This is a world without water, and it has arranged itself accordingly. The sward's blood is a light paraffin. The candlewood groves that rise from the plain in pale, bone-smooth columns run on oil-sap; the sump pines at the seeps drive their black needles into deep oleic strata and pump crude up a kilometer of root. The animals drink refined sap. The people of the Grafts drink clarified table oil from the stills. Nothing is wet anywhere — instead, everything is glossed. At night, when the engines dim and the air drops toward its cellar-cool minimum, a fine light oil condenses on every blade and rail and roof-plane, and the world wakes slick and faintly sweet-smelling. The tenders call this the glossing, and it is the only precipitation there is.

Nothing rusts. Rust is a property of the old poison, and the old poison is a bestiary thing here, a legend with a chemical formula, a corrosive horror the deep-time creatures supposedly swam in. The iron of the Underlight is either perpetually oiled-bright or lacquered black, and the copper of the engines stays copper-colored forever, because verdigris, like rust, belongs to the other chemistry. This gives the whole region a strange, suspended newness — as if it were unpacked recently from cosmolene and never quite put to use.

Dawn arrives as a line. The eastern engines take current first, and a bright edge sweeps the vault from rim to rim like a lamp being carried across a ceiling, and shadows pour westward and shrink. Dusk is the reverse: the engines go down one by one, a field of lamps being cupped, until only ember-points remain overhead — the false stars. The wick-moths rise to them nightly, wings of pale parchment, a cold blue ember at each thorax, fooled again, and their wings against one another in the swarm make a soft patter that is the closest thing this world knows to the sound of rain.

The day is a public work. That is the first rule, posted at every waystation in the plain lettering of the Grid Wardens, and it is not a metaphor: darkness down here is total, absolute, a substance, and so the light is maintained the way other places maintain dikes. The hum of the grid is the bass note of everything, low and even, more felt than heard, and when it flickers — which it does, in storm season — you can hear a whole Graft hold its breath.

The second rule is that nothing rests on the ground. The sward takes what you set down. Slowly — a boot-print springs back in an hour; a dropped tool is gripped by morning and half-swallowed in a fortnight — but without exception. So the towns stand on stilts, the storehouses on stone piers, the trams of the Causeway on stilted trestles, and the people learn young to hang things up, to use skids and sledges, to never, ever pitch a tent without a floor. The dead are rendered before the ground can claim them; it is considered only practical, and only slightly a kindness.

The third rule is that fire is licensed. Everything here is fuel. The sward, the groves, the gloss on the rails, the table oil, the waxed wool of your coat. Open flame belongs to the rendering houses, where combustion is slowed to a cold blue creep that digests the plain's dead into fat and fiber and fuel oil without ever quite becoming a fire, and to the licensed hearths, and to no one else. In storm season the lightning wardens walk the Causeway with grounding chains, because the dry storms — the fourth strange thing — do not bring rain. They bring static: violet fire crawling along the telegraph wires, standing the waxed blades of the sward on end, crackling like paper in a pocket, and never once speaking above a whisper. The storms of the Underlight are silent. Almost everything is.

That is partly the ground, which drinks sound the way other ground drinks rain, and partly the third rule's unwritten sibling, which no waystation bothers to post: speak low. Sound carries strangely under the vault, and the herds are skittish, and the moths startle, and so the Grafts keep time with soft bell-codes rather than horns, the factories hum instead of roar, and conversation on the open plain happens at the volume of a library. In the deepest quiet — wind stopped, a grove between you and the grid's hum — your boot-soles will report something else: a slow double pulse, very low, far below hearing. The sward moving its blood. The wrights say it is plumbing. The wardens say it is a heart. Both say it quietly.

The fauna match the place: slick, quiet, abundant, unhurried. Sward-sliders move in herds of hundreds — low as a table, broad as a door, smooth as a seal and the color of dark honey, gliding on a skirt of cilia over a film of their own exuded oil. Where they pass they comb the sward into polished trails, and everything else uses the trails after them: the moths follow them, the trams in their oldest stretches simply follow herd-roads fossilized into fiber, and overhead, riding the warm updrafts under the vault without a wingbeat, the vault-kites follow them too — long silent deltas of leather whose shadow is usually the only announcement you get. At the stills and rendering houses the whole web comes home: there is no rot here, only rendering. Beetle or industry, something reclaims everything, quickly, and the air smells of paraffin and resin and, near the stacks, a fatty contained smoke that the towns downwind count as wealth, because the stacks breathe warmth into air that otherwise never quite leaves cellar temperature.

And the towns — the Grafts — are the fifth strange thing, though you stop noticing quickly because it is so coherent: they are grown. Each began as an armature, a framework of iron and cable planted on piers in the sward, around which candlewood was grafted and trained and grafted again, tier on tier, until the building became a district and the district became one vast living structure of pale wax-wood and iron bone, sap lines running in insulated arteries through the walls, windows cut where the growth permitted, floors planed from fallen limbs. They are numbered in the order they were planted — Second Graft, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth — and the gaps in the numbering are not much spoken of. A graft that fails to take is a grief the size of a town. Between the five of them lies most of forty kilometers of empty, humming, gently rippling plain: waystations, signal towers, a surveyor's sled, a herd crossing the trail-roads at its own pace. Sparse is the honest word for it, and the sparseness is not emptiness but interval — the space a thing this size needs around itself.

Everything is under construction. That is the last thing, and the one that reframes all the rest. The Grafts wear scaffolding year-round, botanical and architectural at once. The Causeway is always being extended one more span. Maps are revised the way almanacs are, because the ground keeps its own draft and updates it annually. And at the rim — at the Front, where the sward thins to a fur, then to isolated blades in fractures, then stops — the rock is young. Fresh-broken, unpolished, unpatinated, clearly only just a surface at all. Here the auger beetles and the root-tips work the same seams, and it is not always clear which one is leading; here the survey stakes stand a little farther out every year; here, in the cool dry silence, the stone ticks and ticks, and once an hour, somewhere out of sight, it lets go with a sound like a slow door. The loudest thing in the Underlight is the sound of it getting larger.

Beyond the last stake is a new chamber, raw and dark, waiting for its engines. The tenders speak of it the way farmers speak of a field under snow.

And below everything, the deep note. Every taproot ever surveyed angles, at depth, toward the same point. The galleries the rendering trusts call mines are, by another accounting, approaches. Nobody has reached the bottom of the sward, and the people who work the deep shafts come up quieter than they went down, with a look that the Grafts recognize and do not question. The wardens have a name for what is down there, though they do not use it officially. They call it the Heartseed, and they point out — quietly, always quietly — that a seed is not a ruin, and not a tomb. It is the opposite. It is the thing before.

So the Underlight keeps its unhurried pace. The engines come up at dawn in their traveling line and go down at dusk like cupped lamps. The gloss forms. The herds comb their slow roads. The tick-lines chatter, and the moths roost on the wires, and the operators learn to read through the interference of wings. The stills breathe. The Front ticks. Time here does not wear anything down — there is no water to wear it, no rust to eat it, no rot to take it apart. Time in the Underlight does not erode. It accumulates. It grafts. It adds another tier, another span, another kilometer of root, and stands back to see what it has made, and finds it not yet finished, and continues.
