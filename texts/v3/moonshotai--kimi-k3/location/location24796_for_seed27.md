The light arrives rationed.

By what the almanacs would call noon, the sun hangs over the plateau as a small, dark-red coal, dim enough to look at, and the sky around it is thin enough — high enough — to hold stars even at midday. Across half the vault of heaven runs the Veil: a lattice of shading elements, each one too far up to see clearly, visible only as a grain in the light, a slow glitter, a texture imposed on the sky like the weave of cloth held against a lamp. The Veil is old. It is the oldest thing here that still works. It turns full day into a permanent bronze dusk, and it is not perfect — it has gaps, and the gaps drift. Weather, on the plateau, is made of shadow. Cells of deeper dusk migrate across the land at walking pace, and rarer, brighter things move too: blades of raw unfiltered sunlight called blaze-slots, sliding across the salt like searchlights, and everything that lives here has an opinion about them.

The plateau itself — call it the High Orchard, because someone once did — is a tableland lifted into thin, cold air, ringed by what remains of mountains. Not peaks: stumps. Ranges worn down to their roots, rounded as molars, the ghosts of ranges, with passes like missing teeth. To one side the land simply ends at the Drop, an escarpment that falls away into lower deadlands the color of ash, and on still days you can see a hundred kilometers of nothing from the rim. Up top, the ground is an old seafloor and does not let you forget it. Salt flats stretch in every direction, cracked into polygons a pace across, their edges lifted and curled by frost. Dunes of gypsum, whiter than the light deserves, pile against anything that stands still long enough. The stones are all rounded; the ridges are all streamlined; even the gravel has been polished by wind until it gleams like beadwork. Nothing here is sharp except broken things, and broken things do not stay broken long before the wind rounds them off too.

There is no water. That is the first fact and the one everything else answers to. No rain in living memory, which is a long memory; no snow; no ice, except one kind, which will need explaining. The last river on the plateau left its ghost in a canyon the locals call the Last River — a dry, meandering cut whose bed is paved in halite, the water's shape preserved in salt the way a fossil preserves a bone. The sea did not dry up. It was taken. Drinkable water exists on the plateau in exactly one place, and that place is alive.

Stand on a salt flat and look at the horizon, and you will see them: the standards. They stand kilometers apart, each one alone in a wide disc of dead ground, and they are unmistakably plants and unmistakably buildings. A hundred meters and more at the crown. Trunks fluted like columns, buttressed like cathedrals, forty meters through at the base, black-barked, the bark fissured into plates. Their foliage is not green — green is a color for bright worlds — but a deep absorptive black, a soft felt of vanes and panels arranged in tilting arrays that turn, slowly, all day, tracking the coal of the sun. Each one is hollow in ways that feel intentional: chambers in the heartwood, corridors, cisterns of sap under pressure, spiral stairs grown rather than built. They are spaced in a pattern. Not a grid exactly — something older, a spiraling phyllotaxis that repeats at the scale of the region — and around each standard the salt is sterile for a fixed radius, poisoned by the roots, kept bare. The effect, from the rim, is of a city plan: great dark towers on regular plots, joined by pale avenues of dead salt, a settlement spread across a fifth of the horizon. It is a city. It is also one organism. Every standard on the plateau is a shoot of the same individual, joined below ground, a single genet that has been alive so long that the question of its age stops being a number and becomes a landscape feature.

Between the plots run the root-ridges: long, low, pipe-like rises crossing the flats, warm relative to the ground, faintly resonant. They carry sap, and they carry more than sap. At night — and night here is a deepening of dusk, not a darkness, the stars were already out — the ridges light up. Slow pulses of bioluminescence travel along them, blue-white, deliberate, crossing kilometers of salt, meeting at nodes, pausing, continuing. From the Drop's rim on a clear night the plateau looks like a city seen from high altitude: lit streets, lit junctions, traffic of light. It is the Orchard thinking. No one is left who knows what it thinks about.

Someone built things here, once. Roads of fused glass run straight across the flats from standard to standard and then, disconcertingly, continue past the last one and stop at nothing. Vaults open onto stairs that descend into warm dark where the roots have already moved in, patient as water, prising the doors apart. The erosion has blurred every category: there are machines in the wood and wood in the machines, and the pollen that drifts off the black vanes in the cool season glitters, under a lens, in ways pollen should not — half of it is not pollen at all but maintenance motes, still at work after geological time, still patching, still polishing, still fulfilling instructions no one remembers issuing. The Veil is theirs too, presumably. On certain charged nights the tips of the standards discharge upward in slow, violet streamers, dry lightning running from tree to sky, and the Veil seems to drink the current — the Orchard feeding its own shade. Whether the gardeners built the Orchard or the Orchard grew the gardeners is a distinction the salt has worn away.

It is loud. That surprises everyone, because the air is thin and the wind is gentle and nothing, at first, seems to be making noise. Then you notice that you hear the plateau through your boots before you hear it through your ears. The loudness of the Orchard lives in the ground: infrasonic, bone-borne, a standing hum that rises through the soles and settles in the chest. The standards boom — deep, unhurried reports, like doors closing in a house the size of a country — as sap shifts and chambers breathe. At dusk, when the dim light fails further, the black wood contracts and the trunks crack, a rolling cannonade that passes grove to grove across the whole region, and the flats carry it the way ice carries the sound of a lake freezing. And there is the Choir. Dead standards do not fall; the cold and the dryness mummify them, and they stand for tens of thousands of years while the wind carves them into flutes and organ-pipes, and the plateau plays its dead. The Choir is a field of husks on the windward side, and when the air moves, it sings — chorded, dissonant, sometimes almost a voice, audible a day's walk away, felt even farther.

Against this, everything alive has made a decision about sound. The herds that migrate the salt avenues between standards — flask-beasts, squat, barrel-bodied, their humps not fat but stored water, living canteens — communicate by drumming on the ground, coded thunder through the substrate, because the ground talks farther than the air. Their predators know it. The thing the herders call the siren hunts by boom: it has a resonating chamber of its own, scavenged and regrown, and it sounds, convincingly, like a standard. Everything within earshot freezes, because in a loud world stillness is armor — and then the siren listens, patient, for the one small sound of something flinching. Silence is not safety here. Silence is merely a bet.

Because the law of the plateau, the one under all the others, is this: all water is property, and the Orchard owns it. There is nothing to drink that did not come out of a tree. So everything negotiates. The drill-moths bore into the sapwood with proboscises like diamond-tipped augers, and the standards tolerate a quota of them — the price of pollination — sealing the wound the moment the tithe is exceeded. The flask-beasts are granted access to the cistern-chambers in exchange for carrying seed, culling parasites, and dying, eventually, somewhere the roots can reach; their water is a loan against services, and they spend their whole lives paying it off. Predators drink blood, which is sap at one remove. Scavengers render every carcass down to the salt and deliver the rest, and nothing — nothing — escapes the loop. It has been closed for so long that the loop is the landscape.

It is a closed loop that still has events. When a standard is breached — a wind-thrown husk falling against a living trunk, a siren's boring gone too deep — the sap comes out under pressure, sweet and glycerol-thick, antifreeze-sap that floods the salt and only then, robbed of the tree's warmth, hardens into sheets of amber ice. Sap-ice. It is the only ice on the plateau, and it is food and drink at once, and a breach draws everything for a day's walk around: herds at a run, motes glittering in the wound, sirens circling the feast they did not make, the Orchard itself sealing and rerouting while the riot feeds at its side. For weeks afterward the ice sublimes away in the thin air, shrinking in strange scalloped shapes, until the salt is bare again and the only sign is that the local circle of sterile ground has been resown, sharpened, redrawn — the Orchard revising its own plan.

There are other movements. The shadow-cells drift, and the herds follow them, grazing the dimness the way herds elsewhere graze grass, because the flask-beasts' symbiotic algae — in the felt of their black flanks, in the plates of their backs — do better out of the blaze. When a blaze-slot does sweep through, the world reacts like a struck instrument: the foliage vanes fold edge-on in a slow black ripple, the moths drop, the herds turn their plated backs, and for a few minutes the raw light of the small red sun lies on the salt, and everything waits, and the slot moves on, and the dusk closes over it like water over a stone. At dawn, for one hour, on sun-facing slopes, certain salts drink the night's trace of atmospheric moisture and weep a cold, bitter brine — perchlorate tears, the only free liquid that is not alive, and poisonous, and gone by full dim. Small desperate things lick the salt anyway, for the minerals. They do not lick the brine twice.

The rules, in the end, are few, and you could carve them on one of the glass milestones along the roads to nowhere, if the Orchard's motes did not polish every inscription away within a century. Water is owned; negotiate or steal. Silence is armor; stillness is a bet. Salt is history; the sea is in the trees now. The light is rationed; take what moves. Everything returns to the root.

And the root is in no hurry. That is the last thing, the thing that takes longest to see: the plateau is finished. The mountains came down, the sea went up into the wood, the builders dissolved into their building, and what remained settled — an ecosystem at its oldest stable state, biomass constant across eons, every niche filled by something that has been filling it since before the word for niche. It is not dying. It is idling: a great engine at rest that is nonetheless humming, turning over, loud in the bones. At night the root-ridges pulse their slow light across the dark flats, junction to junction, a lit city that is a single thought thinking itself over and over, and the Choir sings on the windward side, and somewhere a standard booms — once, twice — like a door opening, like a door closing, in a house the size of a country, that has no intention, ever, of being empty again.
