**The Kettle**

You hear it half a day before you see it. The old mountains up here are ordinary, rounded, sheep-bitten, worn into long green saddles by ten thousand years of weather — and then the trail crests a shoulder and the sound arrives: a low double tone, two notes a fifth apart, steady as a held breath, threaded through with cracking reports like lake ice, like far-off artillery. The herders in the valleys call this place the Kettle, for the noise. Some call it the Egg, for what it has not finished becoming. Both names are accurate. It steams and whistles, and it is hatching.

The rim is the first wrong thing. Every other ridge in this range is soft at the top, slumped, lichen-crusted. This one is sharp enough to cut cloud. The rock is dark, almost black, and warm — body-warm, wrist-warm — and it has grain. Not bedding planes: grain, like wood, like it was laid down in deliberate courses by something patient. Run your thumb along it and you feel the tooling, a fine regular striation, and under that, faintly, the hum. The whole rim is vibrating at the edge of audibility. Fog pours over the crest in slow braids, condensing on the new stone faster than it can run off, so the rim weeps continuously, everywhere, a curtain of drips with no eaves to hang them from.

Inside, the basin is smaller than the sound suggests — cathedral-scale, a hamlet-scale, a twenty-minute walk across — and dimmer than noon has any right to be. A cap of cloud sits on the rim and does not burn off, so the light comes from everywhere and nothing casts a shadow. It should be cold at this altitude. It is not. The fog works like glass, and the young ground itself gives off heat, and the air is warm and wet and thin all at once, so you breathe hard in a greenhouse. Between the rim walls the floor of the basin is mostly bare grey stone, gently terraced, holding its few objects the way a mind holds a few clear thoughts: a column here, a pool there, a black pylon at a distance. Emptiness is the dominant material. Nothing crowds anything.

The columns — there is no better word, though no one built them in any way a mason would recognize — rise from the floor at wide intervals, spaced like the piers of a nave. They are the same grained dark stone as the rim, warm, humming, and they are not finished. Some stand complete, thirty feet tall, their tips swelling into the first suggestion of an archspring. Most are mid-gesture: waist-high stumps, knee-high buds, a few paired like gateposts with no gate yet spanning them. Each one is a fountain. Fog beads on their warm flanks and gathers and runs, and every column stands at the center of a ring of vivid green moss, a perfect circle of growth watered by the drip-line, so that from the rim the basin floor looks scattered with green coins on grey cloth. Between the rings, bare new stone. There is almost nothing else alive in the vegetable way — a rust-red biofilm in the wet seams, a bottle-green scum on still water, one species of small fern that keeps strictly to the spray zones. You could nearly count the green things.

The water is everywhere and it is busy. Rills thread the terraces in a branching network too regular to be accidental and too beautiful to be only plumbing. The pools step down the basin's gentle tilt in rimstone basins, like travertine, except that travertine takes centuries and this crust is fast — you can find its edge and see that it is fresh, see the pale new growth-band of this season, this month. The pools divide into two kinds, and the distinction is the basin's first rule. The clear pools are clear the way air is clear: colorless, bottomless-looking, sterile, so pure they leach. Nothing lives in them and nothing drinks from them. The colored pools — ochre, milk-blue, rust, a sulfurous yellow — are the living ones, mineral-saturated, microbe-thick, and these are where everything gathers. Drink where the water has a color. Never where it doesn't.

On the basin's far side the colored seeps crust over into licks: white salt, blue copper-stain, bitter yellow. And here is the explanation for the second strange thing, which is the animals. The basin is nearly barren and it is full of life. Trails converge on this place from days away — real trails, the only true roads in or out, cut not by the makers but by hooves. The salt-ibex come down off the old mountains in file, cross the rim at the low notch, and stand for hours at the licks, tongues polishing the crust into hollows. Gorge swifts nest behind the columns in their hundreds, gluing cup nests to stone so new and slick that nothing can climb to it. A slate-grey heron works the colored pools with unbearable patience. And the frogs — the bellfrogs — own the soundscape.

Which brings you to the noise, because the basin is loud, loudly and layeredly loud, and the loudness has a schedule. Every six or seven hours — not aligned to the day; the cycle drifts around the clock across a week, the basin keeping its own time — the pulse comes. You feel it first through the soles: a deep whomp, subsonic, like a great door shutting somewhere far underground. The columns change tone, climbing a fraction of a semitone. Every rill in the basin swells at once, the whole network flushing, overflow spilling down the terraces. The clear pools drop an inch; the colored pools rise. And the bellfrogs, which have been ticking over quietly, erupt — hundreds of them, one species, chiming in synchrony with the column-tone, tuned to it, a choir locking onto a pitch pipe. The warm flush triggers the midges, and the pools dimple with hatching, and the swifts come off the columns in a shrieking wheel to harvest the rain of wings, and for twenty minutes the little bowl is one of the loudest places you will ever stand. Then it all winds back down to the steady hum and the ticking.

The ticking never stops. That is the sound of construction cooling. For the basin is not ruined — nothing here is broken — it is unfinished, and the work is still underway, and the work is geological in tempo. Watch a column-tip for an hour and it does not move. But the tone it emits deepens week by week as it lengthens, and the herders swear that the paired gateposts on the south side are noticeably closer to arching over than they were a generation ago. The whole bowl is a resonant body, an instrument being built by the playing of it, and it retunes itself continuously as it grows. New rock cracks as it cools — those rifle-shot reports, rolling around the rim — and at dusk the ticking quickens into a brittle chorus, the day's warmth contracting, the basin settling into its night voice.

Afternoons bring the other event. The warm wet air rises off the floor, hits the cold cap, and comes back down as a small, local, nearly daily rainstorm, trapped under the cloud lid, thunder banging around the bowl like something looking for the exit. It lasts an hour. The rills run bank-full. Then the fog closes again and the dripping resumes and the columns steam.

The scattered works of the makers sit among all this with the calm of furniture in a house being built around them. A hut, grown rather than built — seamless walls, an oval door, a roof that stops short in a rounded lip, mid-sprout, as if the stone simply paused. Inside it is dry, warm-walled, and bare except for a bench and a shallow bowl fused to the floor. No tools. No ash. Whoever used it left on good terms with time. Black marker pylons stand at long intervals across the floor, waist-high, grained like the columns, their surfaces textured in bands that change under the thumb — writing, maybe, or survey, or tuning marks; they are warmest in the hour before a pulse. A stair climbs the west rim and ends at a neat notch, a viewpoint over a valley of old mountains, finished and eroded and done. And on the floor below the stair, the basin's emblem: a generous, beautifully proportioned stone arch spanning a rill you could step across. A bridge sized for a river, standing over a trickle. It is not folly. It is early. In a century or three the flush-cycles and the afternoon storms will have cut this rill into a gorge, and the water will rise to meet the arch, and the bridge will simply, at last, be right. Everything here is like that. There are paths of flat laid stone that end in mid-air at the lip of terraces that do not yet exist. Do not follow a finished path to its end. The infrastructure is ahead of the land; the land is hurrying to catch it.

Near the center, in a rectangular array too exact to be anything but intention, stand the infant columns of a hall — a few dozen knee-high buds in rows, each ringed in a pale filament like scaffolding, each growing its centimeter a year. In three hundred years, if the process holds, it will be a hypostyle chamber under the fog, a cathedral inside the cathedral. Moss has already claimed the drip-lines of the rows. Green graph paper, ruled in stone.

The fauna keep two shifts. Day: ibex at the licks, heron at the pools, swifts in the air, a single lammergeier riding the thermal over the rim with a wing-span out of scale to the little bowl, come to check the carrion situation. Night belongs to the light. After dark the rill-network reveals its second nature: a faint green phosphorescence threads the waterways, biofilm seeded or volunteered, and the entire drainage system of the basin glows dimly against the black stone like a schematic of itself, like the plumbing made visible. The heron is replaced by night-herons, or the same herons working late — it is hard to be sure in the dark — standing ankle-deep in glowing water, striking at the phosphorescent churn of feeding fish-fry and frog-spawn. The frogs sing on, tuned to the columns. The columns hum on, tuned to their own slow growth.

And there are the wardens, if that is what they are. You will notice one as a cairn, a knee-high stack of dark stones in a place where you are fairly sure no cairn stood yesterday. Watch it long enough — an hour, most of an afternoon — and it may unfold a single jointed leg, reach down, and re-seat a marker stone the rain has nudged, then fold again into stillness. Lichen grows on their backs; they permit this. The ibex step around them without looking, the way you step around furniture. Machine, animal, or the basin's own equivalent of a tortoise — no one has stayed long enough to learn, and the wardens are in no hurry to clarify. Nothing here is in a hurry. That is the final rule, and the one that explains all the others.

The rules, then, as the place itself teaches them. Drink colored, never clear. Trust the colored crusts underfoot; the clear stone near the pools is slick as glass. Expect rain in the afternoon and the pulse when the pulse comes — you will feel it gather before it arrives, a tightening in the teeth, the frogs going quiet an instant before the whomp. Sleep anywhere; the ground is warm as a hearth and the night never truly chills. Do not walk a paved path past its last stone. And if the fog cap tears — which it does, rarely, in the early morning — stop whatever you are doing. The light that comes through is not ordinary light. It falls in a single column, cathedral light, a moving shaft that crosses the bowl slow as a sundial's shadow, and the swifts pour into it and spiral up the beam as if it were a chimney, and the moss rings flare emerald, and the new wet stone takes on a deep translucent gleam, and for the few minutes it lasts you can see the whole of it at once: the rim, the columns, the glowing rills, the bridge, the rows of the unborn hall — all of it small, all of it loud, all of it radiantly, deliberately provisional.

Nothing in the basin is old. The oldest moss ring could be younger than you. Not one stone on the floor has been rounded by water; nothing has had time to be carried anywhere; there is no past here at all, only schedule. It is the opposite of a ruin. A ruin faces backward, mourning. This faces forward, and it is not mourning anything, and it is not waiting for its makers, because its makers are not coming and were perhaps never needed twice — whatever was switched on here does not require supervision. It requires only fog, and rain, and centuries, and it has arranged for all three.

At night, if you lie on the warm stone with your ear down, the pulse comes up through the ground like a second heartbeat under your own, and the basin hums its two-note chord into your skull, and the frogs answer, and the glowing rills draw the land's circuit diagram in the dark, and somewhere a column cools and cracks like a log settling in a fire. The bridge stands over its trickle, patient as arithmetic. The paths wait at the edges of their absent terraces. The work goes on in the dark, at the pace of stone, whistling while it works.
