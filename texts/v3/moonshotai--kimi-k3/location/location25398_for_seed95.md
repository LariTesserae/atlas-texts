The climb takes days, and it is gentler than it has any right to be. The mountains here did not fall — they finished. Worn past peaks, past ridges, past even foothills, down to their roots, so that what you finally top out onto is not a summit but a plain with altitude: a warm, wind-touched flat so level that the horizon sits at eye height in every direction, and distance stops meaning anything except *more of this*.

From a rise at its edge, the plateau is the color of old paper — bone, ash, faint rust. Up close, the paleness resolves into stone that is not honestly stone. It is reef. The compacted, mineralized bodies of an ocean's worth of colonial dead, raised up over one age and then worn flat again through another. Tap it and you get a dull knock that goes nowhere. The rock is honeycombed, chambers within chambers, and it drinks sound the way dry ground drinks rain. Your footsteps arrive like felt. Shout across the flat and it gives you nothing back — not even the courtesy of an echo.

Kneel. The stone wears a skin.

It is a crust of colonial animal-flesh, patchworked across the whole region, each patch a single organism acres wide. Where two patches meet — rust-red against slate, bruise-violet against ochre — the border holds in a clean line that has not moved in centuries. The crust is faintly warmer than the air and gives slightly under the palm, the way a sleeping animal's side gives. In the dry weeks it lies tight and matte against the rock, conserving. There are a few true plants — a grey cushion-thing huddled in the shade of old stonework, a crust-lichen threading the pores — but they are guests here. The vegetable work of this world, the catching of light and the holding of ground, is done by animals that forgot, long ago, how to walk.

The animals that still walk are beyond counting.

At first you read the middle distance as a cobbled field, a moraine, a litter of boulders. Then the entire field takes a step. Domebacks — grazers the size of huts, shell above and a broad sanding plate of a mouth below — move across the crust in herds of thousands, at a body-length an hour, cropping the living skin the way sheep crop turf, and the crust regrows behind them at roughly the pace they consume it, a circuit closed for a hundred thousand years. Their migrations are measured in generations. A calf is born beside one dew-pond and is old by the time the herd reaches the next. Between ponds, the feet of the herds and the feet of their ancestors have polished trackways knee-deep into the reef-stone, long shining channels that converge on water like the rays of a dim star.

The water comes down out of the sky on a schedule, the way a tide comes in — because it *is* a tide. Once, an ocean covered all of this; the ocean has been worn down to weather, but it keeps its old appointments. The fog rises out of the low country over days, unhurried, a sea of cloud climbing to reclaim its floor. When it floods the plateau, the flat flowers. The crust stands up off the stone and opens ten million pores. On the low swells, the fog-fans unfurl — stalked colonies raising nets of filament finer than hair, acres of total surface straining the mist, droplets beading and running down grooves into cistern-organs, tended by small quick symbionts, the quickest things on the plateau, which is to say: slow. The fog carries its own population, an aerial plankton of drifting larvae and ballooning juveniles, a faint snow of bodies that the sessile world filters out of the gray. Everything here is marine in its anatomy and its patience — gill-lace reworked to breathe air, tide-pool hearts retuned to wait a whole season between visits of the sea.

Between tides, the water lives in basins and in bodies.

The dew-ponds sit in solution pits, in the foundations of fallen stonework, in the worn bowls of ancient carcasses. Their water is skinned with dust, mineral on the tongue and faintly sweet — seasoned, you come to understand, by a million years of passage through living things. Nothing on the plateau spends water. It only moves it: fog to fan to grazer to pond to fog again, a closed account, the oldest ledger in the region. The pond margins are the densest places on the flat — a solid rim of waiting bodies, patient as furniture, sipper-tracks overlapping in the mud like brushstrokes layered over decades.

And then there are the great still ones. The cistern-beasts are translucent, barrel-bodied, each a reservoir on slow legs — water made ambulatory. In the dry seasons the herds gather to them, and they allow the drinking: a valve at the hip, a rationed sip, a queue that lasts for days and no one minds. The oldest of them are centuries old. They remember, in some tissue-deep way, where ponds used to be before the stone wore down around them, and when drought comes they walk to the memory and stand there, and in a sense the pond returns.

Scattered across all of this are the remains of those who built. The spires are stubs now — knee-high, shoulder-high, blunt teeth of chambered calcite, the foundations of tower-cities raised by a colonial builder gone so long that even its ruins have ruins. The builders left no tools because they had none; everything they made, they grew. Local opinion, insofar as the plateau has one, holds that the crust is what they became — a civilization of flesh that let go of its architecture one chamber at a time until only the living floor remained. The burrow-cities are easier to read: the honeycombed reef-stone, half found and half excavated, occupied to its last chamber, emptying at dusk in a slow exodus of small bodies, filling again by dawn. There are ring-mounds too, nest-basins of something large and social and long extinct, reused now by everything, the way a good house outlives its family.

The quiet has texture, once you sit inside it long enough. Standing, you hear wind that finds almost nothing to catch on, so it sounds like breath rather than weather; the soft tick of condensation falling into ponds; your own blood. Lie down and the plateau gives up its real sound, transmitted through the ground: the rasp, in chorus, of ten thousand sanding-plate mouths cropping the crust — a vast soft abrasion, the region's heartbeat. When a whole herd changes its collective mind about direction, there is a ground-note you feel in the soles of your feet before you know you've felt anything. And every few days, a call — one cistern-beast to another across the flat, low, carried through the earth, felt rather than heard. It says *water here*. Or it says *I am still alive*. On this plateau it may be the same sentence.

Even violence is patient. You can watch a hunt last a week: a grazer marked out, pursued at walking pace, the herd parting around the chase like a slow river around a stone, no one running because running is a resource no one here can afford. Death is the fastest construction method on the flat. A carcass is colonized within days, roofed and chambered within a season; it becomes a basin, then a pond, then a neighborhood, and it is still being lived in a century later, by then indistinguishable from the ruins, which are indistinguishable from the reef, which is the dead of an ocean that the living still crawl across, eating.

The one ordinary thing is the light. An honest sun rises and sets. Stars come out that could be anyone's stars. Days turn over like pages. It is the light that tells you how strange everything else is — and at dawn in fog-season it does the strangest thing of all: the world goes diffuse gold, and the domes of the herds surface out of the whiteness like the backs of whales, and for an hour the plateau is an ocean again. As if the light itself remembers, and keeps trying to repaint what the ages wore away.

At night, lie down on the warm crust with the grazing-rasp coming up through your shoulder blades and the fog beginning its slow climb out of the lowlands — the sea's ghost, keeping its tide. The plateau does not wait for it. Waiting implies impatience, and nothing here has any.

It simply continues, at the pace of stone, alive to the last acre.
