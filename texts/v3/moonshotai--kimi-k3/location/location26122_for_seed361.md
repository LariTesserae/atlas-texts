The range finished a long time ago. Whatever it was once — teeth, wall, spine of a continent — it is now a swell of stone so worn that "summit" and "valley" have stopped meaning much. The ridges are dunes of rock, rounded at every scale, pebble-shaped from boulder to mountain, as if the whole massif had been rolled in a river for an age. Which, in a way, it has: the rain and the rock argued here for longer than anyone can count, and every argument between them is settled. Nothing is still becoming. Everything arrived.

The plateau along its shoulder is dry, warm, big-skied. Ordinary sun by day, ordinary stars by night — the light here is the light you know, and that matters, because it makes the exceptions legible. The wind moves over thin soil and says almost nothing. You could walk this plateau for days. You would walk past the place. Almost everyone does.

There is one sign, and it only shows for a few minutes: in the last dark before dawn, a breath of mist stands over a certain shallow depression, and it is cut off flat at the rim of the hollow, sheared level as if by a blade. By full light it is gone. If you see that once, you can find the place again. Otherwise, never.

The rim is low. You step over it the way you'd step over a sleeping dog. And the sound arrives all at once, the way heat arrives when a door opens — because sound is one of the things this place keeps. Inside, it is loud. Ten steps outside, on the plateau, it is nearly inaudible. Shout at the rim and your voice falls at the lip like water reaching the edge of a table. Some old decision was made here about what gets out, and the decision still holds.

The hollow is a few hundred paces across. A room. You can see every thing in it from anywhere in it — and it takes a long time to see it, because the eye is trained for clutter, and this place has almost none. Emptiness is the medium here. The floor is warm, dense, bone-colored stone, worn to the softness of old teeth, and across that floor the presences are countable at a glance: a dozen cup-plants, seven cold stones, a stair of three steps that climbs to nothing, one niche in the rim wall, and the keeper, wherever in its rounds it currently is. That is nearly the inventory. It takes an afternoon to learn how much that inventory contains.

The seven cold stones are the heart of it. They are colder than the night — colder than any night this warm country gets — and they stay that way at noon, in the heat-shimmer, the coldest thing in the blaze being a stone you can put your palm on. They sweat. Water beads on them in the warm dark, gathers, and runs in threads along grooves so smooth it no longer matters whether water wore them or something made them and has declined ever since to let them fill. No one has a physics for these stones. They are simply, permanently, cold, and the whole hydrology of the hollow runs off them.

Because here is the thing about water in this place: it is all routed. There is no stream, no pool bigger than a basin. An hour before dawn the stones go colder — you can feel the air lean toward them — and fog condenses out of the warm night and drifts low across the floor, and is cut off at the rim, and is spent by sunrise. That is the day's water, arriving on a schedule that has nothing to do with weather. True rain comes a few times a season and is an event: an inch of standing water, the keeper drinking while standing in it. The rest of the time, moisture moves along fixed paths — stone to bead to thread to cup to root — and much of it moves hidden, drawn up through hair-fine filaments of a foil-like material that wick water from below, so that each plant stands on dry stone and is secretly watered. The hollow looks dry. It is damp the way a lung is damp: internally, on purpose.

The cup-plants are the vegetation's main statement, and they are lush the way the place is loud — in kind, not in number. Each one is an event: a trunk like old rope, a crown of leaves fused into a basin the size of a bath, holding dark water. Between them, bare stone, room to walk, room to be seen. A few tall sentinels stand apart, each carrying a single broad leaf that ticks and creaks through the day as it tracks the sun. Moss exists only along the drip-lines, green calligraphy spelling out the water's routes across the floor. Turn any leaf and the underside carries a sheen like oil on water — the roots go down farther than roots should, into what lies beneath, and they come back up changed. The sap carries a faint charge. The bells prefer those leaves.

What lies beneath: call it the substrate. It is never seen whole. It is a few meters down, or it is everywhere as dust, and it is the last body of the machines. They are very old — old enough to have finished. Self-repair met erosion here and the two came to equilibrium, and the equilibrium is smoothness: the substrate repairs everything toward pebble-form, because smooth is what survives. It still maintains a few articulations above the surface — the cold stones are its organs, the rim's acoustic property is its policy, the filaments are its hands — and it keeps a few faint annotations: the buried trace of a wall you mostly feel through your feet, the three steps, the niche, which holds a smooth object that fits a hand and does nothing anymore, or does something no one does anymore. Whatever this place was built to be — a garden, a tank, a listening post, a cradle — the work is over. What remains is maintenance. The machines' last task is keeping the damp inside this one stone room, and they perform it perfectly, and there is no one it is for. Unless they have decided that what lives here now is who it was always for.

What lives here now is, by count, almost nothing — and by effect, overwhelming. Put your face to any cup-plant's basin and the dark water seethes: larvae, fleas, a small fierce hunter, rot and bloom, a cosmos at the centimeter scale. The hollow is sparse at the scale of meters and teeming at the scale of attention.

The bells hold the middle ground. Thumb-long, translucent, one per cup-plant crown, spaced across the bowl like lamps — perhaps two dozen animals in the whole place. Each is deafening at arm's length. Together they build the wall of sound that hits you at the rim, and their calls interlock into patterns that repeat on multi-day cycles. It is not random. It has the shape of a message — a checksum, repeated, the way you'd repeat something you wanted to survive. The likeliest story is the strange one: long ago the machines' last transmission was eaten by something small and loud, and the small loud things have been singing it ever since as courtship. They don't know what they're saying. The substrate, presumably, doesn't mind. The signal got out. It just got out as frogs.

The keeper is one animal, and it is enough. It has the mass of a small rhinoceros, carried low and long, soft-footed, warm-skinned, patient the way stone is patient. It tends the hollow: it scrapes the drip-lines clear with a horny lip, noses algae off the cold stones — its breath steams against them even in the warm night — spills over-full cups so the threads below can drink, and eats what it clears, converting maintenance into metabolism. A sheen of the substrate rides its skin. Whether it was made or adopted is a distinction the place stopped honoring long ago. Its rounds take days, and you tell time by where it is. It almost never voices. When it does, the sound is felt in the sternum and the teeth, every bell stops at once, the bowl holds its breath, and the hum that underlies everything — the subsonic floor you'd been feeling rather than hearing — swells and answers. Then the chorus restarts, re-phasing around what was said.

There is also the glider: a forearm of membrane and appetite, the only silent thing in the hollow, hunting bells at dusk by their sound. After it strikes there is a gap in the pattern, which closes over a day or two as a neighbor shifts its call to fill the hole. Even the predation here is absorbed into the transmission.

Listen in the noon lull, when the bells go quiet in the shimmer, and you hear the place's deeper music: each cold stone's drip has its own note, and the notes together make a chord, and the chord does not change. The bowl shapes echoes so that anything repeated comes back slightly corrected toward that chord. Sing something, and a half-breath later the hollow sings it back, in tune with itself. It has been keeping one chord the way a held bell keeps one tone — for how long, there is no one left to ask.

The rules here are few and firm. Water has a route and keeps it; nothing is wet that isn't on the route. Sound stops at the rim. The mist comes before dawn; the keeper's rounds are the week; rain is the season. And smooth survives. Leave a sharp thing here — a tool, a cairn, a broken edge — and come back in a year: it will have been rounded, gently, unwitnessed. The hollow is still finishing. New things are edited toward the final form. A moved stone finds its way back overnight. Nothing hurries; nothing is refused.

Stay through a night. The dark is warm, the stars are in their usual places, the hum swells, the cup-water reflects the sky with suspicious faithfulness. Before dawn the air leans toward the stones and the mist comes and the chorus rises with it, and for a few minutes, from far out on the plateau, a low cloud stands over nothing and is cut flat and burns away, and the dry wind moves over the swells of the finished mountains and knows nothing about it.

Step back over the rim and it all stops mid-note — the loudest silence you will ever stand inside of. The plateau takes you back. Behind you, in its stone room, the machines do their last job perfectly, the keeper makes its rounds, and the bells repeat the old message to no one, at full volume, forever, in secret.
