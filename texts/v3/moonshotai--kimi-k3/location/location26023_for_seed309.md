The plateau gives no warning. It is gray thorn country, dry enough to split your lips, wind-combed and humming with nothing — and then the ground exhales. A breath comes up out of the earth, warm and wet, smelling of green pepper and hot pennies, and with it a sound: a vast, continuous hiss, like a kettle in a house the size of a valley, always just about to whistle. Follow the breath and the land simply ends. A pit, two hundred and fifty paces across, dropping ninety meters into shadow. Its mouth is plugged with leaves.

The folk below call it the Kettle. The leaves are the Lid. The one gap in the Lid, a ragged oculus where a limb died back generations ago, is the Eye — and it is through the Eye that the cage-lift descends, swaying on chains that run down into the gloom, down past the pale limestone band where the walls weep seep-water and the swifts stitch the air, down past the line where bare rock gives way to root-felt, down into the haze.

Because everything below is one tree.

She is called the Mother, and she is a forest the way a single person is a crowd of cells. Centuries ago — the folk say three hundred years, the geologists above say more — a seed fell into this collapse-doline, into the one pocket of moisture in a hundred miles of dry country, and the tree did the sensible thing: she never left. Her trunks rose as pillars and fused into architraves. Her branches reached the light and then, finding the pit mouth exactly the size of her appetite, she capped it. The Lid is hers entire — acres of interlocked canopy that hold the pit's breath in, turning the whole hollow into a hothouse lung. Water that rises here does not escape. It condenses on the underside of the Lid and comes back down at night as the weep: fat, slow, interior rain. The pit hoards its dampness the way the plateau hoards its drought.

The light that gets through is what's left after leaves have had their way with it — green, diluted, cathedral light, arriving in the nave of trunks as if through old glass. At midday, for perhaps an hour, the Eye admits the Pillar: a single column of undiluted sun that strikes the plaza floor and crawls, across the year, along a groove worn in a flat stone. The folk keep their calendar by where the Pillar falls. The rest of the day the Kettle is lit by accident and industry: furnace mouths, the low blue flames of the lamp-room, and seams of glow-moss trained along the walkways in pale green lines, mowed to shape by helmet-snails whose grazing rewrites the paths a little every week. At night, the swarm provides the rest.

The swarm is the first thing you stop being able to think over. They are called singers — hand-sized, four-winged, somewhere in the country between moth and cicada and bat — and there is no counting them because counting requires a pause and the swarm does not pause. They move in strata, in visible currents, layer on layer through the trunk-halls, feeding on the Mother's nectar and her tallow-pods and on each other. Their stridulation is the boil: the kettle-hiss that the whole pit is named for, a sound with texture and weather in it, thickening at feeding hours, thinning toward dawn. Under it runs the frog-bass from the seeps, the drip-percussion in the sump pools, the slow creak and glug of the Mother moving sap and adjusting her weight — and over it, every few seconds, the Presshouse stamp falls, a heartbeat the size of a building. Speech is nearly useless here. The folk whistle across distances, a fluted shorthand, and for everything else they tap.

The tapping is the Kettle's nervous system. The Mother conducts vibration better than any wire; the folk learned it generations ago. Knuckle-code on a root, on a pipe, on a house-frame moored to a trunk, and it arrives — faint, legible — anywhere in the pit. Children learn taps before words. The whole settlement is wired like a body, and the body is mostly tree, and the tree — this is the part the traders from above don't stay long enough to notice — sometimes taps back. Twice, three times, in the deep night, in patterns nobody sent. The Listener, the eldest of the kettle-folk, attributes these to the Mother dreaming. The boiler crews attribute them to eels knocking against roots. Both parties continue, politely, to log them.

The town itself is sparse — a dozen presences in a great deal of listening space. Nine households, home to forty-odd pale, soft-footed people, stand scattered through the trunk-halls, each house a brick foundation wearing an upper body of woven root-panel, each moored to the living architecture with slip-joints and give-ropes, because the Mother grows, and a house that cannot ride her growth is, within a decade, lifted or gently crushed. The slow crush is simply a fact of tenancy. Nothing here is new and nothing here is finished: the brick is rounded by three centuries of drip, the stair-treads are cupped by feet, the iron is polished bright only where roots grip it. In the wall of the oldest trunk, a row of iron teeth emerges from the bark — the Old Drill, the founding machine, lowered down by the oil-seekers who first smelled wealth rising from this hole. The Mother swallowed it without pausing. The folk consider this the fairest possible account of their history.

The industry runs on the Mother's body and the Mother's guests, in a loop closed so long ago that no part of it remembers being separate. At dusk comes the evening crack: the tallow-pods burst in synchronized volleys, a sound like a field of knuckles cracking, releasing their smell — that green-pepper richness that saturates everything — and the swarm surges to feed, and the night shift begins. Folk in oiled cloth wade the strata with pole-nets and collecting baskets. Pods go to the Presshouse, where the great stamping screws render them into kettle-oil: lamp fuel, engine fuel, and the pit's one famous export, hauled up the Eye in the trader's cage once a month in exchange for iron, salt, needles, and news. The pressed cake goes to the sump — three black, warm ponds at the plaza's edge, ghost of the cave-river that carved this place — where pale eels thick as a thigh churn it into loam, and the loam goes back to the root-beds in baskets. It is the only farming the folk do: feeding the Mother. At dawn the night's dead come down — singer-corpses falling in a soft, continuous sifting the folk call the fall — and the plaza's polished-root floor is swept, and the fall goes to the drying lofts up in the roots, and the dried meal feeds the eels or goes up the Eye as trade goods. Nothing is wasted. Nothing can be wasted; the loop has no slack in it.

The rest of the fauna occupies every gap the loop leaves open, at a density that makes the air feel inhabited like a room full of furniture. Among the singers swim their own predator-caste, the hounds — heavier, hook-jawed, silent, taking feeders on the wing in mid-current. Door-spiders the size of dinner plates hang their snares across every useful opening; the folk keep them deliberately, like geese, and hang tiny bells on the anchor-lines, so that a thrumming web announces anything large moving in the dark. The eels rise at evening to take fallen singers, and during the weep they come partway ashore and walk the roots on stiffened fins, hunting, their skins catching the moss-light. Frogs hold the bass line in the seeps. Swifts own the Eye at dawn. And then there is the Hush.

The Hush comes down from above, rarely — a climbing thing, cat-snake-shaped, never clearly seen, because when it enters the Kettle, every singer in the pit goes silent at once. The boil cuts out. The frogs cut out. In three centuries the folk have learned the rule in their marrow: when the Kettle goes quiet, stop the engines. The Hush hunts by listening, and the Presshouse fears nothing, so someone — it is an honor, and a terror, and there is a roster — must run through the silence to throw the cutoff, and then everyone waits, lamps doused, in a pit gone suddenly enormous, until the boil swells back up like water refilling a footprint and life resumes at full volume. The machines are the only things here that have to be taught fear.

The folk themselves have gone strange in the gentle, thorough way of people sealed in a warm green room for three hundred years. They are pale and shine-eyed. They anoint their skin with kettle-oil, not for vanity but for passage: the swarm lands only on the still and the un-oiled, and to be landed on — to feel that soft weight settle — is a small shame, a sign you have gone motionless, half-dead. The dead themselves are oiled one last time, laid out on the fall-plaza at the evening crack, and given back — to the singers, to the sump, to the loop. There are few laws. Do not cut green wood — the Mother drops her dead limbs, and has been observed, uncannily, to drop them where the boiler crews gather fuel; taking living wood is the one crime, punished by the lift, exile to the dry. Keep the lamps low. Stop the engines at the silence. Feed the root-beds. Tap back when tapped.

And the Kettle turns through its day like a wheel that has worn its own track smooth: the dawn swirl of swifts at the Eye and the sweeping of the fall; the Pillar crossing the year-stone at noon while the presses idle in the heat; the evening crack, the surge, the night shift stamping; the weep beginning after midnight, fat drops finding every barrel and catchment and bare head; the eels walking the roots; the ember-abdomens of the night-swarm drifting like a cinder-field between the trunks; the haze of smoke and steam and breath hanging under the Lid, never escaping, never quite thickening — the whole system balanced at the edge of excess, the tree growing exactly as fast as she is cut, the swarm exactly as dense as the pods allow, the town exactly as large as the loop can carry.

Stand on the plaza at the deepest hour and it resolves into one sound, the way a chord resolves into a note: the boil, the bass, the heartbeat of the press, the weep, the creak of the Mother settling her weight a fraction closer around her houses. A kettle, always coming to the boil, never whistling. A town rafted on a single living citizen, in a pocket of wet held up out of the dry. And somewhere in the dark, felt through the soles of the feet more than heard, a root taps twice — and waits, and the Kettle, politely, taps back.
