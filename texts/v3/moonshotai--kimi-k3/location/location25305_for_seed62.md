The path reaches the rim just before dawn, and the first thing wrong is the light.

Below you — a long way below, down a slope of pale, unweathered ash — lies a bench of land perhaps eight kilometers across, a high table slung between sharper peaks. It should be dark down there. Instead the whole basin is already luminous, a vast low glitter, as if a glacier had somehow learned to be a forest. The survey sheets call this place the Bright Table. The charter of the company that worked it calls it the Condensary. The keepers, the few who ever remained, called it simply the Quiet. You will understand all three names within the hour.

You notice the silence second. It comes up out of the basin like a pressure. There is no wind-hiss, no bird, no water — no water sound anywhere, which you will learn is the point of everything here — and the thin cool air of the altitude carries what little there is badly. You hear your own boots on the pumice, obscenely loud, and then you hear your breathing, and then, descending, you begin to hear your own blood. The path down is lined, at intervals, with small stones painted white. The last of them, at the rim itself, bears the only written text in the valley: a small slate plaque in two scripts, asking you, courteously, not to clap.

The land is young. That is plain the moment you stand on it. The ash fields still hold their original rind; blocks of pumice lie where they fell, edges sharp enough to cut leather; flakes of black volcanic glass wink in the grey tuff like scattered coinage. Nowhere — nowhere on the whole table — has water cut a channel. There are no streams, no gullies, no alluvial fans, none of the softening that even a few centuries of rain will write across a slope. It is not that the land is dry, exactly. It is that the land has never learned to drain. What moisture exists here lives in the stone itself: the pumice is a sponge, a fifty-square-kilometer wick, and at dawn, when you put your palm flat on a boulder, it comes away wet.

There is a tilted survey obelisk near the path, bronze-capped, carved with a date. Its foundation has been cracked and re-seated on fresh shims — recently, judging by the bright cement. The whole table, you will be told, is still rising. A coin's thickness a year, give or take. The ground here is not a fact. It is a process that somebody is minding.

Dawn comes up over the eastern rim and the light detonates.

This is altiplano light, thin-aired and ultraviolet-hard, and the basin has been arranged — bred, built, it becomes difficult to say — to multiply it. Every surface returns it. The foliage is pale and waxy and silvery; the stone is pale; and at first light every one of the valley's uncountable surfaces is wearing a skin of water, so that for about an hour the entire place burns like a struck match. Mid-morning it will settle into a steady, almost white shimmer, shadeless-seeming, the shadows black-edged and absolute where they fall. You will want dark glasses. You will want a hat. You will not want to speak.

Because the water belongs to the night. That is the valley's first law, and everything else follows.

Rain falls here perhaps four times a season, and each time it is logged in the keepers' books the way other places log the visits of comets. The real water comes up as cloud. At dusk the inversion sea that pools below the rim — you stood above it on the climb, a floor of grey fleece — begins to breathe, and pours through the cols in slow white rivers of fog, and lays itself down across the table. All night the basin sits drowned in still, cold vapor. And all night the valley does what it was made to do: it condenses.

Everything here is a condenser first and a living thing second. Walk out into it — you will wade more than walk — and the layers close over you. Underfoot, cushion-moss to the knee, spongy, near-black green, each cushion a slow century wide. Over that, mats of rosette plants, each rosette built around a central well, a cup of water the size of a thimble or a teacup, kept. Over that, the fin-scrub: shoulder-high shrubs whose leaves are not leaves but blades, combs, upright paddles with enormous cold surfaces, pale as money. Here and there an emergent fin-tree lifts a crown of vertical slats like a ship under full sail. From every branch hang beards of filter-moss that comb the fog. Everything is near-touching. The density is not jungle-lush, not hot and quick; it is the lushness of accumulation, a ledger of patient years, nothing in it fast and all of it kept.

And then you begin to notice the machines.

They are everywhere — that is the strange part — and you have been walking past them for an hour, mistaking them for the landscape, because every one of them is dressed in green. A ceramic fin-stack, man-high, radiating cold into the night fog, its drip-line rooted through by moss. A low pump housing, its alloy dulled under a pelt of lichen, humming at a frequency you feel in the sternum and cannot hear. Cables run along the ground under ridges of moss like long green dikes. Gauge posts stand in the rosette mats with their brass dials fogged. Small cistern sheds hunch in the scrub, meniscuses trembling inside dark tanks. There is one of these presences every few paces, in every direction, to the far rim. The factory has no building. The valley is the plant; the floor of the works is fifty square kilometers; and every engine stands in a ring of slightly richer growth, drinking its own waste warmth, so that from the air the machinery reads as a constellation of lushness against the merely dense green.

At full dawn the mirrors wake.

They are small, most of them — a hand's breadth, a dinner plate — mounted on slow gimbals, thousands upon thousands of them scattered through the canopy on stalks. You do not hear them move. You only notice, as the light climbs, that the basin has begun to turn toward the sun in pieces, ten thousand small suns tilting in silence, and that the frost which silvered the fin-stacks overnight is being loosed, exactly on schedule, as meltwater — released without a sound into waiting cups. Not a trickle. Not a plash. Nowhere in this valley does water ever make the sound of water. All flow is filmed, cupped, piped. The largest body of standing water you will see in a day's walking is the dark still surface inside a cistern hatch.

Here is why, and it is the second law, and the keepers will fine you for it: sound sheds the dew.

A sharp noise — a clap, a shout — strikes the beaded water from every fin and leaf in earshot. Stand in a clearing and clap once, and for a minute you stand in a small local rain, glittering, everything around you stripped of its night's gathering, and the nearest machines pause in their slow work as if the valley had flinched. So the place has gone quiet the way other places go rich. The machines are built below hearing — magnetic, hydraulic, felt-shod. The harvesters that move through the scrub on padded treads take a season to cross a field; you could visit in spring and again in autumn and find one a hundred meters along, mid-row, patient as erosion. The keepers' huts are lined with felt, their doors gasketed with cork, and the keepers themselves, when there were more of them, went soft-soled and spoke little and wrote much, on slates, in a tidy hand.

And life — what little life there is — has gone the same way. Selection here taxed loudness the way drought taxes leaves. The birds of the table are mute: a small grey watchbird hops the fin-tops, and its alarm is not a call but a flash, both wings opening to show pure white beneath, a semaphore of alarm. The great dew-moth, white, a hand's span across, flies at dawn without a whisper and dusts the leaves as it feeds, its scales shedding water down into the rosette wells — pollinator and watering can in one animal. In the wells themselves live glass snails the size of a fingernail, translucent as the drops they drink. A pale lizard basks on the warm casing of a pump, motionless enough to be a fitting. That is nearly the whole census. You will mostly read the fauna by its traces: a silver snail-trail across a fin, moth-dust on a well, one white underwing feather caught in the scrub. Even the animals are condensation that learned to move.

The human traces are sparse, and going quieter. A keeper's hut stands open: stove cold, boots by the door with felt soles, a shelf of slates and pencils, a logbook whose entries have stretched from daily to yearly, the handwriting changing three times across a century. Rain-events are recorded like births. There is a disused listening house on a knoll — a concrete dish, lichened now, that gathers what little sound the valley makes. Stand at its focus and you can hear the whole near-nothing: a single drop ticking into a cup three fields away, spaced like a clock; the grit of a crawler's tread arriving through the soles of your feet rather than your ears. And at the basin's west end, half-swallowed by cushion-moss, lies the evidence that it was not always like this: the first generation of engines, iron, riveted, loud, their burst pipes frozen mid-flood under a century of moss. The loud years, the logbooks call them, when the yields crashed and no one understood why, until someone did. Silence was not always the custom here. It was invented here, the way the steam engine was invented elsewhere, and for the same reason: it worked.

Time on the table runs in nested, patient loops. Each night the fog pours in and the dew sets; each dawn the mirrors loose it; each morning the light burns the basin dry and white; each dusk the cloud returns through the cols like a tide that has never once been late. Each decade the ground rises its stack of coins and the survey crews — whoever still sends them — re-seat the markers. Each century the cushion-mosses widen by a handspan, the bred rows of the condenser forest blur and hybridize into something no charter describes, and the enterprise shades by degrees into wilderness, or the wilderness reveals, by degrees, that it was an enterprise all along. The harvest is measured in milliliters per square meter per night. But the nights run three hundred a year, and the square meters run to fifty million, and somewhere down at the rim a penstock line no wider than a man's torso slips over the edge and falls toward the lowlands, sweating. The valley manufactures a river and never lets it touch the ground. Down there, taps still open. Whether anyone still collects payment for it, the last keeper's final entry does not say. The entry is four words long, dated eleven years ago: *Yield steady. Quiet holds.*

Stay for the dusk. The cloud sea rears below the rim and comes in through the passes in slow white bodies, and the light goes from white to pearl to grey without ever once dimming past usefulness, and the fin-stacks begin to cool, and the first beads of the night's gathering form on ceramic and leaf alike, indistinguishably, the made and the grown doing the same work by the same patience. A crawler halts mid-field for reasons of its own and will not move again for a week. A dew-moth opens its wings. Somewhere out in the scrub a droplet gathers at a fin-tip, swelling past the weight it can hold, and falls the length of a hand into its cup — and makes no sound, because no sound is what it is for. The valley is counting its fortune, one silence at a time, and it has been counting for three hundred years, and it is nowhere near finished.
