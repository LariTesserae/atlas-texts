From the valleys, on certain nights, you can see it standing on the rim of the high country: a shard of green light, upright, holding its glow long after the plateau has gone dark. The tanker drivers call it the Kettle. The drawings in the ministry office call it Column One. What it is, plainly, is a category error — a lake stood on end, forty meters of warm water pinned vertical in thin mountain air, held in a skin of glass and bright alloy at the edge of the escarpment. A segment of some patient sea, lifted out of the world and set down where the eagles ought to be.

There are no eagles. Get to that later.

The road switchbacks up to it through scrub and dry grass, through ordinary high-altitude weather, and ends at a pump-house the size of a chapel, enameled white, so new the stenciled warnings haven't faded. The tower rises out of it. The glass is a handspan thick, double, and through it you can see the water — not blue, not quite, but a luminous tea-green, lit from inside by the sun it swallows. Nothing natural touches anything. There is no stone in this place, no soil, no grain of sand. The floor of the world here is cast slab and polymer grating; the walls are glazed panels with the makers' marks still etched sharp; the beds the garden grows in are alloy racks, factory-true, weld beads bright as the day they cooled. The whole thing was filled a year ago. It is still exhaling its construction air — small silver pockets pinned under beams and hatch coamings, shrinking week by week, giving up the last of the atmosphere the builders breathed. Every few days one lets go and goes up through the green with a soft, apologetic sound, and there are fewer of them now.

You enter through the pump-house lock with tanked air on your back, because the atmosphere of this place is water, and it closes over you warm as a bath, warm as blood, tasting faintly of mineral, faintly of cut stems, faintly — still — of cement and new gasket. It is very clear. It is also very full.

The garden is packed the way a held breath is full. The racks rise in tiers from the slab to just under the surface, a honeycomb of planted cells, and every cell has closed over itself: rope-leaf hanging in curtains, veil-moss furred across every mesh, lantern kelp standing upright in congregations, each frond kept erect by engineered air-bladders tuned to perfect neutrality. Copperleaf. Staircase vine climbing the uprights rung by rung. The swim-ways between racks are green corridors a body wide; reach out and you touch leaf on both sides. Pipes run through foliage and foliage grows through railings. The harvest carriage — a slow yellow machine on its overhead rail, the only keeper this place has — prunes constantly just to keep the aisles open, and the prunings drift off like slow green snow that never lands.

Because nothing falls here. That is the first rule. What is cut drifts; what drifts joins the gyre; what joins the gyre is gathered, eventually, by the intake screens at the base, pulped, and returned to the broth. The garden eats nearly all of its own harvest. Fruit ripens and is taken, or comes loose and wanders. Flowers open underwater, on schedule, pale and waxy, scenting only the water — there is no one to scent it for. The pitcher plants hold their little cups of broth and catch nothing, lids never closing, re-bred to sip from a dosing line. It is a garden with the shape of a wild ecology and the wildness removed: every pollinator, every grazer, every worm and midge and snail, absent by design. No fish. No bird at the glass. If there are swifts over the plateau, they do not come here. The only sanctioned life is green, and even the microbes are curated, dosed, filtered, logged. Nothing darts. Nothing startles. Everything that moves, moves at growth-rate or machine-rate.

The light keeps hours, like a staff. Morning comes in low through the east glass in blades, fanned and softened by the green. By noon the sun stands over the ceiling and drives a bright core straight down the tower's axis — a second column inside the first — and the whole garden leans toward it a degree or two, phototropic, a crowd facing a speaker. Caustic nets slide over the racks, slow, because the surface that makes them is slow. The water holds the light after the sky lets go of it; dusk here is a long green afterglow, and then a few daylight-balanced lamps on the rack frames, and then dark, and the bubbles still going up.

The ceiling is the strangest thing, and you get used to it, which is stranger. The tower's cap is open water — a rooftop pond, the skin of the whole column held up where a roof should be. Looking up, you see the silver underside of the surface, trembling, and the sun through it like a coin in a fountain, and the garden doubled in it at steep angles, hanging upside down in reflected light. Weather happens overhead. Rain is a ceiling event: the whole skin of the world dimples at once, a soft applause you feel in your chest rather than hear. Oxygen beads off the leaves all day in ticking chains, rises, flattens under the surface, slides in mercury sheets to the lantern rim, and is ducted away — the tower's breath, collected, though nobody will quite say for what.

And the room turns. That is the second thing you get used to. Heaters at the base send a warm riser up the axis; the water cools a single degree at the glass and settles; the whole column rotates once a day, imperceptibly, a clock with one hand. A leaf at the wall makes one full circuit between dawn and dawn. Every free thing leans into it — you can read the gyre in the angle of ten thousand fronds, all tilted the same few degrees, like wheat in a wind that takes twenty-four hours to pass. Sometimes a cloud comes to the glass at mid-height and presses itself against the far side of the world-membrane, and you watch it from underwater, from above the valleys, warm, and the vertigo of that is not like anything else: to be inside a lake, and above the weather.

Down at the base is the only room with a voice, and even that is a hum you hear in your teeth more than your ears — slow pumps bedded deep in polymer mounts, gear trains reduced to patience. The dosing manifold hangs here like a pipe organ's private chapel: glass bulbs of concentrate, green and amber and rust, feeding drop by drop, a drop a minute, the year's recipe. Gauges with needles that barely move. A log-plate printing lines no one reads daily. A ladder, a hatch wheel, a yellow rain-slicker hooked by the lock, left by the filling crew and not yet retrieved. And the draw-off valves: four times a year the instruments judge the steeping ready, and a tenth of the column is let down through insulated lines into the tankers, and fresh spring water is let in at the top, and for days the garden tastes of cold and stone, and then warms, and begins again. The vintage goes down the switchbacks to the valleys, to whoever has acquired the taste. The drivers say it sells for more than wine. The drivers do not say what it does.

The catalogue of sounds is short enough to memorize. The tick of oxygen leaving a leaf. The soft gulp of a dosing valve. The carriage passing on its rail, a low chord felt through the water. A trapped air pocket surrendering. Rain on the ceiling, which is pressure, not noise. Your own heart, loud by comparison, an intruder. The water would carry sound for miles if there were sound to carry. There is not. A garden with no garden-sounds — no buzz, no birdsong, no splash — is quieter than any empty room, because everything in it is alive and none of it speaks.

It is all so new. That may be the strangest thing, in the end: the sharpness of it. No silt anywhere — the filters are young and zealous. No patina, no rust, no scar, no history. The louvers that track the sun still move on factory grease. Nothing here has ever failed, and nothing here has ever died — the plants are clones, continuous with stock older than the tower, but in this water nothing has yet ended, and the place carries that the way new houses carry it, faintly waiting. Its firsts are still ahead of it. The first storm on the ceiling. The first drawing-off is done, but the first spoiled batch is not. The first pump failure. The first crack, the first leak, the first weed that is not in the manifest.

Until then it stands on the rim, warm and lit and full, turning once a day, steeping in its own green light — a lake with a view of the clouds from inside, a cathedral with one machine for a congregation, a kettle the size of a held breath, on the edge of the high country, patient as weather, waiting for nothing in particular to happen next.
