From the air, in the flat light before a gale, it looks like a flaw in the sea — a place where the grey water keeps breaking for no reason, white horses rearing in lines and chevrons miles from any shore. There is no shore. There hasn't been one for longer than anyone's charts admit. What there is, sixty kilometres long and half that wide, is a mountain range with the sea standing over it.

This was old country even when it was dry — one of the primordial ranges, folded and refolded before there were fish to witness it, then ground down, then lifted, then ground again. The sea that finally took it did not drown a young, jagged land. It drowned something already worn to its essentials: whaleback ridges, long domes, valleys wide and soft as the creases in a sleeping animal. Every summit is rounded. Every edge is blunt. You could swim the length of the range and never cut your hand on anything the world made — only on the things people left, and those are going soft too.

The relief is enormous. The highest crests rise to within a few metres of the surface, where the swell, feeling them, stands up and falls over in roaring, landless breakers. One rock — the very last — still clears the water at low spring tides: an acre of slick, rounded stone, and it is the loudest acre in the region, crusted with seals and screaming with birds, every body fighting for a place on the only dry thing left. From that rock the bottom falls away in stages — saddles at forty metres, valley floors at a hundred, and one fault-chasm that drops past three hundred into blue that no noon fully reaches. Beyond the last ridge to the west, the shoulder of the range rounds over and simply continues down, into open-ocean dark, kilometres of it. The whole architecture is there, complete and final. It will not change again. Only the water changes, and the water never stops.

Go down through the surface skin on a storm day and the noise comes with you — rain hitting the sea like static swelling on a bad radio, then, deeper, the bass of the breakers, then the thing you notice once the others settle: a crackle, everywhere, like fat frying, like a million tiny sparks. Snapping shrimp, in densities that defy counting, in every crevice of every stone. Cold water carries sound the way cold glass carries light, and this water is cold — four degrees in the deep valleys, maybe nine at the storm-stirred top in late summer. It is dense on the skin, and it is full of voices, and the voices travel.

The light is ordinary light, which underwater means something specific: green-gold and plentiful down through the top forty metres, amber at the edge of things, blue dusk by a hundred, and below that the long dark where nothing bothers with colour. The upper water is not clear in the empty way of tropical seas. It is crowded light — a suspension of living dust, plankton so thick in the summer bloom that the whole column goes the colour of pea soup and the sun becomes a pale coin behind gauze. Things eat that dust, and things eat those things, and everything is close enough to touch.

The slopes are forests. Kelp on every rounded shoulder and dome, thirty and forty metres tall, holdfasts gripping stone and — with no preference at all — rusted rail, cable sheathing, the rims of drowned wheels. The fronds are the colour of strong tea and they never hold still. In the surge they stream and haul, stream and haul, a motion like slow breathing sped up, and the sound of it is a vast wet hiss layered under everything. Between the kelp trunks the light comes down in shifting columns, and through the columns move the schools — baitfish in their tens of thousands, turning as one silver sheet, so dense they shadow the bottom like passing weather. Cod hang under the schools, unhurried. In the valley silts, eelgrass meadows bend all one way and then all the other, twice a day, on the tide's exact word.

Twice a day the world reverses. That is the first rule here, and most of the others follow from it. The sea that fills the inner valleys comes in through the passes, and the passes are races — eight, ten knots of green water shouldering between the domes, standing waves held in place like flexed muscle, whirlpools spinning off the edges and wandering down the slope before they die. The race can be heard through a boat's hull as a continuous roar, and felt through the water as a tremor in the bones. Kelp torn off the ridges streams through it like hair through a drain. Fish ride it deliberately, a conveyor belt, feeding as they go. Then slack: twenty minutes when the whole region hangs poised, when the eelgrass stands undecided and the shrimp's crackle is suddenly the loudest thing in the world. Then everything runs backward.

The storms are worse than the tides and respect nothing. Gales come through most weeks, and their reach goes deeper than seems fair — surge is felt at thirty metres, and in the shallows over the crests the storm moves the cobbles. They are all round, those cobbles, every one, worn to spheres and eggs by the oldest patience on Earth, and the storm rolls them together in beds, and the beds grind. It is a millstone rumble, a deep geological purring under the shriek of the wind, the mountain range still slowly polishing itself in its sleep. After a gale, sediment hangs in the valleys for days and the wreck-kelp comes down in rafts, grounding in the meadows, where isopods and crabs boil up out of the silt to take it apart. Nothing falls here that is not met on the way down. That is another rule.

The people were here briefly, at the very end of the dry time, and the sea keeps what they left the way a dog keeps a bone — not carefully, but permanently. The dam is the biggest of it: a curved wall across the chasm, its concrete gone soft-edged as soap, its spillway gates frozen open under a centimetre-thick rind of barnacle and mussel and anemone, tessellated so tight there is no bare patch anywhere. The tide moves through its turbines both ways, and one of them still turns. You hear it before you see the dam — a hum, low and steady, swelling with the flood, easing with the ebb, a sound with a pulse like something alive. The hum goes up a cable that crosses the valley in a long slack catenary furred with anemones, faintly warm, humming in the lateral lines of every fish that passes. It ends at a drowned switchyard where the power goes nowhere. An octopus lives in the transformer. The only thing the dam still does for anyone is burn one amber beacon on its crest, all night, every night, for no ship in particular.

The rest is smaller and stranger for its smallness. A rail grade along the eastern valley floor, the rails worn to bright threads where the current scours them, a few freight cars off on their sides, each one a cave, each cave with one huge old fish in it that has clearly been there for decades. A mine pit high on a dome, its benches softened, its ladder of rails descending into still, clear, black water. A pump house. A bell buoy, still chained, still ringing in the chop, its chain vanishing down into the kelp. A trawl net snagged on a saddle long ago, still fishing — half-buried now, mussels dragging it into the gravel, crabs working its margins like a slow demolition crew. That is all of it, across sixty kilometres. Scattered bones of a brief interval. The kelp has found every piece.

Life piles onto life until the eye runs out of layers. Every hard surface carries its rind; every rind carries its grazers; every grazer is watched. Octopuses den in the machinery and leave their shell-middens in the doorways. Seals corkscrew through the kelp columns after the schools. In the passes, at the right season, whales come through on the tide, and their calls arrive long before they do and leave long after, folded into the cold water. Birds — auks, cormorants — punch down through the surface like dropped stones and fly underwater between the kelp trunks, forty metres down, working the same columns of light as the fish. And at dusk the whole region performs its one scheduled miracle: the water column fills from below, everything rising — the plankton's night shift, and behind it everything that eats, until the surface boils and the shearwaters batter it with their wings and the noise of feeding carries across the valleys like rain that hasn't arrived. Anthill energy, bazaar energy, every hour of it, in water the temperature of a cellar.

It is loud here. That is worth stating plainly, because a drowned world sounds, from above, like silence. It is not. The shrimp-fry crackle is the floor of it; over that, the hiss of kelp, the grind of the ball-cobbles, the roar of the race, the moan of the turbine, the clank of the bell buoy's chain, the drum of storms and rain on the ceiling, the seals barking on the last dry rock, the whales threading long notes through it all, and under everything the deep purr of the range finishing its long work. The cold water mixes it faithfully and delivers it intact to every corner. There is no quiet here. There is only slack water, twice a day, twenty minutes, when the loudness narrows to the crackle and the hum — the sound of the world idling.

Because the world is idling. That is the deepest fact of the place. The land reached its final shape long ago and will not be argued with; the currents have found their permanent paths; the kelp has claimed every stone it will ever claim; the builders are gone and their works are being absorbed at the rate the sea prefers, which is its own. What remains is maintenance — furious, crowded, deafening, beautiful maintenance, performed daily over the bones of something finished. The top forty metres belong to the light. Nothing stays bare. Everything reverses twice a day. Nothing sharp survives. Everything that falls is eaten before it lands.

Stand in a boat over the chasm at slack water, in the grey hour after a gale has passed, and you can feel the whole arrangement beneath the hull: the cold green layers, the forests standing straight in the stillness, the schools hanging like held breath, the amber beacon already lit against the dark, the turbine's hum coming up through the water and into the planks and into you. In twenty minutes the tide will turn and all of it will start again — the race, the hiss, the grinding, the feeding, the long notes of the whales moving through the passes like weather. It has started again every day since the sea came in. It will keep starting, loud and cold and crowded, over the oldest, stillest bones in the world.
