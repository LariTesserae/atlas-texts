The highest place in the world is a room you cannot stand up in.

It sits at the growing tip of the tower — the Soft Course, the builders call it, because it is the course that hasn't hardened. A dome of mud brick four paces across, low as a bread oven, crowded to the ceiling. Below it, through a square trapdoor, the shaft goes down into the finished: stair after stair of identical brick, dimmer, cooler, more decided. And here is the strange thing about the descent — nothing down there is old. Nothing anywhere is old. The tower has never weathered. The lowest course looks as fresh as the one laid this morning, because wearing-away has not been invented yet, or has been invented and not approved, or is still in committee among the builders, who have more urgent things to hold together. Time passes here, but it doesn't *accumulate*. Descending the shaft is like reading a book backward that refuses to yellow.

Everything is made. That is the first rule of the room and the condition of it. There is no stone that was not shaped, no surface that was not laid. Mud brick, hand-thrown and thumb-signed — every course is fingerprints, thousands of them pressed into mortar that stays just soft enough in the wet heat to take a print and keep it forever. Reed rope lashed in nodes. Scaffold poles of peeled wood. Bowls of hammered copper, soft as clay, dented by thumbs. Lamp dishes of unfired earth that somehow hold oil anyway, because they have been told to. Even the air feels constructed: ladled, stirred, portioned out among too many lungs.

The water comes from the gap.

There is one opening in the dome — an unglazed aperture the size of a face, where the last bricks have been left out. Beyond it is not sky. Sky has not been installed. Beyond it is a bright gray nothing, hot as the inside of a kiln, blank as an unwritten page pressed to the eye. And where that nothing touches the made world, it condenses. It beads on the rim bricks, swells, slips, runs. The whole room weeps with it — reality-condensate, the builders call it, when they call it anything — and they have built for it: half-round gutters of reed along every ledge, copper catch-lips under each joint, jars nested in jars. The water is milky with lime, faintly warm, faintly sweet. They drink it. They mix the mortar with it. They wash the green with it. What the walls sweat out, the gutters return to the bowls; what the bowls spill, the moss drinks; what the moss breathes off, the gap takes back. A complete weather, four paces across.

The green is winning, or trying to. Moss arrives in the mortar seams before the mortar cures — nobody plants it; the damp invites it, or the bricks dream it. Ferns unroll from wall cavities where the condensate pools, filigree the color of bottled lightning. Algae films the water jars overnight, velvet-thick. And the built things keep remembering their lives: the scaffold poles bud. The rope sprouts. A ladder left leaning too long against the same wall puts out pale, apologetic leaves, and a coil of cordage in the corner has gone soft and green and is now, arguably, a small vine with ambitions. Matter here has not yet agreed to be inert. Wood remembers leaf; clay remembers river; the copper vessels bloom with verdigris that forms tiny crystalline fronds, a metal moss, which the builders pinch off and keep in a pouch because nobody is sure what it is for, and everything is for something.

Pruning is the dusk-duty. Eating is the other half of pruning. The room feeds its builders the way a wound feeds a scab.

There are nine builders at present, which is four more than the room has any business holding. There is no empty surface. Bodies, brick stacks, mortar troughs, rope coils, water jars, tool baskets, lamp dishes, the green, the damp — everything touches everything, and the builders move through the room the way eels move through a weir, sliding along each other, never quite out of contact. The heat is total: body heat, lamp heat, the curing heat of fresh mortar, which is exothermic and faintly proud of it, and the kiln-breath of the gap. Sweat does not dry. It joins the general water and is built into the walls with everything else.

It is loud. This surprises everyone the first time and never stops being true. Copper mallets on wet brick — not a clang, a deep *thud*, a drum-sound, a heartbeat. The chant that never fully stops, only thins to two voices, to one, to a hum, and thickens again. The creak of loaded rope. The slap and knead of mortar. The drip-drip-drip in a room of hard surfaces, hundreds of small water-clocks all disagreeing. The tower's own groan as it grows, a sound like a ship's hull remembering depth. And sometimes a boom from very far below, or very far beyond, as though something deep has just agreed to be founded.

The chant is not worship. The chant is scaffolding.

Here is what the builders know, which is the closest thing this place has to physics: the world is not finished, and unfinished things are soft, and soft things can be argued with. So there are rules, and the rules are load-bearing.

Speak the name of a thing before you use it, or it will not hold. A brick named *brick* stays brick; a brick handled carelessly, anonymously, may soften by morning back toward mud, apologetic, formless. A measurement sung is true. A measurement spoken is approximate. A measurement merely *thought* is a lie, and the wall will know. Never look through the gap while the hammers rest — the nothing is most persuasive in silence, and builders have come back from that blank gaze with their names loosened, and had to be re-named by the crew, and were slightly different afterward, and knew it. Do not follow a drip upward; some of the water here falls that way, especially late in a cycle, and it is not going anywhere you want to be. If your echo answers early — and it will, the room is still practicing causality and often gets the order wrong — answer it politely and change your plan. The lizard may go anywhere.

The lizard is the only animal, if it is only one. It is pale, translucent as a fingernail pairing, and it lives among the brick stacks, and it is not quite the same lizard between sightings — the room iterating, trying out lizard, adjusting the toes, the eyes, the patience. Occasionally an insect *happens*: condenses out of the damp the way the water does, completes a single wing-beat or a single circuit of the lamp-flame, and is done — fauna as weather, a moth-event, brief as a spark. The lizard eats what it can catch of these. The builders consider it the soul of the room, or the room's first rough draft of a soul, and they step around it with a tenderness they show nothing else, not even each other.

The work is the laying of courses, and the courses are days. There is no sun to measure by — the light in the room is the dim red-orange of lamp-flame and glow-moss and the faint gray seep of the gap, a womb-light, a forge-light — so the builders keep time by chant-cycle and by the mortar trough: one trough, one course, one day. And the room *rises*. You can feel it if you lie still on the warm floor with your ear to the brick: a slow, cellular lift, the tower growing the way a plant grows, at the apex, from within. The trapdoor deepens behind them. The shaft lengthens. Yesterday's Soft Course is today's firm wall, fingerprints and all, already part of the decided.

Sometimes everything synchronizes. The hammers, the chant, the drips, the groan of the tower, nine heartbeats — they converge, find one pulse, and for the length of a breath the whole room *takes*: the water hangs mid-drip, the lizard stops with one foot raised, the lamp-flames stand straight, and the world is noticeably *realer*, denser, more itself. Then the pulse releases, the drip falls, the work resumes. The builders call these moments *settings*. Something has been decided, they say. Nobody ever knows what. Once, after a setting, all the water fell down for three whole days, and the crew wept with relief, and then it began, occasionally, to fall up again, and they understood that the decision had been revised.

The dome is nearly closed. Only the gap remains, and around the gap the bricks are shaped differently — angled, beveled, socketed, waiting. The builders do not say what the socket is for. Perhaps they do not know. Perhaps they are building a mount for the sun, a fixture for a light that has not been delivered, and the lamps are only rehearsals, practice light, holding the idea of brightness open until the real thing arrives. Perhaps the gap is where the sky gets poured in. Perhaps it is an eye, and the tower intends to see.

For now the Soft Course stays what it is: the highest, smallest, loudest, wettest, most crowded room in existence — a bud at the top of an unweathered world, thick with green and fingerprints and song, nine builders shoulder to shoulder in the red dim, laying brick over brick over brick, naming each one as they go, while the nothing beyond the gap presses close and condenses, and the whole unfinished universe leans in to drink.
