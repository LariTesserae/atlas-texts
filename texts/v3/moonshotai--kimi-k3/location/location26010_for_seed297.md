**The Held Basin**

You come over the rim at four in the afternoon, and it is four in the afternoon for as long as you stay. The light is the first honest thing about the place — warm, white-gold, ordinary as bread. It does not flicker. It does not lower. If you wait for evening you will wait forever, and the basin will watch you wait with the patience of something that has already eaten every evening there was.

Below you, impossibly, it is green.

A shallow bowl of land, sixty or eighty kilometres across, its floor sitting low and warm beneath a sky of unremarkable blue. From the rim you can see the whole of it — the pale pan at the center, the dark comb of forest around it, the spire fields glittering like a struck bell — and all of it overgrown, layered, crowded, shoulder-to-shoulder with life. There is no water in it. Not a stream, not a seep, not a film of dew. Nothing here is wet, and nothing here is thirsty. The green is not the green of drinking. It is the green of something else.

You descend, and the basin begins to explain itself, slowly, the way it does everything.

The ground is young. That is the second thing you notice, and eventually you understand it is the same as the first. Every edge is fresh. Fault shards stand up out of the slopes with faces sharp enough to cut, scree lies in angular heaps that have never been rolled or rained on, crystal veins crop out of the dust unchipped, unrounded, unpatinated. It looks like a world made last week — a world recently emerged, recently broken open, recently set down. But there are no tool marks, no ruins, no writing, no litter of any making. And when you have been here long enough to check, you find that a shard you photographed on arrival has not weathered by a single atom. Freshness is not a phase here. Freshness is the permanent condition. This is not a young landscape. It is a landscape that is never allowed to get old.

About a meter down, beneath the dust and the salt, the world has a floor.

You find it where a root has split the crust, and later you see it properly in the Seam — a canyon with no river in it, a long clean gash where the basin's skin parts and shows what's underneath. Not bedrock. A lattice. Black, warm, tessellated, nested hexagons stepping down and down into a depth that your eyes keep revising. It pulses, very slowly, at a rate you eventually recognize as your own heartbeat, because after you have watched it for a minute it *is* your heartbeat — the two have come into agreement, and you cannot say which conceded. Nothing about the lattice is a structure in the usual sense. It does not sit within the physics of the place; the physics of the place sits within it. Whoever built this built it out of laws, and laws leave no litter.

From the lattice comes the refresh. That is the name you will give it, for want of any other. The world is continuously re-rendered toward some held template — repaired, corrected, kept. Break a leaf and it is whole again by the time the wind next comes. Scuff the salt pan and the scuff fades, not by filling in but by being quietly overruled. The basin does not heal the way flesh heals, through change. It heals by refusing to.

And the vegetation — the astonishing, dense, impossible vegetation — is the reason why.

Up close, the plants are not quite plants as you know them. They are solid-state things, warm to the touch, grown rather than metabolized. The combwood trees branch like hoarfrost scaled to architecture, translucent lattices of bottle-green and honey and smoke, their leaves thin fused blades that chime very softly when touched. Beneath them, cushion-mats of piezoelectric moss-analog pave every shadow, and beneath those, crust-gardens of black-leafed drinkers tile the ground itself. Three strata, four, foliage near-touching foliage, roots interlaced into the dust until there is no bare ground anywhere but the pan. Where they can be said to have sap, it is a warm oil that moves without flowing — driven by the refresh, not by any heart of the plant. They feed partly on light, yes. They feed partly on the air, which is dense and warm and freighted with volatile mineral, so that the atmosphere does the work water does elsewhere. You breathe it and taste hot glass, crushed pepper, something faintly sweet the combwood exhales on a schedule.

But their true diet, the diet that shapes everything, is change.

The vegetation eats deltas. It metabolizes differences-between-moments — drinks the gradient before the gradient can act. A leaf begins to fall; the falling is consumed; the leaf remains. Erosion starts; the starting is eaten; the shard stays sharp. Sound is change traveling through air, and so sound is taxed: your voice dies within arm's reach, footsteps arrive muffled and apologetic, and you learn to touch a companion's shoulder to speak. The basin is lush precisely because nothing is allowed to leave. It is a garden at permanent climax, at frozen equilibrium, a standing wave of life — dense not because it is growing, but because nothing in it is permitted to stop.

The rules are few, and they are kept perfectly. Nothing flows. Change is food. What is eaten does not happen. The world is refreshed, and what the refresh cannot explain, it keeps. The wind comes on schedule. The sun does not set, because there is nowhere for it to go: this is not a world with a sun, it is a world with a light setting.

What the refresh cannot explain, it keeps. That clause is where the weirdness lives, and the weirdness lives everywhere, stable as furniture.

Your shadow, cast by the unmoving sun, is a fixture — but check it against your body and you will find it lagging, a heartbeat behind, showing you the posture you just left. Reflections in the salt pan do the same: your reflected self is always who you were a moment ago. In the combwood forest there are trees whose shadows belong to larger, older versions of themselves — and stranger, there are shadows on the salt with no trees at all, the dark full crowns of ungrown things, thrown by nothing, permanent. The going theory, if a theory is wanted, is that shadows are where the eaten change goes. The world pays for its freshness in shadow, and some of what it pays is the future.

In a vale below the western rim there is a suspended seedfall. Thousands of seeds and leaves hang fixed in the air at every height, a green rain stopped mid-drop — and each one occupies several positions at once, six or seven ghost-copies slightly out of phase, so the whole grove shimmers at its edges in afterimage violet. It has never resolved. It is an heirloom error, a glitch the refresh filed under *keep*. You can walk through it. When you brush a hanging seed it chimes against its own copies, faintly, and your fingers seem to arrive a fraction of a second before you do.

Distance is economical here. From the rim, the far side of the basin is rendered in broad strokes, painterly, low-resolution, a sketch of itself. It sharpens as you walk toward it — over days, not steps — as though the world does not spend detail on anything you are not near.

And at the center, the White Floor: a salt pan flat to atomic tolerance, humming just below hearing. Water exists in this basin only as a rumor — a few hydroxyls locked in mineral lattices, a chemistry of absence. There was a sea here once, perhaps. It did not dry. The evaporite crusts are too perfect, the shoreline terraces too crisp. The sea was *deleted*, not evaporated — edited out, the way everything here is edited. Standing on the pan you sometimes remember it: cool green water, reeds, a shore bird. The memories are false, arrived from nowhere, and they fade like the scuffs do. The basin keeps one copy of everything it removed, and it keeps that copy in you.

Mostly, it is quiet. Your own heartbeat becomes the loudest thing you know; the blood in your ears becomes weather. Then, on its timetable — though nothing here needs one — the wind keeps its appointment. It is the world's one permitted verb, a fixed pattern of moving air, the same gusts on the same schedule forever, the refresh's slow breathing. When it arrives the combwood canopy gives one soft chord, glass and reed, and out in the spire fields the Flutes — those young, sharp towers of faulted stone — answer with a low note you feel in your sternum. Then the stillness closes over again, complete.

Animals are rare, and each one is a budget. Living creatures are pockets of un-eaten time moving through a world that has spent all of its own, and the basin affords few.

The long oryx are the most of them — fewer than a thousand, three meters at the shoulder, translucent as blown glass with a slow oil-light moving inside. They graze the black leaf-tips in the understory, one step every few minutes, ageless, unhurried, watching you with the calm of something that has never once needed to hurry. Where one passes, the world must spend: footprints linger on the pan for long minutes before the refresh overrules them, and the trails they keep open through the dense growth are the only paths in the basin, corridors of permitted passage. When an oryx dies — and they die only by accident — the refresh does not repair it. The body vitrifies slowly into the salt, and the growth around it stands taller for a season, fed by the dividend of all that un-eaten time released at once. A death is the only event the basin has.

The stutters and smears are inhabited by something small that is never quite in one place — call it the undecided. You never see one; you see a heat-shimmer shaped like an animal, a smear of almosts. It drinks the gaps inside the glitches. If one crosses your path, the last minute of your life goes briefly plural, and you cannot say which foot you stepped with.

There is one bird. It is not refreshed; it is old the way animals are old. It flies the same slow circuit over the pan and the forest, and where it lands the lag of the world deepens for a moment, as if the basin turns its attention. Perhaps the world keeps it the way a sleeper keeps one ear open.

You will wonder, eventually, what you are spending. You are a walking expenditure of time in a place that ate all of its own, and the longer you stay the more you feel it: your shadow lagging further, your thoughts arriving pre-resolved, decisions thinning as the world sips at the small changes of your mind. Sleep is easy and dangerous; you wake rested after minutes or years, and the unmoving sun agrees with any account you give. If you were to stop — truly stop, hold still long enough — the refresh would begin to include you. Roots would thread your shadow. You would become a fixture, corrected forever toward a held template, mid-thought, mid-afternoon.

On the White Floor there is a pillar of salt, or a person. It has been there as long as anything. It is either sitting down or standing up; the decision is taking a very long time. The refresh does not touch it. The undecided will not cross it.

When you finally climb back out over the rim, you look down at the whole of it one last time — the green impossible forest, the white floor, the seam of black geometry, the spires catching four-o'clock light that will catch that same light forever. Not a garden, you understand now. Not a machine. A single kept moment with landscaping. A world that was given a choice between changing and lasting, and chose, and is choosing still.

Behind you, on schedule, the wind arrives. The canopy gives one soft chime — not for you.
