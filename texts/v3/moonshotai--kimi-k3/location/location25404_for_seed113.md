The shaft comes first: a square-shouldered bore driven down through wet rock, its corners still sharp as new pottery. Where the cut crossed the water table, the stone weeps — thin seep finding the flutes the constructor left for it, running down in straight bright lines. The air warms as you descend, and moistens, and the sound rises to meet you: a broad smooth roar that you mistake, at first, for your own blood.

At the bottom the shaft opens into the cistern, and the roar resolves into its parts.

The hall is small as cities go — you could walk its length in ten minutes, a hamlet's footprint — but its ceiling is cathedral-high, and almost all of it is empty. A canal runs down the spine between bare quays. Stepped basins descend to it like terraced fields. At the far end, the throat: a black arch where the water accelerates and leaves, a smooth glottis of grown stone that gives the hall its bass note, a chord you feel in the sternum more than hear. Everything else is middle and high — the tick and ring of falling drops, the occasional valve-song — laid over that low pedal tone. The surfaces are hard and wet and the reverb blooms for seconds. In this much emptiness, a single drop striking a basin a hundred meters away arrives at your ear intact, introduced by its own echo.

It is dim. The light is mostly borrowed: a coin of grey sky at the top of the shaft, fitted with a grille, admitting one slow column of daylight that crosses the hall like a sundial hand and goes out. For the rest of the day there are the guide-lines — thin amber strips at ankle height, meant for nothing with eyes — and a faint steered phosphorescence in the walls themselves, which the hall turns up where it is working and lets die where it is not. Colors reduce to black-greens and wet greys. Your skin learns the place before your eyes do: body-warm air, water slightly warmer than that, and one cold surface, the vault overhead, which the outflow cools from behind. Warm below, cold above. So the cistern has weather.

The weather runs on a process clock, not a solar one. Warm wet air rises off the basins, condenses on the cold vault, and the ceiling — extruded with a deliberate field of drip-nubs, a designed stalactite garden — grows drops that fall four or five meters and land as a soft random polyphony, thousands of ticks an hour. When storms come to the surface, the grille above ropes water down in white columns, the canal rises, the throat deepens its note, mist thickens until the far arch vanishes, and for a few minutes the vault genuinely rains: an indoor shower, brief and total, every drip-nub delivering at once. Then the surge passes and the hall wrings itself out and returns to its idle chord.

It is very young. The constructor grew it in a single season — sintered the bedrock, drank the aquifer, extruded the whole volume in one continuous gesture — and the making is still on it. The floor keeps a faint corduroy of tool-passage. The walls are warm from within and sweat their cure-water in beads, so that the weeping you saw in the shaft continues indoors, everywhere, by design and by afterglow. Nothing is rounded. Nothing has patina. And nothing will, for a while: the water that leaves through the throat is too pure to carry anything away. It neither stains nor dissolves. The cistern does not erode; it settles, warping by millimeters as it tunes itself, a body finding its posture.

For a city, it is almost empty. Perhaps one percent of the volume is occupied by anything but air and water. Yet the grammar of settlement is all here, laid out with civic confidence: quays with bollards every thirty meters, mooring rings, a dock with no boat, a rail no tram has ever run on, catwalks, steps, undercrofts glimpsed through grates where the water goes dark. At the intake, a stencil: SUBCATCHMENT 4, and under it a line of machine script, and under that, addresses — each segment of quay, each basin, each pilaster is numbered, as if expecting mail. The template builds amenities for citizens the template does not know it lacks. The cistern is a city waiting for its noun.

Its actual presences are few and far between, and the distances between them are the truest thing about the place. Half-sunk in the canal: a pump-torus, a black ring whose rim just breaks the surface, no visible moving parts, water simply agreeing to pass through it in one direction. At the throat, the valve-choir — field apertures without mechanism that throttle by shaping the water itself, and that sing as they do it, pure harmonic overtones sliding up when the flow is pinched. On a rail above the gardens, the gardener: a jointed arm folded into itself, beaded with condensation, dormant for days at a time. Two drones asleep on a high ledge like tools hung up. And under the floor, felt rather than heard, a subsonic heartbeat from the sump. That is all. When one of these wakes — when the gardener unfolds to comb a root-mat, when a drone lifts off and crosses the long dim air — it is an event on the scale of the whole hall. Everything audible turns toward it.

The gardens occupy their terraces and rafts in deliberate ribbons: umbrella leaves near-black from drinking low light, reed beds in combed lanes, root-hair trailing in the canal like hair in current. The plants are the plumbing — chemical pumps, each bed solving some fraction of the water's remaining impurities — and the hall feeds them through climbing films, thin bright sheets of water that ascend fluted pilasters against gravity, wicking upward to the high beds. When the day-column crosses the pilasters the climbing films catch it and shine, one by one, then go out as the light passes. Before a backflush, the leaves' veins flush faintly luminous, a slow green warning rippling through the beds.

And here is what you notice after an hour, the wrongness: no leaf has a hole in it. Nothing nibbles. The water is clear, warm, rich — and fishless. No wings cross the day-column. No webs bridge the bollards. No croak, no chirp, no splash that isn't geometry. Partly it's the ultrasonic fence that stands at the waterline like a pressure at the edge of hearing; partly the chemistry; mostly, perhaps, the youth — nothing has arrived yet. The garden grows uneaten and unpollinated, entire, edited only by the gardener-arm and its own chemistry. A green place without a single animal motion in it, and loud anyway — loud with water and work. There is no smell of decay anywhere. Wet chalk, ozone near the valves, green sap, warm stone. Nothing here has ever died, and the air knows it.

The cycles keep it active. Surge, when it rains above. Backflush, when the basins reverse and run uphill for an hour, the floor rinsing itself toward the throat, the loudness peaking, a standing wave rising in the arch like a held breath. Idle, which is most of the time: the drip polyphony, the sliding light, the walls exhaling their cure, the mist banks walking slowly against the draft — shepherded by charge, kept in lanes, moved where the work is. In the stilling basins the flow stands in fixed ripple-lattices, the water doing its own mathematics, and if you watch long enough you see the patterns shift as the chemistry is solved and the solution sent onward.

The rules of the place, once you've sat with it: water is never still, and never lost. Light belongs to the sky for one hour and to the machines for the rest. Sound never stops — there is no silence here, only softer chords. Nothing wears. Nothing arrives. Everything is addressed, and no one is home.

What leaves the throat goes down lightless lower galleries and rises, eventually, to somewhere — taps, a river, a reservoir — cleaner than any rain that ever fell. The cistern keeps no record of where. It keeps its ledger in drops and chords, and when the day-column goes out like a lamp and the garden veins come up green and a drone turns once in its sleep, the hall continues: young, warm, loud, and empty, a new gland of the watershed doing its one perfect thing in the dark — patient the way only things that do not die are patient.
