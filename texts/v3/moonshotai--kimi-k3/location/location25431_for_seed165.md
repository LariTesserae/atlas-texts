The first thing is the sound, and it is also the last thing, and everything in between is a way of listening to it.

You come up the Stair — a ramp of placed stones, thousands of them, none cut, all carried, set into the headwall above the cloud floor by hands that had patience instead of tools. You climb through the underside of the permanent overcast, wet and dark and blind, and then you come out onto the roof of the region, and the chord takes you.

It is loud the way a weir is loud, the way a cathedral organ is loud — felt in the sternum and the teeth before it is heard. The plateau is fluted. Frost-split pinnacles and weathered tors stand in fields across the whole upland, and the wind has spent longer than anyone can measure rounding them, hollowing them, tuning their throats. The wind itself has one speed and one direction and has never had another. It does not gust. It does not lull. It leans on you with the same steady hand every hour you are here, and it plays the stone the way breath plays a flute, ten thousand flutes at once, and the sum of it is a single held chord — deep in the ground, bright overhead — that does not swell, does not fade, does not resolve.

Travelers below call this place the Held Note. Older maps say the Long Evening. Both names are accurate.

The light is a dusk that stands all the way around the horizon, a full ring of dim amber-grey afterglow with no sun in it and no dark quarter anywhere. Overhead the zenith is deep violet, dark enough for a handful of faint fixed stars. Nothing casts a clean shadow; the light arrives from everywhere at once, sourceless and even, and it does not change. There is no morning to wait for and no night to fear. When you sleep, you sleep in the same dusk you woke in, and you learn to stop asking your body what time it is, because the answer is always: this.

The land is old and finished. The scree locked into place long ago under crusts of black lichen and rime; the stone runs halted mid-cascade and were never sharp, even then. Freeze and thaw have sorted the gravel of the flats into rings and stripes — perfect natural circles, waist-wide, that you will mistake for the work of hands until you find the circles that hands actually made, and then you will not be sure of either again. Everything is weathered to its final shape. Nothing here is still becoming. The region reached its form and stopped, and the form it reached was an instrument, and the instrument was already tuned. You will find, wedged in the mouths of certain flutes, single carried stones — placed there to close a throat, to flatten a note, to correct the chord. Someone finished the mountain. Then they left, or they didn't, and the chord has been holding ever since, exactly as held.

Hold on to the central fact: **nothing here changes state.** This is the whole law, and everything else is a consequence. Water that should be ice lies black and liquid in the tarn basins, supercooled past all sense, kept from freezing because freezing is an event and events are not permitted. Ice that you carry in your fist stays ice. The frost your breath lays on your sleeve is there when you leave. Damp stone sweats its condensation beads and the beads do not run; they sit on the lichen like a held thought. The cloud floor far below the Edge never moves — you can look down from the cliffs onto a white ocean with the chord's bass note seeming to rise up through it, and a shape in that ocean will be the same shape tomorrow. Fire has never burned here. Flame is a change of state. The cold is a deep, damp, bone-settling cold, survivable and inescapable, and it is the same cold everywhere and always.

And nothing is erased. The ground keeps everything. Your footprints do not weather; they join the record — and the record is deep. Faint trails cross the region in their hundreds, every crossing ever made still legible: the bare long-toed prints of the first walkers, the stacked-stone cairns that mark their routes, the silver grazing-script of the sleeper shells. The whole plateau is a palimpsest of passage, and since the land is finished and only visitors add to it, everything ever added remains. On the Block Sea — the vast boulder field that occupies the region's heart, hours across, empty of everything but stone and chord — you may cross a line of bootprints and recognize your own. Do not be afraid. Space is loose here. The far and the near are not reliably stitched: a flute-pinnacle sounds as close at a league as at a stone's throw, until you step past a ridge and it cuts off like a door closing. The great tor called the Axis is visible from every district, always at the same height above the horizon, and no two travelers agree on which direction they saw it in. Walk far enough and the land hands you back to yourself.

Whoever tuned the mountain came looking for silence, and they found it, and they marked it. The chord, being a standing wave, has nodes — pockets where the phases cancel, scattered across the upland like rooms in a roaring house. Step into one and the sound falls away to almost nothing, a hush so abrupt it rings. These are the holy places, and they are easy to find: follow the cairn-lines. At every node, stacked stones. A seat of placed slabs, facing the dusk-ring. A ring of boulders around ground where the moss grows thick — because silence is fertile here; in the still air the condensation settles instead of being stripped away, and the only softness in the region grows in the quiet. On the largest node stands the region's one building: a low beehive of stacked stone, the Hearing House, big enough for one person to sit upright inside. In its center the chord cancels completely, and what replaces it is your own body — your pulse, your breath, the small roar of your blood — which becomes, at last, the loudest thing in the world, and the only thing keeping time. There is a listener in the House. They sat down a very long time ago to hear their own heart, and the place's law holds for the dead as for the water and the frost: nothing changes state. They have not decayed. They are not, perhaps, entirely finished. Sit with them a while. Most people do, once.

Almost nothing lives here, and what does is strange with the place's strangeness. The gliders hang on the standing wind at fixed stations in the dark sky — slate-colored, wide-winged, never seen to flap, never seen to land, riding air that never changes and so never requiring anything of them. Perhaps they sleep up there. Perhaps they have been at their stations since the tuning. In windless moments you can catch the thin high thread their wing-feathers draw through the chord, a single harmonic moving slowly against the held notes. The sleeper shells creep the lichen fields on decades-long errands — pale grazers the size of a hand, stony, deaf, indistinguishable from stones until you watch long enough, and each shell tuned to the note of the ground it crosses, so that if you lift one (don't) it hums faintly against your palm like a struck glass. And on the Block Sea, once, perhaps twice in a crossing, you may see the other one: a large pale shape at the edge of sight, four-legged or not, standing among the boulders at the same distance no matter how you approach, never closer, never gone. It may be an animal. It may be the mountain entertaining the idea of an animal. It does not leave footprints. Only you do.

So the rules, for as long as you stay. Navigate by ear — the map of this place is a map of timbre, and the cairns lead from quiet to quiet. Drink from the tarns; the water is cold past reason and will not hurt you. Bring nothing you are not willing to leave, and leave no mark you are not willing to keep forever, because forever is what marks get here. Do not wait for evening; it is already evening; it has been evening since the tuning was finished. And when you notice that you have stopped hearing the chord — that your mind has folded it into silence — leave soon. The ones who stop hearing it altogether tend to stay, and the nodes have room, and the law is patient.

The way down is the Stair, back through the wet dark of the cloud floor, and the chord thins with altitude and lets you go, note by note, until the moving world resumes below — weather, mornings, water that freezes, things that rot, events. Above you, at the same dusk, your trail is already part of the record, lying alongside the barefoot tracks of the tuners, exactly as fresh as theirs, forever.
