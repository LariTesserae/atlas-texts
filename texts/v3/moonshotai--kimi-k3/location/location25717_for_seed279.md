The lid comes first. From the rim of the basin you stand in a light that has no direction — a low grey deck of cloud that has not broken in living memory, bright the way the inside of a shell is bright, sourceless, shadowless. It sits close overhead, a few hundred meters up, so the whole world feels compressed between that soft grey ceiling and the wet floor below. The air is cool and stays cool, somewhere above freezing and well below comfort, and it is saturated. Your breath barely shows. Everything else drips.

Below you, the basin opens — wide, shallow-bowled, fading into its own haze before you can find the far side. And it is full. Not of water, though water is everywhere in it, but of standing stone: a field of chimneys, tens of thousands of them, packed so close that from up here they read as texture, a grey-white nap across the land, like the pile of a carpet, like frost heave, like something combed. The eye keeps trying to resolve them into ruins — a drowned city, a petrified forest — and keeps failing. They are too dense for a city and too regular for a forest and too young for either.

And before you have finished seeing it, you hear it. A drone, deep and composite, rising and falling on a period of minutes, felt in the sternum as much as heard. The basin is making a sound, and it has clearly been making it for a long time, and it is not in any hurry to stop.

---

The descent tells you the first fact about this place: it is new. The slopes below the rim are cut with strandlines — crisp benches, one above the next, each marking where water stood and stood and then didn't. They are sharp enough to cut a hand on. Nothing here is rounded. The fault scarps that ring the basin shed rockfall with faces fresh as broken bread. There is no soil anywhere, and no plants to make any. This is a seafloor, or a lakebed, that has recently — on the land's own clock — been handed up into the air, and it has not yet had time to soften. The water is leaving, and has been leaving for a long while, and will be leaving still when you are done with it.

Then you are among the chimneys, and the density becomes a fact of the body. You cannot walk a straight line ten paces long. The spires stand a stride apart, less in the old neighborhoods — knee-high stubs and three-story organs, shoulder to shoulder, crowding. They are precipitate tubes, grown by mineral-laden water around its own rising paths: bone-white, banded, streaked rust where iron runs in the seep, pale sulfur-yellow in sheltered seams, black-green where manganese sheens the wet. Their rims are crisp as the day they formed. Many are hollow straight through, open-mouthed to the sky.

And they weep. Each chimney is colder than the air it stands in — chilled from below by the cold groundwater that still threads them — so the saturated air condenses on them without pause. They harvest the fog. Every surface carries a film of water in slow motion; every rim has its pendant drop, gathering. The whole field ticks and whispers with drips between spire and spire, a fine private rain that falls whether the sky does or not.

The ground between is marsh-edge world: grey ooze, crust-sheets of travertine that ring hollow underfoot, shallow meres where the spacing widens. In the pools lives the closest thing this place has to vegetation — films. Not plants, not fungi, something older and simpler than either: chemotrophic skins feeding on what seeps out of the stone. Rust-red where iron is on the menu, dark olive-black, a greenish slick here and there that is chemistry, not life as you'd name it. They are the basin's whole standing crop, and they are the reason a place this wet smells of almost nothing. Nothing rots here. There is no rot. The air smells of cold stone and metal, like a cave, like a coin held in the mouth — clean in a way that is itself unsettling, because your nose keeps waiting for the marsh-smell and it never comes.

---

Now the sound, because the sound is the place.

The chimneys are pipes, and the basin plays them. Wind crossing the open mouths of ten thousand tuned tubes draws a chord out of the field the way breath draws a note from a bottle — except here the bottles number in the hundred thousands and the breath is the weather itself. When the wind freshens and aligns with a slope, whole hillsides sound at once, and the drone becomes a chord you could lean against: loud enough, at the resonant nodes, that speech stops being worth the effort, loud enough that you feel your ribs sympathetic. Between the nodes lie queer dead zones where the crust-sheets muffle everything to wool. You can walk from a roaring choir-stall into a padded cell in six paces.

Under the drone, the slower instrument. The water table still breathes — rising a finger's width in the wet weeks, falling back — and as it moves it displaces air through the tubes below the waterline. The field glugs. It swallows and exhales. Long sub-audible swells run through it, and individual chimneys give wet belching ticks as their throats clear. Add the drip-polyphony, the occasional sharp crack of a growing spire stressing its own fresh stone, and — rarely, enormously — the boom of a crust-sheet collapsing somewhere far off, or rockfall on the scarp, arriving like weather of its own.

And the strangest thing: the basin keeps what it hears. Sound goes into the packed tubes and comes back late — not as a clean echo but smeared, deepened, returned from the wrong direction. Your own footstep may come back to you minutes after you take it, wrong. A shout addressed to the field will be answered by the field, in its own time, in a voice assembled from yours. Loud places that are also patient places do this: they talk back on a delay, and the delay here is long enough that it never feels like an echo. It feels like a reply.

---

Walk the basin downslope and you walk its history backward to its present. Upslope, near the strandlines, the chimneys are tallest, densest, deepest-voiced — the old field, centuries dry, layered generation on generation, new spires sprouting from the stubs of drowned ancestors, the whole stack going down who knows how far into the wet dark. Descend and the spires thin, shrink, fall silent by degrees — their tubes still water-filled, their mouths not yet opened to the air. And at last you reach the waterline: a grey mere that stretches away into the haze, standing water that has no far shore you will ever see, and at its edge the youngest land on earth — thin new chimneys the width of a wrist standing in the shallows, mute. The basin has not yet learned their notes. Each year the water drops a little and a fresh rank is uncorked; each year the chord migrates a body's-length downhill. The whole instrument is being tuned by its own draining, and it is only partway through the piece. Somewhere out in the dim, beyond the standing water, there are other basins — siblings at other stages: some still drowned and silent, some older than this one, nearly dry, sounding so deep their music is mostly a thing you feel in your feet.

Scattered through the field — one every few hectares, no pattern you can catch — are the works. Small structures: cairns of spire-splinters, stacked. Low arcs of crust-sheets pried up and leaned like cards. Thresholds, unmistakably — two spires bridged by a wedged slab, a doorway to nothing, marking nothing. All of it friction-fit. No cut marks anywhere, no cord, no fire-cracked stone, no tool has ever touched this place; whatever builds here builds with hands or mouths or patience alone. And the works are old in layers: some are fresh, pale, sharp; some are half-swallowed, coated in flowstone, sinking into the field as the field grows over them — a century, two, five. The one regularity is this: they stand at the loud places. Whoever or whatever piles stones in this basin piles them at the resonant nodes, where the chord is strongest, where the ground hums against your bones. Whether that is worship, or marking, or appetite, no one is going to tell you.

The likeliest builders are the grazers, though likely is doing a lot of work in that sentence. There are not many animals here — a handful of kinds, and few of each. The grazers are the large ones: low, broad, slick-skinned things the length of a rowboat, amphibious, slow as geology, that move between the meres and crop the films with wide grey mouths. You will probably not see one. You will hear one — a long water-shouldering in a pool at the limit of hearing, a breath like a bellows, once, far off. You will find their trails: broad polished runs through the ooze that stay sharp for months until the precipitate coats them. And you will find, now and then, what becomes of them: a grazer dies in the shallows and nothing eats it and nothing rots it — instead the mineral water takes it, coats it, builds on it. A mound, a knoll, then a chimney growing from the carcass, and in fifty years a new voice in the chord, a spire with a low strange note. Graves that sing. Some of the deepest-toned chimneys in the old field may be exactly that. Nothing here is wasted; it is mineralized.

In the meres there are smaller lives — translucent swimmers, slow as everything else, filtering the films. And at night there are sounds that belong to neither of these, knocks and stone-clicks out in the dark field that might be the grazers at their stacking, or might be the third thing, whatever the third thing is. The basin declines to specify.

---

Night here is not a dimming but a switching-off: the grey deck goes black, and since there is no other light — no moon disc ever seen, no glow but a faint chemical shimmer on some pool-films — the world becomes pure sound. You lie in the dark inside the chord, and the chord breathes around you, and drips spell out their long arithmetic, and somewhere a grazer moves through water with the patience of a tide, and your own small noises come back to you later, changed. It is not frightening, exactly. It is the opposite of silence so complete it takes a day to stop bracing against it.

Nothing in this place hurries. The water leaves by the finger's-width per year; the spires grow by the glaze per season; the cloud deck lifts perhaps once in a decade, briefly, and the field stands astonished under pale sky, and then the lid returns. Come back in fifty years and you would find the same place tuned lower — the waterline crept down, a new rank of chimneys finding their voices, the old stacks an inch deeper in stone, some spire you knew now cracked or drowned or swallowed, the chord migrated a little farther from the rim. It would recognize you, in its way: it has kept everything you said to it, and it will be giving it back, slowly, for some time.
