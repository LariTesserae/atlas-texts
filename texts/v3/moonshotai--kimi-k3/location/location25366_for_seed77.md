The first thing to understand is that there is no ground here. There has not been ground for a very long time.

Stand anywhere — and anywhere is available, the world offers nothing but standing room — and what lies under you is a pale, dense, mineral substance with the faint grain of growth in it, like wood, like shell, like bone sawn across. It goes down. If you could go down with it, through the floors of the old rooms, through the seams where one age closed over another, you would pass through hundreds of meters of the same secreted stone before reaching anything the planet made rather than anything the planet grew. The original crust is down there somewhere, buried under the accumulated body of the thing that covers it. Nobody calls that crust the world. The world is what has been built on top of it, and it is still being built, and that is the second thing to understand.

From any rise — the rises are shallow, domes and swells of a few tens of meters, because this is a low world, a horizontal world — the construction runs to every horizon. It is not dense. That is the strange beauty of it. It is a line-work rather than a mass: struts and ribs and vaulted spans, pale as chalk, with more emptiness than substance. The eye can travel through it for kilometers, threading arch after arch, cell after cell, until the distance dissolves the structure into a kind of pale weather of its own. A drawing in bone on an empty page. Most of the world, by volume, is air held in rooms.

The light is ordinary light, a sun, a day. The heat is not ordinary. It stands over the flats like a medium, shimmering at the far line-of-sight limits, pressing down through the open vaulting into the upper rooms. By midday the outer surfaces are too bright to look at comfortably and too hot to rest a hand on, and the world ticks — faintly, constantly — with expansion.

Nothing here was made by hands. There were never hands. There are no tools, no devices, no straightedge, no intention anywhere in it — and yet it is entirely constructed, every last surface. The builder is a thin living skin spread over the growing edges of the structure, and it builds the way a shell builds: by accretion, by local response, by rules so simple they look like genius at planetary scale. A strut thickens where it bears load. A rib forks where stress forks. Where two flows of growth meet at a balanced angle, something very near a right angle appears, unplanned. The whole world looks engineered because physics engineers it, generation on generation, the way bone is engineered by the weight it carries.

Only the edges are alive. This is what makes the green so sparse and so strange: from above, the world is a dead white immensity veined with life. The living skin follows the growth-fronts — the lengthening tips of struts, the rims of closing domes, the lips of cistern-cells — in seams of grey-green, verdigris, dull gold, a few meters wide and kilometers long. Elsewhere there are crustose films on the sunward faces, rust-orange and lichen-pale, doing slow photosynthetic work. That is all the vegetation the world has, and it is enough, because the world is not feeding a crowd. There is nothing else to feed. No animal has ever moved here. Nothing walks, nothing flies, nothing calls. The construction is not a habitat. It is the organism.

And yet it is never still. The pace is biological, which means you must learn to see it on its own clock. A strut lengthens by a hand's width in a good year. A dome draws itself closed across a century. Watch any single cell of the lattice for a decade and it will be visibly, undeniably different — a span completed, a wall grown where wind used to pass, a cistern mouth narrowed to a slot. Collapse is the other half of the rhythm. Spans overreach; the mineral fatigues; some warm night there comes a crack like a glacier calving, and then a low rumble that takes whole minutes to roll across the flats, and a brief white bloom of dust. Within a few years the skin has seamed the rubble. Within a century the fall is a set of under-rooms, and the rain is in them, dissolving the wreck grain by grain and laying it down again as flowstone — the only erosion this world has, since there is no exposed rock anywhere to make sand or soil or sediment. Wind has nothing to pick up. The surface never ages. Everything you can see is recent — this century, this millennium at most. Deep time is vertical here. The antiquity is underneath.

Water is rare, and water is the point.

The heat drives the whole economy. All morning the living skin transpires, and by noon a haze deck has formed at a certain height, thin and luminous, and in the afternoon the convection over the flats organizes itself into cells — and it rains, but it rains with an address. One cell of the lattice, a few kilometers across, takes a hard sheet of warm rain for twenty minutes while the neighboring cells stay dry and white. The addresses are not random. The geometry of the structure traps and releases heat; the shape of the roofscape decides where the thermals rise; the weather, in effect, is drawn by the architecture — which was grown in response to the last ten thousand years of weather. Growth shapes rain shapes growth. The world has been having this conversation with itself for longer than anyone could count, and it has become very good at it, and what it has been optimizing, all this time, is the catching and keeping of water.

High thin fins rise off the upper spans and radiate their heat to the night sky, and dew beads on them before dawn and runs down engineered-that-is-not-engineered gutters into cistern-cells: grown basins, some open to the light, some roofed and slot-mouthed, some sealed entirely. The open ones hold warm, still, mineral-heavy water with a white rind of salts at the margin, and stepping near one is a change of climate — a pocket of humidity, a softness in the air, the smell of wet stone in a world of dry bone. The sealed ones hold water that has not moved since the dome closed over it, and it is as still now as it was then. In the deep under-rooms, where the light arrives only as a faint diffusion down the light-wells, cool air pools and condensation sweats off the flowstone and drips, each drop arriving seconds apart, each one audible, because there is nothing else to hear.

The soundscape is the near-total silence of a world with no voices in it — no calls, no footfalls, no wings, ever — and yet the silence has texture. Wind moves through the lattice and flutes, a low hollow note that shifts as the direction shifts, sometimes two notes at once from spans of different size. At dusk the whole construction ticks as it cools, softly, everywhere at once, like some immense engine banking its fires. Occasionally, from the growth-fronts, a sharp crack as new stone takes load. Very occasionally, far off, the rumble of a collapse, and then the silence closing over again like water.

In the hottest season there is one more motion, and it is the closest thing this world has to flight. On certain afternoons, when the thermals stand strongest over the flats, the living skin releases its spores, and they rise in visible columns — pale smoke going up through the haze deck, slow, sparse, separated by kilometers of empty air. They drift for days. Where one lands on a growth-front and takes, a new seam of green begins, and new stone follows it, and the world gains another few meters of itself.

This is the whole of it: a planetary masonry with no mason, a still the size of a world, running on sunlight, catching its own thin rain, keeping its few warm cisterns against the heat, building and falling and building again at the pace of bone. There is no one to drink the water. There is no one to walk the halls. The construction is not for anything, and it has never been for anything, and it goes on — tick of cooling at dusk, drip in the dark rooms, green seams lengthening at the edges — a vast, sparse, half-empty drawing of a world, endlessly finishing itself, endlessly beginning.
