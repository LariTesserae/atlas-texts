**The Bench**

The first thing is the dark. The second thing is the warmth, and they arrive together, which feels wrong — warmth should come with light, should slant, should have a source you can face. Here it rises through the soles of your feet. The rock itself is warm, blood-warm in places, and the thin wind that crosses the plateau carries no chill worth naming. You stand in a blackness like the inside of a held breath, at an altitude that makes your lungs work for their living, and the ground beneath you is keeping you. That is the Bench: a continent lifted high into its own sky, heated from below, wrapped in a night it made for itself.

Because the dark here is not an absence. It is a product. For longer than the grooves are deep, the living machinery of this place has been exhaling — fine soot, mineral fume, the slow smoke of ten million patient metabolisms — until the sky filled and sealed. There is a ceiling now, a continental lid of haze, and above it there is presumably a sun, though the sun has become a rumor. Twice each long cycle the gloom leans perceptibly grey, a smudge of lighter dark crawling across the ceiling: the ash-noon. Some of the old vegetation still tilts toward it, remembering. Everything else keeps time by other means.

Very rarely — a few times in a season — the ceiling tears. A shaft of actual light falls through, unpracticed and enormous, and for the length of a held minute the Bench has color: rust-red aprons of weathered gravel, black basalt benches stepping down like the seats of an amphitheater, verdigris stains around the seep-hollows, the dull gleam of a bloomfield flashing back. Then everything happens at once. The herds throw their heads up. The seep-hollows boil with movement. Things that have never once hurried, hurry. It is the only fast hour this world has. And then the ceiling knits itself closed, the light dies upward, and the shadows — because that is what the shaft made, the only shadows there ever are — stretch, lean, and die with it. On the Bench, shadows are weather. They arrive, they pass, and nothing casts them twice.

The land itself is old in a particular way: not crumbling, not sharp, but *finished*. Broad terraces of dark basalt step across the horizon in long, rounded benches, every edge worn, every slope settled into the angle it intends to keep. Nothing here is jagged except the strike-glass, and the strike-glass doesn't last — the crews of the small find it within days and begin the work of rounding it down. Give the Bench a decade and it will have softened even a lightning strike. The whole plateau reads as a form in equilibrium with everything that acts on it, a shape that has stopped arguing. It is not stagnant. It is *practiced*.

Some of the rounding is weather. Much of it is chewing.

This is the thing the Bench does instead of having industry: it *is* industry, run as biology, and it has never built a single artifact. Nothing here is made. Things are grown, worn, excreted, exhaled. The roads are not roads — they are the grooves, migration tracks worn ten and twenty meters into solid basalt by the passage of feet across a hundred thousand years, canyon-systems of pure repetition, kilometers wide where the routes braid. Walk one and the walls rise smooth on either side, polished by the flanks of the dead.

The feet belong to the smelter kine, and the smelter kine are the engines of the world.

They are vast, low, and slow — a body like a furnace wearing armor. They graze the ore-crusts and rust-crusts that spread thin across the benches, and in the long cathedral of the gut they do what a foundry does: they burn, reduce, concentrate. Waste metal migrates out through the shell and plates there, so that an old kine carries a carapace of slag and native iron, furnace-black, cracked along the seams. Those seams glow. Not brightly — the Bench permits nothing brightly — but with the dull red of coals seen through a door left ajar, and a herd at rest is a field of low embers scattered across the dark. Around the body, great radiator fins stand up off the spine to bleed the heat away, and because the fins are the coolest thing on a warm animal in thin damp air, they sweat. Dew beads on the iron vanes of a sleeping kine and runs down the channels of the shell, and in the rest-hour the small things of the plateau come to the herd to drink. The engines are also the water points. Nobody designed this. It is simply what happens when a furnace learns to graze.

A herd moves in phase. This is uncanny the first time you feel it, and you do feel it — a thousand leg-beats merged into a single slow stroke, the herd walking as one distributed piston the size of a valley, thump, thump, thump, each stroke separated by long seconds of silence. It does not carry through the air. The air here is too thin and too felted with soot to carry much of anything, and sound dies young on the Bench. It carries through the *ground*. Everything on this world with bones listens through its feet, and the gait of a distant herd arrives in your ribs long before the herd exists as anything else. When a herd finally stops to rest, the dark around it fills with a small sound, delicate and industrial: ticking. The tick of cooling iron as the carapaces contract. An engine shed after shift, cooling in the dark.

Nothing runs, on the Bench. Running costs more air than anyone owns.

The kine molt their plates as they grow, and they die, eventually, out on the routes, and both of these things are harvests. Where a carcass lies, or where a generation has shed, the ground becomes a bloomfield: acres of slag, nodules of bloom-iron, half-refined metal weathering back toward ore. And then the grit arrives. The grit is the collective name for the crews of the small — rock-gnawing, metal-nibbling swarms, numberless in aggregate and invisible in any given glance, who dismantle carcass and molt and strike-glass and dung and carry every atom back into circulation. A dead kine becomes a field of clean ingots in a season. The ingots weather; the crusts re-form; the herds return along the grooves and graze what their grandparents were. The loop is closed and has been closed for so long that the closing itself is a landform.

Above it all, in the haze and just beneath it, the drift-eels ride the convection — long gas-cell bodies, buoyant, trailing charged filaments like the seine nets of a fisherman. They graze the ceiling where the soot forms, never winning, only pruning. And they are the lightning. Their discharges are the bright events of this world: a sudden arc flowering under the ceiling, sheet-light rolling across miles of haze, silhouetting the terraces for an instant and printing the afterimage on every eye below. The strikes do more than illuminate. Each one cooks the thin air into nitrates, and the warm rain — when it comes, and it comes only sometimes — carries the fertilizer down to the fog-combs and the felt. The electrical grid feeds the soil. The soil feeds the crust. The crust feeds the engines. The engines feed the sky. Ask what the refinery produces and the answer is: itself. Air, warmth, metal, more refinery.

The fog-combs stand in sparse groves wherever the wind is steady: black fins, delicate as baleen, combing the moving air for its wet. Moisture here is mostly not fallen but *harvested* — the damp rides the wind, the combs strip it, and it drips into hollows at their roots, where it collects as dew ponds: black water, warm, mineral-heavy, skimmed with an oxide film that goes briefly, heartbreakingly iridescent when an arc lights the ceiling. Between the groves and the seeps there is nothing for very long stretches, and that nothing is also the Bench — the truest part of it, maybe. This is a sparse world that happens to be crowded. The emptiness between presences is measured in days of walking, and then a seep-hollow will be so thick with life you cannot set a foot down honestly. The abundance is real. It is simply dilute, poured across a continent, and it comes in pulses: a seep teems, a herd passes like a weather front, the sky flowers with arc-light — and then the long quiet returns, and the plateau goes back to being mostly space.

Every seep has its still.

The stills are the predators, if predator is even the word for something that does so little. A still finds a water-hollow or a herd-route and stops, and waits, and waits — weeks, seasons — patient past the point where patience is a virtue and into the point where it is a landform. They sense hearts through the ground, the small electric stutter of a body drinking, and when they finally move it is one movement, already finished. They space themselves like fence posts of appetite, one to a seep, rare as lighthouses and as fixed. The ecology gives them what the ecology can spare, which in the thin-air economy of the Bench is the slow, the old, the unlucky. Nothing flees a still at speed. Nothing does anything at speed. Except during the tears.

Listen, in the ordinary dark, and you will learn the taxonomy of almost-nothing. The tick of cooling iron. The drip of a comb-grove into its hollow, unhurried, out of rhythm with itself. A faint aeolian tone when the wind combs through the fins at the right angle — the nearest thing this world has to music, thin as a hair. Far off, sometimes, a boom that you feel more than hear: a great discharge, or a slab of carapace letting go, or the deep house-warming of the plateau's own heat shifting its weight. The herds' slow piston-stroke in the soles of your feet. The grit, if you kneel and put your ear to a bloomfield: a sound like the memory of rain, which is the sound of the world being taken carefully apart and put back into itself.

It keeps its own time, this place. Not days — there is no day, only the grey rumor of ash-noon — but pressure-seasons in the ceiling, migration-years counted in herd-passings, the slow calendars of molt and bloom. Even the warmth migrates: the deep heat beneath the Bench is not fixed, and where it gathers the ground blooms warmer, and life thickens there, and over decades the bloom drifts as the plumbing far below rearranges itself — oases that move slower than empires, trailing their groves and herds and stills behind them across the dark.

Nothing here is new, and nothing is finished. The stillness is not stagnation; it is a balance so practiced it looks like rest. The engines graze. The grid arcs overhead. The ceiling holds. Somewhere out in the dark a herd is walking in phase, one slow stroke at a time, wearing the road a fraction deeper, and the road will be there in a hundred thousand years, and so will the herd.

Stand still a while. Let your feet learn to listen.
