From one of the high domes at noon, the country resolves into three things: light, distance, and the dark islands of the living.

The light first. It falls hard and white from a sun that seems nearer than a sun should be, through air too thin to soften it, until the salt pans below flare like hammered tin and every stone throws a shadow blue as ink. The sky at zenith is not blue but a deep transparent indigo, and in it, even at midday, the brightest stars hold their places — day-stars, small and steady. There is no haze except where the dust is moving, and the dust is always moving somewhere. For all that light, the air stays cold. The sun warms the skin and not the wind — the old high-altitude paradox — and the nights are bitter enough to split stone. They do split stone. You can hear it.

Then the distance. The plateau runs beyond the curve of the world in every direction: ranges worn smooth as river stones though no river has touched them in a hundred million years, basins wide enough to hold weather of their own, passes like gates between provinces of emptiness. From the highest domes the horizon visibly bends. Nothing interrupts it except the storms — slow continents of dust, amber and bone-colored, tall as the ranges, trailing veils, taking days to cross a basin. They are the oceans now. They have the oceans' moods and the oceans' unhurried traffic.

And then, scattered across all that bright barrenness like something dropped and not yet reclaimed: the islands. Dark at this distance, soft-edged, days apart. The felt.

Descend, and the basin floors announce what they were. The pans are the floors of a sea that left — ivory, blinding, cracked into polygons ten paces across, each plate's edges curled and ridged where the salt has crept and re-crept over the bones of the seabed. Around the rims, the old shorelines persist: wave-cut benches high on the slopes, tier on tier, precise as masonry, and dry cataracts pouring over them in single dark streaks — falls that have not fallen in ages. Water survives here only as a memory held in crystal: a few molecules locked in the lattices of certain salts, bound since the seas died, never liquid, never free. When the ocean went — uplift, thinning air, the slow leak of a world's sky — the life of this place did not go with it. It changed its mind about what it was. It gave up swimming, gave up drinking, and learned to eat the sky's leavings.

Everything since has been rounded, planed, polished by the one remaining tool. Ventifacts stand in the passes like chess pieces, faceted and grooved by ten thousand years of wind each. The dust itself has been ground for eons, finer than flour, so dunes move like slow smoke and anything pressed into it fills back like a cut in cream. The land is finished changing its mind. Only its surface still moves.

Up close, the islands are lush in a way that seems impossible until you touch them. The felt is a guild of many growths knitted into one body: a springy, waxy mat, knee-deep in the young patches, waist-deep in the old, pewter-silver on its upper face where it armors itself against the light, and beneath, in the sheltered underlayers, the working colors — wine, copper, oxblood, a deep absorbing black-green. It does not drink. It eats dust. Each grain that settles on it is gripped by cilia and passed down through the mat in a slow relay, dissolved at last in a solvent that is not water: a heavy golden oil, closer to resin than to rain, which never freezes and almost never evaporates and which the felt guards under waxy skins the way other worlds guard wells. At dawn, in the deep cold, the lightest fraction of that oil condenses on the mat as a glittering rime — sap-frost, the only dew this world knows — and by mid-morning it has sunk back inside. The felt's chemistry runs cool and unhurried; a patch may take a full season to swallow one storm's gift. But where it is established it is luxuriant: layered, many-colored, fruiting, dense with a patience that has nowhere to hurry to.

Rising from the older islands are the spars. A spar begins as a felt-growth that starts keeping its own dead: instead of sloughing its digested mineral, it lays it down beneath itself, crystal by crystal, building a trellis of ivory and honey-colored stone, and grows up over it. The living tissue is only ever a skin. What looks like a tree is a lineage — sixty, a hundred thousand years of one family standing on its own accumulated ancestry, the eldest spars thick as towers, fluted, hollowed by wind into organs. The groves gather at the passes and in the dust-shadow of ranges, where storms reliably drop their loads. Between groves: three hundred kilometers, five hundred, of bare bright stone and pan and dune, empty of everything but wind. That emptiness is not a gap in the world. It is most of the world. The islands are punctuation; the sentence is space.

The weather is a closed cycle of dust, complete as any water cycle. By day the sun on the dark felt drives thermals, and dust devils — the only hurried things in this country — spin across the pans in loose columns. By night, cold dense air slides off the high domes in katabatic rivers, audible long before it arrives, carrying dust downslope and laying it in the lees: dust-fall, dry and silent as snow, an inch by morning. In season the great storms stand in the basins for days, dry lightning flickering inside them, charge building in the parched air until the dust itself sparks, the thunder arriving not as sound but as a thump felt in the sternum. Then comes the calm — and the calm is when the felt fruits.

The timing is exact. Groves and felts release their spores into the settling air so they land in fresh dust, fertile the instant they touch down. The release is the world's one extravagance: for hours, sometimes days, the air fills with silver motes, each spore a fleck of mirror, and the whole plateau glitters as if lit from inside. It happens once or twice a year and is worth a year. The spores ride the katabatic streams down through the passes, which is why the groves stand where they stand: the passes are the rivers of this world, and their water is wind.

The animals are few, and a sighting is an event to be remembered. The stonebacks are the great presence: low, broad, slate-armored beasts the size of boulders, their plates grown from the same mineral their ancestors ate. They graze the felt's silver face, and the felt tolerates them — needs them, in fact, because stonebacks are the long-distance vector. Spores lodge in the seams of their plates and ride for years. A stoneback's life is measured in journeys and its journeys in seasons: island to island across the bare pans, straight as a ruled line, three years between groves, sleeping out storms beneath its own shell. They live for centuries and nothing hunts them. When one dies, the carapace remains — a dome of ready mineral on open stone — and the spores find it, and within a hundred years the carcass is a groveling, a new island at its birth.

Higher up ride the mantles: translucent drifters an acre across, faintly iridescent, filtering spores and dust from the thin streams of the upper air. A mantle's shadow crossing a pan is the nearest thing this world has to an eclipse. Mostly they feed on the sky itself, but they are not above hunting — a mantle will fold out of the heights onto a felt-shrew, and the shrews, fist-sized and warm and quick, the felt's darting tenants of the underlayers, are the only hurried animals alive. That is the whole web: dust to felt, felt to shrew and stoneback, shrew to mantle, mantle in the end to a long fall and a soft incorporation. Nothing rots here. The dead are filed in dust, layer upon layer, and in time they are reissued.

Sound is a local event. The thin air tires of carrying it, and the catalog of what remains is short: the tick and groan of cooling stone at dusk; the hiss of dust on the move, like sand poured inside a wall; the low fluting when wind combs the hollow spars — a chord of organ-tones you must be standing inside a grove to hear, the only music, played rarely and to no one; the creak and slow breath of a stoneback; the approach of the night-wind, heard minutes before it arrives. Beneath it all, a silence so complete that the body's own machinery becomes the loudest presence. The plateau listens through its bones, and so does everything on it: the felt feels footfalls through the ground days before anything arrives.

The light runs the day like a regimen. Dawn: long blue shadows, sap-frost glittering and sinking back into the mat. Noon: the silvering, when the felt turns its reflective faces upward and the pans flare and every color goes flat under the white hammer. Dusk: a brief amber hour, the world's shadow rising up the eastern sky like a tide. Night: bitter cold, and above it the stars do not twinkle — there is not enough air for that. They burn steady and numberless, the galactic band bright enough to cast a faint shadow, and now and then a mantle drifts across it, briefly unstarring a piece of the sky.

The rules, once learned, are total. Dust is the water. What falls from the sky is fertility; what flows downhill is dust; the passes are rivers and their water is wind. Height is not grown but built — every spar stands on its ancestors. Life keeps to the band between the scouring heights and the pickled deeps. There is no straight line anywhere but a crystal's face and a stoneback's track, and nothing made by anything but time and appetite. Sound is local. Distance is the medium. Time is slow except for weather, and weather is the slow thing made visible.

Dusk on a pass. The world's shadow climbs the sky. A grove begins to tone as the night-wind finds it — one low chord, unhurried, unwitnessed. Below, on the fresh dust of last week's fall, a stoneback's track runs out across the pan toward the next island, straight as a ruled line, three years of walking in it. The storm on the horizon will arrive or it won't. The felt will be ready either way. The world lost its ocean and made a slower one of air and stone, and it has been at peace with the terms for longer than peace has needed a name.
