The dark here is not an absence. It is a medium — warm, saturated, close as the inside of a mouth. It has depth and you can feel the depth: distances register in the hum that comes up through your boots and the slow drift of wet air against your face. This dark is continent-sized. It has weather of its own, and the weather is moisture moving through the body of a single organism, because the organism is the continent.

There is no ground. There is ankle-deep black water, warm as blood, lying over a felt of roots so densely woven it rebounds underfoot like a mattress. The felt gives, and holds, and gives. Set into it, breaking its surface like knuckles, are nodules of grown metal — iron, copper, magnetite — plated out of the warm brine by the roots themselves, and they are sharp. Everything here is sharp. The nodules are angular, crystalline, unworn, because they are always new: the land is young and it never gets older. Where the felt opens, it opens into sumps — vertical wells of hotter water, convection cells breathing heat upward at a constant rate, and the water laps the rim of the wells at a constant level. Stand still in the shallows and you feel a faint tingle across the skin of your ankles. The water conducts. You are standing in the electrolyte of a battery the size of a world.

Listen. The catalogue of sound is short and it never changes. Condensation drips from an unseen roof — sourceless, steady, the same drops forever. Inside the trunks, check-valves click like a heartbeat slowed to geologic patience. Press your ear to a bole and you hear water hissing upward under pressure, lifted against gravity in a land too flat for rivers — the slope here is measured in centimeters per hundred kilometers, so the rivers run inside the wood, uphill, downhill, direction a rumor. Beneath everything, the hum: infrasonic, below hearing, felt in the sternum, in the teeth, in the jelly of the eyes. No call. No rustle of anything that hunts or flees. Nothing here moves toward you or away from you. There is movement everywhere and behavior nowhere — process without a single witness.

The understory is a pump-forest. The trunks are standpipes, laminated in alternating conductive and insulating layers like the cores of transformers, and among them hang the pressure bladders: translucent pods swollen tight with warm water, pulsing slowly with the valving cycle. Deep inside each pod, if your eyes are dark-adapted enough to be trusted, sits the faintest coal of red light — the only light for kilometers, so dim you doubt it, brightening and fading by a shade as the pod fills and empties. The water goes up to the roof and rains back down through every level of the structure, an interior rain that falls at a constant rate and has always fallen at that rate. It is not weather. It is plumbing that has become indistinguishable from weather.

Higher, the works proper. Vines run trunk to trunk in catenaries so regular they look surveyed — nothing surveyed them; growth followed the field lines, and the field lines do not move. Bundles of conductor-vine, thick as your torso, sag in perfect curves between the boles. At intervals the bundles swell into nodes of amber resin that store charge: approach one and your hair rises and you taste copper. The junction thickets tick in slow overlapping patterns, routing current around the continent, and the ticking is constant — a texture like rain that never begins and never stops. The power comes from below. The crust is young, thin, warm; the roots go down into hot brine and the fins go up into damp air, and every plant is a cell, and the whole of it discharges through its own body and recharges from the deep heat at exactly the rate it spends. No hand assembled any of it. Nothing here was built. It grew the way a crystal grows, into the only shape its laws permit — and then, having reached that shape, it stopped. Selection did this, and then selection was finished. Its work was complete.

You notice the cleanliness only slowly, and then it frightens you. There is no litter. No rot. No deadwood, no duff, no loose soil, nothing fallen that is not already being reclaimed at the exact rate of its falling. In certain basins the roots have grown millstones of siliceous tissue that turn slowly, endlessly, grinding plated mineral back into ions so the grid can plate it out again elsewhere — matter going around and around like water in a fountain. Zero waste. A clean-room the size of a planet, kept at operating temperature. The smell is ozone and hot resin, wet greenness, a breath of sulfur from the young depths — and underneath it all the held tension of a thunderstorm that never breaks, because breaking would be change.

Above the pumps, more layers — strata of the one body, dozens of them, with fog banks drifting between the levels, weather happening indoors. The roof is a condenser: interlaced fins, pleated, waxy, perpetually wet, their edges sharp as fresh fracture. Beyond the roof there is a sky, in principle, but it gives nothing — no stars, only a warm low deck of cloud, featureless, dark. Weather exists up there but it has crystallized: convection cells the size of nations stand fixed in a hexagonal grid of upwellings and downwellings, permanent as grain in wood. You could map the climate once and the map would be true forever.

There is no day. There is no season. Time has exactly one marker, and it is called the pulse: a slow wave of heightened charge that travels the continent on a circuit of weeks, identical in every pass since the beginning. When the pulse enters your region the hum deepens, the pods brighten by a shade, the pumps quicken almost imperceptibly — and then it passes, and everything returns, and this is not change. It is a clock with a single tick, repeating. Where the wave forms standing patterns there are node-hollows of absolute silence, places where the hum cancels itself and your own blood becomes the loudest sound in the world; and there are antinode groves where the infrasonic pressure holds the black pools in permanent dappled patterns — ripples that stand still, fixed as hammered tin, wet sculpture. Trail your fingers through one and the pattern restores itself the instant your hand withdraws. It was never, in any way that matters, disturbed.

In the oldest capacitor thickets, deeper in the dark than you should go, a signal circulates — a pattern of charge looping and looping, eons old, neither decaying nor growing. Call it a memory. Call it a thought the biome thinks eternally without ever changing its mind. There is no one to read it, and perhaps nothing to read: a standing chord, the sound of an engine idling to itself.

And light — light is not a condition here. It is an event. On its own long schedule, as regular as everything, a region discharges: slow lightning crawling branch to branch through the works for hours, deliberate, the grid exhaling a surplus it has no use for. For those hours the world is unveiled — the colonnades of standpipe trunks, the strung bundles sagging between them, the black mirror of the floor, the finned roof far overhead — all of it sudden and silver and utterly indifferent — and then the dark closes again like water, and nothing in the dark has changed for having been seen.

Nothing here regards you. No eyes have ever opened in this place; there is no niche for an animal and never has been. You are safe the way you are safe standing beside a clock — the machinery is vast and it will never once take you into account. It built nothing and was built by no one. It grew, and it finished growing, and what ended here was not life but becoming. What remains is being: warm, wet, layered, humming, at idle, in the dark.

If it needs a name, call it the Works. Call it the Idling.

Press your palm flat to a trunk. Warmth. The slow certain pulse. Water climbing inside the wood toward a sky that gives nothing back. And rising up your arm with the hum, the knowledge, simple and unbearable and calm: it will be exactly this, exactly this, exactly this, for as long as the deep heat lasts — and the deep heat will last.
