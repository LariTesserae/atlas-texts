You hear the Basin before you see it, and for a long time the sound has no explanation. From the rim road it rises out of the haze below as a single sustained pressure — not any one noise but the sum of all noise, the way a waterfall is total. Then the road crests and switches down, and the place opens under you: a great sunken bowl, kilometers across, its far shore only a rumor in the vapor, its floor a patchwork of water and gleaming mud strung with the lights of machines. The lights burn at midday. They have to.

The Basin holds its own weather. Warm water pumped up from the deep workings meets the cooler air above the ponds, and the moisture condenses into a permanent low ceiling, a lid of gray that never quite lifts. Noon here looks like the hour before dusk elsewhere. Shadows are rare and soft. The sun is a coin of slightly paler gray pressed into the overcast. The sodium lamps on the gantries and pump houses burn around the clock, smeared by the haze into orange blurs, so that from the rim the whole valley floor looks like a ship lit at anchor. The air itself is wet enough to drink. It smells of wet lime, diesel, the ammonia of the bird colonies, the green reek of algae, rust, warm stone.

The ground is the youngest thing in the valley — younger than the machines that made it, younger than some of the frogs singing from it. This is a reclamation fill: slurry from the dredges pours out of the discharge mains in a gleaming rope and spreads into numbered cells, fanning into deltas of raw silt. Every age of ground is on display at once. Fresh slurry, liquid and glistening, that would take a man to the waist. Week-old crust that bears a boot for one second, then weeps water up around the laces. Year-old mud that holds a plank road, ringing faintly hollow over voids. Older fill skinned with white salt and a film of algae. Stamp your foot and the surface answers with a slow traveling shudder, a wave moving through ground that has not decided to be solid. In a single wet season you can watch bedding form — fine laminae of silt and sand sorting themselves in the settling ponds. Geology on a work schedule. The Basin is not eroding; it is accreting, and it wears its unfinishedness openly, in survey stakes and marker flags and the long straight welts of the pipelines.

Water is not a feature here. It is the medium. The cells step down from the discharge faces in a decant ladder: milk-white where the slurry enters, then jade, then near-clear, the clear water lifted at last over the rim by the big pumps. The Basin keeps the mud and gives the water back. Because the feed comes up from the deep workings, it arrives at blood temperature, and steam stands off the ponds in the cool mornings, thickening the haze. The water table sits a finger's width below the surface or a finger's width above it, and the distinction hardly matters. Land here is just water with mud suspended in it. The ditches run straight as rules between the cells, warm, brim-full, and every one of them is occupied.

The machines are few, enormous, and patient. The dredge is a floating factory with a bucket ladder that eats the bottom of the deep ponds and delivers the spoil to the stacker, which lays down new country in slow arcs at the fill face. The dredge advances about a meter an hour. Nobody hurries it. Pump houses squat on pontoons in the ponds, corrugated iron humming through the soles of your feet before you reach them. Slurry mains on low trestles web the cells like a distributed anatomy of pipe, valves sweating, flanges dripping. Transformers on stilted platforms sing one note each, all day, all night. A narrow-gauge line creeps along the causeways behind a small diesel that moves at walking pace, because there is no reason to move faster.

The rest of the built world is scattered thin and half-drowned, because the Basin has already buried two earlier drafts of itself. Under the clear water of Cell 9 you can see the old dike line as a pale seam. A pump house from the first phase stands to its eaves in Cell 6, herons nesting along its ridge. Fence lines walk down into the ponds and do not come out. At the far edge, where the subsidence took the road and the hamlet with it, a brick chimney stands alone out of Cell 4 with ibis nesting in the flue. On the rim road there is a benchmark post painted with dated rings — the sinking of the valley, decade by decade, recorded in descending stripes. The living infrastructure keeps to the margins: an office barge, a fuel berm, gauge towers, a canteen shed with one planted willow beside it and a hose run permanently to its roots. It is the only tree in the Basin. It is treated as a pet.

Vegetation otherwise barely exists, and what exists is mostly microscopic. The ground is too young, too salty, too frequently reburied. The oldest cells carry a stubble of reeds, shoulder-high, rattling in the wind off the ponds; the rim has scrub. Everything else is film and mat — algae that blackens and peels off the mud in sheets, bacterial growth at the warm inflows banded rust and cream and sulfur. Green is an event here. You notice each occurrence of it.

What the Basin lacks in plants it repays in animals, and this is where the valley's density lives. There are no empty places. There are only different densities of fullness. The frogs are beyond counting — at dusk the chorus rises until it has physical pressure, sectioned like an orchestra: the high sewing-machine trill of the little ones along the ditch edges, the deep jug-note of the big toads booming from the old cells. On the first warm nights of the season the toads cross the haul roads in moving sheets, and the traffic simply stops for them. Stilts and avocets stand shoulder to shoulder in the shallows. Ibis move over the fill in white weather-systems. Ducks raft in thousands on the clear cells, and herons picket every weir. There are eels in every ditch, carp mouthing the turbid margins, turtles stacked three deep on every pipe flange, snakes swimming with their heads up like periscopes. Midges rise in columns like smoke. Dragonflies work the steam. In the deep old cells live catfish the length of a man, pale from the dimness, that take ducklings from below. The fauna have claimed the machines too: nests in the dredge superstructure, birds riding the conveyor like a moving perch, frogs in the cabs. Nothing is unoccupied.

And over it all, the sound. The animal roar, the pump drone — a bass note felt in the teeth and the sternum rather than heard — the wet hiss of slurry at the discharge, the far-off clank of the buckets carrying for miles across open water. Speech is impossible at twenty paces. The crews talk with their hands and with lamp-flashes, and they locate the dredge by ear the way sailors locate a foghorn. Once or twice a year the pumps go down for maintenance, and the mechanical layer of the noise falls away, and the crews stop what they are doing and stand in the remaining wall of frog-song and bird-scream. That is the quietest the Basin ever gets. Nobody calls it silence.

Everything that matters here happens slowly. The dredge will take three years to cross a single cell. The ring dike rises a meter a decade. The fill consolidates on a thirty-year clock, grain settling against grain in the dark, deciding to be land. On the wall of the office barge hangs the plan map with a completion year that nobody now working will live to see. Against that immense patience, life is the quick skin on a slow animal: a fresh delta has birds within days, frogs within weeks, and then the pipes move on and bury it all, and the next cell begins.

The rules are simple and total. Flotation first: nothing rests on the ground until someone has calculated whether it will swim, and everything of value either floats or stands on stilts. Water level is law, read off the gauge boards each morning like scripture. Sound is the sense of distance; sight, rationed by the haze, is for close work only. And the Basin does not remember you. A footprint weeps full and smooths over within the hour. A road ungraded for a season goes back to pond.

At dusk — which arrives gently here, a slight thickening of a dimness that was never far away — the lamps come up across the cells in ones and twos, doubled in the water. The frog wall rises. The buckets of the dredge lift out of the far pond one every few seconds, streaming, a slow clock counting off the construction of a valley. A line of ibis crosses low, white against the gray, heading for the chimney in Cell 4. In thirty years this will be a plain — dry, quiet, green, unremarkable. But not yet. Now it is loud, and dim, and packed full, and unfinished: a landscape being born at the top of its lungs, in the dark, under water.
