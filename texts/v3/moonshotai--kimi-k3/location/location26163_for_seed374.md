**Vesper, the town that is always almost finished**

You hear it half an hour before you see it. Coming over the rim of the basin with the mist against your face, the sound arrives first — a roar with no event in it, steady as surf, made of ten thousand small voices and the ringing of stone. Then the fog thins and the basin opens below you: a wide, low bowl of pale ground, dim-lit, glowing faintly from within, its floor terraced in cream and ochre, its town crowded into the middle of it like embers gathered in a hearth. Lamps. Thousands of lamps, doubled in wet stone and dark water. And everywhere, the sound.

The light never changes here. This is the first rule of the world, and everything else follows from it. The sky is a low warm ceiling of cloud the color of wet ash, and along one horizon — always the same horizon, always at the same height — there is a band of dull ember-orange, a smolder that has never brightened and never faded in all the days anyone has counted. The people of the basin call that direction Sunward. Walk Sunward and the ground steams and the water scalds and no one comes back. Walk the other way, Darkward, and the mist turns to rime and the world ends in black ice. Between those two deaths runs a belt of warm wet dusk, and the towns are strung along it like beads on a cord, linked by canals, trading eel-fat and shell and copper and news. Vesper is one of them. It has always been evening in Vesper. Evening is simply what the world is made of.

The land is young — young in a way that has nothing to do with time. Nothing here weathers. Nothing erodes. The basin builds itself faster than anything can wear it down, because the water that rises through its floor comes up warm and freighted with dissolved stone, and wherever that water spreads and breathes, the stone comes out of it. The whole basin floor is a growing crust — terraces of travertine lipping over themselves like slow foam, aprons of sinter, rims of pearl-gray tufa around every spring-mouth, all of it raw, fresh, sharp-edged, newborn. Stand still long enough on the low terraces and you can watch it happen: a wet sheen thickening, a rim gaining the width of a fingernail over a season. The ground is not a thing here. It is a process.

And there is nothing green. No leaf, no blade, no stem, no moss. The light is too thin for that, and there has never been soil for it anyway — only stone, water, and the dark living slick that coats everything the water touches. This is the mat: films and felts and veils of simple life, fed not by light but by the chemistry of the warm springs, by sulfur and iron and whatever else the deep water carries up. In the hottest channels it hangs in orange and rust-red banners. In the cool canals it lies black and glossy as poured fat. In the seep-walls of the town it is gray, felted, faintly sweet-smelling. It is the beginning of every meal in the basin, and it is everywhere, and it is the closest thing this world has to a field.

Out of the mat comes everything else. Snails the size of a fist graze it in slow constellations. Eels work the canals, thick as a forearm, and every household keeps a weir-basket at its outflow. Shrimplike things cloud the warm basins; larvae skim the still pans; and the vesper-frogs — the vesper-frogs are beyond counting. In a world where it is always evening, the evening chorus never ends. They sing from every rim and roof and canal-edge, tens of thousands of them, a shrill bell-chorus braided with the dry sawing drone of the winged things that swarm the lamps. Wading birds stalk the terraces, gray and patient, and roost shoulder-to-shoulder on every flat roof in town, and their clatter and bark is a third weather over the other two. In the pens below the terraces the mud-calves lie huffing — huge amphibious grazers, placid as boulders, fattened on harvested mat, giving meat and hide and the rendered fat that burns in every lamp in Vesper. The town swims in animals. They are underfoot, overhead, in the water, in the walls. The people are simply the loudest of them.

Because it is loud. It is deafening. The frogs and the drone and the birds are only the floor of it. Over that comes the water — a million small water-sounds, seep and trickle and drip and bubble, steam hissing at the vent-mouths — and over the water, never ceasing, dayless and nightless, the chisels. Stone rings under copper-edged tools on every street, in every district, a scattered, interlocking ticking and clanging, like a city of bellfounders all tuning at once. Add the bellow of mud-calves, the clatter of sledge-runners on wet sinter, and the people — the people of Vesper have evolved a culture of shouting, because nothing quieter survives. Market prices are bellowed. News is bellowed. Gossip is bellowed. Between districts, through the fog, messages go by horn-code, long moans and stutters off the tower platforms, and the hours are struck on great bells because there is no sun to read. Children are born into the roar and sleep through it like a lullaby. Travelers from the canal-towns get headaches for a week. And the one luxury in Vesper, the one true wealth, is silence: deep cellar-rooms, hush-rooms, walled in thick stone below the water table, where the roar thins to a hum and people go to remember what their own thoughts sound like. When a spring shifts and some district's frog-chorus falters — when a pocket of actual quiet opens in the town — people come out of their houses and look at each other. Silence here is not peace. Silence is an omen.

The town itself is dense as a held fist. The houses crowd terrace over terrace, sharing walls, near-touching across alleys so narrow that two sledges cannot pass, bridged and re-bridged overhead until whole districts are roofed in a second ground-floor of stone slab, and you can cross a quarter of Vesper without ever coming down to the street. And there is no wood. Not a beam, not a plank, not a handle — there has never been a tree in this world. The town is built of what the basin gives: fresh-cut tufa, soft as cheese when quarried and hardening in the air; unfired clay; bone and hide and sinew; shell; felted mat-fiber for rope and cloth and caulking; and copper, native copper, cold-hammered into chisel-edges and bowls and the bells. A fired clay pot is an heirloom — fuel is too precious for kilns. Doors are hides. Tools are stone and bone. Cook-fires burn eel-fat and dried mat and dung-cake, smoky and rank, though most cooking is done the cheap way, in the springs themselves, pots sunk in the hot outflow, dinners poaching all day in mineral water.

But the strangest thing — the thing that makes Vesper itself — is how the town is made. They do not only build it. They grow it.

A wall in Vesper begins as a frame: hide stretched on bent bone, a clay mold, a wattle of mat-rope. Then the builders divert a seep over it — spring water, stone-heavy, guided down clay gutters — and they keep it wet for a season, and the water lays its stone down on the form, grain by grain, until the frame is a wall. Construction here is irrigation. A house is watered like a crop. And the water does not stop when the builders stop, which is why the chisels never stop. Doorways creep narrower year by year; windows film over; a drain left untrimmed for a week seals itself; a house abandoned for a year is swallowed — you can see them in the old low districts, houses gone to pale bulges with a lintel showing like a bone in flesh. So every household trims its frontage as a daily duty, the way other towns might sweep. The great public labor of Vesper is not building but restraint: cutting the town back out of the stone that keeps trying to finish it, keeping the spring-mouths open, keeping the streets from closing like healing wounds. The scaffolds are cribbed stone. The tools never rest. The town rings like a quarry, forever, and it is never, ever done.

This has been going on for as long as anyone has counted. The archives — clay tablets, stacked in the hush-cellars — go back through hundreds of bell-counted generations, and the oldest plans scratched on them show the same skyline, the same terraces, the same tower-footings, the same canals. Vesper has always been almost finished. It is a town in a state of permanent arrival, an embryo that never completes its birth — and the people have made a doctrine of it, because what else can you do. They say a finished thing is a dead thing. Every house keeps one wall deliberately rough, unsmoothed, still weeping — the growing wall — and to smooth it would be the worst luck imaginable. Nothing in Vesper is allowed to end. Everything moves — water, chisels, frogs, crowds, sledges, smoke — and nothing changes. The motion runs in circles and returns to the same shape, like water through a pipe, like a standing wave in a channel. The roar is the same roar your grandmother heard. The light is the same light. The town is the same town, recut every single day.

Time, such as it is, is kept by bells and water-clocks, and the people sleep in shifts called wakes, so that at any hour some third of the town is up and trimming and cooking and shouting. The public baths are the heart of everything — great warm terraced pools where the whole shift soaks at the end of its wake, loud with talk, the water milky with stone. The dead are given back to the basin: laid out in the seep-gardens up on the rim, where the stone-heavy water flows over them, and over years they accrete into pale figures, kept, legible, visited. Families go up to stand before their stone-kin in the frog-noise and the mist, and no one finds this sad. It is only the town's own process, applied to the town's own people. Everyone in Vesper is under construction, one way or another, from birth to stone.

Down on the outflow flats, where the canals leave the basin and wander Darkward toward the cold, the water goes black and slow and the mat hangs thick as cloth, and the coracles of hide come and go to the sister towns up and down the belt. That is the whole known world: a string of pale, loud, lamplit towns in an endless warm evening, each one ringing with chisels, each one almost finished.

Stand on the rim at any hour and look back, and Vesper glows up at you through its own breath — wet white stone, ember lamplight, the smoldering band on the Sunward horizon that has never moved. Somewhere below, a chisel strikes. Ten thousand frogs answer. The town is arriving. It has been arriving forever. It will never arrive, and no one who lives there would have it any other way.
