There is a field, ordinary in every regard — grass, thistle, a leaning fence wire — and in the field a stone the size of a skull, and inside the stone, or just beneath it (the distinction has not yet been decided), a room.

It is a hand's depth down. Or a fathom. The measurement depends on how you arrive, and nothing arrives. The only door is a hairline crack, and the only things that pass through it are water going in and light going out. This is the whole traffic of the place, and it is enough.

The room is a cave that has not happened yet.

You must understand: everywhere else in the world, stone is worn away to make space. Here the process runs the other way. The chamber is not being carved but *accreted* — grown inward, knitted, assembled grain by bright grain from some blueprint no one is reading aloud. The floor is still sketching itself. Ridges of wet calcite rise a fraction each season, like sentences being written in a slow hand, and if you sat long enough — longer than anything here lives — you could watch a pillar commit to its own existence. The place makes a faint fizzing sound as it builds, like soda heard from another room. That, and one other sound, is nearly all it spends.

Because it is unfinished, it is lit by something impossible: its own dream of daylight. The stone has never seen the sun — it is new stone, prenatal — but it comes from a lineage of stone that was once surface, once warm, once stood in weather, and the memory is inherited the way eye color is. The walls exhale it. Brightness seeps from the rock the way damp seeps from rock elsewhere, pooling in hollows like skim milk, beading on the ceiling alongside the condensation, so that droplets of water and droplets of light hang together and sometimes merge, and a falling drop will occasionally land glowing and go dark only when something drinks it. The light is cool. It gives no warmth whatsoever. It is noon-colored, honeyed, abundant — and it is wrong, all wrong, two feet beneath a field at midnight.

The temperature never varies: the cool of well water, of root cellars, of a held breath.

The wetness is real but rationed. There is no pool, no stream — only a film over everything, thin as thinking, and the beads that gather on the ceiling until the ceiling is heavy, and then the rain. The rain here takes an hour to land. Drops let go and fall so slowly that they qualify as weather rather than event, and the small citizens treat them accordingly. Springtails ride them down, skating on the curved skin of the water, unconcerned, sometimes stepping off mid-descent into a leap that — gravity being one of the things this room has not finished deciding — carries them in a long drifting arc, a slow weather of floating springtails, before they come down somewhere unrelated to where they left.

Each droplet, once landed, is a pocket of water in the larger dryness, and each is inhabited. One droplet, one rotifer, turning in its bright globe like a thought being thought. A tardigrade crosses a droplet the way a bear crosses a valley — it takes days, and its eight footfalls are the softest thunder in the world. The fauna are abundant and the room is mostly empty, and both of these are true, because the emptiness is load-bearing. The species are many; the individuals are few; every presence has a great deal of room around it, the way a cathedral with three worshippers still holds a full choir of echoes.

The vegetation can be counted. A single root, come down from the grass above through the ceiling's one pore, hangs beaded and pale — the umbilical of the place. It drinks the chamber's moisture up into the tree by day and weeps it back by night, and the tree does not know what it is plugged into, though it may dream of it. Two fungal fans, ghost-white, grow where the light-leak is strongest; they metabolize the dream of daylight directly, and they are faintly warm, which makes them the only warm places in the world, and things come to rest against them the way things elsewhere come to fires. One slime veil, strung between a ridge and the floor, which is a plant that eats like an animal — drifting springtails stick to it and are absorbed slowly, and nobody here finds this strange, because the categories are not load-bearing. The categories are decorative.

The census, in brief: glass isopods, translucent, carrying beads of pooled light inside their bodies like lanterns they have swallowed; mites in copper and white; nematodes threading the film; the rotifers in their globes; the springtails; the tardigrades, unhurried and effectively immortal, the room's old souls. And one predator — a velvet worm, ancient of lineage even by the standards of the surface, soft as a finger, which hunts by casting threads of quick-setting slime. Here, where everything mineralizes, the slime-threads harden almost at once into bright cables, and an isopod that tears free leaves behind a perfect statue of itself in the glue. The floor is scattered with these small escapes. Nothing here erodes, so nothing is ever cleaned up. Every footprint fossilizes behind its maker; the floor is an archive being written faster than it is read, trackways of clay and calcite recording, in stone, walks that ended minutes ago.

Then there are the builders — a pallid, termite-like people, grain-layers, who carry spicules of bright calcite and set them in spirals. Their works are scattered and few: five or six spires, each a finger's height, or an ant's height; a low wall that stops; an arch that spans nothing; and, most strangely, a staircase of tiny beads descending into a droplet — a pier, apparently, for the rotifer within, built at a scale for citizens the builders are not. Nothing they make is ever finished. The spires never approach the ceiling. The arch closes around emptiness and halts, as if completion were a cliff edge everyone can see and no one steps toward. This appears to be a law of the place: nothing in the chamber has ever been completed, and everything in it is entire. It is suspected — by what, do not ask — that if one thing here were ever truly finished, everything else would have to finish too, all at once, and the room is not ready.

It is impossible to say where the builders' work ends and the room's own accretion begins, and that may be the answer: the room builds through them, the way a body builds through its cells. There are no tools here, nothing worked — everything is grown, secreted, deposited. The only artifacts are the monuments: the mineralized carcasses of ants.

For sometimes — once a season, once a decade — the hairline crack admits a citizen of the mythic overworld. An ant falls through: a meteor with opinions, a titan staggering in light that pools, armored, enormous, subject to rules it has never heard of. If it dies here, it becomes a mountain range that feeds the chamber for a generation, then petrifies where it lies, and the builders incorporate the carapace into their spirals. The room reckons its history in these events. Three ant-eras back, the mountains are still visible. The current era is young and the floor is smooth.

The silence is not empty; it is unspent. The room can afford perhaps one true sound per hour — a bead letting go of the ceiling, the tick of a ridge committing, the papery hush of isopod feet, which registers less as noise than as a texture of the quiet. Once in a very long while the whole chamber spends everything it has saved on a single low tone, one note, felt in the teeth of the tardigrades, and afterward the silence is deeper for a while, as if stunned.

The cycles keep it all moving: the slow tide of the light, brightening and dimming over days on no schedule the surface keeps — the room keeps yesterday's noon at midnight, a letter delayed. The breath of humidity through the crack, in and out, carrying in a spore, a mote, a rumor of the field. The rain-events. The hunts. The visible rising of the floor, so that a creature's whole life is lived on ground that grew beneath it, and geology here moves at a pace that life can watch, which may be the strangest thing of all.

And its size is as large as your attention. Look at a droplet and the droplet is an ocean with leviathans; look at the far wall and it is near; stop looking and it recedes into a country. The room has not decided whether it is a teapot or a cathedral, and it is under no pressure to decide.

Someday it will finish. One of two things will happen. The crack will widen — true erosion will begin, the childhood will end — and the chamber will open into an ordinary cave among caves, and the real sun will fall across the floor and find it dimmer than the dream. Or the accretion will complete its inward knitting and seal the room shut, and it will become a geode: a world preserved at the exact moment of finishing, its citizens mid-step in stone and light, the staircase into the droplet descending forever.

Until then it is a rehearsal — geology practicing, the world trying out the idea of a cave, bright and cool and nearly silent and full of small entire lives.

If you walk the field and stop at the right stone, and put your ear to the ground, and wait the hour the room requires, you will hear it: almost nothing. A faint fizz. A single tick. A cool breath against your ear, and possibly — though you will never be sure — a faint bright stain against your closed eyelids, as if something below were dreaming about the sun, and getting it nearly right.
