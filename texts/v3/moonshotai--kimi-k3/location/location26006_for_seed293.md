The rim comes first, though it barely deserves the name. Whatever once stood here — a caldera's lip, a housing, the foundation ring of some machine the size of a weather system — has been worn down across a span of time that doesn't bear thinking about into a necklace of low grassy swells, knee-high in places, chest-high at best, veined with nubs of fused glassy material that the rain polishes and the lichen eats at a rate of about a molecule a year. You can step over the rim without breaking stride. And from it you can take in the whole of the place, because it is small: a shallow basin a person could cross in under an hour, floored in green and open water, holding its few great presences wide apart, like furniture in a room that has been emptied on purpose.

The warmth meets you on the way down. It is not hot — it is the warmth of a held breath, of water left standing in sun, and it never leaves. Nothing here has ever frozen. The air is thick enough to drink, and everything drips, and the smell is wet peat and crushed green and, when the Engine is working, a faint clean sharpness like the moment before lightning.

The floor of the basin is not soil. It is the Floor — a single living mat, ankle-deep and spongy, one organism, clonal, unbroken, older than the rim, older perhaps than anything else alive. It is plant as substrate, plant as the very idea of ground: a dense wet felt of stems and rootlets and trapping hairs, and over it lies water in a warm sheet, inches deep, clear as tea, threaded with slow channels that wander and re-wander themselves over the course of weeks. The columns rise out of this. There are nine. They stand far apart — a hundred paces, two hundred — so that from any one of them you can see all the others, and the space between presences is itself the landscape: open water, open felt, glare, sky.

Each column is buttressed like a mangrove and fluted like a choir-stall, bark sweating, hollows at every junction holding warm still water where frogs raise their young. The columns are not separate trees. Their roots feed the Floor; the Floor feeds them; the nine of them are organs of the one being, towers of a single distributed body that has had a million years to settle into this, its final shape. And the water on their flanks runs the wrong way. Threads of it climb the fluting upward, slow as sap, weeping in reverse, drawn by the same means that does everything else here — means that current physics does not have clean words for.

Because the basin is a machine, or the machine has become a basin; the distinction dissolved a long time ago. The Engine is nowhere you can point to. It is in the Floor, in the columns, in the moisture of the air, and its medium is condensation. It writes in water. Watch a chosen patch of air above the sheet-water and you will see it: the mist thickening at a site, dew gathering into lines, lines knitting into a membrane, the membrane stiffening into a wall. By afternoon a pavilion stands there — wet ceramic-white, sweating, faintly warm — with arches and a stair that climbs to nowhere and a roof it never bothered with. It will stand for hours, or days. Then the Engine unwrites it: the edges soften, the thing slumps gracefully into its own rain, a downpour the size of a courtyard falling out of a clear sky, and is gone, and the frogs come.

This is the settlement: a hamlet's worth of structures at any given moment, never the same ones twice. A dozen, maybe — a bridge over a channel that will itself have moved by next month, a colonnade, a spiral stair, something like a greenhouse whose panes are living reed-flesh grown in an hour and digested back into the Floor by Thursday. The Engine builds from memory, and its memory is very long and no longer entirely reliable. It compiles architectures from eras stacked a million years deep, and it mis-remembers: a colonnade grafted to a bathhouse grafted to something no one would ever have built, and sometimes — this is the detail that stops visitors — it builds ruins directly. It compiles a wall already leaning, an arch already broken, stairs already worn by feet that never existed, as though what it remembers of the building is its ending. Where a new wall closes over a foundation the Floor hasn't finished digesting, you can see strata in the cut edge, layers of construction separated by days, an archaeology in fast-forward, the whole built world of the basin compressed into a depth of weeks.

The fauna have learned the grammar better than anyone. The basin is loud with them. Dragonflies work the sheet-water all day in the humid glare. Snails are everywhere, and their shells are sometimes compiled straight into the new walls, small spirals fossilized on a Tuesday. Little fish flick through the channels on generations so short the channels outlive species. And there is a heron — tall, slate-grey, unhurried — that has learned to read the signs of an unwriting before it comes. It stands at a doomed pavilion, motionless, an hour early, waiting for its personal rainstorm and the stunned small life the rainburst brings down. The frogs time their choruses to the bursts; at dusk, after a day of building and dissolving, the whole basin detonates into song, and the sound off the water and the rim comes back doubled, and for an hour the place is genuinely, almost unbearably loud.

Under everything, always, the Chord: the Engine's held note, felt in the teeth more than heard, rising a half-step when it writes, falling when it releases. And over it, the tick and hiss of condensation forming, the patter of the perpetual drip, and two or three times a day the sudden local roar of a courtyard-rain where a building used to be, with a small perfect rainbow standing in the ordinary sunlight afterward. The light, mercifully, is just light. The strangeness here is all in the matter.

Almost everything in the basin is less than a week old, and the basin is the oldest thing in the world. That is the trick of it — the resolution it reached so long ago: it holds its final form by endless rapid replacement, a fountain whose shape never changes though no drop in it is ever the same twice. The Floor creeps visibly. The channels migrate. Walls condense out of clear air and rain themselves away. Nothing is discarded; everything is re-read — the matter of a dissolved tower drawn up as mist and compiled as a heron's nesting arch next season. Almost nothing enters but sunlight and air; nothing leaves. Once in a while the Engine compiles something that is not architecture at all — an animal, a memory-fauna, a creature matching no taxonomy of the living world, which stands a day in the wet warmth while the herons ignore it, and then goes back to rain. No one is sure whether these are errors or sentences.

There are no people. There has been no one to build for in longer than the Engine can reliably remember, which is very long indeed. And yet it keeps the settlement going — bridges, stairs, rooms open to the warm sky — a town with no citizens, a garden kept for itself, anthill energy in the service of nothing but continuation. Stand at dusk on the one dry hummock, blood-warm water sheeted out to the nine dark columns, the frog-song rising, the Chord under your feet climbing a half-step as, somewhere behind you, the mist begins to thicken into tomorrow's brief architecture — and you understand that this place finished becoming itself eons ago, and has spent all the time since saying so, over and over, in a grammar of walls and rain, and has not yet tired of the sentence.
