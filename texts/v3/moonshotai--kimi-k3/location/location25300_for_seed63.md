High on a dead volcano, in a country where it has not rained since before memory, the slope cups inward into a bowl a few hundred paces across. The world outside is bright — white sun on black clinkstone, heat shimmering off barren talus — but the bowl holds its own night. A canopy of ash and exhaled fume stands above it, held aloft by the column of warm air rising from the vents below: the Roof. It has never cleared. Under the Roof it is always dark, always hot, and the dark is old.

You could walk the rim in an hour. Nothing here hurries.

The first thing a visitor notices is that the dark has substance. It is not the blue, star-pricked dark of open night but a mineral dark, smelled as much as seen: sulfur, hot dust, a sharp ammonia note near the vent-spires, and under it all a faint sweetness that comes and goes with the seasons of the place. The air is thin — this is high country — and hot as fever, and it lies in layers that a body learns quickly: cooler spills sliding down from the rim, a stifling pooled heat at knee height, rising columns above the vents where unseen things are always circling. Deep in the cracks of the floor, if you let your eyes adjust for a long time, there are threads of dull red, ember-light, felt on the skin more than seen. Otherwise the only sky is a narrow notch in the rim where the rock has fallen away, and through it, stars.

The floor of the bowl is not stone. It is the Felt: a continuous living hide that carpets everything — floor, talus, the lower flanks of the walls — tessellated into scales and pore-mouths and clusters of siphons that sip the mineral breath seeping up from below. It is not a plant, not a fungus. It is an animal, or a nation of animals joined flesh to flesh, and it may be that the whole bowl is a single resident with many tenants. It is warm. It gives slightly underfoot. It flinches from a footstep and then, slowly, closes over the print. Where the rising gas is richest, the Felt grows upward into chimneys and organ-pipe spires that stand breathing in the dark, some taller than a person, their tops rounded by centuries of ash-blast. Nothing here is raw. The rockfalls from the rim are old and felted into smooth humps; the tallest spire fell in half long ago and lies as the Fallen Chimney, its broken edges worn soft, its hollow length a den. Beneath the living skin lies reef-ground — the compacted shells and carapaces of all the dead, meters deep, the whole bowl floored with its own history. The Felt digests its fallen from below and builds the next layer of the world out of them.

The mountain breathes, and the bowl keeps that calendar. On a cycle of many days the vent-flow strengthens and slackens, pulled, some say, by the same moon that slides past the notch. At high breath the warm gas surges, the spires exhale in steady columns, and the Felt blooms — thickening, flushing, throwing up fresh folds. At low breath the whole place tightens and pales and waits. Everything that lives here knows the difference the way a farmer knows rain.

There is no water. There has never been water — no pool, no dew, no seep. What moisture exists is mined: certain veins in the crater walls, called sweet-stone by no tongue that remains, are a hydrate mineral, and slow armored gnawers chisel into them and hoard what they win in bladder-flesh until they waddle, heavy as wineskins. Everything else in the bowl drinks them. Water passes body to body, mouth to mouth, and is never once set down. When a hunter or a rockfall cracks a cistern-beast, the spill lasts seconds — and for those seconds the bowl keeps its one truce: nothing hunts at a spilling. Grazers, mites, even the killers themselves converge and lap the drops before the hot ground drinks them. It is the only rain this place has ever known: brief, red, and caught between bodies.

Over the Felt move the herds. Felt-oxen — low, broad, soft-footed, shelled in overlapping plates like bark — graze in slow crowds shoulder to shoulder, rasping the hide with tongues like files, following the bloom in long spiral migrations around the bowl. At high breath they pack so densely that the floor seems to move as one fabric, and there is truly no bare ground anywhere: reef-ground below, Felt at the surface, herds on it, mites in every seam, the air itself occupied. Their combined rasping is the bowl's standing sound, soft and continuous, like rain that isn't.

Otherwise, silence — and the silence is a behavior, a law with teeth. Beneath the herds lie the listeners: flat, broad things textured exactly like the Felt they press against, lying still for days at a time, feeling footsteps through the ground. The floor tells on you. Vibration travels through the hide like sound through a drumhead, and a shell-edge clicked carelessly, a pebble turned, a stride too quick — these ring out like bells. So everything here has evolved quiet: padded feet, shells damped with mucus, movement at the pace of growth. A felt-ox puts a foot wrong and the floor blooms upward and takes it in a single motion, and the wet thump and brief crackle of it is the loudest thing that will happen that day. The herds do not startle. They lean slowly away, close the gap, and within the hour the shroud-moths arrive. And here is the inversion a visitor only learns by touch: the bowl is not quiet at all. It is full of music the air cannot carry — courtship tapped shell to shell, territorial drumming transmitted through the Felt, duets sustained for days between mates a field apart. Lay a hand flat on the ground and you feel it, a faint continuous drumming, the pulse of something too large to see. The bowl is only quiet in the air.

Above it all, the ash-kites ride the heat-columns on silent membrane wings, steering by the ember-threads in the cracks, sensing the world as warmth. And once each cycle, at the peak of high breath, comes the Seeding: the Felt releases its spores, and for a few days the bowl admits its only standing light — a slow snow of faint glowing motes drifting up through the dark until the air itself seems made of dim sparks. The grazers lift their heads and follow the glow, for spore-fall means feeding. Which is why the glimmer exists: a small cold light, bluish, patient, that winks in the dark in exact imitation. Light in this world means food, or the thing that eats what seeks food. Nothing else. Do not walk toward a light here.

But two or three times in a year, the geometry of the world lines up — the full moon, or the sun at its seasonal extreme, crossing the notch — and then a single band of light no wider than a door leans over the rim and slides across the bowl: up the far wall, across the spires, over the backs of the herds, gone in minutes. Nothing here has eyes for it. Yet the Felt tightens under its touch, the falling ash shows within it as silver, and the kites cross and cross it like shuttles on a loom. At the head of the stair, facing the notch, there is a seat: a hollow worn into the stone by long sitting. Whoever sat there was watching for exactly this.

Because there was a whoever. On the rim stand cairns, some toppled by old shakes. A stair climbs to the notch — some four hundred steps worn into scoops by feet — and at its landings the rock bears glyphs: concentric circles, tally-marks, the tessellated pattern of the Felt itself, and small figures with no eyes and great outsized ears. The makers knew the law of this place. At the stair's foot stands one low dry-stone cell, roofless now, its threshold stone holding a cup-shaped hollow worn smooth — a libation dish, perhaps, for what gift could matter more, here, than poured water? The lowest glyphs are furred over with reef-stone; the Felt has grown a thumb's thickness of itself across them, and the Felt grows very slowly. Notably, the makers built nothing below the rim. Reverence or good sense — they kept off the hide.

Weather here is not weather as the outside world knows it. Ash sifts down from the Roof in long dry falls, the place's only precipitation. Cool air spills over the rim and the Felt at the fringe grows paler and tougher for it. Once in a great while a vent flares and a spire is born or broken, and the bowl absorbs even catastrophe as routine. Time is kept not by day and night — there are none — but by breath and low breath, by the Seeding, by the blade of light through the notch. Everything is old, everything is settled, everything is in motion. It has been like this for longer than the reef-ground is deep.

These are the bowl's terms, then. Do not be loud; the floor hears, and the floor tells. Do not walk toward a light. Drink where you can, and know that nothing here ever has. Keep the mountain's calendar. And understand what you are in this place: the anomaly — a warm, wet, ringing body, trailing a wake of stillness through the herds, attended by a dark that has all the time there is.

When you go — up the scooped steps, out through the notch into the hammering daylight — the stillness closes behind you like a held breath let go. The rasp of the herds rises again, soft as rain that isn't. The spires exhale. The bowl resumes its long breathing, which is all the weather it has ever needed.
