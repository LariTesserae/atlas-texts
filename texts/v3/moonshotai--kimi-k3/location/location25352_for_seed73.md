From the marsh at the bottom, the Stair does not look like terrain. It looks like a decision the world made and refused to reconsider.

Five tiers — some say seven, counting the two drowned ones beneath the Drown — each a vertical kilometer or better of gray-gold cliff, stacked one behind another until the highest vanishes into a sky that is somehow steamier than the air you're standing in. The wall runs beyond sight in both directions, unbroken except where water has negotiated passage. At dawn the sun strikes the Roof first and pours down the tiers like something liquid, lighting them in sequence, and for a few minutes the whole escarpment stands in bands of gold and blue shadow, and the sound arrives before the heat does: the falls, the insects, the birds, and underneath all of it the low note you will spend the first day insisting is in your head.

It is not in your head.

**The Drown.** The base is a marsh the size of a small sea — black, tannin-stained water threaded with channels, dotted with the crowns of drowned trees that leaf out gamely above the flood. Fish move through the sunken branches. The air is a wet cloth. A causeway of fitted stone, its blocks the size of houses, arches out from the treeline and stops, mid-water, mid-air, going nowhere it hasn't gone for a very long time. Egrets nest on its last pier. Whoever built it built for a water level, or a destination, that no longer exists, and the marsh has not troubled itself to notice.

The heat here is ordinary tropical heat, which is to say considerable. It is also, you will learn, the *coolest* this country gets.

**The First Rise.** The cliff lifts straight out of the marsh margin, stained dark by ten thousand years of seep. This is waterfall country, and the waterfalls are established — not young, hollowing things, but old water in old channels, falling from notched lips that have not moved in living memory into plunge basins worn smooth as serving bowls. The talus at the cliff's foot vanished under jungle so long ago that the jungle itself now reads as geology: buttress-rooted giants, their trunks furred with ferns, their canopies stacked in three and four layers until the light comes down green and aged, like light through bottle glass.

Under the canopy it rains at noon on clear days. The trees condense the humidity and shed it — drip-tip leaves, each one a slow tap — so that the forest floor receives its weather secondhand, delayed, redistributed. Locals in another age must have had a word for it. The word is gone. The under-rain is not.

The switchback stair is cut directly into the cliff face, and it is the first honest evidence of hands: treads worn into shallow dishes by feet, a balustrade polished where palms ran along it for centuries, sections collapsed and re-jungled, sections sound. Garden terraces cling beside it at intervals, gone feral — but only half feral. The fruit trees still fruit on their old schedule. The orchards remember being orchards.

Here, on the First Rise, you see the first thing that does not obey.

A thread of water, no thicker than a finger, is climbing the rock. It rises along a seam in the stone at about the pace of a snail with somewhere to be — a droplet might take a day to ascend a meter — glistening, unhurried, wrong. Follow it up and it joins others, braiding into a vertical watershed, an up-well, a waterfall in reverse at one ten-thousandth the speed. Where the climbing threads pass, the rock is lush even by this country's standards: moss in thick pelt, orchids in ropes, and everywhere the pale bulbous shells of tower snails, some a meter long, grazing the wet stone at the speed of growth, their silver trails looping up the cliff like slow calligraphy. A colony of them is a garden that migrates. Some of the older shells carry their own canopy — ferns rooted in the moss that has rooted in the shell. Nothing here wastes a surface.

**The Works.** By the Second Rise you have stopped pretending about the low note. It comes from the machines.

They are embedded in the cliffs at long intervals — housings the size of cathedrals, half-swallowed by the stone and the forest, made of a gray-gold ceramic that weathers *almost* like rock and therefore reads, at first, as rock. Roots have cracked them. Moss has furred them. Waterfalls fall off their upper ledges. And they are not ruins, because ruin implies the work stopped. They hum — a chord, actually, several notes at once, felt in the sternum and the teeth, shifting slowly across the seasons as a chord changes under a very patient hand. They breathe. Each housing has vents, and the vents inhale for years — drawing in mist, spores, midges, the whole thick living broth of the air — and then, on a rhythm measured in decades, exhale: a season-long exhalation of warm engineered fog that carries unfamiliar scents, unfamiliar pollens, the chemical signatures of plants that exist nowhere now except in the forest that has crept closest to the vents and grown strange.

The flora nearest the housings is *off* in ways that take a moment to name — leaves in geometries the rest of the jungle doesn't use, flowers that open at night and track nothing, vines that braid themselves into regular lattices. Descendant lineages of things the machines once made, persisting, naturalized, gone half-wild. The strange has not taken over. It has simply been here long enough to have a place.

Small stone shelters stand at some of the vents — shrines, with benches worn by sitting, where people once came to be in the exhalation. Most are empty. One, on the Third Rise, has a swept floor and a lamp with fresh oil. You will not see the person who keeps it. The country is large and the people, if they are anywhere, are few and unhurried.

**The inversion.** Here is the rule the land runs on, and it runs on no other rule more firmly: *you climb into the heat.* The Works hold this country in thermal equilibrium, and their waste warmth rises, so the altitude does what altitude does nowhere else — nothing, then the opposite. The Third Rise is hotter than the Drown. The Fourth is hotter than the Third. The cloud that should mean cold means warmth; the summit plateau, the Roof, is the hottest, wettest place of all, a steam-jungle at the top of the world, where the rocks are warmer than the air and the clouds condense on the stone and run down it in rivulets — the cliffs drinking the sky — and sweat and rain become the same event.

The up-wells grow commoner as you climb. Whole seams of the upper cliffs are soft with reduced weight, and the fauna have made an economy of it. Sail-lizards — membrane-winged, hand-sized, in constant motion — ride the seams in loitering spirals, expending nothing, snapping midges from the air. Their young drift on the updrafts like seeds, and the seeds of several trees drift like sail-lizards, and after a while the distinction is mostly taxonomic. Eels come up the climbing threads in the warm season, a migration measured in vertical miles — down through the Drown as larvae, up through the seams as adults — to spawn in the summit tarns, in water that has never in its history been cold.

The sound, up here, is total. The falls produce a white roar that never resolves and never stops. Cicada-analogues chorus in waves that travel up the cliff face at dusk, a wall of sound climbing tier by tier, and when the wave passes through your body you feel it in your fillings. Cliff-fowl in colonies of thousands whitewash the ledges and argue all day. Rain-frogs begin their calling an hour before the storms arrive, and they are never wrong, and the storms arrive most afternoons, warm as blood, loud as applause, over in an hour, leaving every surface steaming. At noon the stone itself ticks and ticks, expanding, a sound like a vast clock that has nowhere to be.

**The Roof.** The plateau at the top is not a summit so much as a held breath: a rolling country of bog and bloom, tree-fern forest, kettle-plants in the wet hollows broad enough to drown a bird, orchids in drifts, mist moving through everything in slow body-warm currents. The largest of the Works lies half-buried here, a low continent of gray-gold ceramic, and its vents are the size of valleys, and it is, by the long count, mid-inhalation — drawing the fog in across years, tasting the world, composing whatever it will exhale next decade.

Everything alive here is tuned to that patience. There are trees that flower once per turning-over, saving a decade of nectar for a single season, and when the exhalation comes they will bloom in a wave that starts at the vents and rolls across the Roof, and the frogs will erupt, and the sail-lizards will darken the thermals, and the snails will not noticeably change their plans. The country is between breaths now. It has been between breaths for a while. It is in no distress about this.

**Evening.** At dusk the shadow climbs the Stair the way the light poured down it at dawn, tier by tier, and the cicada wave climbs with it, and the falls go on, and the chord of the Works settles into its night register, lower, almost below hearing. The stars come out — ordinary stars, ordinary dark — and the foxfire kindles in the rot-logs, and the mist keeps arriving off the warm stone in slow exhalations of its own. In the summit tarns the eels are spawning in water as warm as the bodies that made them. Somewhere far below, the lamp in the swept shrine is lit.

Nothing here is in a hurry, because nothing here needs to be. The land reached its form long ago and wears it the way the snails wear their gardens — completely, at walking pace, in balance with everything that presses on it. The water falls, except where it climbs. The heat rises, and the height does nothing to stop it. The machines breathe in and will, eventually, breathe out.

The Stair keeps its own time. It has all of it.
