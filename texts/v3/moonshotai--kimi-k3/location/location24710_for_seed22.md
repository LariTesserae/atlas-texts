The light comes last here, and least. What arrives first is warmth through the soles of the feet — the ground itself is warm, everywhere, a lowland basin the size of a province lying under a ceiling of cloud that never lifts and never quite breaks. The deck hangs a few hundred meters up, grey-amber, lit from behind by a sun that can only be inferred: a brighter bruise in the murk that crosses overhead once a day, brightening the world from dark-dusk to light-dusk and back again. Noon looks like a heavily overcast evening. Midnight looks like nothing at all. The warmth is not the sun's. It rises from below, out of the crust, out of the machines beneath the crust, and it is the warmth that makes the weather: vapor ascending off a warm world into cooler air, condensing on everything that reaches up. The dimness and the dampness and the warmth are one system. The world is a still, and everything in it is distillate.

Stand on the flank of a tower and the scale announces itself slowly, because there is so little to measure it by. The next structure is an hour's walk away — a dark shoulder against the deck, its outline sharp as a fresh break. Beyond it, at the edge of visibility in the murk, another. Between them: nothing. A plain of black glassy ground, faintly iridescent, faintly steaming, absolutely empty, stretching farther than the eye can work. This is the defining fact of the region — its near-void. The builders made infrastructure for crowds: avenues a hundred meters wide, plazas the size of lakes, conduits, kerbs, staircases, underpasses, all of it organized, legible, urban in every gesture. And it stands empty. A tower per valley. A family per tower. Footsteps carry for kilometers out there, and so does everything else, which is why nobody who lives here thinks of distance in terms of walking. They think of it in terms of sound.

Nothing on the plain is old. That is the second fact. The crust is young — weeks, months, a few decades at the very most — extruded from below and annealed into dark ceramic-metal that cools with an audible ticking, like a stove after the flame dies. Edges do not round here. Seams are sharp enough to cut. Where condensate has run down a new face for even a few days, chemistry happens at a sprint: copper-green and rust-orange streaks, the brightest colors in the world, waterfalls of patina on black glass. The only things that erode the place are its own moisture and the tongues of its animals, and both are outpaced by growth. The city builds faster than weather can wear it. It is everywhere newborn, permanently unweathered, a town-sized machine with no archaeology. Ask a listener where the oldest part is and they will tell you: there isn't one. The city recycles what it no longer needs. History is not stored in the ground. It is stored in throats.

A growth event is worth watching from a safe distance, and there is always one happening somewhere in the region. The plain swells along a seam too clean to be a crack. The crust lifts, splits, and a wall rises out of the ground over the course of hours with a sound like a glacier calving slowed to a crawl — a deep granular roar you feel in the teeth. When it finishes, the seams seal, the surface anneals, and lights come on inside it: sparse status-glows, ember-orange and the occasional cold white, shining out of openings no one will ever enter. The new structure stands there, humming, empty, doing whatever it does. Because the whole arrangement *does* something — that much the listeners agree on. The towers are not identical; their spacing is not random; the avenues connect them with the insistence of traces on a board. The region is laid out like a component. The empty plazas have the look of cooling gaps. Whatever is being computed here, its output appears to be structural — the answer, if there is ever an answer, will be a shape. The city is a thought that builds its own scratch paper.

Water is pocketed and upside-down. It almost never rains — a few times a season the deck thickens and drops a brief warm rain, and the event detonates the ecology: everything drinks, blooms, screams, mates, and then it is over in an hour. The rest of the time, moisture lives in the air and on surfaces. The towers, cooler than the vapor rising past them, sweat. Condensate beads on their upper reaches, gathers, runs down in flutes, and drips from every ledge — and here is the signature strangeness of the place: because the warmth rises from the ground, undersides collect more than topsides. The forests hang. Every ledge, every overhang, every sheltered underface of the city wears a dripping garden — black-leafed rope-plants, moss-velvet an inch thick, violet fans that open toward the warmth below, all of it speckled with faint bioluminescence to signal the things that carry spore. In a dim world, the brightest living things are advertisements. Where a conduit leaks steadily — and a leak may run for years, then stop without warning — a temporary river cuts a fast sharp canyon through the soft young crust, and for a while there is a corridor of loud green chaos, the nearest thing this world has to abundance concentrated. Then the city reroutes its cooling, the river dies, and the canyon stands as the oldest-looking thing in the district until the builders reclaim it.

But none of this is apprehended visually. It is apprehended by ear, because the region is *deafening* — 2.8 on any honest scale, a soundscape so total that the few people born here navigate by it the way others navigate by sight. The layers stack up like strata. At the bottom, below hearing proper, the builder-engines pulse: an infrasonic heartbeat every few seconds, felt in the sternum and the sinuses, the tempo of construction itself. Above that, the mid-range bed: vent-hiss, steam, the tick of cooling crust, and always — always, somewhere in the province — the hours-long roar of an extrusion, so that the background of life is the sound of something being born too loudly. Above that, the choir. The fauna, abundant and spread impossibly thin, have evolved to be heard through the industrial bed, and the result is a perpetual shrieking, drumming, whistling canopy of voice that rides the machine-noise the way surf rides an undertow. When the deck brightens toward noon, all of it rises together. The chorus of a world that cannot see itself, announcing itself to itself.

And within the roar, the mercies: the nulls. The towers tune one another — each district hums in a chord, the whole region is one sustained note slowly transposing over years — and where standing waves cancel across the open plains, pockets of silence form and drift as the engines shift. A null might hold still for a season or wander a kilometer in a week. In a deafening world, silence is the scarce resource, the water of the acoustic desert, and everything that sleeps seeks the quiet wells. Where a null happens to coincide with a drip-line — silence *and* water together — you get the gardens of this world: lush, wet, still, gentle, and therefore contested. Every creature wants in. The most dangerous one usually is in.

The fauna are everywhere and almost never seen — spread thin across the void, abundant as voices are abundant. The crust-scrapers cross the plains in songlines: herds strung out over kilometers, individuals hundreds of meters apart, held together purely by song, each animal singing its position into the dark, the herd less a body than a melody crawling across the emptiness. They graze the youngest ground, scraping mineral precipitate off fresh crust with hardened tongues, and so the herds map the city's growth — where the builders have been busiest, there the songlines converge. Gliders ride the thermal updrafts along tower flanks, skin-winged, echolocating, grazing the hanging moss. In the underforests live the deaf ones — creatures that opted out of the sound war entirely and speak by vibration, touching the structure, drumming messages into the shared ceramic like telegraph operators. New walls become cliff ecosystems within a season: spores arrive on the updraft within days, moss within weeks, gliders behind the moss, hunters behind the gliders. Succession runs at a sprint because demolition may come at any time.

The apex of it all is the thing the listeners call the hush. Rare, solitary, and wrapped in an acoustic shadow — its pelt, or something stranger than its pelt, damps vibration in a radius around it, so that it moves through the world as a hole in the sound. In a place where everything is known by its voice, the hush is functionally invisible. Prey notices the roar thinning, the way a swimmer notices the water going cold, and by then the arithmetic is mostly done. The hush dens in the silence gardens, in the overlap of null and water, and the listeners — who also want those places — have learned to tell a natural quiet from a carried one. A null is still. A hush is *taken*. The difference is felt, not heard, and getting it wrong is how families lose a tower.

The machines, for their part, are not hostile to life so much as incurious about it. Extrusions route around a sleeping herd, walling it in gently, leaving a courtyard shaped exactly like absence. Occasionally the city grows straight through a drip-forest and seals it inside a new wall — a tube of jungle entombed in glassy dark, still alive, lit by status-glow, watered by its own captured condensation: an accidental terrarium, audible from outside as a muffled green dripping. The listeners break into these when they find them. So does everything else.

The people are a rumor across the region, thin as everything else that isn't stone. A family to a tower, towers a day's walk apart, and yet they are a culture, held together the way the herds are: by voice thrown across void. They farm the ledges, harvest condensate, collect the pure metals the crust precipitates like dew. They train their ears from birth, and some of them — the deep-listeners — have their hearing altered or simply ruined young, on purpose, the better to feel the infrasonic pulse through bone without the choir's distraction. Their maps are songs: you do not draw a route here, you sing it, tower-chord to tower-chord, each structure's voiceprint a landmark that no murk can hide. Their one absolute rule is that nobody builds. It would be pointless — the city provides, and absorbs, and ignores — but the rule is older than the reasoning, and it is kept the way taboos are kept: without discussion.

What they debate, endlessly, across the kilometers, in whistle-speech pitched to thread the machine-hum, is the transposition. The region's chord is changing — a fraction of a fraction of a tone per year, measurable across a lifetime, the whole province slowly retuning itself as the computation advances. Growth comes in pulses: weeks of furious extrusion when three districts roar at once, then weeks of consolidation, quiet-ish, when the herds move in to graze the new ground and the listeners go out to learn the altered music. The deep-listeners hold that construction is not a process with an end but a note approaching resolution, and that when the chord finally resolves — when the shape is the answer — the engines will simply stop. The roar will fall away. The nulls will close. The silence will become total and permanent, and no one agrees whether that will be the world's completion or its ending.

At false-dawn the deck brightens from black to grey-amber, and the chorus rises to meet it, ten thousand voices in a province that could hold ten million and mostly doesn't. Somewhere far off a spire finishes with a final crack like a mountain breaking, and a new hum enters the chord — the listeners tilt their heads, already revising. On a ledge an hour old, condensate gathers, gathers, and lets fall the first drip that ledge has ever made. The spores are already in the air. They were in the air before the wall finished cooling. The dark plain ticks underfoot, warm, sharp, young, and the songline of a herd crosses it at the edge of hearing, strung out over kilometers of nothing, singing itself together through the loudest, emptiest, most nearly vacant world — a world that is, despite everything, full.
