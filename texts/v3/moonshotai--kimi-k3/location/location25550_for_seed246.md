The mountains up here are old enough to have given up their peaks. Long ridges lie along the sky like the backs of sleeping animals, rounded and gray, ground down by ice and uncountable winters into a final shape they will now keep more or less forever. The treeline is a day's climb below. The air is thin enough that breath arrives a half-second late, and the cold is the damp kind — not the dry, squeaking cold of deep continental winter, but a wet, patient cold that finds the wrists and settles behind the ears.

The bowl sits just below the highest of these worn summits, a cirque small enough to see across, an hour's walk rim to rim. And in the bowl: a city.

It was never built. That is the first thing to understand, and the last thing you ever quite finish understanding. It was grown — the way a body grows an organ, the way coral grows a reef — by something that was, or is, one single living thing. Streets, plazas, towers, halls, stairs, cisterns: all one substance, all seamless, a pale material like shell, like bone, like old porcelain, secreted layer by layer over a span of time that shows in the broken places as concentric rings, like a tree's, like nacre. There are no tool marks anywhere. There are no right angles. There is nothing small enough to pick up that the city made — every portable object in the bowl is moss, feather, shed fur, or ice. Everything else is architecture, and all the architecture is one body, and the body is old beyond the reach of the word.

The rim towers have been weathered down to stubs. The oval doorways are worn smooth as river stones. The stairs are cupped in their centers — not by feet; by drips of water, falling in the same place for ten thousand years. Nothing in the city has a corner anymore. It is not a ruin; a ruin is a city still dying. This city has finished. It has arrived at the shape it will hold until the mountain itself gives way, and it holds that shape with the composure of a thing that has stopped changing its mind.

The light here is ordinary daylight, which may be the strangest thing about the place. No colored sun, no permanent dusk — just gray morning, bright noon when the clouds lift, gray evening. The strangeness does its work in plain view.

Water arrives as fog, on a schedule, twice a day, like a tide. At dawn the city sighs — literally: a slow intake of breath through thousands of ribbed vents and fins that comb moisture out of the passing cloud. It is audible from anywhere in the bowl, a long, low, drawn chord of airflow, and it lasts perhaps ten minutes. By mid-morning the fog usually thins and the sun comes through and the whole city steams. Rime sublimates off the streets in glittering veils that never become liquid. The shell-stone goes warm-toned and, where the walls are thin — at window-edges, at the lips of arches — faintly translucent, glowing like the edge of a held-up seashell.

Then, in the afternoon, the wind rises, and the fog comes home over the rim like cream poured back into a cup, slow and total, and the city goes blind and loud.

Loud is the default condition here, and the emptiness makes it louder. The towers — there are nine, or nine that still stand — are hollow, and their tops have eroded into open mouths that face the prevailing wind. When the wind crosses them they sound: a chord, low and enormous, an organ chord filling the bowl the way the bowl fills the mountain. It was once, perhaps, a pure chord. The mouths have worn unevenly over the millennia, and the tuning has drifted a quarter-tone flat, sour in a way that is not unpleasant — the way a very old bell is not unpleasant. The city's song has gone flat with age, and no one has ever minded, because there has never been anyone to mind. In the fog hours the chord is joined by the dripping: every hall has its own tempo, every cistern its own pitch, and the polyphony of water falling on shell-stone fills every street. Real rain is rare and arrives like an event — sudden, drumming on the porcelain avenues like applause in an empty theater.

The cold should have killed everything green here, and outside the rim it nearly has: the fell-field is frost-sorted stone and little else. But the city is faintly, stubbornly warm. Its metabolism is a pilot light — banked, minimal, nearly out, but not out — and the streets carry a low heat up through the soles of your boots, the warmth of a sleeping animal's flank. Snow melts off the avenues first, so that from the rim in winter the city is drawn in dark lines on the white: a vein-map, a circuit diagram, a picture of itself. Along those warm streets the vegetation has taken refuge and gone lush: moss a foot deep, wet as felt, deep enough to swallow a boot; cushion plants hard as masonry that are, if you look closely, solid flowers; lichen rosettes on the north walls that grow a hand-span a century. Here and there, standing alone in the plazas at enormous intervals from one another — one to a plaza, always — are the great rosettes, tall as a person, trunked and felted, shedding their dead leaves in skirts around their own stems. They stand like the last parishioners of something, spaced out across the emptiness as though distanced for a ceremony still in progress.

The emptiness is the city's true material. It is a settlement built at cathedral scale and inhabited at the density of a desert. Whole halls stand with nothing in them but a drift of rime and one green stripe of moss along the warm seam of the floor. Distances do not behave like street-distances; they behave like rooms. You cross the city not block by block but chamber by chamber, each plaza a room of air with a single object in it.

The fauna are rare enough to be known individually. There is a small herd — five, some years six — of heavy, frost-colored grazers, long-lipped, shaggy, built like a memory of something larger, who browse the moss along the warm streets and sleep at night in the heated halls, their breath smoking in the doorway-light. Their hooves click on the shell-stone, and in the fog you locate them by that clicking the way you locate the towers by their chord. They process to the same hall every evening in the same order, and it is impossible, watching them file in out of the white, not to think of them as the congregation — the towers their bells, rung by no one, answered anyway. There is a pair of small white birds that nest inside the flue of the third tower, the closest thing the city has to an organist; their call is a single clear note, like a wet finger drawn along glass, and the bowl takes that note and completes it into a chord. Hearing one is an event. Most days you hear only traces of everything else: a shed pelt hooked on a condenser fin, droppings like dark beads on the moss, a feather turning slowly in a drip-pool. In the cisterns, if you wait long enough, blind white newts the length of a finger rise at noon when the meltwater freshens, and hang there, pulsing faintly, the only inhabitants of the city's reservoirs, unhurried as everything else.

The rules of the place are few and kept absolutely. The fog comes twice a day. The streets are warm; the walls are not. Sound carries absurdly far — a footstep in one plaza can be located from another, wingbeats are audible, a whisper in a hall arrives intact across it — and so the loudness and the emptiness are the same fact stated two ways: everything here can be heard, and there is almost nothing here to hear. Nothing is maintained, and nothing collapses. The city is not being kept; it is keeping itself, in the loosest sense, the way a coal keeps itself overnight under ash.

And it will go on doing so. That is the final thing the bowl tells you, if you stay through a dusk. The fog pours back over the rim. The towers take up their old flat chord. The congregation files into the warm hall, breath steaming, and the rosettes stand in their separate plazas holding their skirts of dead leaves, and the melt-water ticks in the cisterns, and the warmth moves up through the streets into the moss, keeping one small green argument alive against the cold. From the rim above, in the last ordinary light, you can see it: the bowl cupping its weather like cupped hands, the dark vein-work of the avenues, the faint steam rising off a body that has outlived everything it was for, and has not noticed, and does not mind, and is not done.
