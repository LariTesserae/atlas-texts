# The Soft Country

You leave the old land at the Strandline — a low scarp of shell-gravel and rusted chain, the beach the sea left behind when it was sent back — and step down into the dark. *Down* is the only word for it, though the grade is gentle and the whole country lies near the level of the sumps. Down is where the dark is.

The dark here is not an absence. It is a medium: warm, wet, close-grained, pressing against the skin like condensation that hasn't committed yet. Your lamp builds a small room around you — wet walls of black, a floor of young mud — and beyond the room the country registers only in warmth-gradients, breath, drips, the give of the ground. No one has ever reported anything above the cables. The old books mention a sun the way they mention the sea: as something that used to be underneath things.

Take off a glove and touch the ground. It is blood-warm. This is the first thing no one believes and everyone checks. The whole country runs at the temperature of a body — the mud, the standing water, the rain when it comes, the engines' skins, the animals' breath, your own hand — so warmth tells you nothing about what is alive. The kettle in the bothy is the same temperature as the hand that isn't holding it. Nothing here cools, so nothing tells you when it was last touched.

---

The ground is so new it shines. Pewter mud, fat and gleaming, holding every ripple the sea left on its way out — the country hasn't learned to forget yet. For all its softness it cuts: slag-ridges where the Works have tipped their waste, clinker aprons sharp as broken pottery, channels edged like a knife pulled through clay. Nothing has had time to blunt. Surveyors chain the distances every year, and every year the causeways come back longer — inches, then chains. *No defect found,* the books say, in a careful hand, and later, in the same hand: *not settling.*

Water stands a finger's breadth under everything. Press a heel and it wells up around the boot. The dredge-cuts run straight as ruled lines — for now; the water remembers older geometries, and the cuts go slightly curly with every passing year. Mostly it doesn't rain. Mostly the air simply condenses: the cables drip as after a storm that never fell, your lamp-glass sweats, the moths come to the lamp broad as plates and pale, attending the light the way you'd attend weather from home.

Some of the water is elsewhere. A puddle stands full of night sky — the only stars in the country — or a blue noon, or once, famously, a sunrise that two stations watched upside-down for most of a year, neither willing to log it first. The tenders call it *elsewater*, and the rule is absolute: drink from the cuts, never from standing water. Reach into a puddle of stars and your hand comes out cold — a real cold, the only cold there is, space-cold — and the books say the cold does not always come back out of the hand.

---

What grows first on new ground is not exactly a plant. The felt: a black bacterial plush sheeting every warm acre, faintly briny, the pasture of the country. It is cropped by droves of dullards — eyeless, patient, hippo-bodied things, hundreds per drove, finding the felt by smell or by whatever passes for memory in something with no use for sight. Your lamp finds a flank, a wet nose, never a whole animal. They crop with a sound like cloth tearing, slowly, in the dark, all around you, always.

For an open country it is crowded. You never walk a straight line: pipe-runs at ankle height, cable-poles at hat height, puddles of possibly this world, hummocks of iron-sedge sparse as hairs on a knuckle, stands of glass-grass that chime faintly when the droves push through, sleeping dullards, and the *habits* — the persisting ghosts of removed things. The dike that was taken down in the early books still stops you some nights. You lean on nothing, and nothing holds. The country is slow to forget, slower than it is to learn. So the tenders teach it: new buildings banked with old bricks, new ground walked in set paths, everything repeated until it takes. Nothing here is real until it has been repeated.

The cuts are lit from below, faintly — lanternfish in slow schools, a dim grain of light your lamp drowns when you look directly. Not enough light to see by. Enough to be seen. Handfish walk the mudflats on their fins. The cuts have leeches broad as leaves; the books say do not wade, and the books do not say why twice. In the pylon forks nest cable-ravens, who keep the country's sounds the way the felt keeps its ripples: engine-knocks from pumps scrapped forty years ago, the voices of tenders rotated home long ago. If one says your name, answer nothing. It is not asking you. It is shelving you.

---

The Works run on what they stand in: a warm thin crude that rises in the sumps under a skin of rainbows. Nine pump-stations, low iron buildings sweating rust, each housing a beam-engine patient as a heart — you feel it in the soles before you hear it, the true timekeeper of the Soft. A narrow-gauge line threads the stations; the Pug hauls ballast-bricks and fuel, and when you hear it on the far loop you check the shed anyway, because once the Pug was heard on the far loop while cold in its shed, and the entry says only: *logged.* The walking dredge Old Mule moves its own length in a year along its cut, buckets clanking once a minute — a sound so slow it never becomes a rhythm, only a series of events.

On the far side of the block the Calcining Works burn with a deep red steadiness the ledgers call the Ember. It fires the ballast-bricks from the Soft's own mud, and something in the firing makes them older than the land — anchors, ballast for reality. The Ember is the only light you did not bring and may trust. Navigation is simple: the Ember is the pole star of the Soft. All other lights are bait, folds, or elsewater. Another lamp moving far off is not a tender. In the morning book there is never another tender logged on your side of the block.

Between the stations runs the wire, on poles furred with dripping wire-moss. It carries the pump-counts and the weatherless weather reports, and sometimes — the rule is log it and hang up — it carries shifts that have already happened. Or haven't. Voices you know. Yours.

---

There is a bothy by Station 4: stove, bunks, tin cups on hooks, boots under the bench, the ledger open on its lectern. The books are kept in pump-revolutions, because *day* is the wrong word here:

> *R. 410,000–414,600: causeway to Stn 4 re-chained. Nine chains longer than built. No defect found.*
> *R. 415,002: elsewater in the sumps showing stars. Tasted cold. Do not drink.*
> *R. 419,555: heard the Pug on the far loop. The Pug is in the shed.*

The entries run in one careful hand across forty years and five signatures. The tenders rotate — a tour is a few years, no more — and on the inside of the bothy door the rules are painted, the last line in a different hand than the rest:

1. Bring your own light. The land has none to lend.
2. Trust the Ember. All other lights are bait, folds, or elsewater.
3. Drink from the cuts, never from standing water.
4. Answer nothing that isn't real. When in doubt, answer nothing.
5. Bank new things with old stones. Walk the same paths. Repeat.
6. Step over your own footprints when they precede you. Do not fill them. The country is practicing you.
7. Keep away from the masts. If you must pass, go quiet and go once.
8. Write down what changes. Write down what doesn't, when it should.
9. *Don't stay past your rotation. The country keeps what it repeats.*

The masts stand a dozen in the whole block — white trunks, leafless, older than the ground they stand in, marked on the charts as hazards. Near them the dark thins — not lightens, *thins* — and sounds arrive early, and if you stand quiet you may hear the sea that used to be here, still practicing. No one planted them. They were found standing, after the first Note.

The Note is the Kiln's klaxon, and it has sounded three times in all the books. After the first: the masts. After the second: Station 4's causeway. After the third: the first elsewater. The interval has halved each time, which means the fourth is due inside the current tour. No one knows what it means. Everyone writes down what happens next.

---

Once in a long while the country goes quiet in a particular way. The felt retracts. The droves move to the far banks of the cuts. Then the Pilgrim comes through — and no lamp opens enough of it to make an animal: a warmth like a weather, a wake in the cuts that arrives an hour after the passing, a hush that takes most of a month to lift. The books date by it. *Third rev. of the Pilgrim's fourth passage.* It is the century, walking.

Everything here moves at that pace. The land rises a thumb's width a year — that is the point of the Works, though the old hands say the pumps' true job isn't drainage but conviction: pumping the elsewater out, pumping the country solid, teaching new ground to be ground. The fraying advances by inches a decade. The dredge advances by its own length a year. The Ember does not change. Your lamp burns a shift to the fill.

And the dark stays warm and close as a held breath, dense with presences — the droves tearing slowly at the felt, the engines' pulse in your soles, condensate ticking off the cables, the ravens shelving their archive of voices. You will find your own bootprint ahead of you on paths you haven't walked yet. You will step over it. You will write it down.

The Soft is in no hurry. It rose out of the sea into a place without a sky, and it is still deciding what it will keep — the cuts, the Works, the habits, the herds. Possibly you. Spend enough rotations here and your walking slows to the country's pace, and your entries stay in the same careful hand past your rotation date, and somewhere a raven repeats your voice to no one, shelved in the wires. No one is buried in the Soft. The ground won't take the dead — or takes them too well.

Far off, the Ember holds its steady red. The pumps beat under your boots. The country rises by a thumb a year toward a sky that hasn't set yet, patient as curing stone, learning itself — and gently, thoroughly, learning you back.
