## The Crater

High on the mountain — higher than the world's weather likes to go — there is a crater with no name, because nothing that gives names has ever needed one for it. It is small as volcanic features go: three hundred and some meters across, a single held breath of a place, rimmed all the way around, deep enough that the walls rise a hundred and fifty meters from the floor like the walls of a nave. You could shout across it, if the noise would let you be heard. It would not let you be heard.

The mountain is young. Everything about the crater is young. The walls are unweathered lava and tuff in alternating bands — black glass, rust-red scoria, gray ash — laid open in section like the strata of a cut gemstone, the edges still sharp enough to catch cloud. Dikes rib the walls where they were split open, standing proud in fins because the softer ash around them has only begun to fall away. Nothing here has been rounded. Nothing has had time. The talus at the wall bases is all angles, blocks the size of rooms still wearing the fracture marks of the blast that made them, and the ash underfoot crunches like the place is still unpacking itself.

The rim is notched on the windward side, and through that notch, all day and most of the night, the cloud pours in.

It comes over the lip like a slow cataract, a river of weather falling in silence, curling under itself, sliding down the wall and spreading across the floor. This is the only river the crater has. There is no stream. The young rock is too porous — rain sinks through it like through a sieve and vanishes into the mountain's plumbing. Water arrives here as fog and stays as damp: the cloud banks against the walls, and everything that grows in the crater has learned to drink the air.

---

Descend. The light goes with you, giving up in stages.

The summit cap almost never lifts. On the rare days it does, the sun crosses the rim only briefly — a blade of light at midday that sweeps the far wall and is gone. The rest of the time the crater lives in a dim pewter gloom, and beneath the canopy the gloom becomes a green dark, close and saturated, the kind of dark that has texture. At night it is total, except for what the mountain itself provides.

And the garden. The garden is beyond reason.

The floor's sheltered half — the hummocks, the lee of the walls, anywhere the air drains and moves — is upholstered in moss a meter deep, moss over moss over moss, liverworts and filmy ferns in the seams, clubmosses standing in stiff little forests of their own. Tree ferns rise in tiers, their fronds black-green with adaptation to the dark, each crown a drip-catcher. Between them, gnarled elfin trees, no taller than a ceiling, so burdened with epiphytes that their own leaves are the minority of what they carry — ferns in the crotches, moss on the bark, moss on the moss, vines threading crown to crown until the canopy is a single stitched fabric. Everything touches. Everything is layered. You could not take a step without pushing something aside, and what you pushed aside would spring back wet against you.

Nothing here rots quickly in the usual way, because nothing here eats. There are no insects. There are no birds. When a tree dies it does not fall — the neighbors hold it up, and it stands for years, slowly being digested standing by the moss that climbs it, until one day the tremor brings it down into the mattress of the floor and the green closes over it like water over a stone.

The floor has a second topography, invisible, made of gas. Carbon dioxide seeps up through the young rock and pools in the hollows, heavier than air, motionless on still nights, waist-deep in the low places. These dead zones are bare of even moss — pale crusted ground, silent — and what falls into them does not decay. The gas preserves. A frond that drops into a hollow keeps its color for seasons. Very occasionally a bird crosses the rim, tired, at altitude, and chooses the wrong place to rest; the hollows keep these too, intact, folded, like specimens. The crater has an archive, and it is made of everything that ever breathed in the wrong place.

The garden grows lush *around* this — furiously lush, fed by the gas at its margins, rooted in mineral ash, watered by the cloud, fertilized by the mountain's own exhale. The boundary between the dead hollows and the riot of green is sometimes a single meter wide. Life here is zoned by weight: the air is layered, and everything that grows has learned exactly how high it may reach.

---

Now listen, because the crater is *loud*.

It is loud the way a factory floor is loud, and there is not one living voice in it.

Under everything: the tremor. It comes in drumbeats, near-periodic, a deep *thud* every few seconds that you feel in your sternum and your teeth before you hear it — the mountain's piston, magma moving somewhere below in strokes. Over that, the main fumarole on the floor's far side roars like a jet at takeoff, a roar with grain in it, never quite steady. Secondary vents hiss at every pitch, a whole rank of steam-whistles, some of them tuning themselves up and down as the pressure breathes. The notch sings when the wind is right, a low organ note. The walls, never at rest, shed rock: a clatter, a bounce, an echo that slaps back and forth across the nave until the sound loses its source and becomes weather. Freeze-thaw at night, thermal shock by the vents, the drumbeat underfoot — the walls have every reason to fall, and they take them all.

And the drip. Thousands of drips, in every tempo, off every frond and lip and ledge, ticking into the moss, into the pools, onto stone — a vast soft percussion section that never once repeats itself.

At the center of the floor rises the spine: a shard of black glass the size of a church organ, extruded in the mountain's last bad year, still degassing, still cooling. It ticks. It pings. Years into its cooling, it ticks like a parked engine, contraction cracking new seams across its flanks, plates of obsidian sloughing off and ringing down around its base. At night — and night here is most of the day — a dull cherry-red glow shows in one of its seams and in the throat of the main vent: the only light the place makes for itself. Furnace light. The garden stands around it in the dark, dripping, and the red glow finds the wet edges of the fronds and stops there, as if the dark were too thick for light to travel far.

---

Because this is the thing about the crater: nothing in it was built, and everything in it is manufactured.

The mountain is an engine and the crater is its plant floor, and the whole operation runs on a perfectly ordinary appetite of heat and pressure and chemistry. Rainwater and cloud seep down through the fractured rock — intake. The magma chamber heats it — boiler. It comes back up as steam through a fracture network that works like pipework, valved by mineral deposits, metered by the day's barometric pressure, and it vents through the fumarole field, where the mountain fractionates its own breath: sulfur crusting in yellow flowers around the hottest throats, white salts benched a little farther out, iron rusting red at the margins, each mineral deposited at its own temperature like product zoned along a cooling line. Landslides carry debris off the walls like conveyors. The daily storm cycle — cloud in through the notch all morning, pressure building all afternoon, thunder trapped and rolling in the bowl by evening, volcanic lightning crawling inside the ash puffs when the vent coughs — turns over and over like a flywheel.

And the garden — the impossible, light-starved, deafening garden — is the strangest thing on the production schedule: a green byproduct growing in the exhaust, moss and fern colonizing each new ash fall within seasons, succession running down the floor in visible stages from bare new flow to mature dark, like an assembly line of becoming.

Nothing here violates anything. That is perhaps the most unsettling thing about it. There is no magic in the crater. There is only physics, run at full throttle, in the dark.

The cold sharpens everything. At this elevation the nights drop below freezing a body's length from ground that would burn you. Rime feathers itself over sulfur crusts, white on yellow, frost on brimstone. The ephemeral pools — rainwater caught in hollows, gone again in hours, sunk into the porous floor — skin with ice beside seeps warm enough to keep algae in orange and emerald threads. Steam condenses on cold leaves and rains back down. Your breath and the mountain's breath fog in the same air and are indistinguishable.

---

The crater keeps its own rules, and they are few:

Everything that rises here returns here. Ash, steam, cloud, sound — the bowl traps its own weather and its own noise, and nothing escapes but heat.

The air is layered by weight, and life is zoned by the layers. Breathe deep only where the green is thickest.

Nothing breathes here but the mountain, and it breathes poison.

The mountain keeps machine time: the drumbeat, the daily storm, the slow tick of the cooling spine. Nothing here was made, and everything is produced.

---

It will not last. That is the final condition of its youth. One wet year too many and the floor will hold water, and there will be a lake where the garden stood. One bad night of tremor and a wall will fail through the notch and the bowl will drain, gas and cloud and all, and the dark will leak out onto the slopes. Fumarole fields migrate; the garden will be buried and unburied and buried again; the spine will finish cooling and stop its ticking and become just another stone.

But that is later. For now the crater is what it is: the mountain's first draft, written in steam and moss — a room at the top of the world where the cloud pours in and never leaves, where the air itself will kill you kindly and keep you, where a forest grows in the dark beside a furnace no one lit, and the whole loud, dripping, poisoned, gorgeous engine runs and runs, unwitnessed, at full throttle.
