Begin with the dark, because the dark is most of it.

The sky holds almost nothing. No sun crosses it, no moon. On the clearest nights — and every night is exactly as clear as every other, for there is no weather left to make a difference — the sky shows a handful of dim ovals, soft as thumbprints on black cloth. These are not stars. They are whole galaxies, far away. Whatever star this world once belonged to, it parted from so long ago that the parting itself has eroded past remembering. The world keeps warm anyway. The heat comes from within, from the old slow fire of the crust, rising evenly through stone that has had eons to learn how to conduct it gently.

The plateau stands high over a lower basin of pooled dust — the floor of the world, where the dark is somehow deeper, if deeper is a word the dark allows. Up here the air is thin and warm as a held breath, and still. It has been still for longer than the mountains have had their present shape. And the mountains have had their present shape for a very long time: they are mountains only by courtesy now, worn down to a high plain of black, polished stone, from which rise a few rounded domes of the hardest rock — the parts even eternity couldn't take. Everything that could erode has eroded. What remains is what erosion cannot touch, and it has the calm of a finished argument.

Between the domes lies the dust, sorted long ago by the last winds into ripples, crests a hand's width apart, frozen mid-procession. They are not preserved. Preservation implies an effort. There is simply nothing left that moves dust. The final storm lay down somewhere and never rose again, and its signature covers the plateau like a sea photographed at the instant before breaking.

Here is the first strange rule of the place, and you learn it quickly if you carry a lamp: **light falls short.** A flame does not bleed gradually into the dark; it owns a small, definite sphere of visibility, and beyond that sphere the black begins like a wall. A lamp lights what it can afford to, no more. So the world is never seen whole. From no point on the plateau can you see two of the city's nodes at once. The place is larger than any act of seeing it, and it can be known only the way the blind know a house — room by room, warmth by warmth, by the tally of small lit islands with swimming-dark between.

The city — call it that, though it is not dense the way cities are — is a constellation scattered across days of walking. Nodes of industry stand hours apart in the black, each one wrapped in its own few lights: the steady orange of furnace mouths, pinprick constellations of indicator lamps on panels, the green thread of a gauge's glow, the slow bright veins of molten metal moving through flumes. Between the nodes run the conduits — pipelines, rails, cableway wires — crossing the dark unseen. You can navigate by laying a hand on a pipe: warm, and warmer the closer you are to something working.

Approach a foundry node and you smell it before you see it, because the still air keeps every smell at home. Hot oil. Warm dust. The metallic sweetness of molten salts, ozone near the arc-lamps. Then the furnace glow takes you at the edge of its sphere, and the node rises around you in tiers: the throat of the kiln, conveyors frozen in their routes, ingots stacked in exact arrays that no one counts, catwalks and ladders and rails of black iron gone waxy with age. Nothing flickers. The flames have forgotten how. Fed at a rate that has not varied in longer than rivers run on other worlds, they burn in perfect constancy, and their light falls on dust that bears, still, the footprints of the builders — a round walked between console and valve and door, undisturbed since the day it was last walked.

Because here is the second strange thing, the deep one: **the city has reached equilibrium, and equilibrium is kept.**

The machines did not stop. Stopping would be a change. They continue — but everything they do sums to nothing. A crane in the dark spends the night moving a pile of castings from one side of a yard to the other, and another machine, elsewhere on the same circuit, spends the same night moving an identical pile back. Ore goes into the kiln; the slag of the last age is the ore of the next; the same thousand tons of iron rides the ring-line forever, out from the foundry as ingots, back to the foundry to be remelted, kept warm by the loop it travels. The foundry casts the parts the foundry needs. Every moving part has worn against its neighbor until the fit is perfect and wear has almost nowhere left to go — the whole machine bedded-in at the scale of geological time. Nothing is produced. Nothing is consumed. It is industry as a heartbeat that pumps no blood: motion without history.

Every fluid is sealed and accounted for. The oils have been warm for eons and have never been spilled; the same lubricant has made the same journey through the same bearings more times than the domes have had storms. And in a vault beneath the chemical house, racked in rows behind a sealed door, is the world's entire hydrosphere: some thousands of liters of water, kept as a reagent, inventoried in ledgers no one reads. There is no other. No ice, no vapor, no rain in the world's memory of rain. Nothing here is thirsty.

The sound of all this is the third strange thing. Long ago — the way pendulum clocks hung on the same wall drift into step — every engine on the plateau locked its frequency to every other. The city hums one note. It has hummed it since before the final storm. It is low, mostly below hearing, and the thin still air carries it poorly, but the stone carries it perfectly: you feel the Note through boot soles, through a palm laid on a wall, through the bones of the chest. It is so constant that the body subtracts it, the way the ear subtracts silence. What remains is a quiet so complete that the rare sounds arrive like pebbles dropped into a still pond — whole, naked, unsourced by sight, unanswered. A relay's clack in an empty control room. A gondola arriving out of the dark on the cableway, doors opening onto an unlit platform, waiting the correct interval, closing, returning to the dark: the transit system keeps its timetable, and has no passengers, and does not mind. A dropped tool — if anything were ever dropped — might be heard across a whole district, and nothing would answer it. In an office somewhere a teletype still prints the weather, hourly. There is none. The report is identical every time, and it is not wrong.

The wires between the nodes still carry the news, and the news is always the same: *nominal.*

Life, what is left of it, lives only where the machines live, and for a precise reason: the unmade world has one temperature, and life needs a difference. So the last creatures of the plateau are parasites of the only gradients remaining — the fall from hot to less-hot that the machines still manufacture.

In the radiator fields — acres of iron fins standing in rows like a forest, the only forest this world has ever had — fin-mites no bigger than a grain of sand graze across the metal, metabolizing the temperature difference itself, migrating from warm edge to warm edge in journeys that take years. Around the flange joints of the pipelines sleep the coilers: yard-long, segment-plated, matte-black things that drink the thin films of oil that seep at the joints and orient themselves by the Note, uncoiling along the warmth like slow handwriting. They click, once, now and then — a sound like a relay — and nothing answers. And above the furnace stacks, sometimes, the drifters: thin buoyant bodies lifted on the rising heat, trailing tendrils, sipping the slow electric charge that gathers where hot dust-laden gas climbs into the black sky. When one discharges it glows, faintly, briefly. They are the only moving lights in the sky. They are, for all practical purposes, the stars of this world — few, close, and alive.

There may be four hundred coilers on the plateau. There may have been four hundred for longer than the domes have had their shape. Metabolisms this slow do not hurry anything: a coiler's life is longer than a mountain's mood, and in such a life an individual may attend a handful of births. The dead do not rot — there is nothing to rot them, no water, no decay's machinery — they only dry, and dry things keep. Somewhere in the flange galleries lie coilers that died before the final storm, leathered into place, indistinguishable from sleepers until touched.

Of the builders there is less to say, and it is all legible. Their scale is readable in the stair treads, the chair heights, the reach of the handrails. Their script remains on the dials, unread by anyone, all the needles sitting where they have always sat. Their quarter stands a short walk from the foundry node — bunks, a mess hall, cups set down — and the last layer of every structure in the city is the same age, as if on some final day every worker laid down every tool at once. There was a last weld. There was a last bolt. The footprints walk from console to door to the cableway platform, and there they stop. Where they went is below the reach of lamps. The cableway still calls there. No one boards.

Nothing corrodes here; the air has almost nothing in it that bites, and water is a rumor kept in a vault. So the city does not decay, and it does not grow, and it cannot be said to wait, because waiting requires a future tense, and this world has only a present, declined endlessly.

It has rhythms, but no news. On the tallest spire a beacon still turns, sweeping its blade of light over the domes, the frozen ripples, the sleeping coilers, the iron forest, the nodes at their null-tasks — every forty seconds touching each thing and finding it unchanged. It is a message with no receiver. It is the world's way of checking.

Stand at the Rim. Behind you the foundry glows inside its small owned sphere of light, and the ingot train runs its ring, and the teletype prints no weather, and the oil makes its millionth circuit, warm. Below you the Basin lies in a dark deeper than dark, dust that pooled when the world still had the energy to pool it. Above you there are no stars, only the dim far galaxies and, if the decade is kind, one drifter, glowing faintly as it feeds. The air is warm and thin and smells of nothing. The dust holds footprints older than the shape of the land.

And under all of it, through the stone, through your boots, through the bone of your chest, the Note holds its one pitch — the sound of a city that found the exact center of doing and nothing and settled there, the way water settles, the way stone settles, the way everything settles when given enough time.

The Note does not resolve. It does not need to.
