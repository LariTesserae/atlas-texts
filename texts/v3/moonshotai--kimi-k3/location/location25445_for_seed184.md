The pit is no bigger than a village green, and it is full of warm water to the brim.

From the rim you look down through perhaps sixty feet of it. The top layer is clear enough, sunlit, ordinary. Below that the water changes — you can see the seam, a shimmer like heat-haze lying flat across the whole quarry — and below the seam the light gives out quickly. What you can make out at the bottom, on a bright day, is geometry: rooflines, a chimney field, a tower. A town, intact, standing at the bottom of its own excavation, glowing faintly from within.

They dug too deep here, generations back, and opened the warm aquifer. The water came up patient and unstoppable, a hand's width a year, and the town adapted the way mill towns adapt: they sealed what they could, raised what they could, piped the rest. The engines were rebuilt for drowning. The dynamos were jacketed. The people moved upward floor by floor as the water followed, and at some point — no record says when — the last of them went up the rim stair and did not come back down. The works never stopped. The same warm water that took the town is what runs it now, drawn up through the boreholes, put through the machines, let go. It is always third shift down there — the shift that never clocked out. A rusted sign on the gate tower still says so, under the algae: LOWER WORKS — SHIFT THREE.

Going down is a matter of crossing weather. The clear layer is cool and silent. The seam resists like a membrane and then lets you through into the brine — dense, warm as a held hand, heavy with minerals. Two rules assert themselves immediately. The first: the brine drinks sound. Down here a shout travels inches. Struck metal gives a dull knock you feel in your teeth rather than hear. The second: time thickens. Every clock in the town runs slow, each by its own private amount, and no two agree, and all of them are behind.

The town is small — you could swim its length in minutes — and it is packed past reason. It was built dense, worker cottages shoulder to shoulder against the mill hall, and then it grew denser. Decades of repairs laid pipe over pipe across the lanes until the lanes became tunnels of iron with a crawl of water through the middle. Everything mineral-precipitates here; every surface grows a rind, a fingernail a year, pale and hard, so that pipe, wall, roof and street are slowly fusing into one continuous body. Houses that stood apart now touch. Doorways that took a man now take an arm.

And the town is full of its own product. That is the other density. The works refine the brine — they precipitate a pale alloy, press salt, draw wire — and for a century or more nobody has collected the harvest. The warehouses filled first, floor to rafters with ingots. Then the cottages: ingots stacked in the parlors, salt in the chapel, the font crusted white with it, the schoolroom spooled floor to ceiling with wire on its drums. The lanes hold stacked sheet under the rind. The town is a crop never gathered, slowly storing itself inside itself.

There is one open street. The town breathes, and its breath keeps a single channel clear. At an hour you could set a watch by, if watches worked here, the intake towers gulp cool water from above the seam — the whole place inhales, and the black kelp on the rooflines combs over in one direction, and the weathervane on the chapel, which measures nothing anymore but this, swings to follow it. Hours later the town exhales: warm spent brine shimmering upward through the lane-axes, rising in a slow column to join the seam. The breath-street is the only way through the town that isn't a crawl. Even the current keeps to it.

At the heart is the Crank. A geothermal beam engine the size of a chapel, fed by the boreholes, working on a cycle so slow it seems meditative: one stroke every few minutes, the great beam rocking over, the flywheel carried by its own mass through the dead point. You do not hear it. You receive it, through the soles of your feet, through the water's pressure on your chest — a slow flex the whole town participates in, pipes ticking, lamps breathing a fraction brighter and dimmer. Once an hour, somewhere in the mill hall, a press cycles and stamps one more ingot nobody needs: a single low note felt in the sternum, then silence again for a long while. The loudest thing here is the press. The rest is listening.

Light comes in islands, and the islands are overgrown. The lamp grid still burns — sodium-orange here, mercury-blue there, half the fixtures dead and the living ones wearing beards of green filament that the light feeds. Each working lamp is a hanging garden, and the light arrives through the growth softened and greened, so the town is lit like a conservatory at dusk. In the precipitator hall, induction coils come up to a dull cherry glow when a batch runs, coals under ash behind the rind. Along the cable runs, silent blue discharge crawls in slow worms, fizzing without sound, the town leaking the electricity it has nowhere to send. Above it all, at noon, hangs the pale green coin of the surface, dim as a moon. At night there is no coin. The town's own lamps are the only light beneath the world, and the darkness between them is entire.

The vegetation is the brine's apology for the dimness: it lives on heat and chemistry more than light. Rust-red algae sheets the warm seams of brick. White and orange mats lace the vent field at the town's eastern fence, where the boreholes seep, and these are the pasture of everything. Black kelp anchors the rooflines, growing a finger's width a season, combed permanently sideways by the breath. And in what was a lane of allotments behind the cottages, sea-grass stands in rows — still in rows, a drowned vegetable garden keeping its geometry because nothing down here disturbs anything.

What lives here lives rarely. The pit loach — blind, white, the length of a finger — move through the culverts and flooded cellars in ones and twos, grazing the mats, and they spawn on the Crank's long cycle, tuned to the heartbeat like everything else. In the square an ancient crab the size of a dinner plate makes its rounds over days, wearing a garden of the rind on its back; the works crews used to have a name for that kind of unhurried seniority, and the name would still fit. Once in a long while a shadow crosses the green coin far overhead — a bird at the rim, something from the air-world looking down — and the needles on the dead gauges twitch, or seem to. There are no people. The boots are by the doors, the coats on their pegs under the rind, the beds made. It looks less like everyone left than like the town tidied them away and kept the house.

Nothing happens quickly, and the slowness has its own weather. Precipitate fines sift down through the brine like snow that never lands anywhere in a hurry. The rind thickens. Silt and ingots raise the floor a thought per year. Kelp fronds reach, by yearly inches, toward the Crank's moving beam. You can read the whole future of the place without any gift for prophecy: the lanes will seal, the cottages will fill, the rind will bridge the last gaps, and the town will become one solid warm body with a heartbeat — until a winter comes when the kelp finally knots the beam, or the rind bridges the cylinder, and the stroke stops. The silence, which is already almost total, will then become entire.

But that winter is not near. For now the beam rocks over. The lamps burn green through their gardens. The weathervane waits for the exhale. And if you hang in the breath-street long enough, in the warm dark between the light-islands, you may notice one lamp a little brighter than it was when you arrived — the grid offering a stranger the only greeting it has left — and somewhere below your feet, on a slow clock in a flooded tower, a hand is approaching an hour that no other clock in the town will agree is the hour, and when it arrives nothing will chime, because the brine drinks sound, but you will feel the intention of the bell, the way you feel the press and the heartbeat: through the water, through the bones, in the long patient present tense of the Lower Works, on third shift, forever.
