From the rim hills — the only old rock for a day's walk in any direction — the Soft looks like a city someone laid on unfinished dough. The basin spreads out low and flat under a plain, workaday sky, and the light is the strangest thing about it: perfectly ordinary light, the kind that falls on markets and washing lines anywhere, falling here on a world that has not finished becoming itself. Nothing below is weathered. Nothing is rounded by time. The hills you stand on are grey and patient, but the basin floor is the color of fresh-turned earth and living green, patched and re-patched like a quilt still being sewn, and at its center, packed so tight the roofs seem to hold each other up, is the city of Hush.

The ground of the Soft is young — not young the way a field is young, but young the way a child is young, growing in visible spurts, awkward, trying shapes. The basin accretes from below. Mineral-rich silt wells up through itself in slow swells the locals call heaves, and a heave can raise a street a hand's width in a season or a house's height in a week. Where the pressure concentrates, the ground extrudes: blisters of wet earth that split and push up forms. And here is the thing the surveyors write reports about and the locals simply live with — the forms are suggestive. A blister opens and inside is a staircase, four steps of compacted silt, climbing to nothing. A ridge rises overnight into a wall with something very like a doorway in it. There are fields on the eastern side, out toward the Raw, where arches stand in the mud like a thought the ground had and forgot mid-sentence. Old-timers swear the extrusions used to be shapeless, and that lately they have plumb lines. Whether the Soft is imitating the city or auditioning for it, nobody agrees, and the Listeners refuse to speculate on record.

There is almost no erosion here because the land builds faster than weather can undo it. It is a country with no ruins, only rehearsals.

Water is present the way a rumor is present — everywhere, rarely seen whole. It never really rains in the Soft; rather, moisture occurs. It condenses on every cold surface, and in the Soft almost every surface is cold. The great iron housings of the factories sweat continuously and are guttered to catch their own perspiration; the foundries drink from their own skin. Wells are dug, used for a year or two, and abandoned when a heave lifts them dry or tips them sideways. The real water table lives inside the silt itself as lenses and veins, and the city mines it: enormous screw-presses in the filter-works squeeze fresh sediment and yield a cloudy mineral water the locals call milk, which is settled, filtered, and piped uphill — uphill being wherever the ground recently went. Sometimes a lens bursts on its own and a new swell slumps into a brief pond that lasts a week, green with colonizing life before it drains back into the body of the land.

The cold is a steady, undramatic cold — breath-fog cold, wool-in-all-seasons cold. It never quite freezes the wet ground solid, but it slows every kind of decay to a crawl, which is why the buried parts of Hush are kept so well. And Hush is deeply buried in itself. The city is layered like sediment: streets that were ground floors a generation ago are cellars now, lit and inhabited, connected by staircases that descend past doorways at odd heights into the Underfloors, where the air is stillest and the lamplight never goes out. People say Hush has its basement full of itself. Cold preserves it all — paint, shopfronts, a child's chalk marks on a wall that is now thirty feet down.

The green comes fast behind every change, and the Soft is greener than any cold place has a right to be. Feltmoss roots in a day on fresh silt and spreads in a thick grey-green pelt. Greenwire, a sedge with roots like rebar, knits new ground into crust a cart can cross. Stitchgrass, coldwort, dwarf willows no taller than a knee, copper-birch that leans with the heaves and straightens after — all of it colonizing, all of it working. Because here is the engineering secret of the Soft: vegetation is infrastructure. A heaved street is raw soup until the turf goes down, so every road crew travels with rolls of living sod the way other cities travel with gravel, and the guild of turf-wardens is older and better paid than the masons'. Walls in Hush are not cleaned of moss; they are planted with it, deliberately, in patterns that tell the knowledgeable eye how stable the building's footing is. A wall bare of green in Hush is either brand new or bad news.

And everything is packed. Whatever ground has stayed stable longest — the ribs, they call them — is built on shoulder to shoulder, no yards, no gaps, houses sharing walls and chimneys, markets stacked on workshops stacked on Underfloors. Even out toward the Raw, where no one builds, the land is saturated: moss, sedge, eel-runs, birds, burrows, no empty inch. The density is partly necessity, partly temperament. In a world that won't sit still, people hold on to each other.

The industry is everywhere and almost impossible to hear, and that is the law beneath all other laws. Fresh silt is thixotropic — it remembers being water. Stamp your foot on a young street and feel it go soft under you. A dropped pallet, a slammed door, a shouted argument at the wrong moment on the wrong swell can turn a lane briefly liquid. So Hush is quiet the way a library is quiet, enforced and habitual and a little reverent. Hammers are wrapped in felt. Forge-bellows breathe through baffles. The great induction furnaces glow behind brick and roar at the volume of a pantry. Machinery is electrified — the basin runs on itself, heave-power, enormous counterweights and stone-filled crates that the swelling ground lifts over days and that then descend through geared accumulators, feeding flywheels, feeding the humming cable-web strung under every eave. The city quite literally runs on its own restlessness. Signals travel by flag and lamp and a swift, full sign language that children learn alongside speech. The Drift Bazaar, the great market that relocates weekly as the ground's moods dictate, is conducted at a murmur — merchants and buyers haggling furiously, whole fortunes changing hands in whispers and fingers, while the sledges creak softly and the felt-footed draft oxen shift their weight like snowfall.

What you hear, mostly: the low hum of the accumulators, a note you stop noticing and miss when it's gone. Dripping. The press-works breathing. Distant soft thumps, felt through boot soles as much as heard, of ground rearranging itself. And occasionally, in the night, the groan — a deep, slow sound with no clear direction, more in the teeth than the ears, that means something big is moving below. The Listeners, stethoscope-horns pressed to the soil in their little kiosk-towers, will have heard it coming days before. So will the heave-gulls.

The fauna of the Soft is abundant but mostly small, quick, and amphibious in spirit. Silt-eels thick as a wrist surface whenever the milk ponds form, eating the first algae, gone again when the water is. Burrow-owls nest in extruded arches the day after they rise, and the heave-gulls — grey, loud by local standards, tolerated anyway — gather over a swell two or three days before it breaks, having learned to hear what the Listeners hear, and serve the city as a feathered alarm system. Moss-mice and tunnel-voles riddle every turf roof. In the Underfloors live the pale things: eyeless eels in the seep-channels, white moths, a slow blind beetle the size of a thumb that the cellar-dwellers consider good luck and will not harm. The draft oxen, bred over generations for the basin, are broad-hoofed, patient, and unnervingly quiet; a good team moves a house on its sledge at walking pace, and in Hush houses move often.

The customs arrange themselves around the impermanence. Nothing precious is kept on a ground floor. Everything that can be bolted is bolted; everything that can be sledded is sledded. Every adult carries a probe-rod and uses it the way other people use an umbrella — reflexively, without looking. And there is the matter of the Returns. What the Soft swallows, the Soft gives back: a tool dropped into a bursting lens, a doll lost in a slumped lane, turns up years later in a fresh extrusion, mineral-cold, intact in the cold's keeping, and slightly wrong — grain raised on the wood, the doll's face faintly recast, as if the ground had been thinking about it. Families keep Returned objects on a specific shelf. Some people bury things on purpose, as an investment in strangeness. The dead, sensibly, are not buried; they go to the kilns, the only fire in the Soft permitted a voice, and the ash is worked into the turf-rolls, so that everyone, eventually, becomes infrastructure, holding a road together.

The city changes constantly, and the change is its own kind of weather. A heave begins, the gulls wheel, the Listeners raise the yellow lamp, and the anthill pours out: crews unbolting, sledges gliding, turf going down on the new contour like a bandage, the bazaar unpicking itself in an afternoon and reassembling two streets over by lamplight, all of it done at a whisper, thousands of people in choreographed haste making almost no sound. It is chaos conducted in a reading room. Stand on a rooftop — they are flat, for this purpose — during a heave event and watch a district move, feel the slow tide of it through your feet, see the green crews already seeding the fresh scar, and you understand that Hush is not built on the Soft so much as negotiated with it, daily, in low voices.

Out east, past the last ribs, is the Raw: the frontier of newborn ground, where extrusions happen daily and nothing has been decided. People go there on rest days, out onto the wicker watching-platforms, to sit in the ordinary daylight with their flasks and watch the world be made — an arch rising, a pond occurring, eels arriving from nowhere, the moss already rolling in like a slow green tide. It is the only place in the basin where you can hear the process itself: a wet, soft ticking and sighing, geology breathing through its nose.

And on the rim hills, where the rock is old and finished and does nothing, ever, there is a small hostel for Soft-born travelers who come up to experience stillness. Most cut the visit short. The silence up there is ordinary silence, dead silence, and it turns out to be the strangest thing of all — a ground with nothing to say. They go back down into the basin, back to the hum and the damp and the felt-wrapped urgency, back to their doors at odd heights and their mantel-shelves of Returned things, and sleep better for the groaning under the floor, the way you sleep better in a house where someone is awake, working, not yet done.
