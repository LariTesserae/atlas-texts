The Spire stands where the world runs out of floor.

It rises from a gorge that no one has ever seen the bottom of, because the bottom is where the cloud begins. From below — from the valleys, on the rare clear days — it is a pale needle drawn up out of a white sea, the last thing the earth says before the sky takes over. The treeline is a vertical mile beneath it. Weather is manufactured somewhere in between. The air up here is thin enough that a visitor breathes twice for every step, and water, if anyone tried to boil it, would give up and steam before it was ever hot enough to cook with. The old visitors knew this. They drank cold and ate dry. The stone bowls they left say so.

The stone itself is pale, fine-grained, and old in the settled way — not crumbling, not young and shedding, but long since worn into the shape it intends to keep. Its flanks are fluted, vertical channels cut by ten thousand years of wind-driven ice and running drip, smooth as the inside of a shell. It has stopped arguing with the elements and come to terms. Lichen grows in slow orange and black maps across faces that have not shed a pebble in living memory. Whatever the Spire is going to be, it already is.

It has climates stacked on it like bands on a shell. The crown — the summit, if a place this size can be said to have one — stands in clear cold sun most days, scoured, rime-growing, nearly bare. Below the crown lies the weeping belt, where the passing clouds press themselves against the stone and the stone drinks. Below that, the flutes descend into the gray, and then into the cloud, and then into whatever is under the cloud, which is not part of the place. The place ends where the sightline ends.

The crown is small. Forty paces across at its widest, and most of that is wind. A pocket of peat has gathered in a shallow basin there, centuries deep, black and spongy, holding sedge and cushion-moss and one dwarf shrub that grows flat to the ground like something pressed under a book. At the basin's center is a tarn no bigger than a room, fed entirely by drips and mist, dark as tea. Its level rises and falls with the cloud — high after a gray week, low after clear days — but it has never been seen to empty and never been seen to spill. It breathes. In it lives the Spire's only ground creature: a small dark salamander, slow with cold, that has never in its species' memory touched soil. The tarn is their entire earth. Around its rim, springtails stipple the wet moss, and a cold-loving bee works the sedge flowers in the single warm hour of noon.

Below the crown, the weeping belt is where the place keeps its green.

Every ledge down the fluted flanks is thatched. Moss hangs in beards. Ferns lean out of drip-line shadows. Saxifrage grips the runnels with roots like wire, and the turf of the wider ledges is deep enough to sink a hand into, built grain by grain from windblown seed, dead insects, and the white guano that stains every resting place in long streaks down the stone. The rock drinks fog through the night — you can watch it happen, the flutes darkening as they wet — and gives the water back through the middle of the day, weeping from a thousand seeps, each drip-line a slightly different tempo, a slightly different note where it strikes stone or pooled water or the lip of an old bowl. The gardens live on that schedule. They are lush the way a reef is lush: a thin, furious skin of life laid over something that is otherwise nothing.

Because that is the truth of the Spire: it is mostly nothing.

The place's real volume is the column of air around it, and that volume is empty in a way that takes time to feel. Stand on the crown and you are, most hours, the only animal on the summit acre. The colony — ten thousand spireswifts, and more in breeding season — clings to ledges a stone's throw away, but the stone's throw is all air, and the air is the point. Everything here is spaced by void. A bird crosses the gap between ledges and the crossing is an event. A feather comes loose and turns in the updraft for a full minute, alone in a cathedral of emptiness. A pebble kicked off the rim falls and falls and is never heard to land. Nothing that falls from the Spire has ever been found. That is the first rule the place seems to keep.

The second rule: there is no echo. A lone pillar in open sky returns nothing. Shout from the crown and the world takes your voice and gives no answer, and after a while that begins to feel like the loudest thing about the place — because the Spire is loud, almost always, and none of its noise is ever answered. The colony wakes at dawn in a screech-surf that comes off the flanks in waves. The flock turns all at once, hunting the wind, and the sound of it is canvas tearing. The drips tick and ping in polyrhythm through the warm hours. Ice cracks off the flutes at morning with a sound like distant planks breaking. A raven works the updraft in the afternoons, croaking into a void that refuses to croak back. It is the loudest silent place there is.

The swifts are the abundance here, and they spend it across an ocean of nothing. They feed on what the wind carries up — the aerial plankton, the midges and spiders and winged seeds lofted miles from the lowlands on the noon upwelling — so the Spire feeds from the sky the way a reef feeds from a current. The birds sleep clinging inside the flutes, packed head to tail, and some of them, outside the breeding season, never land at all: they ride the cloud-sea for months on the wing, and the Spire is the only ground their kind will ever know. With them come the others. A falcon pair holds the place as their hunting ground, and the hunt is the day's sharp punctuation — a whistle dropping out of the sun, the flock pouring sideways like water, a single note cut short. Snow-buntings glean the crown's bare stone. Spiders balloon in on threads and colonize the rims of the wet ledges. Everything arrives by air or not at all. Nothing lives here that cannot fly or cling. That is the third rule.

And then there is the Note.

When the wind comes from the west at a particular strength, the flutes take it like organ pipes. The whole Spire sounds — a low chord, felt in the breastbone as much as heard, with reedy harmonics braiding over the top. It is only physics: wind, stone, geometry. But when the Note rises, every bird on the mountain goes silent at once. Ten thousand voices, cut like a signal. The stillness holds until the chord dies, and then the noise floods back. The falcon has learned the rule too — it hunts in the Note's aftermath, when the colony will not call — and the colony has learned the falcon. When the stone sings, keep still. The rule protects nothing and exposes everything, and no one wrote it, and everything here obeys it.

People came, once, or come still, rarely — the mountain does not say. Their marks are few and old and being slowly taken back. On the last pitch below the crown, hand- and footholds were pecked into the stone long ago and have been polished since by use and then by weather; lichen grows in the palm-cups. At the crown's rim stands a low arc of stacked flat stones, chest-high, a windbreak or a watching blind, its lichen skin unbroken. And in the weeping belt, set precisely where three drip-lines fall, are three bowls hollowed from stone. Two still fill. The water in them is the clearest water imaginable — condensed from cloud, dripped off clean stone, water that has never touched ground anywhere in its existence. Whatever the climbers believed about it, they carried it down. One bowl is cracked and dry, and the ferns have moved into its shadow.

There is a cairn near the windbreak, knee-high, and it is not made of stones alone. Woven through it are feathers — swift feathers, hundreds of them, some fresh, most gone gray and soft with years — and wedged in a crack beneath it is a midden of eggshell fragments, layer upon layer, generations deep. Eggers came, then, and took, and left something back, whatever the taking cost them in conscience. At the cairn's heart, wrapped in what used to be fiber cord, is a single ornament of cold-hammered copper, green as the moss now, the most worked object on the mountain and nearly the only metal. A perished rope hangs from an anchor lower down, its bone toggle still knocking against the flute in wind — a small dry clack, like a bead counted, over and over, in a room where no one is counting.

In the hour after dawn, when the cloud-sea is high and the sun is low, the Spire casts its visitor's shadow down onto the cloud: a colossus a mile tall, haloed in rings of colored light, standing on the white floor of the world. It is the only other figure a person ever sees up here. It moves when you move. The birds ignore it entirely, which somehow makes it worse, or better.

The day turns over the place like a page. Dawn rime and the falling of ice-beads; the first birds out, wheeling; the noon upwelling and the feeding clamor and the weeping at full voice; the afternoon front arriving as a visible gray wall that walks across the sky and takes the Spire whole; the west wind finding its angle and the Note standing up out of the stone and every voice going down before it; the dusk return, the flutes filling with clinging bodies, the noise settling like sediment; the night freeze, the ticking, the wind worrying the old rope. Everything here is in motion, and almost none of the motion touches anything. Life is a thin film on a pillar of air. Between every living thing and every other living thing: a great deal of nothing, and the nothing is most of the place.

The valleys have their names for it — the Flute, the Ear, the thing the weather comes from. Up here it has no name and needs none. It drinks the cloud, weeps at noon, sings when the wind is right, and keeps its small green gardens and its ten thousand birds and its three stone bowls, two of them full of water that has never touched the ground.
