Begin with the mountains, because they are gone.

This basin held ranges once. Now it holds their memory. What remains is a plain so old it has finished changing — bedrock ground down to long bare swells, whalebacks of grey stone that rise no higher than a house and slide for half a mile before slipping under the water again. Between the swells there is wetness. Not marsh, exactly — marsh needs plants, and there are no plants here, none anywhere on the plain. Call them skins of water. They lie on the land the way condensation lies on cold glass, an inch deep, three inches, moving so slowly that *flow* is the wrong word. The water table lives at the surface, and it breathes. Over some days the sheets widen, creeping up the flanks of the whalebacks; over other days they sink back, leaving tide-lines of darkened stone. No one has found the sea this tide answers to. Perhaps there is no sea. Perhaps the deep rock itself inhales, and the plain is the slow fog of its breath.

The light never quite arrives. The sun, when it shows at all, is a pale coin rubbed thin behind layered cloud, spending a dim warmth that barely denture the cold. Mornings smoke with breath. Mist stands in the low reaches and refuses to lift. It is a day that never happens, repeating endlessly; shadows are faint and come from everywhere at once. Eyes, here, are auxiliary organs.

Every wet surface wears the film. Neither plant nor fungus — something older than the difference, a grey-velvet chemistry that eats the water itself: dissolved iron, sulfur, salts, the ground-down bones of the dead mountains. It is the first link of every food chain on the plain, and it glows. Faintly, coldly, a blue-green like the memory of a color. In the permanent dusk the whole basin is veined with this dim light, brightest where the water gathers, and it is the only saturated color the world permits itself.

The grazers are stonecattle: broad as overturned boats and shelled like them, moving in files across the whalebacks at the pace of weather, rasping the film with mouths like files. Where they pass, the film is stripped to bare stone and then heals back luminous, so each herd writes a bright sentence behind itself that takes days to fade. The dim plain is overwritten everywhere with this slow calligraphy. The herds are miles apart — you can watch a single file take an hour to cross your entire field of view — but the plain is enormous and they never stop moving. Nothing here is rare. Everything is far away. In the small scale, the water teems: skaters dimpling the skins, translucent thumb-sized things pulsing in the pools, sievers straining the film's loose spores. Scarcity is a matter of distance, never of number.

And then there is the sound.

It builds in layers. First the near sounds: the rasp of a herd, like files drawn down slate; the glug and gulp of vents where the breathing water swallows air; wind worrying the stones. Then, when the day's thin warmth peaks, the bellowsnewts begin. They lie in every sheet and pool, throat-sacs like wet leather bellows, and the first one sounds like a drum struck underwater, and the next answers from a half-mile off, and then ten thousand answer, and the chord assembles itself from horizon to horizon. It is felt in the sternum, in the fillings of the teeth. It sets water dancing in a cup. Overhead the drumwings clap their wing-bladders in rolling volleys; the vents gulp bass beneath. At the dusk-peak it is not possible to speak. It is barely possible to think in words. And on certain nights, when the chord wanders onto the right pitch, the land itself answers: particular whalebacks, hollow-flawed by some ancient accident, boom in sympathy — the answering stones — a note so low and slow that you hear it with your skeleton and your balance, not your ears.

Everything on the plain hides inside this noise. The pale stalkers hunt inside it — tall, soft-padded things with ears like folded trumpets, stepping through a din that masks their tread, because under the chorus their prey cannot hear them coming. Which produces the plain's central rule, learned early by everything that lives here: **silence is the dangerous weather.** When the chord falters — it happens, rarely, a wrongness sweeping through the pools — the whole basin freezes mid-rasp, mid-stride, and listens with its skin. Then the bellowsnewts find the note again, the herds resume, the world exhales.

The people of the plain are few, and they are weir-keepers. Their camps sit a half-day's walk apart on the lee of the whalebacks: rings of fitted stone roofed with stonecattle hide stretched over frames of rib. There is no wood. There has never been wood. They burn film-peat, cut in black sod from places where the film has died and layered for ages, and it burns low and blue and smells of brine and struck stone — the only warm light in the world, set against the film's cold one. They knap the only sharp edges in the world, because nothing natural is sharp anymore; the gravel is rounded like eggs, and the fossils inside it are ghosts, suggestions of shells worn past naming. A lucky hammer-strike splits a cobble on the shadow of something that lived before the mountains died, and the keepers set such stones aside without quite knowing why. Their metal, when they have any, is native copper found as nuggets in the wash, cold-hammered into blades that bend rather than break — precious, and worse than good stone.

Their works are scattered and patient. Weirs of fitted stone across the slow channels, funneling the swimming herds at migration. Causeways raised through the sheets, drowned and re-laid, new stone on old, generations deep, still following the same lines because those lines are the only dry logic in a wet world. And along every route, the singing cairns: one every mile or so, each built around a stone bored through with sand and a hollow drumwing bone and uncountable patience — tuned, so that the wind flutes a note out of it, and every cairn's note is its own. In the dim, the keepers travel by ear, walking from note to note the way other peoples follow stars.

Their language is shaped by the noise. Speech happens in the troughs between waves of the chorus — a sentence thrown into a lull, an answer caught in the next one — and at the great hours they abandon voice for gesture and for hide-drums, talking across a camp in codes of threes and fives. Children learn that the plain has accents: the chord voices deeper near the Rims, reedier in the shallow south, and a child can tell where she is with her eyes shut, and usually keeps them shut, since there is so little to see and so much to hear. They count the year by blooms — after each rain, when grey veils walk the flats and the film brightens and thickens, and the herds turn toward the brightness, and the bellowsnewts double, and the whole world gets louder.

The Rims stand at the basin's edge, impossibly far, in haze: the stumps of the mountains, low and rounded and done. All the plain's water is born up there, seeping down into the sheets. No one goes. The chord grows thin at the edges of the world, and the keepers say the quiet there is inhabited. They call their country the Sounding, when they call it anything at all.

Stand at a cairn at the dusk-peak and the note of it flutes past your head while the chord swallows every other thing. Out on the sheet, a file of stonecattle writes its slow bright sentence across the dark. The water in your cup shivers. Somewhere far off an answering stone takes up the bass and holds it, holds it — and then, for one breath, the entire chord falters. The plain goes silent as a held breath; everything that lives stops and listens — and then the bellowsnewts find the note again, the herds resume their rasping, the stone lets the bass go, and the Sounding goes on being what it has been for longer than there are words: finished, and full, and loud.
