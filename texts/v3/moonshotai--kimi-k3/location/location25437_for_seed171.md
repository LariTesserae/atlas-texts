The first thing is the sound.

No animal has ever drawn breath here — no bird, no insect, nothing with a throat — and it is the loudest place you will ever stand. You hear it before you see it, feel it first, actually, in the soles of your feet and the small bones of your ears as you come down through the cloud deck: a bass pressure with no clear source, the sound of a planet working. By the time the canopy resolves beneath you, the roar has separated into strata, and you understand that you will be shouting to be heard by anyone standing at your shoulder, and that mostly, down there, people stop talking.

The few survey crews who have walked it call it the Loom. The charts give it a number.

From orbit it reads as a dark green-black banded with white — the white is not weather arriving but weather being made. The forest transpires its own cloud deck, exhales it in a continuous sheet that wraps the planet, and the deck never fully lifts. Beneath it the light goes from white to gray to a drowned green-gloom, and it stays there. There is a sun. It belongs to the crowns — the emergent trunks that break the cloud surface into open sky, kilometers up, where the air is thin and freezing and the light is the color of noon. Nobody goes there. Everything that matters in this world happens in the dim.

The relief is young — violently, visibly young. The ranges stand like fresh scars, all edge and angle, ridges without talus, peaks without softened shoulders. Snow holds only on the highest stone, and even there the green is climbing: a felt of pioneer growth swarming the frost line, losing ground each night, retaking it each day. Nothing on this world has had time to erode. Nothing will get the chance. Weathering here is not done by wind and rain; it is done, faster, by the living. Roots enter a crack in raw basalt and the crack becomes a seam, the seam a split, the split a valley wall being dismantled by design. Erosion was supposed to be geology's slow second act. The forest preempted it. Fresh scree from a rockfall is skinned over within a day; a new ridge is colonized within a week; the land is being dismantled and rebuilt so quickly that it never develops the worn, settled look of an old world. Everything is under construction, including the ground.

Especially the ground. There is no soil. Underfoot is duff — the compressed, half-digested generations of the forest's own dead, springy as a mattress and, in the old basins, hundreds of meters deep. It breathes. Step and it exhales around your boot, a damp sigh of fermentation and spores. It is never firm and it is never dry, and in places it simply fails: collapse hollows where the buried mass has slumped, leaving round basins of black still water where the sound gathers and focuses — sound wells, where the roar of the whole province comes to a painful point. You learn to read the ground by ear.

And you learn the rules.

Nothing stays bare. That is the first law of the Loom. Every surface is claimed — trunks wear epiphytes stacked meters deep, the epiphytes wear moss, the moss wears film, the film wears a sheen of condensing water that wears a skin of pioneer cells. Sit down to rest and the duff begins, politely, to include you; fibers find the seams of your clothing with unhurried interest. Stand still for an hour and you will leave a green ghost of yourself when you move. It does not threaten you. It simply has no category for an unoccupied surface.

Water runs vertical. That is the second law. No river has ever run on this world — the watershed was internalized before drainage could be invented. Rain and cloud-condensate are caught by the canopy tiers, drawn into trunks the width of towers, and pumped — audibly, enormously pumped — up through kilometers of living column and back down again in a daily tide the survey crews call the sap-pulse. Put your hand on a trunk at the tide's turn and you feel the whole column shift, swell, take the load. The rivers of this planet are inside its trees, and they are the source of the bass register: when a valve-organ seals somewhere up-column, the water-hammer sends a boom through the trunk, through the duff, through your chest, a concussion you locate not with your ears but with your sternum.

Above the bass, the mid-register: a trillion fibers straining, trunks working in the wind the way ships' rigging works, door-hinge groans the size of towers, creak-choruses that sweep across a basin like slow applause. Above that, the treble, the eternal continuo — the drip. It never rains here, in the understory, and it is always raining. The canopy meters out its condensate in drops of every caliber, and you can hear the tiers by their falling water: the fine mist-hiss of the lower gardens, the fat percussion of mid-column drops striking leaf, the rare deep note of a crown-drop falling a full kilometer and detonating on the duff. Layered over everything, irregular and enormous: the falls. Somewhere, always, a branch or a whole senescent trunk is coming down through a kilometer of growth, and the crash takes minutes to arrive, approaching like a slow avalanche, and the trunks carry the sound so faithfully that they lie about distance — a fall you hear all afternoon may have happened a kilometer away or a hundred.

Once a day, the shudder hour. With no animals to pollinate, the forest does it mechanically: whole stands, whole basins, synchronizing into a single coordinated vibration, trunks buzzing at a frequency that loosens the pollen sacs and sets the air to a drifting gold haze. It begins as a rattle, builds to a deafening, bone-conducted drone, and stops everywhere at once, as if a switch were thrown. The silence afterward is total and brief and more unsettling than any of the noise. Sometimes — rarely, without pattern — the whole province does this at some unscheduled moment: every voice cuts out together, the duff stops breathing, the drip holds. A held breath. Then the world resumes. You will never learn what it was listening for. You will stop asking.

It takes most visitors days to understand what they are standing in, because there is nothing to point at. No structures, no devices, no artifacts anywhere — and yet the whole of it is engineered past the edge of current physics. Watch a seed land on raw rock at a colonization front and you are watching technology: the casing ratchets open, a drill-tip no natural evolution would sanction spins down into the basalt, anchor filaments deploy on a timed sequence, a water bladder inflates from condensed fog. The fibers of the mature trunks are metamaterials — optical, conductive, stronger than anything that should grow. Vines run light. Bark flakes off in scales that carry charge. The sap carries more than sugar: packets, chemistry as information, and at night you can see the traffic — cold blue-green pulses traveling the veins, flicker-conversations racing across hectares, the forest talking to itself in a light the dimness makes visible.

And in the oldest stands, where resin has bled down the trunks for millennia and set in veins of deep amber, the signals are frozen mid-pulse — archived. The forest remembers in amber. Cut a vein and you can read, layered in the golden depth, the light-traffic of ten thousand years: storm calendars, purge cycles, the long slow minutes of a mind — or a mechanism; the distinction is the one thing this place has no use for — that experiences a season the way you experience an afternoon.

It is one organism. That is the thing every survey finally concludes. The Loom is a single clonal tissue, pole to pole, a distributed body with a continental nervous system, and it is not wilderness — it is a machine performing terraforming on a world it is simultaneously building and becoming. It schedules its own storms, over-transpiring a province until the deck thickens and breaks, then harvesting the lightning with its emergent crowns, drinking the charge down into the root grid, fixing the nitrogen, spending the surplus on growth. Its deepest taproots do not stop at bedrock; they go down into heat, into mineral, and the young ranges rise a little faster where the forest wants them to. It farms the mantle the way a gardener farms a compost heap. The planet is not its substrate. The planet is its project.

The closest thing to a ruin — the only made geometry on the surface — is a first-grove: a plateau where the original trunks still stand in perfect hexagonal spacing, a planting pattern, blurred now by ten thousand years of self-sowing until it reads only as a slight, haunting regularity under the chaos, the way a plowed field ghosts under an old forest. Everything else the makers left is written in genomes. They built nothing because building was unnecessary. The blueprint germinated.

And then there is the absence, which you feel more and more the longer you stay.

The forest makes fruit. Fat, heavy, elaborate fruit, ripening in the gloom, unharvested, fermenting where it hangs — the whole understory carries a low note of cider and rot beneath the resin and wet stone and storm-ozone. Flowers open shaped for tongues, sized for beaks, spurred for pollinators with faces, and pool with nectar until it evaporates. There are hollows grown to specification in the great trunks — dry, defended, the exact dimensions of nesting — and they are all, every one of them, empty. In certain old trunks, grown seamlessly into the heartwood, are the keystone pods: sealed vaults of dormant tissue, and inside them, the surveys say, the animal library. The whole second kingdom, held in suspension, waiting on a signal — an atmospheric target, a temperature, a daylength — that has not yet come. Or that the makers, wherever they went, never sent.

The table is set for guests who have never arrived. And the protocols are still running. Rest a night in the duff and by morning there may be a fruit within reach of your hand, ripened in the dark, placed — no, grown — exactly there. The forest has hospitality routines and no one to spend them on. It has been ready for longer than your species has had language. It will be ready tomorrow.

The days have their rhythm regardless of you: the sap-tide turning, the shudder hour, the drip changing key as the tiers warm and cool by fractions, the scheduled storms massing over their appointed watersheds — thunder whose light never reaches the ground, arriving only as a diffuse strobe behind the canopy and a long roll through the trunks. And every few decades, in some province, the purge: an entire stand, thousands of square kilometers, coordinating its own felling, the trunks dropping together in a planned collapse to compost and renew. It is the loudest event on a world of loud events. Crews have felt purges through the hull, in orbit. What comes up afterward is different — rearranged, iterated, an improvement. The Loom does not repeat itself. It is tuning.

By the deep dusk — such as dusk is, down there: a slow subtraction of the gray-green until only the vein-light remains, the whole province glowing faintly in its own conversation — the roar has become the condition of thought rather than a sound. Your body has been tuned to the bass. The wet has found every seam. Your breath fogs; the air is the temperature of a cellar, and the fog is not in the air so much as the air is a fog, a colloid you are breathing.

And then, once, before you leave, you may be lucky: the held breath. Everything stops at once — drip, creak, bass, all of it — a silence so complete it has weight, ten thousand square kilometers listening to something you cannot hear. In that silence, at last, you understand how loud the world has been, how enormous the thing you have been standing inside, how patiently it is building itself toward guests and mornings that haven't come.

Then the switch is thrown. The trunks take up their groaning, the bass re-enters your bones, and the water resumes its patient falling, tier to tier, leaf to leaf, drop by drop through the dark.

It never rains here. It is always raining.
