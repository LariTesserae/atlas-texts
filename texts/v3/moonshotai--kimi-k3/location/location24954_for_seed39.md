The first thing is the air, which has been thinned to a working minimum. Every breath arrives half-empty. Your heart — a small, underpowered motor — has to run twice its normal speed to do its ordinary work, and it lets you know. The light is the second thing: dim, grey, even, falling from everywhere and nowhere, the way light falls in a hall with very high windows. The sun is a pale coin behind a haze that is not quite cloud. It is steam. The whole basin breathes, and its breath never quite dissipates in the cold, so the place works all day inside its own exhalation.

Then, beneath the silence, you notice the hum. It is not loud. You would not call it a sound at all until you kneel and put a hand flat on the rock, and then it is unmistakable: a low, continuous tremor, a machine-room note, felt in the wrist bones and the teeth. Something below is running. Something below is always running.

Because this is, in every way but the literal one, an industrial site. Stand at the basin's edge and the resemblance is total. Steam rises from a hundred vents in white columns, each with its own pitch — hiss, chuff, roar, kettle-whistle — and when the wind crosses the field it draws low chords out of the throats, an organ being idled. Around the older vents the rock has built itself into chimneys and collars of pale sinter, crusted with sulfur in yellow flowers, ringed with rime that grows in feathers *into* the steam, against the wind, the way hoarfrost insists on growing. The runoff channels are dyed in bands — rust-red, ochre, arsenic green, black — mineral discharge staining the ground in production colors. And on the far wall, exposed where a young glacier has just peeled back from it, stands a cliff of hexagonal basalt columns, upright and bundled, regular as a pipe rack, as cable conduit, as organ pipes — every one of them cast by nothing but cooling.

Nothing here was made. That is the rule you have to keep relearning. No wall, no tool, no road, no mark. The floor is tiling itself — the cold sorts the stones into nets and rings and long stripes, frost pushing pebbles to the edges of invisible cells until the flats look paved, laid, grouted — and it is only physics, laying its own tiles. The terraces below the hot springs accrete a fingernail's width of silica a month, pouring themselves in white steps like slow concrete. It is all resemblance, and it is all real: an industry without industry, production without product.

Or rather — the product is the place itself. What is being manufactured here, ton by ton, century by century, is altitude. This range is young, still rising, and the work is visible everywhere. The ground is first-generation ground. There is no soil worth the name, only rock flour, grey and alkaline on the tongue, milled by ice that was here last winter. The scree is sharp enough to cut; nothing has had time to round. There are no old moraines anywhere, no terraces from previous glaciers, no inherited drainage. The rivers have not decided anything. They braid and unbraid themselves across the outwash flats overnight, trying out signatures, abandoning them by morning. The lake at the glacier's snout — milky, opaque, the color of wet cement — is dammed only by its own fresh rubble, and it does not keep. Every few years it lets go all at once, and a flood rewrites the lower valley in an afternoon, and the basin tries a new draft.

Water here is a commuter, crossing the same border three times a day. At dawn the flats crunch: the ground has spent the night extruding needle ice, filaments of frost pushing up like filings, lifting the grit on tiny glass legs. By noon, in the sun, everything weeps — the rock faces below the snowbanks run wet, the flats soften, the milky streams swell. By dusk it is all glazing over again. Boiling streams leave the springs at near a simmer, run a few hundred steaming meters through air fifteen degrees below zero, and freeze progressively into their own beds, so that a single channel is liquid, then slush, then a ribbed sheath of ice over still-running water, then silence. Snowfields give back the dim light from below, so the gloom glows. Occasionally the cloud rips and a blade of real sun comes down and spotlights one vent field, one stretch of terraces, the way a foreman's lamp picks out a workstation — and then moves on.

The shift never stops, and once in a while the plant tests its wiring. When a new vent opens — a cough of ash, a torn place in the ground — the rising plume crackles and sparks to itself, brief violet stitches inside the steam, charge separating in the roiling column. In dry storms the ridgelines build static until your hair lifts; now and then the tips of the basalt organ glow faintly blue. At night, when the aurora comes on, it comes on like a busbar energizing — great silent conductors of green light closing a circuit across the sky, and below them the steam columns stand silvered in the starlight, and the hum continues, and the mountain works the dark shift as it works the day one.

What is startling, in all this manufacture, is the life — because there is a great deal of it, provided you understand how it is arranged: as an archipelago. The basin in total is rich, and any given acre of it is almost nothing. You can walk three hours across ash and fresh talus where nothing lives at all, not visibly, not yet — ground no foot has touched and no foot will touch again for years — and then come over a rise into a vent-fed oasis and find it crowded.

The oases run on stolen heat. Certain talus slopes are warmed from below, so the rocks are frost-free in midwinter, and in these live colonies of small hay-making lagomorphs, round-eared, shrill-voiced, stacking little cocks of dried cushion-plant between the stones against the winter, their sharp *eep, eep* one of the few sounds the basin makes on its own. Along the warm spring channels the water carries a felt of algae and bacteria, green and rust and orange, the true meadows of this place — most of the green here is not plant at all. Midges hatch from the warm water in columns of gauze, even in the cold months, and on the midges come down shorebirds with long decurved bills, and snowfinches that work the steam-line where melt meets frost, and — in one outflow, in water that never leaves the temperature of a tepid bath — a frog the color of weak tea, which lives its whole small life within the warm reach of a single spring, and would die of the climate an arm's length away.

Foxes work the talus colonies. A condor rides the vent thermals, using the rising steam-heat as a freight elevator, spiraling up without a wingbeat to its enormous altitude, reading the basin for what has died — because what dies here keeps, freeze-dried, and the condor's patience is legendary and usually rewarded. At night, sometimes, geese cross high overhead on their way over the range, invisible, and their calls fall out of the dark like small hardware spilled in a stairwell, and then the dark closes again.

Between the islands: the void. This is the other law of the place, the one that governs all the rest. The distances are enormous and mostly empty, one animal at a time, one oasis per half-day's walking. The silence is a physical substance — no leaves to stir, no insects in the open cold, the thin air carrying sound poorly and strangely, so that a pika sounds near and a rockfall sounds far and both judgments are wrong. The loud events are rare and therefore enormous: the rifle-crack of the glacier calving into its milky lake, the brief clatter of a fall of fresh rock, the geyser on its rough schedule clearing its pressure in a rush of steam like a shift whistle. Otherwise: the hum, the vents, your own working heart.

And the emptiness has this one consequence — the ground keeps a ledger. With no traffic and no thaw to speak of, a footprint in the frost-sorted silt lasts for weeks. Crossing the flats you occasionally find tracks you cannot explain: a fox's line of dots going nowhere, ending at nothing; the stitched trail of a shorebird between two vents; once, perhaps, your own, from a previous pass, already becoming archival.

The light never strengthens. The dimness is permanent weather — steam-haze by day, and at night the hard stars and the green wiring of the aurora and the silvered columns of the vents. It is, you realize, working light. It is the light of a place that is not finished and is not resting: light to build by.

Because that is the deepest rule here. Nothing is old. Nothing is inherited. Nothing is finished, and nothing was made — and yet everything is manufactured, continuously, at scale: rock extruded and shattered and milled, terraces poured, floor tiled, water frozen and melted and boiled, altitude itself pressed upward a tremor at a time. Lie down at night on the cold ground and the hum comes up through your shoulder blades, steady as a dynamo. The mountain is building. It has only just begun. What it is building is this — and it will be working on it, shift after shift, long after the steam has taken your footprints and the condors have inherited the sky.
