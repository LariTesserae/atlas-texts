The Stack ends in a fist of stone above the weather, and just below the knuckle, behind a wheel-locked hatch no wider than a doorway's whisper, there is a room.

Inside the hatch the makers left their stencil in grey paint: APEX RELAY — CELL Ø. Beneath it, in chalk, in a hand that clearly lingered over the letters: THE CHOIR. The chalk has never smudged. Nothing in this room smudges anything.

Outside the hatch there is a grate of balcony and then the drop — the Stack's flank falling away into a thin violet sky where stars are visible at midday, the air too lean for weather, for birds, for anything. The last ladder descends inside the mountain past dead landings and dead switchboards into a dark that no one climbs anymore. The last climber went down, once, and that was the end of traffic. What remains is the cell, and the cell remains exactly.

The first thing is the sound.

It is loud — genuinely, physically loud — and it arrives as much through the bones as the ears: through the soles of the feet, the hinges of the jaw, the fillings of the teeth. A bass drone from the turbine in the flue, where the standing wind of the range — a lee wave pinned in place by the shape of the peaks, blowing always from the same quarter at the same strength — spins a rotor no larger than a dinner plate on magnetic bearings that have nothing to wear out. Over that, the mid hum of transformers. Over that, the high clean whistle of the flue itself, a flute bored through the summit stone, singing its single note. And riding on all of it, faint and unmistakable, a chord: soft overtones braided above the drone, unresolving, undissonant, held.

Loud, and yet the room is dead as a studio. The felt on every surface drinks the echo, so a voice dies at the lips. It is an anechoic loudness — the volume of an engine room with the intimacy of a confessional. After an hour the ear edits it all out, and what remains audible is only the listener's own pulse, and a faint, slow crackle from the garden, like applause so distant it never arrives.

The light is the second thing, because there is so little of it. A few amber pilot lamps. Heating elements glowing dull orange behind their grilles, never cycling, never brighter. One red jewel on the relay panel, unblinking — a line-fault indicator lit so steadily and so long that it has stopped meaning something is wrong and has come to mean the room. And along the tips of the plants, a blue-green coronal frost, the garden's own discharge-glow, constant as paint. The cell has exactly one lighting state, the way a painting has exactly one weather. Shadows do not move here. They are part of the fixtures.

The cold is pinned a few degrees above freezing and has never been otherwise. The heaters hum and hold it there; the thermostat is neither satisfied nor unsatisfied; it is simply, permanently, at its mark. Metal bites. Breath would bloom and hang. The only warmth that is not machinery is the mat itself, faintly warm to the palm, like the back of an old set that has been left on.

The mat. The pelt. The garden — because that is what it is, though nothing here has ever been wet.

It covers everything. It lies meters deep across the floor in a springy, teal-black felt whose filaments are silicate and copper at their cores, tipped with the glowing frost. It climbs the walls and swallows the machines until their housings show only as swells, like knees under a blanket. It furs the ceiling with hanging root-felt. It braids through the cable trays in tendrils so exactly like wire — copper-cored, insulating-sheathed — that wiring and vine are one taxonomy now, and no blade could divide them. Beneath the living layer lie the vitrified dead: the garden's ancestors, resorbed and compacted into a glassy peat that floors the room with its own history.

Clustered where the field is strongest stand the reeds: bundled, hollow stalks like the pipes of a small organ, each one tuned. They are what makes the chord. The drone excites them, their resonance pumps their strange internal chemistry, and the chemistry — a conductive ionic gel that flows where sap would flow and never freezes and never evaporates — drinks the stray electricity the machines leak into the air. The plants eat the hum. The piezocrystal lichens crusting the brass eat the vibration directly. The felt damps the machines and is fed by their trembling; the machines are fed by the wind, and the wind is fed by the shape of the mountains, and the shape of the mountains is finished changing. Which of these domesticated the others is a question the room has eroded smooth.

No water has ever touched this place, and the consequences are everywhere and permanent. Rust began on the iron fittings once, when the makers still brought their damp breath in with them, and stopped when they left — half-finished orange blooms, arrested mid-spread, that will never now complete. Nothing rots. Nothing mildews. The logbook on the workbench lies open and mummified, its ink perfectly legible, its last entry banal and unbearable: *Hum steady. Reeds answering at the fifth again. Bearing 4 warm. Closing the hatch.* The pencil lies where it finished rolling, against the bench foot, half-felted into the pelt. A mug stands with its dregs fossilized into a mineral rime. The tools rest in outline under a dusting of felt, each one visible as a shape, like tools in a barrow, like a language the room is slowly forgetting how to read.

Nothing eats anything here. There are no moths at the lamps, no mites in the mat, no webs, no nests in the flue. The trembling of the leaf-tips is the most animal motion the room contains, and there is nothing animal behind it. The only metabolisms are vegetable and mechanical, and both of them sip the same drone.

The gauges have pointed at their marks for so long that the dials have faded everywhere except in the narrow blade of paint each needle has shielded — every needle with its own pale afterimage beneath it, proof of the unpointing, the unwavering. An electric clock sweeps its second hand in silence. It is one of the three motions permitted: the hand, the fan blades, the trembling of the tips. All of them cycles. All of them return to their beginnings. Nothing accumulates.

Even the air is occupied. Fine conductive dust stands suspended in fixed lattices, held in the charge gradients between panel and pelt, tracing the room's invisible field the way filings trace a magnet — a motionless galaxy of motes, a diagram of the electricity drawn in the air itself. The air has furniture. Nothing settles; nothing clears. Disturbance has simply not occurred.

This is the deepest fact of the cell: it is over. Change happened here — all of it — and it has concluded. Erosion did its work and was allowed to finish; every surface is either intact or fully converted, every patina complete, every flake already fallen, every accident already had. A leaf on the pelt dies at exactly the rate its replacement completes, and is resorbed where it lies. The pressure balances through the flue. The temperature holds. The chord neither resolves nor wants to. The relay still holds open its line to a world that stopped calling centuries ago, boosting a carrier tone with no message on it, talking to itself in readiness — and that readiness, too, is constant, and therefore is not waiting. Waiting implies a change at the end of it.

The room does not wait. The room keeps.

Somewhere in the braid of drone and chord, beneath the stenciled designation and the keeper's soft chalk renaming, the Choir holds its one note — loud, dim, cold, packed to the ceiling with life that has never known water, at the top of a mountain that has finished being a mountain — not in hope of an answer, not in memory of the makers, but because holding the note is what the room is now.

The red jewel does not blink. The chalk does not smudge. The reeds go on answering at the fifth.
