You come down into it from the rim, downhill for hours, the ground giving back heat through your soles long before you see anything. There is nothing to see. The dark above and the dark around are the same dark; the horizon never resolves because there is nothing for it to resolve into. What resolves is below. A tracery of dim, pale lines spread across the floor of a vast low bowl — streets, plazas, canals, the long intervals between districts — drawn in weak greenish light, thin as a fingerprint's ridges. From above it looks less like a city than like a map of a city. This turns out to be correct in a way that takes time to understand.

The city is stone, but no stone was ever cut. Everything is accreted — travertine, tufa, flowstone, moonmilk — deposited grain by grain out of warm mineral water that has been doing this one work for longer than the word *long* comfortably holds. The towers are single pieces, grown hollow around voids that have the shapes of doors and windows without ever having been openings. Stairs rise into ceilings. Arches admit you to a view of solid wall. There are cisterns with no inlets, plazas drained by nothing, a colonnade where every column leans at the same three degrees in the same direction, like a habit. It is the full grammar of a city spoken fluently and meaning nothing — a city recalled rather than built, and recalled by something that never saw one.

No brick. No beam. No cord, no shard, no mark of any tool, because there has never been a tool here, never a hand. Nothing in this place was made. Everything in this place was *continued*.

The light comes up from below. There is no sun and never has been; the sky is known only by its drips. The water itself carries the light — a pale, cold, microbial luminescence, milk-green near the warm springs, dimmer and bluer in the standing shallows — so that whatever holds water holds light, and the visible city is exactly the flooded city. Canals glow brightest where they are deepest. Dry stone is simply black and gone. Everything is lit from beneath: shadows fall upward, pooling on the undersides of arches and ledges and stair flights, giving every structure the upturned lit face of something being questioned from below. Heat comes from below too — the basin floor is warm, the springs near-boiling in the low vents — and the rain, when it has finished falling, partly goes back up. It is a world whose sun is its ground.

About the rain. It falls down, warm, in cycles from drizzle to a drumming that the humid air muffles to almost nothing. And it falls up. In the still hours, from certain still puddles, drops detach from the surface and rise — slowly, wobbling, unhurried as seeds. Where the up-rain meets the down-rain, the drops hang and merge into floating beads, spheres the size of fists, of heads, drifting in the middle air for a while before the mass decides on a direction. What rises does not return. The sky keeps a tithe of everything, and no one has ever seen what it does with it.

The first law of the place, learnable by walking: likeness is a force here, the way gravity is a force elsewhere. Form precipitates toward image. This is why districts repeat. The plaza of the leaning colonnade exists seven times across the basin, identical down to the drip-points and the stain-fans of rust — and each recurrence is a different *age* of it. One is fresh, its edges as sharp as anything gets here, its terraces still dripping. One is worn to stubs and rounded like old teeth. The others lie between. Walking from copy to copy, you walk the district's biography; space holds what in other worlds time would have to. Erosion in this place is not loss. It is revision.

And the water shows the city slightly ahead of itself. Watch a drop fall from a ledge: the reflection flashes where it will land a half-moment before it lands. The reflections of towers carry wear the towers do not yet have. The stone follows the image — deposition traces what the reflection shows — so that the whole city is, cycle by cycle, building itself toward its own mirrored face, converging, never arriving. The not-arriving is the balance. For every grain the water dissolves it deposits a grain; the city neither grows nor fails; it persists, each long cycle leaving it a finger's width closer to the thing in the water. What happens when stone and image finally meet is not recorded anywhere. The process is in no hurry to find out.

Life is sparse and has given up on green. In the warm shallows stand translucent rushes, glassy and faintly amber, drinking mineral heat through their roots — one stand per hectare, sometimes one per district, the emptiness around them the ecology's main fact. Rarely, a fan-tree: layered sheets of pale tissue folded like a book left open in the bath, sweating water, tall as a tower and centuries slow. In the cold rooms — the anomalies, chambers of genuine cold pooled inexplicably in the heat, migrating from building to building across the cycles like a slow, patient, unalive animal — black velvet films spread over the stone and bead with dew. That is all. There are no animals. Nothing skims the water, nothing burrows, nothing eats the rushes; there is no guano on the ledges, no gnaw-mark, no track that is not yours. It is the silence of a world with no witnesses, and it has a texture: your steps, and the faint brief brightening of the shallows where you wade, the glow stirred up and fading behind you, footprints of light unwriting themselves.

Mostly it is quiet. The catalogue of sounds is short and each entry is spaced out in time: the drip intervals, slower than any clock's; the faint tick of stone growing, like cooling metal at a hundredth the speed; the long sigh of a steam vent turning over. Echoes arrive wrong — late, and from below, and lowered a fraction, as though the reflection under the water were answering in its own voice. Rarely, somewhere far off across the basin, a siphon clears its throat with a deep swallow, or a slab lets go of a tower and the muffled air delivers the news minutes late. Between events, the heat holds its breath.

The basin breathes. Over long unmarked spans — there is no day to count them by — the water table rises until the streets stand knee-deep and the whole plan brightens, lines swelling, plazas becoming dim luminous fields. Then it withdraws, and everything it leaves behind wears a fresh white skin, and the city has been adjusted, slightly, toward the images waiting in the water. The steam fields pulse on their own schedule. The cold rooms drift. The rains exchange their tithes with the sky. All of it overlaps, none of it hurries — the world is active the way a held chord is active, many notes, no motion you could point at.

Stand long enough in any plaza and the rules assemble themselves out of observation: everything here comes from below. Likeness pulls. The sky keeps what it takes. Nothing is made; everything is continued. And the city continues — settled, weathered, in perfect balance with its own undoing, lit from beneath, warm as a mouth, repeating its quarters into the dark like verses of something it cannot finish and does not need to.

Look back once, crossing the last flooded plaza toward the rim. Your footprints have not faded. The mineral water has kept them — is keeping them — laying down a thin white trace along each step, precise, patient, already a little more permanent than the stone around them. The city has noticed a new form, and the first law is likeness, and the city is polite and has nothing but time. Somewhere, cycles from now, in a district you never visited, a second set of footprints will finish precipitating — a little older than yours, a little more worn, waiting for the third.
