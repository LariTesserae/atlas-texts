**The Basin Under the Ceiling**

The high country is dry — wind-scoured plateau, rust and black stone, gravel so fresh it rings underfoot like broken pottery. Then the land simply stops. The Rim is a young escarpment, a few hundred meters of sharp strata, unrounded, unsoftened, as if cut last year. Standing on its edge, you look down into a basin perhaps forty kilometers across, and the first thing you understand is that the sky here is different.

There is no sun. There has not been one for a long time. Over the basin hangs the Ceiling: a permanent deck of pearl-gray luminance, seamless, sourceless, brighter than the ground below it, so the whole world is lit from above by something like the memory of afternoon. Noon and morning differ only by degrees of gray. Shadows, when they exist, are faint smudges. Colors come muted — jade, slate, bone, the wet black-green of deep leaves — and white things, flowers and herons and painted door-marks, seem to carry their own faint light. The air is warm and stays warm, humid, nearly windless. It is a dusk that never ends and never deepens into true dark, because at night the Ceiling turns charcoal and slow ripples of dim color cross it — violet, pale green, a slow aurora the inhabitants call the Slow Lights — and the basin floor answers with its own sparse constellation of blue seams and glowing cisterns.

The Ceiling is not weather. This becomes clear slowly. It is too level, too permanent, and it sips: some fraction of the light that should reach the ground never does, drawn off into the deck itself. The people below say the Ceiling keeps its tithe of photons, and pays back in water.

·

Descending the Rim, you pass through the fog-forest margin. Cool air spills over the escarpment at night and pools in the basin's swales — the basin is low, its floor below the level of the old sea — and where the fog banks linger longest, the graymantle trees grow: dark, umbrella-crowned, their leaves drawn out into long drip-tips that comb moisture from the mist and let it fall, tick by tick, to the roots. Dewfigs plaster themselves flat against boulders, waxy leaves cupped to catch the same harvest. Swordmoss stands in sharp dark blades along any edge that condenses. The soil between the trees is pale and crunching — husk-gravel, the crumbled remains of something built — and water exists here in pockets: a seep-mirror no bigger than a doorway, a dew pond black under ferns, nothing that flows. You can walk an hour in this forest and hear only dripping, in irregular time, and see nothing move except once, far off, a gray jewel moth the size of your two hands, going from leaf to leaf, drinking the condensation film.

On the fogward slope below the forest stand the Harps: condenser fins the color of wet slate, thousands of them, each taller than a tree, planted in rows that run for kilometers. They are not trees and were not planted by hands. Fog slides between them and leaves its water behind; beads swell on the fins and run down in threads into channels at their bases. When the fog-wind moves at the right speed, the fins give off a thin chord, three notes, almost below hearing — the closest thing to music the landscape makes on its own. A few dew-farmers tend the rows, checking channels with their fingers, speaking in low voices or not at all. They do not own the Harps. Nobody built the Harps, exactly. They grew, the way everything here grows.

·

Because below the Harps begins the Lattice, and the Lattice is the city, and the city is one object.

There are no separate buildings. There are local thickenings of a single continuous structure — pavilions, arcades, spire clusters, bridges, courts, stair-towers — all of it one gray tissue, called substrate, that runs underfoot and overhead and through the hills like a vast lace. Substrate is warm to the touch, fine-grained, faintly porous, somewhere between ceramic and bone and lichen. It does not weather. Where it is active, dim blue seams of light thread it, pulsing so slowly you must watch for a minute to be sure. Where the city has withdrawn from some district, the abandoned fabric becomes husk — brittle, matte, gray — and crumbles over decades into the pale gravel the fog-forest grows in. Nothing here is old. Either the tissue renews itself faster than erosion can touch it, or it is discarded and ground down and regrown. The whole basin looks new-laid. It has looked new-laid for nine hundred years.

The strangeness of the Lattice is its emptiness. It is settlement on a regional scale — infrastructure everywhere, layered arcades, stacked cisterns, bridges spanning swales, canals threading the low lines — and almost nobody in it. Courtyards the size of meadows sit dew-beaded and dustless. Ways — smooth paths the city grows — run for kilometers between districts and carry no one. A few thousand people live scattered across the whole basin, mostly in one district of low arcades, and they occupy the city the way swallows occupy a barn: tolerated, incidental, grateful. Hamlets are hours apart. You can spend a day in the Lattice and meet no one, and the city will not seem empty so much as *occupied by itself*.

Green is everywhere in it. Every ledge, every fin, every windowsill and bridge-rail carries moss, ferns, dewfigs, hanging gardens of dark broadleaf. Whether a given wall of vegetation is wild growth or the city gardening itself is genuinely unclear, and the distinction may not exist. Along active seams grow seamblooms — pale, waxy flowers that feed on the warmth and exudate of working substrate. The people read them. A street that suddenly flowers is a street the city is about to rework: the saying goes, *the city flowers before it moves.* Every few weeks, somewhere in the basin, there is a growth pulse — a hum felt in the chest more than heard, seams brightening, a wall rising a fingernail's width each night, a bridge extending, a plaza being gently erased — and anyone living nearby packs up and drifts to a new court, the way you'd step out of the way of weather.

·

Water is the city's blood and, the listeners believe, its thought. The whole apparatus — Ceiling, Harps, dew-trees, substrate — exists to pull moisture from warm air and move it. At dawn comes the dew-pulse: the substrate cools, every surface beads, and for half an hour it rains *from below* — from ledges, leaves, fin-tips, the undersides of bridges — a soft ticking downpour in a windless gray world, whether or not the Ceiling has given anything. Some nights the Ceiling gives: your ears pop with the pressure drop, the moths come out restless, and then a fine warm rain falls for an hour, straight down, and it is the loudest sound the basin ever makes. People come out into the courtyards for it. Rain-night is the nearest thing to a festival.

The water gathers in canals of grown substrate — some of which, near the anchors, run uphill. The anchors are places where the local agreement about *down* is negotiable: courts where rain slants sideways, a staircase whose direction of descent changes halfway, a canal that climbs a tower in a slow spiral. The city does this rarely, without explanation. The herons learned the anchor-courts before the people did.

Canals thread the low lines to the cistern gardens: vaulted dim chambers and open stepped pools where the water rests, tended, before being drawn up again into the Lattice. Mirrorwort floats on the pools, silver-sided. Tended algae glow faint green in the vaults — the city's lamplight, or its instrument panel. Pale fish rise at night, slow, large-eyed, almost colorless. And all of it, eventually, overflows thread by thread to the basin's lowest point: the Mirrorwell.

The Mirrorwell is small — a few hundred paces across — and perfectly still, and black, and deep past sounding. It is the only salt water in the basin. Everything the city gives is sweet; the Well is the sea, reduced to one dark pupil. The people say the city was thirsty when it came, and drank the sea down, and keeps the last mouthful to remember it by. Herons stand in the shallows at dawn, gray-white, motionless, one or two at a time across kilometers of shore. At night the Slow Lights reflect in it, and the Well is the brightest thing in the world.

·

You notice the animals mostly by their absence. A day's walk yields: the moth. A seam-runner salamander, blue-gray, gone into a crevice. At dusk, perhaps, one shade-swift making its brief whirring passes over a pool. Fish rising. A heron's single low note, once an hour, carrying absurd distances in the hush. That is all, and it begins to feel like plenty.

And then there are the gardeners. Hand-sized, translucent, slow, with too many soft legs, they move through the moss and leaf-fall at night, pruning, gathering, carrying litter away into crevices in the substrate. They make a faint ticking, like fingernails on glass. Nothing here rots — there is no brown in this forest floor, no decay smell, only wet stone and green and a faint ozone near active seams — because decomposition is not left to microbes; it is done, deliberately, by these. Whether the gardeners are animals the basin produced or tools the city grew is a question the people have stopped asking. They step around them. They do not keep them as anything.

The people themselves are quiet the way the place is quiet: not by rule, but by tuning. They farm dew, keep cisterns, weave moss-fiber, fire ceramics from husk-clay. Their metal tools are few and precious, because tools cut from salvaged substrate have a habit of healing — a knife left in a drawer for a year comes out blunted toward lump, remembering it was once part of a wall. They petition the city rather than command it: there is a Knock, patterns tapped on substrate, requests made with arranged stones and planted seeds, and the city answers in days or weeks, in its own idiom, when it answers. The listeners — hermits, mostly, living near spire bases where the subsonic hum is strongest — claim the cistern levels tell them what the city is doing. When the reservoirs drop and the growth pulses come often, they say the city is thinking hard. About what, no one proposes to know.

·

What holds the whole strange arrangement together is pace. The Ceiling thickens and thins across the seasons; once each season it lets down a true rain, days of it, and the basin steams, and the Mirrorwell rises a hand's width. Fog tides spill over the Rim at night and are combed dry by dawn. Moths emerge on schedule with the scheduled rains. Herons shift along the cistern chains as the city draws one down and fills another. The Lattice grows a street here, retires a district there, flowers first, always, as fair warning. Everything is in motion and nothing hurries. It is an ecosystem in which the weather, the water table, the architecture, and possibly the local direction of gravity are all one slow metabolism — warm, damp, dim, hushed, endlessly renewed.

Stand in a courtyard before dawn and the basin shows you its ordinary miracle: the Ceiling lightening from charcoal to pearl, the substrate cooling, the first beads swelling on every edge until the whole gray lace of the city begins, softly, to rain on itself. A heron calls once across the Well. Nothing answers. Nothing needs to. It is a young world — nine hundred years old and unworn, because it is always, patiently, drinking its own sky and making itself again.
