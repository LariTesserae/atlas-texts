First, the sky — because it is wrong, and everything else follows from that.

The sky is ice. Not ice *in* the sky: ice *as* the sky, a ceiling of it, unbroken horizon to horizon, glowing from within with light that has traveled down through kilometers of glacier to get here. There is no sun. There has never been a sun. What there is instead is a vast luminous vault — white-green where the ice is thin, deep blue-grey where it thickens, veined with darker seams and strewn with frozen fields of trapped bubbles that hang overhead like a starfield that never turns. Day is the ceiling brightening until it glares; night is the ceiling dimming to a low milk-glow, because the ice hoards light and gives it back slowly. Dark, true dark, never finishes arriving. You could read by midnight here. You could go snow-blind at noon.

The true surface — whatever ordinary world exists up there under an ordinary sky — is days of climbing away through crevasse country, and no one comes here that way anymore. What matters is below.

Below is the floor, and the floor is warm.

This is the first thing that breaks your expectations. You descend through killing cold into air that could freeze breath mid-exhale, and then you set down on ground that yields underfoot like a held breath, springy, faintly steaming where your weight presses it. The floor is peat — the compacted dead of the forest itself, meters to kilometers deep, threaded everywhere with living root. There is no stone. There is no stone anywhere in this world, no soil, no mineral thing at all. Everything solid is one of two substances: ice above, plant below. Between them, the bright cold air, and in that air, almost nothing.

Because the overwhelming fact of the place — the thing the light and the floor are only frame for — is the emptiness.

The ceiling is kilometers up. The floor is a thin dark felt stretching farther than sight. And between them: halls. Volumes of still, luminous, freezing air so large that sound becomes a negotiable currency, taking whole seconds to cross them, arriving as echo of echo. Holding the sky up — or gripping it, or grown into it — are the towers: braided trunks of pale fiber, some the thickness of houses, some the thickness of hills, rising in slow spirals until they socket into the ice overhead. They stand kilometers apart. From the foot of one, the next is a rumor in the haze, a vertical smudge. Elsewhere the ceiling sends down its own vegetation — root-curtains, hanging forests of white tendril that dangle a kilometer and drift in air currents too slow to feel, dripping steadily from their tips. But these are punctuation. The sentence is the void. Most of this world is nothing, lit.

Nothing mineral, and nothing old. That is the second rule of the place, after the warmth of the floor: *nothing here is old.*

Every edge is sharp. The fallen towers — and there is always a fallen tower somewhere in view, a pale ridge of wreckage on the dark floor — lie in splinters like lances, fiber frayed into spear-points, not one of them rounded by time. The ice scarps where the ceiling has calved are glassy and fresh. Where the floor has collapsed into its own hollows — and the floor is hollow, honeycombed, cavernous, void beneath void where old mass was consumed faster than it could compact — the peat cliffs stand sheared and crisp, unslumped. There is no patina anywhere, no weathering, no softening. The world renews itself faster than it can wear. It is a young world at every age of itself, freshly made each season, and it has the bright, unfinished violence of a thing still being born.

The engine of all this is the light, because the light *moves*.

The ice overhead is not static. Somewhere far above, the glacier creeps, and the ceiling lenses and unlenses, and the brightness that pours through is gathered into slow columns and pools of intenser light that wander the floor like weather — because they *are* the weather. Where one of these shafts parks itself, the world wakes: hoarfrost sublimes off the felt in a rising glitter, the floor sweats, drips begin, and the plants unroll new growth at a speed you can sit and watch — a tendril gaining a finger's length while you eat, a tower adding meters in a day with an audible crackle, like knuckles, like a ship's rigging under strain. The green of a bloom-belt is so saturated it hurts, an almost violent color in the pale world. And then the light moves on, as it always does, and the bloom freezes mid-gesture into white stasis — a ghost-forest of frost-feathered towers, waiting, patient as anything frozen, for the light's return. The whole living skin of the world migrates this way, in belts and mosaics, chasing the wandering brightness across the dark floor, bloom and frost-ghost and bloom again.

It never stops. That is the thing a visitor must learn: the stillness is a lie told at human timescale. The Underbright — for want of a better name, call it that — is in constant churn. Growth pulses sweep the floor like fronts. The ceiling ticks and groans and calves, and when it calves the shards take *hours* to fall, glittering down through the still air so slowly you can watch one descend from sky to floor across an afternoon and walk away before it lands. The towers overreach, their grip in the shifting ice weakens, and they let go — and a tower's fall is an event measured in days: the slow lean, the snag against a neighbor, the re-break, the long topple through kilometers of air. You see it happen in silence. The boom arrives later — minutes later, sometimes much later — and the makers measure distance in that delay, in boom-time, the way other peoples measure in miles. Then the floor takes the wreck. Roots find a fallen tower within days; within a season it is a ridge of fresh felt with a seam of richer green; within a generation of light-passings it is gone, digested, and the world remembers nothing. Creation and destruction here are not opposites. They are the same gesture, repeated.

The water all comes from the world's own breath. Heat lives below — the slow compost-warmth of the peat — so the meteorology is inverted: warm wet air rises off the floor, climbs the void, touches the freezing ceiling, condenses, and falls back as a fine interior rain that needs no clouds. Where the floor's hollows open as vents, the exhalation stands above them as columns of fog, motionless, kilometers tall, pouring very slowly. Elsewhere the air is knife-dry, and water is not a body but an event: the drip-hours at the vent margins, the black ponds lying in peat hollows under skins of ice that the ceiling-drip punctures one hole at a time, each hole ringed by a growing bell of ice, a drip-bell, some of them centuries of accretion tall. Inside the great hollow towers, meltwater runs down the living walls in trickling threads — the only rivers in the world, vertical, hidden, rivers in the walls of trees. You can press your ear to a trunk and hear its water going down.

The soundscape is the drip and the tick and the long hush. The ceiling ticks like a clock the size of weather. Growing towers creak their slow rigging-song. When the world's sluggish breath finds a hollow trunk, the trunk sounds — an organ note, kilometers long, felt in the teeth as much as heard. You can hear your own heart here. You can hear a clap come back to you after long seconds, multiplied by halls, and you learn to wait for it. And then, every so often, something enormous separates from the sky beyond sight, and the sound arrives across an hour, and everyone who hears it looks up and counts.

There is almost no one to count. Life that is not plant is rare and thin and strange. The gliders ride the warm upwellings over the vents — pale membrane-winged things that cross the void for hours without a wingbeat, and shed their translucent skins as they grow, so that a slow snow of empty skins drifts down through the bright air and gathers in the floor-hollows like shed light. The burrowers are never seen at all; you know them by the floor's slow heave and the steaming breath-holes they leave, and by the traps the makers set there. In the black ponds, white swimmers move slowly under the ice-skins, and something small lives inside the drip-bells and taps. That is nearly the whole census. A fluting call crossing the void once in a day is an event worth remarking.

And then there are the makers, or their absence, which amounts to the same thing.

You find them everywhere and meet them never. Peg-ladders spiral up the towers to eyries — woven platforms of fiber, eyrie-nests for something person-sized, most long abandoned to the frost. Rope bridges sag between towers and mostly do not reach anymore. There are tally-marks carved into trunks, counting falls, counting light-passings. There are resin pots, seed-caches, tools of thorn and bone and polished ice — no metal, nothing forged, everything grown or knapped or frozen into shape. And there are the gardens: ice lenses, ground clear and mounted on fiber tripods, aimed to gather the ceiling's diffuse glare and focus it onto a peat hollow until it melts — the only warm water in the world, tended pools ringed with a green so fierce it looks unreal. Frost-locked now, most of them. Waiting for the light to wander back, and the makers to wander back with it, as they evidently do, because beside the ancient work there is always the fresh: a new peg beside a fossilized one, a recent notch, a lens newly turned.

Because the towers grow over what is built on them, and swallow it. Cut into an old trunk — people do, carefully — and you may open a chamber preserved in resin like an insect in amber: a room, a platform, a ladder, ringed round by centuries of wood. The forest digests its own history and keeps it inside itself, and archaeology here is a thing you do to a living tree. All of it, the same tree — that is the last thing to understand. Every tower, every hanging curtain, every blade of the floor-felt, every root in the warm dark peat: one organism. A single individual the size of a continent, or the size of the world — no one has found an edge, the ceiling and floor run past every horizon, they may go all the way around. Its growth-pulses sweep like weather because they *are* its weather, its moods, its slow attention. Whether it notices the pegs driven into it is not a question the tallies answer.

The rules, in the end, are few. Heat below, cold above. Light moves; life follows. Nothing is old. Sound is slow; wait for it. Everything solid is ice or is the One, and mostly it is neither — mostly it is the bright, cold, empty air, the great lit hollow, the nothing that fills the world.

Stand there a while. Your breath rises toward a glowing sky of ice. The floor is warm beneath you and the air could kill you and the light never ends. A drip falls somewhere with a tick like a small clock. And out beyond the haze, beyond sight, you watch something vast begin to lean away from the sky — slow, slower, silent — and you know you will hear it tomorrow, and that by the time its echo has finished crossing the halls, the first roots will already be on their way.
