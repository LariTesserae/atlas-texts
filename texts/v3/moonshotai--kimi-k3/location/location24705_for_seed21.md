The lowland has been there forever — a flat, black-water country of reeds and slow channels, steaming under a sun that never lets up. And out of it, impossibly, rises the column.

It is a single pipe of dark stone, sheer on all sides, standing where nothing should stand. The old rock of the plain is soft, rounded, patient; the column is none of these. Its faces are sharp as broken glass. Its fracture lines glitter. Seen from a distance, it seems less like a mountain than like something drawn up out of the earth — and that is close to the truth. The stone has a grain, and the grain runs vertical, every band and seam pulled long, as if the rock remembers being lifted. Around its shoulders, most afternoons, a collar of white cloud forms and churns and never quite climbs to the top. The summit sits above its own weather: a bare, dished plateau a few hundred meters across, small enough to walk around in under an hour, walled by moving cloud like a room with the ceiling left open.

The top is young in a way that takes a while to understand. There is no roundedness anywhere. No talus slopes, no softened corners. The rim cornices are knife-edges, and here and there a fresh scar shows where a slab has recently calved away and taken its sharpness down with it. Nothing on the plateau is older than decades. The ground is still arriving.

And it is wet. This is the strangest thing, at this height, in this heat: the stone sweats. Water seeps from within the column itself, drawn up from the drowned plain below through that vertical grain, and it rises to the surface warm — blood-warm, because the rock is warm, radiating stored sun and something deeper. Every seep-line is painted with algae in rust-red and black and a startling jade green. Fine trickles wander across bare faces and gather, and the shallow dish of the plateau collects them into a single lens of marsh at its center — clear water over black silt, knee-deep, warm as a bath, ringed with sedge. In the first hours of morning the seeps breathe faint threads of steam. By noon the whole top shimmers, and the black rock is painful to touch, and the marsh gives off a slow, vegetable warmth you can feel through your boots.

Out of the marsh, at the exact center, rises the filament.

It is a meter thick, dark, and difficult to look at for long — not reflective, not matte, a surface that takes the ordinary daylight and returns slightly less of it than it should. It goes down into the water, into the stone, and it goes up. Up past where the eye can follow, past the cloud collar, past the reach of the swifts, straight and absolute, until perspective swallows it. On rare clear dawns you can watch it for an hour and see no end, no sag, no sway — only a line darker than the sky, ruling the world in half.

It is cold. In this landscape of warm stone and warm water and warm rain, the filament drinks heat, and so it condenses the air continuously — and the droplets that bead on it climb. They gather, tremble, and run upward, wicking into the sky in a slow, unbroken procession. Stand within twenty meters of it and you feel its voice: not a sound, exactly, but a hum below sound, a pressure in the sternum and the teeth, pulsing on a cycle of about ninety seconds. The marsh answers it. Standing ripples hold themselves in place on the water, breathing with the pulse. Loose grit on the surrounding slabs has all walked inward over the years, toward the line.

Every so often — every few days, on no schedule anyone has found — the hum quickens, and the filament goes taut. You feel it before you hear anything: a stillness, a gathering. The whole column rings once, faintly, like a struck bell heard through a wall. The marsh stands up in a slow, shallow dome, holds, and settles. Near the center, for a moment, things fall wrong — a dropped leaf drifts down at half speed, a pebble rolls uphill. Then the pulse returns to its slow ninety seconds, and the plateau goes back to its dripping.

The people who raised this place left little, and most of it is incomprehensible. A field station of three small rooms stands on a bench above the marsh, one wall fallen inward, its window-panes gone milky. Inside: bunks, a stove, a keypad whose layout matches no alphabet, a spool of dark thread that has never been cut by any tool brought against it. Near the rim, a ring of squat pylons, some upright, some slumped, each with a socket on top that now holds rainwater and a single sedge. A path of fused-stone pads runs from the station to a notch in the rim where something once docked or landed. On a slab by the station door, someone — a keeper, long after the builders — kept a tally: thousands of fine scratches in neat columns. Counting the tautenings. One pylon still bears a stencil, flaking but legible: ANCHOR 7. Which implies the existence of Anchors 1 through 6, somewhere beyond the haze. There is a newer camp too — a patched tarp, a cache of tins, gear of our own familiar, insufficient technology. Someone still comes here. Not often.

Life arrived fast and stayed thin. This is a pioneer world, and it shows: the presences are countable. Algae first, then moss cushions thick as a fist along the seep-lines, then wiry black ferns gripping the fresh fractures, their spores dusting the wet rock like soot. In the hollows, where nutrients are scarce on young stone, the plants have turned carnivore — sundews glistening with sticky beads, bladderworts threading the marsh shallows. Seven trees grow on the whole plateau. You will count them without meaning to. They are pale-barked figs, stunted, each one an event, each one's roots visible where they grip a crack that did not exist when the seed landed. Soil is being made here in real time: moss, silt, dead algae, an inch of young peat at the marsh's heart. You can stand on ground that did not exist thirty years ago.

Animals are few, and most of them are travelers. Swifts own the place — they ride the permanent updraft that the hot black faces pull out of the lowlands, and at dusk they spiral up along the filament itself, where the lift runs strange and strong, rising until they vanish after the climbing droplets. Ballooning spiders arrive on the wind and string their webs between the pylon stubs, and in the morning every web is beaded with water that creeps slowly upward along the silk. There are midges at dusk over the marsh, brief and gold. And there is one frog — small, dark, the only amphibian that has made the climb or been carried — which calls rarely, a single soft knock in the evening, like a stone dropped into a deep well.

Otherwise, quiet. The thin hot air carries little. Most hours the loudest thing is your own breath, then the tick and drip of water, the dry rustle of sedge, the steady low combing of wind over the rim. Rarely, perhaps once a day, there is a sharp crack like a distant rifle shot: the column growing. For that is the last thing to understand about this place. It is not finished. The filament is still drawing its anchor upward, a finger's width a year, and the plateau is rising with it — shedding its rim, weeping, steaming, ringing its faint bell, accreting moss and tally-marks as it goes.

The rules here are simple, and the keepers' tally-slab implies them all. Don't shelter under the cornices; the young stone calves without warning. When the hum quickens, tie down your gear and stand back from the marsh. Drink the seeps, not the standing water. And don't try to follow the droplets with your eyes for too long.

Evening is when it becomes most itself. The cloud collar rises at last, spills over the rim, and for an hour the plateau is inside its own weather — silver light, everything dripping, the figs gone to gray ghosts. The swifts come down the line in long spirals. The frog knocks once. And on the filament, dim and cold and patient, the water of this small wet summit beads, trembles, and begins its long climb back into the sky — the youngest ground in the world, still being pulled up by its roots, in no hurry at all.
