You hear the Drum long before you see it, and you barely ever see it at all.

It begins in the soles of your feet, a day out from the margin — a pressure in the gravel, a note too low for ears. By the time you reach the first black water it has climbed into your shins, your sternum, the fillings of your teeth. The air is wet enough to drink and cold enough to ache, and the dark is total in a way that takes hours to understand: not night falling, but a ceiling. The Lid, the locals call it — a low, unbroken deck of cloud seeded years ago by the machines' own breath, hanging a few hundred meters up and never lifting. At what should be noon it becomes a brighter gray in one direction, and that is the whole of the sun here. The rest of the time the basin is lit in three ways: by lightning, by frost, and by the cold blue seams of the Anvils at work.

You learn to see by lightning. The Lid carries charge down to the towers, and when the big flickers come, the whole region prints itself on your eyes in one white strobing instant: open black water to the edges of sight, sheet-rain standing in the air like bead curtains, nine dark columns scattered across a valley you could spend three hard days crossing, and everywhere — everywhere — the pale scribble of rime on reed-tips, the frost catching the flash and holding it a half-second longer than your eyes can. Between flashes, the frost still works for you. The air hovers just under freezing while the engine-warmed water stays above it, so everything above the waterline wears hoar, and the world draws itself in faint silver lines against the dark. You navigate by what is frozen. You wade to keep warm. Dry is a rumor from somewhere else.

The ground is younger than your boots. This is the first rule and the one everything else follows. The Anvils make land — that is the entire visible purpose of them — and they make it the way a heart makes pulse, in strokes. What they lay down is raw: gray silt and angular gravel with no rounded stone anywhere in the basin, strata stacked like a cut layer-cake in every fresh bank, scarps shearing in crisp vertical faces that weep and steam. Frost is the only weathering that has had time to work, and it works fast, spalling the new faces into shards; in the loud hours the vibration itself sheds flakes, so the edges of young ground tinkle and patter continuously, like a distant audience. Nothing here has meandered yet. The channels run straight or broken. The levees are knife-edged. Test everything with a pole: ground less than a season old can open under you with a sound like a sucked breath, hissing, and it does not give you back.

The Anvils themselves stand in the open water, rooted in depths no one has plumbed. Seamless, the height of cliffs, surfaced in something that looks like wet basalt and heals a scratch overnight — people have cut them for trophies and found the cut gone by the next hush, which is where quietstone comes from, but that comes later. They are veiled always in spray, their flanks shedding water they pull up from below, and the lightning chooses them, again and again, so that in a storm each tower is a struck bell of white fire with steam rolling off it. What they take is silt, water, and charge. What they put out is ground, and noise. The physics of the middle step is not written down anywhere anyone can find. Instruments brought close to them begin to disagree with each other, politely, each keeping its own opinion. The hum has components that should not travel through air at all, and yet arrive — you feel them in the long bones, in the water of the eye.

Nine Anvils work the basin. A tenth stands dead at the northern reach, rimed solid, silent for longer than anyone's memory of anyone's memory, and it may be the most important thing here, because around the Cold Anvil lies the only finished land: ground a decade old and more, with true soil, a crust that holds, and the basin's only trees — a stand of black, resinous spruce-analog called the First Wood, thirty meters of vertical wood in a world with otherwise none. The heron-things nest there. Everything that wants height wants the First Wood. And a causeway runs out to the Cold Anvil from the southern shore and drowns mid-span, because whoever the causeway served is done being served. This is the closest the basin comes to history: a monument, a forest, and a road to nowhere, all saying the same thing. The project outlived its people, if it ever had any. The charter that sent the Anvils is lost or never existed. They answer no hails. They continue.

The cycle of the place is the Roar and the Hush, and everything alive keeps that time the way coastal things keep tides. When the Anvils work — and they work most of the time, in overlapping shifts, so that the basin's chord is almost always sounding — the noise is not a noise but an environment. There is the hammering, the deep compaction strokes, felt as much as heard, each one a slow blow struck on the anvil of the whole valley. There is the water: outfalls roaring, rain in shifting densities, weirs and fresh-cut falls where a channel was raised overnight. There is the ice going out — each work-cycle's first great note shatters the night's skin of ice across ten thousand pools at once, a crackling detonation like a glacier calving in your lap. There is the ground cracking as it settles. And over and through all of it, the fauna, screaming back. An ecosystem that has learned to live here has learned to be loud, and the biological chorus at full pitch is stranger than the machines: a million voices pitched into the gaps in the machine-chord, the bands the Anvils leave open, so that the total sound is dense as masonry, layered, saturated, with no silence anywhere in it for an ear to rest on.

Then the shift changes, and the Hush comes.

It is never silence — the rain and the running water see to that — but the chord drops out from under the world and the basin seems to lean into the absence like a held breath. Your ears ring. Your body keeps bracing against a pressure that is gone. This is when you travel, because in the Roar you cannot hear the water you are drowning in. This is when the fen-oxen move, the big shaggy amphibious grazers, near-deaf by design, talking to each other through their feet in stamps you feel across a kilometer of mud. This is when the tappers go out to bleed icewort of its sweet antifreeze sap, and when the listeners climb their ladders and take the chord's measure, and when — the listeners all say this, and all of them say it casually, as if mentioning weather — the chord bends, just slightly, the way it has been bending for years, resolving toward something, slowly, the way a very long song resolves.

There are quiet places, and you should fear them. Here and there the hammers of two Anvils cancel, and you will come around a cane-brake into a pocket of dead air where the roar simply stops, and the sudden softness of your own breathing will feel like paradise, and you will want to sit down. Don't. Everything that hunts here knows the quiet pockets too, and the stills — the pale, long-legged vibration-hunters, patient as herons and blind as caves, reading the water's skin for the ripple of a footstep — like to stand in them, motionless, white, waiting in the one place you will not hear them. The quietest safe place in the basin is one small room, and it is made of the loudest thing in it: the Hush-House, a hut on stilts above a black fen, lined floor-to-ceiling with scavenged flakes of Anvil-stuff. Quietstone drinks sound. Inside the Hush-House the Roar drops to a far-off surf and you can hear, some visitors report, your own heartbeat for the first time in weeks, and some of them weep, and some of them cannot sleep for the silence and go back out into the noise. The hut keeps a logbook and one steady oil lamp — the only dependable light in the whole basin, visible on clear nights across the fen, a single warm point in all that flickering dark.

The vegetation is black and it is everywhere and it is not waiting for the sun. The pioneer stock the makers seeded — or that seeded itself from their stores, no one agrees — has long since gone feral and thickened into layers. Underfoot, feltmoss: a floating, interlocking sod that spreads over open water in rafts thick enough to walk, though it closes behind you and erases your path within the hour. Above that, the drumcane, the signature growth of the basin, standing in near-touching thickets you must shoulder through — dark purple-black canes, each stem crowned with a taut round membrane like a drumhead, each drumhead turned to the sky or the nearest Anvil. They drink vibration. Sound is their sunlight here; the loudest water grows the tallest cane, and near the towers the thickets stand three times your height and tremble visibly, feeding. The hoppers — blunt, moss-grazing amphibious things the size of a cat — have learned the drumheads too, and when a still comes ghosting through the fens the hoppers slap the cane in alarm, and the whole marsh talks it ahead in drumbeats, faster than the hunter can wade, so that a hunt in the Drum is announced in percussion from half a kilometer off and usually fails. The fen-oxen eat the cane. The drumfish — eel-bodied, lateral-lined, following the warm outflows — eat everything that falls. In the outflow steam the moths hatch in their millions, timed to particular notes of the chord, shingled wings shedding water, rising like ash inverted, like snow falling upward into the dark.

Nothing is scarce here except light, silence, and time. The water is thick with larvae and spore-fall; the air is thick with both plus spray; break a stem and it bleeds sap that will not freeze; leave a carcass and it is gone in days to the pale shelf-fungi, which give back a little of what they take as faint blue-green foxfire, smeared by travelers on poles and hat-brims as a lamp that never quite goes out. Maps are a form of optimism. Distances are measured in hushes. Nothing here is finished, and nothing here is finished with you: a channel will take your camp in the night, a shoal will strand your boat, a bank you trusted will return to water with that obscene sucking hiss, while elsewhere the basin pays out new land like rope.

Watch it happen once and you understand the whole place. The chord bends — the listeners look up — and a shift-change rolls across the basin, and in the strobe of a lightning flicker you see a shoal being born: black water boiling upward, then parting, sheeting off a back of steaming gray silt that rises and rises while the hammers walk it firm. The island is not a minute old and the spores are already landing on it like soot, because the air is always full of them; the hoppers come skating across the rafts before the steam has thinned; a still is standing on the wet summit before the water clears, white and patient, first citizen. The land here is born already inhabited. And somewhere behind you in the dark, unwitnessed, an acre of last month's ground lets go and slides back into the water it came from, because the basin edits as it writes, and the arithmetic of creation and deletion is the only ledger the Anvils keep.

The people who come — tappers, hide-hunters, scavengers after quietstone, the rotating few who keep the Hush-House and the logbook — go by the old survey stakes sometimes, alloy rods driven at shorelines that no longer exist, standing now in mid-fen far from any water, marking a coast the basin has revised. The barge that brought the Anvils their never-delivered spare parts lies half-sunk in a southern channel, its hold a den of the otter-things the tappers call laughers, who ride the flood-pulses for the joy of it and scream the whole way down. The few who live with the Drum longest stop raising their voices, even in the Roar, as if it were a cathedral. They sign, they touch, they wait out the hammers, and in the hush they say the basin's real name, the one the charts — which call it, in dead administrative hope, *Interim Ground, Status: Pending* — have never managed: the Drum. The making. The unfinished.

The hush ends the way weather arrives. A pressure in the teeth. The listeners closing their logbooks and coming down from the ladders. The first stroke of the nearest Anvil going out through the water and the ice and the bones of everything, and then the chord, all nine towers, reassembling itself layer by layer — hammers, outfalls, cane-leaves thrumming awake, a million voices climbing back into the noise like swimmers into a current — and the lamp in the Hush-House burning steady in the middle of it, warm and small and utterly surrounded, under the Lid, in the dark, in the rain.
