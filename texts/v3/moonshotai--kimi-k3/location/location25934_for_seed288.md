Begin on the surface, because the surface explains almost nothing. A salt pan under a low pale sky, wind-combed, flat to the edge of sight, crusted in polygons that have not held water in longer than the land has memory. Here and there the crust collapses into a sinkhole, and from those holes, cool air rises — and sound. Stand at the rim of one and you hear it before you understand it: a pressure more than a noise, a vast compounded drone ascending out of the earth like the hum of a buried machine. There is no machine. There has never been a machine. Follow the sound down.

The region below — call it the Underlake, for it underlies the bed of a lake that dried in deep antiquity — is a chain of halls and passages the size of a valley, days across if you walked them. Everything here is finished. The erosion that carved these caverns ran its full course eons ago and then stopped, having nothing left to do. The result is a landscape with no edges. Walls flow into ceilings in continuous polished curves. Stalagmites stand worn to rounded stumps, like molars. Flowstone cascades sit frozen mid-pour, their surfaces ground back to satin. The floors are deep in rock flour — dust ground so fine it pours like a slow liquid — and the dust itself lies in long whaleback dunes whose ripples migrate a hand's width in a century, pushed by the faint breathing of the vents. Nothing sharp survives. Nothing new arrives. This is a world that reached its final form and then, finding the form stable, simply stayed.

There is no water. This must be understood first, because everything else is arranged around the fact. The last open water here dried with the lake above; the ponds it left in the low halls are fossil ponds, basins floored with desiccation polygons and slow-blooming crusts of salt that flower outward at geological leisure, gypsum rosettes opening over millennia like stop-motion blossoms. The cool air carries only a ghost of vapor. And yet the Underlake is green.

The green comes in patient, scattered abundance — lush wherever it occurs, sparse in where it occurs. Around the vent chimneys, where the cave's breath moves, hang the veil-gardens: curtains of pale, hair-fine fronds that strain the updraft for its trace moisture and its freight of windblown dust from the world above. In the lit districts lie the swards — low, plush fields of dustleaf, a thick-fleshed grey-green growth more succulent than moss, each leaf a hoard. For that is the rule of this place, the first rule: all the water is alive. It is held in flesh and wax and cell, and nowhere else. The wettest thing in the Underlake is a leaf. Grazers do not drink here; they eat, and drinking is a consequence. Touch a sward and your hand comes away damp, and you are touching something like an open spring, and everything within a hundred paces knows it.

The fauna are abundant, though gathered mostly in one place. Pale isopod herds the size of sheep move across the swards at the pace of weather, cropping the dustleaf, their segmented backs dusty with flour. Ash-swifts thread the high dark of the chimneys, dosing the ledges with guano that subsidizes whole gardens. Scarabs work the midden seams. At the long dusk, when the surface above cools, the dewmoths come down the vents — broad-winged, fur like blotting wool — having spent the cold hours above with their bodies pressed to the salt crust, harvesting its nightly condensation. They return heavy with it. They are the rain of this world, arriving on wings, in fur.

And then there are the cantors, and the cantors are the Underlake's argument that a city needs no tools.

Fist-sized, pale, many-limbed, wing-cased — a creature built around a file and scraper the way a cricket is, but colonial, and patient, and numberless. They built the Thrumm, and the Thrumm is the only city this world has ever needed. It stands in the greatest of the halls, a mound-fabric grown up to meet the roof and fused with the natural columns, towers of spit-stone rising fluted and buttressed and worn utterly smooth. Spit-stone: dust and glandular cement, laid pellet by pellet, mouthful by mouthful, overcoat upon overcoat until a cross-section of any wall would read like tree rings, like tenure, like scripture. The oldest layers at the core are compacted to something near ceramic. The outermost are always fresh, because the Thrumm is repaired at exactly the rate it erodes. It has looked precisely as it looks now for ten thousand years. It is not a ruin and it is not a construction site. It is a standing state.

The city has districts, as cities do. The hatcheries, warm with body heat, where the brood is stacked in wax-celled terraces. The reservoir vaults, where the water-wardens seal their harvest into wax cells rank on rank — the only bank that matters. The gardens, terraced swards and cultivated lantern-caps, the latter a bioluminescent fungus grown in deliberate rows along the thoroughfares so that the city's streets are lit, faintly, coldly, blue-green — lamplight by agriculture. The silk warrens, where tunnels are lined in woven fiber soft as felt. And the choir galleries, which are the reason for everything you heard at the sinkhole rim.

Because the sound. The sound is the second rule, and the largest fact. In the Underlake, silence is the anomaly. The Thrumm sings constantly, and it is deafening.

Its anatomy, from the bottom up: the bass is the city's breath. Like a termite mound scaled to a cathedral, the Thrumm ventilates itself through flues tuned by ten millennia of accident and selection, and as the far-off surface warms and cools, the whole fabric inhales and exhales on a long damped cycle — one slow sigh per turning of the world above — and the flues moan with it, a organ-note so low it is felt in the sternum before the ear accepts it. Over that, the middle band: the chitter and scrape of a million bodies at work, masonry clicking, mandibles, the rustle of the herds below the walls. Over that, the song proper — the massed stridulation of the singer caste in the galleries, file on scraper, a shimmering metallic chorus that never, ever stops, only swells and thins.

The caverns conspire in it. Erosion polished these halls into reflectors, and parabolas occur in the walls by the thousand. There are screaming galleries where the chorus is doubled and redoubled into a physical force that stands the hair up on a dewmoth's back at fifty paces. On the floors of the loudest chambers, the rock flour arranges itself into figures — standing ripples, stationary as iron filings, dust held in the nodes of the drone, redrawn only when the pitch shifts. Away from the city the sound attenuates across the sparse outer halls into something like weather — a far surf under everything, a reminder of where the center is — until, in the deepest dead halls, it is a pressure you notice mainly by its texture.

Except where the silence moves. That is the third rule, the one everything here knows in its joints: silence is never empty. The hush — a felt-padded hunter, long and low, all vibration-sense and patience — walks the sward edges and the causeways, and where it passes, the cantors' song stops, locally, in a moving oval of quiet the size of a room. The herds read those silences the way sailors read a darkening sky. A gap in the drone that drifts against the wind is the only alarm the Underlake has ever evolved, and it works because the drone is otherwise absolute. Noise is the floor of the world. Quiet is a predator.

The light, such as it is, is cultivated rather than found. The lantern-cap rows give the Thrumm its dim constellation. Once per turning of the surface, a shaft of true daylight finds its way down the great chimney and stands in the dust of the eastern hall like a pale pillar, weakening as it falls, never bright, but enough to set the veil-gardens glowing translucent and to give the region its one clock. Outside the city's districts and the chimneys' spill, the outer dark is near-total, and the creatures there navigate by the drone and by the grain of the floor, and sight is a hobby.

The water economy closes the whole system, and it is architecture all the way down. In the outflow of the great flues stand the condensation spires — tall porous vanes of secreted mineral, grown and regrown across centuries, sited precisely where the city's breath carries its ghost of vapor. On their cool surfaces, the trace moisture plates out as dew, bead by slow bead, a drop an hour on a good day. The water-wardens climb them with absorbent tufts and carry the harvest down to the wax vaults, and from the vaults it goes to the gardens, and from the gardens to the herds, and from the herds to everything that eats herds, and at every death the water changes hands one more time, and none of it ever, ever lies open. The dewmoths' contribution is taxed at the garden edge where they land to rest, brushed from their fur by wardens who have been performing this exact transaction since before the surface lake finished drying. The Underlake does not have a water cycle in the way a world with weather does. It has a water ledger, kept in bodies.

Time moves here the way the dunes move. The calendar has few entries: the daily pale pillar; the long sigh of the city's breath; the molt-cycles of the herds. And the Swell — the spawning chorus, arriving on a cycle of years, when the singer caste doubles and the galleries crescendo for a whole season and the dust-figures on the floors jump and re-form nightly and the drone can be felt, faintly, in the salt pan overhead, so that the desert itself seems to hum to itself in the cold nights. Then it subsides. Then the long ordinary resumes. Between one Swell and the next, the towers of the Thrumm gain a fingernail's thickness, the swards advance a body-length across the dust, a whaleback dune travels the width of a hand, and a gypsum rose in a fossil pond opens one more imperceptible petal.

This is what end-of-the-line looks like, and it is not barren and it is not still. Every process here has found its exact counter-process — erosion against repair, growth against grazing, harvest against thirst, song against silence — and locked with it, and the locked whole has been humming in the cool dark, green-fleshed and dust-floored and thunderously loud, since before any living lineage on the surface would recognize the sun. The Underlake is not waiting for anything. It arrived, long ago, at what it is.

Descend by the chimney at the right hour and you will find the pale pillar standing in the dust, the veil-gardens lit faintly through, the causeways glowing their cold green rows, the city's flues mid-sigh — and underneath it all, unbroken, the drone: the sound of a dry world that made its own rain out of architecture, its own light out of gardens, its own weather out of song, and then, having finished, settled in to maintain its work forever, deafening and content, at the bottom of a silence that only ever moves when something hungry is walking through it.
