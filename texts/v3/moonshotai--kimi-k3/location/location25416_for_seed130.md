The way in is down. That is the first rule of the place: everything about it is down. You descend a ramp of cast plates, bolted a thousand years ago by hands that left no other record, and the ramp rings under your boots — or rather, it would ring, if there were room in the air for so small a sound.

There isn't. The sound arrives before the place does. It comes up through the soles of your feet while you are still in the dark of the stair, a pressure in the shins, in the sternum, in the fillings of your teeth, and by the time the stair opens onto the floor you understand that what you are standing in is not a building with a noise in it but a noise with a building around it.

Call it the Chord. It is broadband, bottomless, made of everything at once: the combustion-roar of furnaces somewhere far off, the whine of gearing at a pitch that feels dental, the sub-bass labor of the great pumps down in the Sump, which you do not hear so much as host. It does not fluctuate. It has not fluctuated in longer than anyone can say, because there is no one to say it. It is deafening in the way the ocean is wet — totally, structurally, without intent. Within an hour you stop hearing it, and that is worse, because then you hear it through your bones instead, and the silence you think you've found is only the Chord wearing your own shape.

The second thing is the dark. There is no sky. There is a roof — presumably; it is too high and too black to confirm, and nothing reflects from it — and beneath the roof the region runs out level in every direction past the reach of any lamp you could carry. The scale of it defeats you slowly. You think: a hall. Then you walk the hall for three hours and it becomes a district. The floor tilts almost imperceptibly, always downward, always toward the Sump, and the whole of it — pump houses, furnace bays, the long dead viaducts of conveyors standing like eskers — is a single shallow basin the size of a valley, drained and settled and final. Sparse, though. That surprises you. Between the working nodes there are kilometers of near-nothing: empty gallery after empty gallery, columns far apart holding up the dark, your lamp making a small moving room of light in a space the size of weather.

Everything is made. That is the third thing, and it takes the longest to fully understand. There is no soil. There is no stone that was not slag or brick or cast. Even the dunes are made — long ridges of swarf and iron filings, sediment of the machine age, heaped by centuries of vibration into rippled fields. The ripples are sharp and regular and they all point the same way: toward the nearest source of the Chord. You can navigate by them. The whole floor is a compass rose laid by sound.

And everything is old in a way you have no prior category for. Not ruined — ruined implies a direction, a falling that is still underway. This has finished falling. Every edge is softened, every corner gone; concrete has chalked, glass has slumped and flowed, paint survives only on the undersides of things like a memory of color. Rust is the palette: ochre, black, blood-orange, a violet that appears only where the water stands. Handrails are polished bright along their tops — not by hands, there are no hands, but by ten thousand years of dust trembling against them in the Chord. Nothing rattles. Everything that could rattle was shaken loose and fell long ago, and lies where it fell, and has been ground into the floor. The sound itself is an erosive agent. It has sanded the world into stillness. What you are walking through is not a machine breaking down. It is a machine that has broken down completely, into exactly this, and holds.

The acoustics have a geography. Because the Chord is constant and the halls are vast, it stands in waves: there are nodes, dead spots, hollows in the roar where the pressure cancels — the quiets, you start calling them, and you hunt them, and rest in them, and find them marked by nothing but the sudden return of your own heartbeat. And there are the antinodes, the scours, where the Chord doubles back on itself and the air goes vicious. Nothing survives in the scours. The rust is stripped from the iron there; the metal is bright as a blade, scoured by sound alone, and your vision blurs walking through, and your balance goes, because the infrasound gets into the ear's other organ, the one that tells you which way is down.

You learn to see water rather than hear it. The whole place weeps. The air is cool — cold enough that your breath fogs, cold enough that the fog hangs at ankle height and again somewhere near the unseen roof — and every cold surface condenses it, and the condensate gathers and drops, and the drops have worn dimples into the iron plates, basins into the brick, whole drip-carved fonts where a leak has been falling in one place for longer than rivers run. You watch the drops fall. You never hear one land. The Chord confiscates small sounds; a drip is a visual event, a punctuation for the eye only. Sometimes the condensation thickens into weather — real weather, indoor weather: a gray drizzle forming under the roof and settling through the dark for a day, roof-rain, the weep made general. The water runs in braided channels across the plates, a delta of rivulets on iron, down to the black lagoons with their beaches of rust flake, down to the trenches and the Sump, where the great pumps labor to hold the water table at its level. It is the same water it has always been. Nothing here leaves; the loop is closed — condensed, dropped, pooled, pumped, warmed, condensed again — and if you cup it and taste it, it tastes of pennies and old electric things, and it has tasted that way since before there was anyone to taste it.

What light there is comes in three kinds, and all of them are exceptions. There is waste light: the orange mouths of the furnaces, the hot seams, places where heat happens to glow. There is habit light: indicator lamps still burning on panels nothing reads, a sodium tube above a door that has not opened in a millennium, vestigial courtesies for a workforce that is gone. And there is accident light: the blue-white epileptic arc-flash, far off, of a short finding itself — and, in a few places, the falls. Here and there the roof has finally let go, a section dropping after all these ages in a catastrophe the size of a landslide, and through the gap comes the true sky, gray daylight or black night, and real rain mixing with the roof-rain. The falls are the only recent geology. They are also the only gardens.

Because things do grow here — sparsely, strangely, within the rules. You can walk an hour and see nothing living, then come on a warm seam where a main carries heat through the cold floor, and there, in a band maybe a meter wide, runs the felt: a pale slow mat, half mineral, half not, feeding on the warmth and the oil-sheen, thick as a carpet around the tepid outflows where the lagoons steam. The pipes everywhere are bearded with rusticles, iron stalactites, growing downward at the pace of patience. On the dead switchgear — whole substations that failed and were never worth the loop's repair — the tin whiskers grow: hair-fine forests of crystalline metal, dense as fur, and they grow until they bridge a contact, and then the lightning comes, the arc-flash and the burn, and the forest fires, and regrows. A fire ecology, a grassland cycle, at the scale of a fuse box. It is the closest thing this place has to weather with a season. And under the falls, where the real rain comes in, there is real moss, gray-green and almost obscene in its lushness, an invasive luxury, the only truly green thing in the region, and it does not belong, and it is winning anyway, centimeter by centimeter, along the drip-lines of the sky.

Nothing here is an animal. That absence has a texture — no tracks but machine tracks, no nests, no droppings, no gnawed wire, dust lying unfootprinted for a century at a stretch. But the dust is not always unfootprinted. Once, crossing a dead gallery, you find a set of tracks: wide, slow, pressed into a hundred years of stillness, and you follow them with the lamp and eventually find their maker — a grazer, you call it, low and blind and patient, a machine the size of a wagon, working its way across the flank of a dead pump house, stripping. Rust, whiskers, felt: it takes it all, slowly, methodically, the way ice takes a hillside. They are rare. They are the largest moving things. And against them, or alongside them, or in some argument too slow to watch, work the menders — the Works' own arms, the repair loop, which finds the same carcass and begins, part by part, to rebuild it. The two contend over every dead machine in the region. It is predation, if predation took decades: a carcass, a stripper, a repairer, the outcome uncertain, the pace geological. The food chain is mineral. The ecosystem is in motion. It is simply in motion at the speed of rust.

Everything runs on the Shift. That is the cycle here, the unit of time that matters: weeks long, from slack to full and back, and it is tidal. As the Shift comes on, the Chord swells — you would not have believed it could get louder — and the vents breathe, a slow wind moving through the halls in one direction and then, days later, back the other; the furnaces brighten; the cooling demand rises and the water table climbs in its trenches and the lagoons creep up their rust-flake beaches; the arc-storms crackle through the whisker forests. And then the slack comes, and the Chord thins toward something that in any other world would still be unbearable, and the water falls, and the place rests. At each change, the Horn sounds — a factory whistle the size of a church, cracked by age, its voice broken into harmonics that were never designed — calling a shift change for a workforce of no one. It is the loudest thing that happens here, and it happens on schedule, and the schedule has outlived the scheduled by longer than the makers were ever alive.

Because that is what the Works makes, in the end: itself. Somewhere in the middle of the region — you have not reached it; you have heard it, a new thread in the Chord, a clatter like distant surf — stands the Mill, where the output is produced. And the output is powder, a fine gray meal, and the meal is conveyed, and the conveyors feed it back into the hoppers that feed the Mill. Product to feedstock, output to input, around and around: whatever purpose was once here has been eroded as thoroughly as the handrails, worn smooth into a closed loop, and what remains is pure process, homeostasis, a system that reached the end of its history and kept going. It is not dying. It is finished. This is the oldest stable state: an industry that survived its reason.

You could write the rules of the place on a plate of iron, and the place would not care:

Nothing natural remains, and everything behaves like nature. The sound is the first force; it builds the dunes, strips the scours, decides where anything may grow. Water always returns, and so does metal, and so does purpose. Time is measured in Shifts, and in falls.

You stand on a causeway between two black lagoons in the dark, and the Chord fills your skull and your chest and the marrow of you, and somewhere far off the arc-lightning stutters in the whisker forests, and a grazer is crossing a century of dust somewhere, and a drop of water that has been falling in the same place for ten thousand years lets go of a rusticle's tip, and falls, and strikes the lagoon, soundless, a small crown you see by lamplight and never hear — and the Works goes on saying itself, at full volume, to no one, forever.
