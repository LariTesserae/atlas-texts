# Gallery Two

On the surface there is almost nothing to announce it. A collapsed headframe of rusted channel iron, leaning into the scrub. A diesel tank gone bone-dry and red as a brick. Some drums, a cable spool rotted to its core, a concrete pad with bolts standing in it like teeth. The sparse gray brush grows around everything in the unhurried way of plants that have never once been watered. And at the pad's edge, a shaft — square, timbered, exhaling.

You hear the place through your feet before you hear it with your ears. The ladder rungs hum. By the third flight your teeth are buzzing, and by the sixth you have stopped calling it a sound and started calling it the condition of things: a deep A, around fifty-five cycles, the note that transformer windings and organ pipes both arrive at, laid down so steadily and so long ago that it has become less a noise than a medium. You will be shouting to be heard, down there. You will not notice yourself doing it for long.

Partway down, the lift passes you. It is a cage of black iron with a brass lattice gate, and it is empty, and it is always empty. It descends past the ladder, stops somewhere below, waits a courteous interval, and rises again, its bell giving a small bright *ding* at each end of the journey — a schedule kept for no one. The cable does not come from the surface. It runs up into dark and down into dark, and the power that drives it, thick in an armored conduit clipped to the shaft wall, comes up from below. Whatever feeds Gallery Two lies deeper than the gallery. The shaft continues past it, breathing warm air, and no marking on any wall suggests anyone has gone to look.

At the gallery mouth the wind meets you — a single, constant exhalation, warm as a held breath, dry enough to sting the lips. It smells of hot machine oil, of ozone, of dust, and under those a musk like old paper and warm stone: beast-smell. The light is dim and amber and does not flicker. Your eyes take their time adjusting, and as they do, the place assembles itself out of glitter.

It is the size of a small cathedral — sixty paces long, twenty at the crown — and it has the shape of one, though no one shaped it for worship. A long nave. A rounded apse where the light is warmest. Side niches like chapels, twelve or fourteen of them, each one full. A vault overhead strung with pipework in bundles as thick as a man's body, layered, crossing, dropping in bends to the floor and rising again, so that the whole ceiling reads as a single grown system, root and organ and vine at once. Everything is close to everything else. The air itself is occupied: veils of pale mist hang in fixed curtains at certain spots along the nave, unmoving, and you can walk into them and out again, and they do not stir, because the sound here stands in waves and the mist has gathered where the waves stand still, and neither has ever changed.

The walls are the strangest thing at first. They are fresh. They are sharper than fresh. The gallery is cut through salt — a dome of it, pale gray and faintly translucent — and every face looks as though the cutter passed this morning: crisp arrises, unbroken facets, tool marks ridged like corduroy, corners you could open a thumb on. Nothing is rounded. Nothing is weathered. And nothing ever will be, because the walls are being cut still, and healed still, in exact balance. Somewhere in the side bays a grinder eats the salt back; everywhere, the salt grows forward, crystal on crystal, ticking and snapping faintly under the drone like an engine cooling that never finishes cooling. The gallery is a wound that closes as fast as it opens, and so it is forever new, forever yesterday, forever just emerged. How long it has been this way is a question the place has quietly abolished.

In the apse sits the Engine, and it is very like an organ and very like a heart. A bank of horizontal cylinders, shoulder-high, salt-crusted at their bases and polished bright where the procession rubs past. A flywheel taller than a man, blurred at a speed that never varies — you could set nothing by it, because it tells one time only. A governor spinning its two brass balls in a fixed, patient circle. From a manifold along its back, nozzles atomize oil into the warm air, and this is the weather of the place, and its rain: the mist that beads at every pipe flange in black, perfect droplets that hang and do not fall. A rain photographed forever. The Engine's breath is the wind you felt at the mouth; its warmth is the warmth; its note is the root of the chord. What it is *for* — that takes longer to see.

The floor is ridged with old tool marks, and around the nave runs a track worn a finger's depth into the salt — and no deeper, ever, because wear and accretion meet there and agree. On the track moves the procession. The grazers go counterclockwise, nose to tail, a slow conveyor of domed backs: knee-high creatures, gray as wax, plated, many-legged, blind, their shells crusted with accreted salt until they look half-mineral themselves. Their mouthparts are rasping tongues that strip the floor's thin gray bloom — films of wax and ester that spread in fans wherever the mist settles — and the rasp of a hundred mouths at work runs under the drone like a second, softer machinery. They do not hurry. They do not stop. Individuals are distinguishable by the accidents of their crusts, and if you stayed long enough to learn them, you would learn something you would rather not: the same one passes the same flagstone, always.

The chapels hold the combs — hexagonal cells of fused salt, warm to the touch, stacked from floor to vault. And in the side bay behind the third chapel you find what the Engine is for. A press stands there, old, massive, its dies worn smooth. It takes in salt dust and oil and a fine gray meal from the grinders, and it stamps out blocks the size of a bread loaf, and the blocks are warm, and within days they crack along molded seams, and what unfolds out of them, damp with oil and creased like new paper, is a grazer. It straightens its legs. It walks to the track. It joins the procession. At the other end of everything, tall stilt-legged carriers — the only beasts here that hurry — pick the dead and the broken off the floor and feed them, gently, to the grinder's mouth. Dust to dust, on an industrial schedule, forever. The factory's only product is its congregation. The congregation's only work is the factory.

Above the pipes, where the sound is thickest, cling the singers. They are small and many-jointed, and each carries a comb of pale crystal across its back, and they eat the noise. Draw a breath near one and you can feel it lean, very slightly, into the drone, harvesting the pressure of it through its comb the way a leaf leans into light. Their own answering song, thousands of combs stridulating in phase-lock with the Engine, is the chord's upper structure — octaves and partials stacked on the deep A — and it is the reason the gallery is loud as a loom-floor and yet nothing in it shatters: the choir drinks the excess. A constant sound, held at a constant level, by an audience that consumes it. After some hours you will realize the chord has become inaudible to you — not quieter, just *there*, the way silence is there — and that your heartbeat has drifted into step with it, and that you can no longer say whether you have been standing here an hour or a day.

What grows here is sparse and strange. In the lee of the bus bar — that humming copper spine on porcelain insulators, crawling with blue corona and with the spark-flies that drink the discharge, swarming, flashing, always the same number however many bridge a gap and pop — mats of metal whisker-turf spread like frost-grass, fine conductive hairs grown from the old fittings, cropped to a constant height by grazers that seem to relish the sting. Wax-bloom fans on the floor. Here and there a salt fern, dendritic, white, pruned by rasping mouths into a topiary that never changes shape. Nothing green. Nothing that asks for water. Water is the one heresy the place does not permit: the salt takes it instantly, out of the air, out of your breath, out of your skin. Your lips crack by evening. Your sweat dries before it runs and leaves a rime on your forearms, faintly glittering — the gallery beginning, politely, to preserve you too.

The marks of whoever came before are few and faint and perfectly kept. A cast plate by the mouth, its lettering half-legible: *GALLERY 2*. Of how many, no record remains. A chalked arrow on a rib of salt, white and sharp as the day it was drawn, because chalk on salt, in still dry air, does not fade. A wrench half-swallowed by the wall near the press — dropped against the crystal sometime, and the crystal grew to take it, and then stopped exactly halfway, and holds it there, and will hold it there forever, because here even entombment is an equilibrium, not an event.

That is the law you finally understand, standing in the warm loud dim. Nothing here is used up. Nothing here accumulates. The droplet on the flange does not fall; the track does not deepen; the mist-curtain does not drift; the count of beasts and flies and singers does not change; the Engine spends exactly what the grinders and the salt return to it. Events occur — a birth from the press, a death at the grinder, a flash at the bus bar — but they occur in balanced pairs, like entries in a ledger kept by a meticulous god, and the total never moves. Time passes *through* the gallery the way your breath passes through a flute: it makes a note, and the note is always the same note. Your footprints behind you fill, grain by grain, over hours, and the floor takes back its smoothness. The rime on your skin thickens if you linger. The lift's bell dings for no one, on no schedule, forever.

Leave, then. Climb the humming ladder into the scrub and the ordinary sky. Below you the procession crosses the stone where you stood, and the salt closes over your passing, and the chord holds, and the dew-bead hangs where it hung.

And if you came back in fifty years — older, as visitors must be — you would find the same droplet, the same chord, the same gray dome passing the same flagstone. Not its descendant. Not its likeness. The same one.
