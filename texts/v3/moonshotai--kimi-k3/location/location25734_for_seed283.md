From above — from the rim of the basin, where the land is a little higher and the air a little thinner — it reads as one unbroken field of green going to black at the haze-line. A low country. A day's walk across in any direction and the crowns never open once, fitted against each other like scales, like tiling, so tight the wind slides over them without entering. Heat stands over it in visible layers. No smoke, no clearing, no straight line anywhere, and there never has been: nothing here has ever been made. But do not mistake it for idle. The roof is only the roof.

The place itself is underneath.

You go down through the light in stages. First the green gloom, where the crowns thin into scaffolding. Then a gray hour that has no clock in it. Then the black, which is total in a way that takes time to understand — not empty dark, but *full* dark, dark as a medium, dark that has weight and texture and occupancy. The sunlit canopy is one country, thin and bright and loud. The floor is the other, and it holds almost everything. What joins them is the rain of subsidy: a slow, century-long snowfall of dead leaves, husked fruits, shed limbs, dead birds, falling always, the entire energy income of the upper world paid down into the lower one. The under-country lives on what falls. It has organized itself, over uncounted years, into the business of receiving it.

The ground is not soil. It is felt — a living mat of roots and mycelium and leaf-litter in every stage of softening, springy, saturated, warm as skin, deeper than a man is tall in the low swales. Stone shows only in the karst: smooth-walled shafts, greased with biofilm, and the long solution-grooves called grikes, worn round and blunt, because the land here is old and has finished becoming. Everything is rounded. Everything is in balance. The basin is a settled form, a closed loop, a machine at steady state — and it is a machine, though nothing in it was built. There are no machines here. There is only machinery.

Consider the water, because there is so little of it, for a place so lush. Rain comes, but the canopy drinks it at the top and the karst drinks it from below, and what the floor receives is mostly the drip: the roof's own dew and breath condensing in the night-cool crown and falling, all night, a drop at a time, from a hundred feet up. The drip is the clock of the place. Water lives in pockets — in the tank-plants that hold liters in their cupped rosettes, in hollow trees that are cisterns older than the nations of animals that queue at them, in the shaft-pools far down the karst where the light has never been. Mostly, though, the basin keeps its water in flesh. The great herds carry it. A large share of all the free water in this country is walking around inside bodies, and every animal here knows it.

The bodies. This is a crowded darkness. The floor is more animal than mineral — a carpet that moves, continuously, in every direction, at every scale. Millipedes like slow freight. Slugs pale as candlewax, because pigment is a waste in the dark and the dark has had a long time to edit. Moths in their uncountable kinds, soft-flying, soft-landing, everywhere, always, so that the air itself has a faint fur. Caecilians threading the wet felt. Beetles beyond numbering, beyond naming, working. Nothing here is strange. Everything here is numerous. Stand still for an hour and the place begins to assess you — a tickle of antennae at the ankle, a moth tasting the salt of your arm, small jaws trying a seam of your clothing. To stand still here is to be colonized. The industry inspects everything, because the industry's first law is that nothing is empty and nothing is wasted.

It is an industry. That is the shape the life has taken, without any builder, at full regional scale. The harvesters run in columns — ant processions that function as conveyor lines, stripping fallen leaf into standard pieces and moving the pieces at a steady rate along routes worn into the felt. The refineries are inside the dead trunks: termite galleries where wood is milled, fermented, fractionated, returned. The processors are the grubs and the fungal felts, the great chemical works of decay, and their waste heat is real — the felt is warm because it is working, and on still nights the hollow trees vent slow warm updrafts, chimneys without masonry, so that at dawn the whole basin breathes thin steam up through the canopy. And there are the engines.

They are hippopotamus-bodied, sloth-paced, gray-pale as everything down here, and hot. Each one is a fermentation vat carried on pillar legs, and you locate them in the black by their warmth and their gut-song — a low, continuous bubbling, one of the only sounds the floor permits itself. They crop the fungal belts and the leaf-fall in generational circuits, loops so long that no single engine walks the whole of it in its life; the route is inherited like a territory, like a title. Their backs are the warmest surfaces in the dark, and so their backs are townships: basking moths, beetle remoras, small pale frogs living in the folds of the hide. Their dung is a currency with a queue at every deposit — castes waiting their turn in fixed order, first the flies, then the beetles, then the spores, patient as shifts changing. The engines are also the water tankers, drinking deep at the rare seeps, carrying the basin's reserve across its dry provinces.

Provinces — because the dark has geography. There is the felt country, and the fungal belts, and the shaft country where the karst opens its throats. The shafts breathe: at dusk they exhale cool cave-air into the hot floor, and the meeting makes fog — slow ribbons of it, condensing on everything near the mouths, so the shaft country sweats and drips more than anywhere else, and life packs in thickest there. Down the shafts, in the black water, the grid runs: pale eel-things and knifefish pulsing weak electric fields through the pools, and the pools tingle against a wading foot, and the animals of the floor read that hum the way you would read lamplight. Electricity without a wire laid. And in the shafts, the bats — millions — and their guano falling like a second subsidy, mined and processed by its own waiting economy, every grain claimed before it settles.

It is mostly quiet. That is the strangest thing, in a place so full. The packed growth absorbs sound, and the dark selects against it: in a world without eyes, a loud animal is an address, and addresses get found. So the floor speaks in vibration through the mat, in scent, in touch, and the loudest regular sounds are these — the drip, ticking everywhere like rain that never quite arrives; the gut-song of the engines; the continuous soft rustle of the carpet, a sound like far surf that you eventually understand is footsteps, ten million of them; the hush of wings. Once a day, at dusk, the one great expenditure: the outflight, the bats rising through the shaft-mouths in a column that goes up like smoke, a vast soft roar, the factory whistle of the basin, over in minutes, and then the hush closes back. Water and sound are spent the same way here — carefully, and mostly saved.

And slowly. Nothing hurries, and nothing stops. The drip keeps its patient clock. When a giant falls in the canopy — rarely, once in years anywhere — light strikes the floor, and that is an event on the scale of a gold strike: a decade of daylight, a boomtown of fast green, every seed and spore in a half-mile arriving to work the gap, and then over a century the canopy sews itself shut, vine by vine, and the dark reclaims its province and the books balance again. The engines' loops take generations. The felt deepens by a finger's width in a lifetime. The karst rounds itself by grains. The basin is not going anywhere; it is only continuing, at operating temperature, at full occupancy, in the black.

The rules, then, as the floor states them. Nothing is empty — any hollow fills, first with water, then with life. Everything falls, and everything that falls is carried away in pieces, and there is no waste, only queues. The dark is not an absence; it is the medium, and it is fully occupied. Sound and water are currency. Heat is free, and everywhere, and used. And the place itself — old, low, hot, packed, unbuilt, and running — asks nothing and stops for nothing, a factory the size of a valley that grew the way a forest grows, and is a forest, and is not finished, and does not need to be.
