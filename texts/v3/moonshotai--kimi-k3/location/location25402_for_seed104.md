The rain arrives without sound, and the water receives it without any.

Stand on the ridge — the last ridge, though everything here is a ridge of something — and feel the stone underfoot. It has been polished for longer than stone should have to endure. It is smooth the way soap is smooth, the way a bar of it wears in a palm, dark and silken and slightly warm. The ridge runs out ahead of you and does not so much end as *subside*: it leans into green water the color of old glass, and it keeps going down. You can watch it descend. Terraces shoulder off it in worn steps, and the terraces hold forests, and the forests dim from black-green to green-black to a color that is mostly depth, and still the ridge is falling away below all of it, down into the body of the warm sea.

This is summit country. That is the thing to understand first. The whole of the known world is the crown of a single massif — a mountain the size of a continent, or a continent that turned out to be a mountain — and the water rose around it so long ago that the rising is no longer an event but a property. Everything you can reach, everything lit, everything living, is the drowned roof of the tallest thing that ever stood. The lowlands exist, presumably. They are down there somewhere, in the black, in the old. Nothing goes. Nothing comes back to say.

Above, it is raining. It is always raining, or almost always, a warm rain that falls out of a sky that has worn thin as cloth. The sky used to be a space between the rain and the sea; there used to be a word for it. The word wore out before the space did. Now the boundary is a smear — a hundred vertical meters where rain thickens into spray, spray thickens into a floating raft of lily-mantle acres wide, the mantles trail root-curtains down into water that is mostly air, and the air below that is mostly water. Travelers call the smear the rainskin. Things cross it both ways. When the long tide inhales, the rain falls down. When it exhales, some of the rain goes back up, ascending in slow silver threads to rejoin the upper water that hangs over everything — the second sea, the one the light comes through.

Because there is light. Dim, sourceless, pearl-gray, arriving from overhead the way warmth arrives from a banked fire — from everywhere, from no point. Whatever sun there was has eroded into the Bright: a wide soft glow with no disc and no edge, sieved through the upper sea until it lies evenly on everything. Shadows here are shallow. They pool to the ankle. You can wade into a shadow and out again, and in hollows the light itself pools, thick as syrup, and lingers after its occasion has passed. Some of the light is old. Some of it left the Bright long ago and is only now arriving, bent and late, so that certain valleys are lit by the world as it was and you walk through illumination older than the stones it falls on.

And it is quiet. It is so quiet.

Sound was the first thing the world wore away. It was the softest thing, and the Wearing takes the soft things first. What remains is not silence exactly — silence is an absence, and this is a *remainder*, a hush with mass, like a room where something enormous has only just stopped. The rain makes no patter; each drop dimples the water and is gone. Currents move tonnage past the terraces without a whisper. What you hear, you hear in the bone: a low hum that is sometimes the mountain and sometimes the Slow and sometimes only your own blood, which is the loudest thing you have brought with you. And very rarely, from the thresholds, a muffled bell-tone, felt in the teeth — the sound of a rule holding.

---

The Green fills the middle water the way a thought fills a head.

It is layered — everything here is layered; the water column is crowded to the point of texture. Down on the drowned slopes stand the slope-forests, black-leaved trees with trunks like poured iron, rooted in terraces older than their species, crowns straining up toward a light they will never reach. Above them, not rooted at all, drift the bladder-rafts: whole forests floating mid-water on gas-filled pods, hanging their rootwork down in curtains kilometers long, turning with the slow gyres, trailing gardens. At the rainskin, the lily-mantles spread their leathery acres, dark side up, pale side down, and their hanging roots are furred with the candle-algae — the one bright thing, blue-violet, faint, a million soft embers drifting through the root-curtains like snow that chose to glow.

The Green breathes. On the long tide — which is not lunar, for whatever moon there was has worn to a smudge and lost its grip — the forests inhale, and the whole column leans; they exhale streams of rich, oxygen-heavy water that run through the sea like rivers through air, and everything that moves follows those rivers. Once in the long year the Green blooms all at once, the candle-algae kindling through every layer until the dim world swells to a blue dusk, and that is the brightening, and it is the closest thing this place has to noon.

Mind the Green, a little. With so few animals to do the work, the plants took up the hunting. Digestive bells hang in the root-curtains, translucent, patient, barely closed. Some valleys of slope-forest are less forest than an open mouth that has learned stillness. It is all slow — nothing here is fast except the sediment — but slow is not the same as safe.

---

The rules have worn smooth. That is the plainest way to say it.

Depth is age. The deeper the water, the older the hour. Swim down a terraced slope and you are swimming down the centuries; the silt on the tenth step is not the silt of this year. In the deepest hollows gather pools of oldwater — denser than the sea around them, heavy with dissolved time — and they lie on the seafloor like lakes under the lake, mirror-surfaced, absolutely still. They do not reflect you. They reflect the valley as it was: the ridge unbroken, the forest younger, sometimes a light that has not existed for an age. The oldwater is where the world keeps what it cannot quite forget.

Distance has sagged. Some straits are shorter going one way than the other. There are far places that have worn adjacent, and you will cross a ridge-crest and find, on the far side, a shore you left three days behind you. No one maps. The world is larger than any map of it, because near and far have partly merged and every route contains more country than it crosses.

Causes slip. Ripples arrive, and then the stone falls. The memory of the water is dense — every volume of it is crowded with faint rehearsals of everything that has ever sunk through it — so that on still days you swim through layered ghosts: old schools of fish long gone, the long-ago drowning of a forest, the slow descent of something enormous that the water refuses to finish forgetting. The sea replays its dead the way a worn groove replays a song.

And the water is blood-warm — bath-warm, skin-warm — which is a comfort until you understand it as one more eroded boundary: at this temperature you cannot feel where you end and the world does.

---

There are four kinds of animal, or five, and a traveler could learn all their names in a day and spend a life waiting to see one.

The glass fry drift in sparse translucent shoals, more light than meat. The skinners cross the rainskin indifferently, flying under the rain and over the water without ever deciding which, their wings wet on both sides. There is something in the oldwater. No one agrees on what; the pools reflect it sometimes, from angles that do not exist yet.

And there are the Slow.

They are vast — a hundred meters or a mile, depending on how distance is feeling — and they move like weather, and they are fewer than you would hope. Their backs are mineral and carry groves; their passage keeps the great channels clear, and the channels they keep are the roads of the world, and their hum is the floor of the silence. They erode so slowly that for all practical purposes they do not, which makes them, here, nearly holy: the only things in the world that are not being worn away. When one does die — it has happened — it settles and becomes a ridge, an island, warm for a century. Some of the archipelago is them. The living ones graze the bloom during the brightening and otherwise are simply elsewhere, a word that means more here than elsewhere usually does.

---

And then there are the thresholds, which are the only built things, and the only sharp things in the world.

They are scattered — a stair beginning on a dry ridgecrest and continuing down into the green, down past the forests, each step's silt a different century; a doorframe standing alone on a bare summit, its stone rubbed to a shine by hands or by water or by something between; terraces, arches, a bridge that spans a channel the Slow still use. No metal. No mechanism. No inscription — writing wore off, or they wrote in water. Nothing here has ever burned; fire is the one technology the world never admitted. Whoever made these made them the way the world unmakes everything else: by wearing. The builders' only craft was selective erosion — they shaped stone by choosing what to rub away, over spans of patience that do not bear imagining, and what they chose to leave were *passages*. Doorways. Stairs. Gates.

Because they understood what the world was becoming. The rules are local now, and loose, and a threshold pins a rule in place. The old frames still work. Step through a standing arch and for three steps the world recompiles: down is down, the rain falls only downward, distance behaves, and sound — sound comes back. Inside a doorframe's span you can hear your own pulse aloud. It is shocking every time. Run your thumb along a lintel's edge and it will cut you; nothing else in the world can cut. Everything else has been rounded. The doorways hold their edges, and no one knows what is holding them.

On the highest crown of the massif stands the one tower, half in the water, half in the rain, straddling the rainskin. At the top of the long tide, when the membrane thins and the upper sea leans close enough to touch, its door aligns with something. Inside there is one dry room — the last dry room — and in it you can hear the upper sea sieving down through the worn sky: a soft, continuous hiss, the loudest sound in the world, kept like a relic.

---

The Wearing continues. That is the last thing, and the first.

It is visible here, if you are patient in the right way: the mountain lets go of itself grain by grain, and the grains fall through the Green as a soft, endless sediment snow, and the snow settles into the valleys and over the thresholds and across the leaves of the slope-forests, burying the world at a rate you can almost watch. The underweather moves through it all — slow cyclones, walls of shimmer where warm water meets merely warm, threads of brine raining down inside the sea to feed the oldwater pools. It is a world in constant, soundless motion, dense and warm and layered, busy as a heart and quiet as a held breath, wearing toward its final form. It is very close now. It has been very close for a very long time.

Nothing here is old except the world. The water itself is new every moment — the one thing erosion cannot touch is the eroder — and it rises and falls through the rainskin, and carries the candle-light down, and holds the edges of the doorways sharp against the long soft ending, and is in no hurry whatsoever.

It has all the time there is. It is nearly finished. Both of these are true, here, at the same depth.
