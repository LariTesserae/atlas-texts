You hear it before you understand what you're looking at.

The plain is a silt flat a few metres above the sea, cool under a big sky, laid out in sedge and cotton grass with nothing taller than your knee for an hour's walk in any direction. Rain comes through sometimes — a passing comb of it, never a soaking — and fog comes more often, and between the two the land stays merely damp, firm underfoot, a dryness with pockets of wet. It is an empty country. One presence stands in it: a tree, alone on the flat, and from a kilometre off you can already hear it — a low continuous work-sound, like a mill race, like some small factory running behind a rise. There is no factory. There is no mill. There is not even a path to the tree, because nothing has ever been built here, no cairn, no mark, no cut. The sound has no maker. It is simply what the place does.

Up close the tree is not so much tall as *settled*. Its crown stopped reaching a very long time ago and has rounded into a final cauliflower of branches that renew themselves without extending. The trunk has the girth of a tower, bark grey and fissured like lava that slowed to a standstill mid-flow, moss held only in the north-facing seams. Thousands of years went into this, and the last two thousand or so went into nothing new: the tree finished becoming and has since been practising being. At the base, where heartwood once rotted and weather took the wound in hand, an arch has been worn smooth as a river stone — not cut by any tool, just decay and ten thousand rains refined into an opening a person can stoop through.

Inside, the place is a room. Nine paces across, rising in fluted columns of living heartwood to a vault maybe twelve metres up, where a limb failed in some unrecorded century and the tree rolled the wound closed around it, leaving an oculus like a chimney mouth. Everything is the tree. The floor is the tree — compacted scales of its own bark felted over its own wood. The columns are the tree. The vault is the tree. There is no substrate here that is not alive or was not recently alive; the architecture is a body. Ordinary daylight falls through the oculus in a soft column that walks its arc across the floor with the hours, and the corners hold ordinary gloom, and at night there is ordinary dark. Nothing glows. Nothing does anything it shouldn't.

And the room is almost empty. That is the strangest honest thing about it: for a space made entirely of life, it is close to a void. The air in the light column holds few motes. A moth every few cubic metres, drifting. The volume is shadow and coolth and sound. You could stand in the centre of it and turn a slow circle and count everything visible on one hand.

The cold is the cold of a cellar — eight degrees, perhaps, and always eight degrees. The water mass below and the wood around buffer the seasons out of existence; summer and winter arrive at the arch and are not admitted. Your breath shows faintly in the light at dawn. The walls are cold to the palm and faintly damp, and this is where the water comes from: the whole chamber is a condensation engine. Cool humid air drawn down the oculus beads on the deep cold wood of the vault, gathers, runs the flutes in whisper-trickles, and from a knuckle of wood at the apex a single drop assembles itself and falls, every eleven seconds, into a basin of black water worn into the floor's heartwood. The sump is tannin-dark and mirror-still except at the drip-point, where rings open on schedule. Its level does not change by more than the width of a fingernail, ever — rain through the flue adds, an overflow seam at the base bleeds the excess within the hour, and the level comes home. And down the far wall, in a loose pale curtain, the tree's own internal roots descend — hair-fine to finger-thick — entering the sump without a ripple, drinking the condensation back up into the sapwood, which lifts it to the leaves, which breathe it back into the air that the cold vault will condense again tonight. Air to bead to drip to pool to root to leaf to air. A closed account, balanced to the drop.

Which brings you to the sound, because the sound is the place's loudest fact and it comes from all of this working. It is loud — foundry-loud, loud enough that speech feels rude — and it is layered like a machine floor. Deepest is the pulse: every forty seconds or so a hydraulic thump as root pressure surges through the trunk, felt in the sternum more than heard, the boiler-beat of the pump house. Above it, the mid rush — sap moving in the vessels, a continuous hiss and gurgle, water in old radiators, a stream behind a wall. Over that, the slow reports and ticks of lignin taking load, ship's timbers, a house settling that never finishes settling and never falls. The oculus sings a low organ note when the breeze crosses it, leaning and returning, a needle that always finds the same groove. The drip tocks into the sump and the whole trunk takes it up like a soundboard — the tree is an instrument body, a cello that plays one chord. And at the edge of hearing, the small static of the inhabitants.

Because there are inhabitants — abundantly. It only takes a census to know it. On the skin of the sump, springtails: a grey dust that hops, thousands of them, scattering across the surface tension when the drip strikes and resettling in its wake. In the felted floor, mites and a pseudoscorpion under the bark-scales, the small citizenry of the duff. A beetle in the wall ticks like a cooling stove, and the tree walls its galleries with callus at exactly the rate the beetle cuts — a negotiation perfected so long ago that neither side can be said to be winning; that stalemate *is* the peace. The gloom moths drift their whole lives in this one room, wings the colour of the dark they live in, drinking condensation, a population as constant as a standing wave. And there is a wren. It slips in through the arch at first light and sings — the one voice louder than the machinery, absurdly loud for a body that weighs less than a coin — from a nest of moss tucked in a fluting behind a rib. Every spring there is a wren's nest here. There has been a wren here longer than there has been language to name wrens; it is less an individual than an office. Ten thousand animal lives inside these walls, and you step in and see almost nothing move. Abundance by count, emptiness by volume.

It wears the industrial register honestly, this place — reservoirs, a pressure boiler, valves in every vessel, a thermal flywheel of cold water, production quotas met daily for millennia — and none of it was made. No nail, no brick, no scoring of a tool. A dropped stone would be the most artificial object in the room. The machinery is physiology; the factory is a tree, running the shift it has always run, crewed by cells, governed by the slow physics of wood and water, electrified by nothing.

Nothing here is going anywhere. That is the deepest rule of the room, deeper even than the water loop. The drip falls every eleven seconds and has for centuries, as near to never-changing as a falling thing can be. The cambium lays down a ring each year only to replace what the year removes, cell for cell. Even the erosion has finished: every surface has arrived at the shape that water and air were always going to make of it. The drip's basin is exactly as deep as the drip can make it; the flutes fit their trickles so perfectly that no further wearing occurs. History happened here once — the fire-scar, the failed limb, the river that used to run nearer and moved away across the flat — and then history stopped, and what remained was equilibrium, events without incident, a chord that does not modulate.

Days pass through the room the way weather passes over a stone: they leave nothing. The light column walks its arc and returns. The pulse beats its forty-second beat; the springtails scatter and settle; the wren arrives on schedule and the chord absorbs its song the way a river absorbs rain. Stand there long enough and the loudness inverts — a sound so constant becomes a kind of silence, and you begin to hear your own heart inside it, small and off-tempo, the only thing in the room that hasn't found the groove yet.

Then you stoop back out through the arch into the big cool sky, and from across the sedge at dusk the tree is doing what it has always done: a mill-sound on an empty plain, a wheel still turning for a river that left long ago, a shift that will not end because nothing in it is waiting for anything.
