From the dust plains, there is nothing to see.

The plains themselves are real enough — salt pans the color of old bone, fields of wind-sorted glass sand, long dunes of mineral fines that migrate a finger's width a year. Nothing drinks here because there is nothing to drink. Rain is a rumor the lowlands tell about other worlds. What the plains *do* have is a ceiling: the Murk, a permanent deck of charged dust that begins two kilometers up and boils, slowly, like a charcoal sea thinking something over. On most days the Murk is the whole sky. Compasses on the plains do not point anywhere sensible. They point, every one of them, at a spot inside the Murk — and the caravans long ago stopped finding that strange.

What the compasses know, and the plains do not, is the Spindle.

It is a fulgurite: the frozen track of a single impossible strike, eleven kilometers of black glass extruded upward out of the crust in one event the town calls the Founding Strike. It is young as mountains go — young enough that it has not yet learned to be blunt. Its faces are conchoidal, knife-edged, flaking; the storms that rake it keep it that way, stripping anything soft before softness can accumulate. Veins of native metal run through the glass like quicksilver frozen mid-gutter, and the whole shaft carries a permanent magnetization, a memory of the moment of its making. In high wind the Spindle flexes, almost imperceptibly, and hums — a note too low for the thin air to carry, one you take in through the soles of your feet.

At its tip, day and night, burns the Crown: a faint violet corona, filaments of discharge standing off the summit like hair lifting. It is the first thing a newborn in the town sees and the last thing the dying ask to look at.

Between the Crown and the Murk's upper surface, in a band of cold, thin, astonishingly clear air perhaps four hundred meters tall, is the Winding.

---

From a distance — from the rare gap in the Murk — the town does not read as a skyline. It reads as a texture. The Spindle's upper shaft appears thickened, furred, the way a thread looks when silk has been wound around it. That is exactly what has happened. The Winding is a helix: forty-some turns of deck, platform, loft, and gantry wrapped around the glass, each turn bolted, lashed, and cabled to the one above, until the whole town is half rope by mass. There are no streets. There are ladders, catwalks, cargo slides, and the hoist-lines. Deck plates are grating, not slab — weight is money here, everything has been hauled — and so the underlight comes up through the floor.

The underlight: when storms move through the Murk below, its upper surface flickers from within, and that flicker comes up through the gratings, so that during a blow the Winding is lit from beneath, floors glowing and stuttering, shadows cast upward onto ceilings. Nobody in the town finds this strange. Visitors from the plains — there are a few, a very few — find it hard to eat under.

Nine hundred people live here, in a town you could hide behind a weather report. It is, by any measure, the highest settlement in the world, and it is a secret the sky keeps by the simple method of never letting anyone look up.

---

The Winding is never the same town twice.

The Murk rises. Some years a meter, some years three. The lowest inhabited turn — called the Waterline, a bitter joke, since water is the one thing that has never touched it — is abandoned when the dust starts licking its underside, and a new turn is founded at the top, above the previous First. The town climbs at the pace a fingernail grows, winding itself perpetually new, shedding its old coils below. Down inside the Murk, the drowned turns still exist for a while: skeletal, stripped, slowly sanded to bone by charged dust. In the rare years when the Murk drops, they re-emerge, and the scavengers go down the hoist-lines with their hand-signs and their pry-bars, into the bone-yards, to recover conductor and plate. Everything salvageable comes back up. The rest is let go.

Letting go is close to a religion here. The Winding's whole ethic can be said in four clauses, and children learn it before letters: *Ground it. Muffle it. Lash it. Let it go.*

Ground it, because the ungrounded accumulate charge and eventually attract what accumulates. Muffle it, because noise is waste — a loud machine is a machine leaking energy as sound, and the town's engineering culture treats a rattle the way a sailor treats a leak. Lash it, because the wind at this altitude is thin but it never, ever stops. Let it go, because the Murk is rising and the storms are coming and anything you cannot carry up is already gone; the only error is grieving on schedule.

---

The light is a medium, not an absence.

The sun — the town calls it the Pin — is a small, fierce, white point in a sky that stays near-black even at noon. Stars are out at midday; you have to shade your eyes to find them, but they are there. The Pin casts shadows like poured ink and gives less warmth than a hearthstone, and the cold up here is dry as paper — breath fogs, frost feathers the shade-side railings, and old hands say the air is a third of what the plains breathe, which may even be true.

Against that blackness the town lights itself sparingly. Power is the whole economy, and a lamp is a statement of wealth, so lamps are amber, hooded, and few. What fills the gaps: the pale phosphor of the worm-trays in the cable runs, the underlight strobing up through the gratings during Murk-weather, and at night the Crown overhead, violet and patient, bright enough to walk by once your eyes learn the town's dark — and they do. The Winding is readable at night the way braille is readable: by people who have put in the years.

The quiet is the same kind of thing — a craft, not a lack.

Thin air carries sound badly to begin with, and then the town has spent generations making sure of the rest. Machines are swathed in felt. Relays are mounted soft, and their ticking — the closest thing the Winding has to a soundscape, a dry insectile tic-tic-tic from every junction tree — is considered pleasant, the way rain on a roof is pleasant elsewhere. People speak low and, on the open decks, mostly in hand-sign, since voices tear away in the wind. The town talks to itself across the turns in bell-code, small bronze bells struck once, twice, in patterns a child knows by five. The most common sound, the true folk-noise of the place, is the clink: junction cabinets are kept stocked, deliberately, with clinkers.

---

The clinkers need explaining, which means the felt needs explaining, which means explaining that the Winding runs on lightning the way other places run on rain.

The base of everything is the anode felt — the grey, the town also calls it. It is not a plant. It is closer to a mold, or a lichen, or a rumor of one: a napped grey growth that colonizes any surface held at a potential, and metabolizes the difference. The Spindle, being what it is, is ideal. On the sunward side of the shaft the felters maintain their plots — seeded plates racked in rows, each plot measured and deeded in *plots of sun* — and they scrape the felt like turf and reseed the plate and carry the harvest down in bales. The felt is insulation, bedding, gasketing, filter, sound-deadening. And it is food — not directly, not for people, but for everything people keep.

Mites graze it first, a grey dust that moves if you watch long enough. Then the wire-worms: finger-length, segmented, living in the cable trays by the tens of thousands, stripping felt-cast and in return laying down castings that are mildly conductive — the felters mix worm-cast into every seeding, and the glow of a healthy worm-tray is the Winding's nightlight. Then the clinkers, brass-shelled beetle-things the size of a fist, which eat worms and mites and, incidentally, oxide film — which is why the town seeds them into its junction cabinets as contact-cleaners, and why a startled cabinet clinks like a dropped toolkit as every beetle in it snaps shut at once.

And above all of it, the kites.

The sky around the Spindle is never empty. Several hundred storm-kites work the lift — the permanent updraft column standing off the shaft — and they are two meters of ribbon-wing and silence, with quills of a metal-rich stuff that drinks charge. They are voiceless; they signal by wing-flicker, which in a flock of hundreds looks like light rippling on water. They nest on the lee fins in baskets woven entirely of stolen wire, and the town accepts this theft with weary affection: a kite nest is where the missing tools go. Kite quill, shed and gathered, is the finest contact material the Winding knows, and a coil-wright will trade a day of rations for a handful.

But the kites' real value is older than any use. They feel strikes coming. Minutes before anything happens — before the Crown so much as brightens — the sky empties. Every kite folds, simultaneously, and drops into the crevices, and the Winding has its one unarguable barometer. *When the sky empties, go in.* It is the first sign-language every child learns, and it is not a sign a person makes.

---

Water does not fall, pool, or run here, and the town has arranged its thirst accordingly. Inside the Spindle are the thirst-seams — veins of a hydrated silicate caught in the glass at the Founding — and in the warm galleries of the Bore, the Still bakes the stone and weeps the yield into the cistern, measured in thimbles per person per day. Ice comes up the haul-line too, at a cost per kilogram that makes lowlanders put it down gently. Nobody washes with water. Air and felt, felt and air. The wealth of the Winding is counted in charge — in heavy cells, the dense export blocks the town sends down the line to the plains, which are the only thing the caravans climb for and the reason nobody asks questions about where the cells come from.

The haul-line is the town's one umbilical: twin cables dropping eleven kilometers through the Murk to a waystation in the dust, a full day's descent in a winched car, reeled up entirely in storm season, when the Winding is alone and likes it that way.

Everything else is made here or scavenged here. The electric kilns — fired on harvest charge — melt the Spindle's own flaked glass into kilnbrick, so the town literally builds itself out of the mountain's dandruff. The coil-wrights wind. The haulers winch. The wardens keep the Grounding Code, walking the turns with their bonding straps, and their word is law, because an ungrounded deck in storm season is a match in a powder house.

And in the middle turns is the Exchange — which deserves its reputation as the strangest market in the world, because it is a bazaar with its voice switched off. Shoulder to shoulder, dense as bees, and nearly mute: hand-signs flickering, chalk slates held up and wiped and held up again, the scuff of felt boots on grating, the tic of a price-counter, the clink of clinker-shell beads used as small change. Felt turf, worm-cast, quill, salvage from the drowned turns, kilowatt-hours in brass-cased cells — turnover is ferocious, fortunes move in an afternoon, and a plains merchant stands in the middle of it with his mouth open, unable to believe that this much commerce can make this little noise.

---

The Bore is the Winding's inside and its heart. Generations of miners drove galleries straight into the glass, and the old quarter lives there still: small warm rooms with black translucent walls, doors of layered felt, the Still, the cistern, the cell-vaults, the glow-gardens where the worm-trays are racked like bookshelves. It is the safest place in the world during a strike, and so it is where the town goes to weather being a town.

A storm, whole, goes like this.

The kites fold. The Crown brightens from violet to blue-white. The bells say *in, in, in* — three strikes, over and over, from every turn at once. The vanes feather themselves; the Exchange's slates snap shut mid-word; the hoist-cars lock. Within minutes the decks are empty and the doors of the Bore are felted shut, and the Murk below begins to boil in earnest, underlight stuttering up through the gratings in sheets.

Then the strikes come up the Spindle's veins — not down from the sky, up out of the charged dust, crawling the metal in quicksilver threads, converging — and the town drinks. The harvest arrays take it; the cell-vaults take it; the hum rises through the floor and through the walls and through the sternums of nine hundred people standing very close together in the warm dark, hair lifting, nobody saying anything, because there is nothing to say that the thump doesn't say better when it arrives — felt entirely, heard hardly at all, thunder with the sound switched off, arriving as pressure.

Afterward: ozone, doubled, the smell of the world having been cleaned. The wardens out first, bells already talking. Then the felters, the wrights, the haulers — and within the hour the rebuilding has started, because there is always damage, there is always a vane gone, a turn scorched, a hoist-line to re-reeve. The map of the Winding is redrawn, slightly, after every blow, and nobody considers this a tragedy. It is considered the news.

And sometimes — once in a season, maybe — a strike comes up so vast that the town cannot help but take more than it can hold, and for one minute, by long custom, every lamp in the Winding is lit at once. The Bloom. The town blazes like a chandelier hung over a black sea, visible, for that one minute, to any lowlander lucky enough to be looking at a gap in the Murk — the origin, the wardens say, of every ghost-light legend on the plains.

Nobody cheers during the Bloom. They stand in the light, silent as ever, and look at one another's faces — which, in that dim and careful town, is the one thing they almost never get to see so clearly.

---

Late at night — though night is a formality where noon is already dark — the Winding goes to its quietest register. The Exchange's slates are stacked. The kites wheel by Crown-light, voiceless, hundreds of them, riding a column of rising air no one built. In a room in the Bore no bigger than a closet, someone sits by one amber lamp splicing a kite-harness, a clinker ticking sleepily in the wall cabinet, a thimble of water going warm on the shelf. The hum is in the walls. The Murk breathes below. Somewhere far off the horizon, the other spires of the Brood stand with their own dead windings on them, unlit, unclimbed — the mountains that did not get a town, or could not keep one.

The Winding keeps. A few meters a year, turn by turn, it winds itself new around the glass, small enough to hold in your hand if your hand were the sky, high enough that the world has never once thought to look up.
