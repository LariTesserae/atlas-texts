You hear it before you see it, and you feel it before you hear it: a pressure in the ribs, a two-note hum carried up through the soles of your boots. The upland is dry and white-hot, a tableland of cracked clay and stone where nothing grows taller than a thumb. Then the ground simply falls away, and you are standing at the rim of a green hole.

The pit is perhaps a hundred meters across and sixty deep — the scale of a cathedral nave, if a cathedral had been dug instead of raised. A weathered enamel sign leans at the rim, its mounting bolts bled rust down the post: ATMOSPHERIC WATER WORKS — CONDENSERY No. 7, and below, in smaller letters, MIND THE FLYWHEEL. Whether the hole was quarried for the works or the works were lowered into a found hollow is no longer knowable. The rim's old cut-marks have rounded into the same soft contours as everything else here. Nothing in the pit has an edge left. It has all been here too long.

A stair switchbacks down the wall, treads worn to saddles. With each flight you pass through a weather layer. At the rim the air is dry enough to hurt. Ten meters down, the first damp finds you — a bead of water on a warm brass pipe, then a ribbon of green beneath it. Twenty meters, and you are under a thin permanent cloud, a room-cloud, stalled in the lee of the rim where the pit's breath turns over on itself. By thirty meters you are wet. Not from rain — it rains here perhaps once in a decade — but because you have become one of the pit's cold surfaces. Condensation films your skin, runs off your elbows. The works take their water from everything that enters, and you have entered.

This is the garden's whole logic, and it takes a while to read it, because the garden does not grow from the ground. It grows from the plumbing. Centuries ago the works were laid out to wring water from hot air: engines somewhere below drive a refrigerant through a ramified network of pipes, and the pipes run cold — fiercely cold, cold enough that the wet heat condenses on them in rivulets. Every cold line in the pit wears a living sleeve. Aerial roots have wrapped the pipes in dense felt, drinking the sweat directly off the metal, and from these root-sheathed lines the leaves hang in veils — broad, waxy, near-black green, each one angled to catch what little light there is and to channel every arriving drip down its midrib to the stem. Trace the green and you have traced the pipe diagram. Between lines, the pit is bare warm stone and pale dust. The plumbing is the river. The garden is a map of it.

And the garden is, so far as anyone could ever determine, one plant. Every veil, every sleeve, every hanging curtain connects back through the root mat to a single organism that has spent uncounted years growing into and around and through the machines that water it. It has sealed their leaks with its body. It has insulated the lines that needed insulating and bared the ones that needed to drip. Somewhere in the long negotiation between root and rivet, the garden became load-bearing — part of the works' maintenance, unconsulted, unpaid, indispensable. The machines, in return, became weather. Neither could now be removed without killing both.

On the floor, in amber lamplight, the engines stand in shallow black water.

The flywheel is first, because it is largest: six meters across, rim a meter thick, and turning — you have to watch it for the better part of an hour to be sure — at the rate of one revolution per year. Its hub carries a cam, and once a year the cam trips a trip-hammer, and the hammer stamps a small ceramic disc with a clack that is the loudest single sound the pit ever makes and the rarest. The discs sit in a wooden rack in the keeper's hut, centuries of them, dated. The dates run past the point where the handwriting on the early ones would have meant anything to anyone, and they continue, because the counter needs no keeper. It keeps itself.

The compressor is the pit's heart and its lungs both. Every few minutes — the interval wanders, season to season, as if considering — it fires. The stroke is not sharp. It is a deep whomp, felt in the sternum more than heard, and the whole pit answers it: the walls take it up, the hanging veils lift all together in one slow green inhalation, and then the breath reverses. Warm dry air draws down past you from the rim; a moment later wet warm air exhales upward past you, and the garden settles, and for thirty seconds after every stroke the pit rains — a thousand shaken drips falling from pipes and leaves and catwalk grates, an applause of water ringing off stone and sumps and metal. This is the rhythm everything here lives by. The interval between strokes is the pit's minute. The flywheel's turn is its year.

Under and through the stroke runs the chord. The dynamo house — a small brick room with its door gone soft — contributes an electrical hum. The refrigerant pumps throb a fifth below it. The flywheel's bearing groans a sub-bass note too slow to hear as a note, only as a pressure that swells and eases across days. Together they make a two-or-three-note chord that fills the pit the way the damp does, totally, without direction. It is loud here. It is always loud. But it is loud the way a held breath is loud, everything sustained, nothing hurried. Old keepers, the records suggest, tuned the works by ear. The works now tune themselves by erosion: every worn bearing, every slumped terrace, every thickened root-sleeve shifts the resonances slightly, and the chord drifts across decades toward whatever harmony the current shape of the hole prefers. The pit is an instrument that has been slowly settling into tune for longer than any listener has survived.

The light takes turns with itself. Most of the day the works light the pit: amber pilot lamps, a green jewel on the governor, the orange slit of a furnace door that has not been opened in living memory — of which there is none. The lamps are dim and few and the darkness between them is plush and warm. Then, near noon, the sun comes over the rim and sends down one blade of light, and for about an hour the pit has a clerestory. The whole garden turns to it. You can hear it happen — a soft vast crackle, a million waxy leaves re-aiming through the same few degrees, the loudest sound in the pit that is not the machines'. When the blade slides off the wall, the leaves settle back, the lamps reassert their amber, and the pit returns to its long lit night.

The keeper's hut has slumped hip-deep into the root mat. Inside: a tool rack with the tools still hanging, each outlined in mineral bloom where a drip-line found it — one spanner half-swallowed by lime, being slowly fossilized upright. A kettle on a dead stove. Boots under a bench, with leaf-mold in them. A chalk rota on a slate board, the last line unfinished, the chalk itself still sitting in the chalk-tray. Whoever kept this place left in the middle of a sentence, or simply stopped writing while everything they tended carried on. The rota lists duties by the stroke, by the day, by the flywheel. The flywheel's duties are the only ones still being performed.

After a while you notice what is missing, and it takes a while because the place is so full. Nothing here eats. There are no insects in this wet heat — no whine, no webs, no gnawed leaf-margins. No birds in a green place that should be deafening with them. No tracks in the dust but yours, and yours will persist: nothing disturbs the floor except the breath, which sorts the fallen leaves into the same drifted corners every few minutes, a floor that tidies itself and otherwise keeps every mark it is given. Rot proceeds by chemistry alone, slowly, without assistance. The garden is never browsed, never pollinated, never touched by anything with a heartbeat. It propagates by creeping. It has nowhere to go and all the time there is. The result is a lushness with a strange stillness inside it — green without appetite, density without bustle — and the loudness, once you place it, is exactly the loudness of a factory floor after the shift has gone: all machine, no swarm.

The pit runs on rules you could write on the slate board.

Nothing leaves. The water loops: transpired from leaves, condensed on pipes, drunk by roots, breathed out again. The heat loops. The sound is folded back by the walls until it is indistinguishable from itself. Even the air only borrows from above and gives back warm, wet, spent.

Everything alive is downstream of the engines. The garden's geography is the pipe diagram; its clock is the stroke; its year is the flywheel. If the works ever stopped, the pit would dry within weeks and stand revealed as what it would then be: a bare hot hole with a dead machine at the bottom. The balance here is real — everything worn soft, everything settled, nothing jagged left — but it is a balance made of motion, like a top, like a whirlpool. Stillness would be the only catastrophe.

And the pit keeps its own time and expects you to keep it too. Within an hour you stop checking the position of the sun-blade and start waiting for the stroke. You begin to breathe between breaths. The leaves, you notice eventually, have been turning toward you — not quickly, nothing here is quick, but over hours the veils nearest your resting place have re-aimed their surfaces toward the small warm body standing still among the pipes. You are one of its cold surfaces and also one of its warm ones. It is taking your water and noting your heat, and if you stayed long enough — you would have to stay very long — the garden would grow around you the way it grew around the spanner, patiently, without malice, the way it incorporates everything: by continuing.

Climbing out is like surfacing. The chord falls away below you layer by layer. The cloud lets you go. At the rim the dry heat takes the damp off your clothes in minutes and leaves a salt-ring in its place, and the pit gives you nothing else to carry — nothing leaves — except, for days afterward, a tendency to count the minutes in groups of a few, waiting for a deep sound to come around again, and to feel slightly wrong in a world where the green things and the loud things and the breathing of a place are not all the same thing.
