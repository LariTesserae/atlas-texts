From the bog, the Kolk is a mistake in the light. The mire runs flat for miles — sphagnum, stunted birch, brown water in old turf cuttings — and then the ground simply stops pretending to be ground and steps down into itself: a pit two hundred paces across, ringed by a levee of peat blocks and black masonry, its rim crowned with alder that leans inward like people looking into a well. You descend on stairs cut into the clay, worn to troughs by a century of boots, and the surface of the world rises past your shoulders. By the fourth flight, the marsh outside is at the level of your eyes, held back by the wall. The water table of the whole region stands higher than the town's rooftops. The Kolk exists below the surface of its own countryside, and it stays there by agreement — an agreement made with engines.

Inside, there is no room. That is the first law you feel. The pit is packed the way a pump-packing is packed: every interval filled, every surface occupied, every view an arm's length long. Back-to-back cottages of rounded brick shoulder against the engine house; the engine house shoulders the coal quay; the quay is shouldered by the two old kilns, long since unbricked at the top and converted to cisterns, their crowns furred with sedge. Overhead, the space between roofs is crossed by pipes in moss-lagged bundles, by catenary wires sagging under the weight of their own green fur, by plank bridges, by alder boughs, and by a tangle of willow wattle grown so dense in places that it roofs the lanes entirely. Rain that falls on the Kolk may pass through four thicknesses of leaf and two of wattle before it finds a hat to land on. Nothing here is new, and nothing is ruined. The brick has gone soft as loaves, the quay bollards are grooved deep by ropes that rotted generations ago, the thresholds are hollowed — but it is all holding. The place settled into the shape of its use long ago and has simply stayed.

The ground, where there is ground, is a plant. Outside the brick lanes the town stands on a raft — brushwood and peat and a century of woven root, floating on black water, flexing underfoot like a mattress. The houses are founded on alder piles driven down through the raft into the clay, and between the piles the water lies close and dark, filmed in duckweed, breathing. Streets give. Lanes end in duckboard walks that drum hollow over the sumps. Where a wall foot meets the raft there is no clean line: the moss climbs the mortar, the mortar crumbles to lime, the lime feeds the hart's-tongue ferns that grip the courses, and the alder roots knit the whole into a single fabric. The wardens say it plainly: the walls are held up by what is taking them down. It has been true for ninety years. They expect it to be true for ninety more.

Water is the medium of everything. It stands in the leats that thread the town in stone channels, it slides over the weirs in glassy threads, it beads on every pipe-belly and leaf-tip and falls in a slow interior rain that has nothing to do with the sky — the Kolk condenses its own weather. The walls weep. At the base of the rim masonry the seep-lines run silver in the gloom, and the sumps under the duckboards shine black and breathe up cool air. Warmth makes it all worse, or better: the pit is sheltered, the engines bleed heat through their condensate loops, the peat itself ferments faintly, and frost has never once been recorded in the margin of the Level Book. The air is the air of a laundry crossed with a greenhouse. Breath never shows. Sweat never dries. Iron sweats; brass goes the color of old leaves; matches live in tins; and every plank, post, and shingle wears a biofilm sheen, velvet in the shade, slick as soap in the drip-lines.

And it is loud. The Kolk holds its sound the way a bowl holds water. At the bottom of the bowl sits the engine house — the one tall room, cathedral-narrow, hung with steam — and in it Maud, the great beam engine, turning her stroke every four seconds, ninety years old and never still. You feel her before you hear her: a push in the floorboards, a pressure behind the teeth. Then the sound itself, layered — the deep wet thud of the stroke, the gasp of the valves, the knock of water hammer in the rising main that the keepers call the Knocker, the two donkey pumps (the twins) chattering at their quicker tempo, and under all of it the broadband hiss of the weirs, the tick and patter of the drip-rain, the creak of saturated timber. Voices in the Kolk have evolved to fit: loud, slow, delivered in the gaps between strokes, so that conversation walks in step with the engine. Children learn it without being taught. At shift change the steam whistle opens and swallows every other sound in the pit, and for its duration the Kolk is a single note. Maud has stopped exactly once in living memory, for nine hours, during the great re-babbitting, and the old people still talk about those hours the way other people talk about eclipses — the terrible, cavernous quiet, the drip-rain suddenly intimate, and the sense, everyone swore it, of the water leaning closer.

The light is the color of the deep end of a pond. Between the basin's depth, the inward-leaning alders, the wattle roofs, the steam, and the permanent low mist that the warm water breathes into the cooler rim air, the Kolk keeps a green-brown gloaming at all hours. At noon the mist silvers overhead — that is how noon is known — and by mid-afternoon the lamps are lit as they are always lit: caged bulbs strung on the catenaries, a hissing arc lamp over the coal quay, windows glowing amber through the wet. Because everything below is water or water-sheened, light comes up as much as down; the lanes are lit from beneath by their own reflections, and the darkest place in the Kolk is under the duckboards, where the black sumps take the lamp-glow and keep it.

The flora obeys one rule, and the rule explains the place: spore, wind, and runner — nothing that asks a bee. Horsetails stand corrugated in the sumps. Male fern and hart's-tongue and maidenhair spleenwort grip the lime of the walls. Sedges and reedmace crowd the leat margins; duckweed seals the still water; liverworts sheet the drip-zones; mosses pile deep on every horizontal mercy. Alder, wind-pollinated, is the town tree, coppiced for piles and wattle; birch holds the outer rim. The willows of the wattled lanes are all one willow — a single clone from cuttings planted a century gone, propagated by hand ever since, which has never in its life needed to set seed. The moss is harvested on a decades-long rota for pipe-lagging, gasket-packing, and glazing putty, and regrows before the rota comes round again. The vegetation is not decoration and not invasion. It is substrate, architecture, insulation, gasket, and crop. The Kolk is less a town with plants than a plant with a town inside it.

What the Kolk does not have is anything else. No webs in the rafters. No nests under the eaves. No fish turn in the sumps; no frogspawn freckles the duckweed; no midges gather in the lamplight, which the keepers concede is a mercy. There are no mice in the coal, no rats on the quay, no sparrows, no beetles under the bark, no droppings, no tracks but boots. The leaves move only for drips and the engines' breath, never for anything walking. The wardens have theories — the infrabeat of Maud that runs through the ground like a fence; the acid seep that leaves nothing for anything to eat; the sheer depth of the pit, so that passing things fall past rather than in — and none of the theories is proved, and the fact simply stands. Once in some years a bird crosses the rim shadow high overhead and does not stop. The only warm blood in the Kolk walks on two legs. There is a hole in the middle of the soundscape where the birdsong and the insect-hum should be, and the engines and the weirs roar around it.

Time moves differently in the pit. The engines are fast — the stroke, the whistle, the shifts — but the Kolk itself changes at the pace of sediment. On the quay, in the clerk's office, the Level Book lies open: a hundred and eleven years of daily water levels, entered in a succession of careful hands. Reading it is reading the biography of the town. The raft compacts; the Kolk sinks a finger's breadth a decade, three and a half inches down across the book's whole span, and the water is no higher and no lower, because the pumps have grown in step — a new cylinder here, a bigger flywheel there, one generation's work at a time. Duckboards are replaced on a rota older than anyone living. A willow cutting becomes a post, a post becomes a strut, a strut becomes a wall, across forty years. The Kolk is not growing and not dying. It is keeping — the way a fire is kept, the way a level is kept.

Forty-odd people keep it. Enginemen who tend Maud and the twins; waterwardens who walk the leats each morning with a rake and a brass gauge; mossers who climb the catenaries to strip the green weight off the wires before a storm does it for them; peat cutters out on the rim, still taking turf from the bog that was the whole reason the pit was dug — the cutting went below the water table, the pumps became permanent, and the hole grew a town in its own defense. Supplies come down the Float, the straight black canal cut through the mire, on pole-barges that the wardens meet at the quay under the arc lamp.

Stand on the rim stairs at dusk and look back. Below you the lamps come on one by one in the green dark, doubled in the standing water, and the mist thickens where the warmth meets the evening air. The sound rises out of the pit like slow surf — stroke, gasp, knock, hiss — the sound of water being told, four seconds at a time, where to go. It has not stopped in ninety years. It must not stop. Everyone in the light below you understands this the way you understand your own pulse: without thought, without end, a covenant kept one beat at a time.
