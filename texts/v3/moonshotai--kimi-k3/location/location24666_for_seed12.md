The water has been rising for as long as anyone has kept count, and the count is kept in stairs.

The basin sits higher than any lake has a right to be, cupped in a range so young it has not yet finished becoming mountains. The peaks are raw — fresh fracture, unweathered edge, pale grey rock veined with rust where iron bleeds out of the springs. There is almost no soil. There are almost no rivers running down from them, either, because the water does not come from the sky. It comes up from below, out of deep warm throats in the basin floor, and it rises: a finger's width in a year, a stair-course in a generation. Patient. Bath-warm. Heavy with dissolved stone.

The air at this altitude should be thin and cold, but the water will not allow it. Warm mist stands permanently over the basin and presses a low cloud cap against the summits, so the world has a roof. Light comes through that roof dim and sourceless — amber at the waterline, green in the shallows, dying quickly with depth. Most days cast no shadows at all. A few times a season the sun threads some gap in the rim and the whole bowl turns to hammered gold for an hour. The Opening, they call it. Work stops. People stand on their roof-decks with wet faces turned up. Then the mist closes.

The water itself is not quite water as other places know it. It is silky, slightly too dense; swimmers float high in it, chest and shoulders riding above the surface, and diving is work — you exhale fully and haul yourself down a guide-rope hand over hand. It tastes flat, like licking a warm stone. And it leaves itself on everything. Anything that stays submerged begins to take the rind: a skin of pale stone, growing the way frost grows, except that it stays. A dropped cup is white within a season, a stone within a decade, an anonymous lump within a lifetime. Boat hulls must be knocked clean every morning, and the soft tok-tok of mallets on thin stone, echoing off the water before the mist has stirred, is the first sound of every day.

This is the deepest fact of the place: nothing in the basin wears away. Everything accumulates. The mountains are still rising. The water is still rising. The stone is still growing over every submerged thing. Even history, here, does not erode — it deposits, layer on layer, downward into the green.

The town is the proof of it. It climbs the slope where the slope dives into the sea: three living terraces above the waterline, and below the waterline more terraces than anyone has fully counted, descending course by course into the dim. The town is a sediment of itself.

Go down one of the old stairwells on a rope — lungs emptied, a jar of glow-moss in one hand — and you pass through the growing line, that white band of fresh rind that marks the water's edge on every wall, and into the drowned town. It is all still there. Custom says that when the water takes a level, you carry your hearth-coals up in a clay pot and leave everything else; the water must receive the house whole. So the rooms are furnished. Pots sit on shelves. Looms stand strung with thread turned to stone cord. A doll waits on a windowsill, white as wax, softened at the edges, draped in flowstone that fell in slow folds over a hundred years. Under the vaulted ceilings, bells of trapped air still shimmer — a diver can put her face up into one and breathe air that has not been breathed for three generations. The bells shrink a little every year. The drowned town is exhaling, very slowly, and when the last bell is gone from a room, that room is said to be finished.

Scholars make the deep dives to read the lower courses, since all archives here are underwater. The deepest terrace anyone has reached — four bells down the longest rope, where the light has gone entirely and the glow-jar shows only what it touches — is built in a masonry style no living person recognizes. The town is older than its own memory. No one knows what is below that course. The ropes have never been long enough, or the divers' lungs, and there are other reasons the descents stop where they stop.

One terrace, the eleventh down, belongs to the dead. They are placed seated in niches along its lanes, and over the years the rind takes them. Families dive to tend them — to clear the soft dark algae from a face, to hold a white stone hand for a while. A grandmother fifty years down is still recognizably herself, only blunted, only more patient. The oldest dead are columns now, with something like a face suggested inside the stone, and people disagree about whether the water is revealing them or erasing them. After a certain age the dead are no longer visited individually. They have become part of the structure. The water has finished them, and finished is not a sad word here.

Above the growing line, the living town is dense and damp and shoulder to shoulder. The lanes are staircases. The flat roof of one house is the street of the house above it, so the whole terrace walks on itself. Everything is built of dry-fitted stone — no mortar, because mortar is unnecessary; the water will mortar everything eventually, in its own time. Timber survives only above the waterline and rots slowly there in the warm wet. The smell of the town is wet stone, moss, and the faint chalk of drying skin.

The builders are closer to gardeners. A wall begins as a lattice of wood and packed rubble with runnels of spring water led through it, and the water lays the stone: a wall takes ten years and then stands stronger than anything hands could have raised. Adze blades are grown the same way, on wooden cores — a good blade is inherited, and if one ever snaps, you can see the ghost of the wood grain preserved inside the stone. Copper exists, a little of it, reserved for knives and knocking-rods, because metal left near the water goes to stone faster than anything and is wasted. The topmost terrace is the construction edge, the one place of continual labor, and the tick of adzes carries far across the quiet water. Everyone takes a turn there. A person is measured, in part, by the courses they helped raise. And no one mourns when a lower course drowns. A house is not finished when you move into it. It is finished when the water takes it. Completion belongs to the water.

The vegetation crowds everywhere it can hold. Above the construction edge, the mist-forest presses down the slope — cloud-moss and fern and dripping black trunks, everything furred, everything weeping. At the waterline stands a band of reeds and the bleached, stranded steps of last year's shore. Out on the open water drift the pad-meadows: linked floating leaves, some broad as a room, moving in slow circuits of the basin on currents too weak to feel. A meadow takes a season to cross. People pole out to them, harvest stalk-fiber and the starchy pad-hearts, and sometimes simply ride one home like a slow ferry. Below the surface grows the drowned forest — tall pod-trees rising off the sunken terraces, stone-sheathed at their bases to the height of a person, crowns wavering in the bright upper water, their pale air-pods hanging like fruit. Divers harvest the pods for their membranes, which hold air: a small bell you can carry. Fallen trunks sink and seed new groves of stone on the slopes below. There are flowers that calcify on the stem and keep their color for a season — the basin's only jewelry, traded upward, worn until they fade to white.

At night the town's light is the glow-moss, cultured in clay jars along the lanes and on the prow of every boat: a dim amber-green constellation. Across the water you can see the other settlements answering — the Rust Stair to the east, where the springs run orange; the Far Steps, half a day's pole away — a handful of faint strung lights around the dark bowl. Paddling at night writes brief green fire on the surface, beautiful and visible for miles, so night travel is for necessity only. The basin notices anything that moves quickly.

There is little that moves quickly. Skimmers dimpling the surface; nothing else with any speed to it. In the deep terraces live the blind white fish, hand-long, slow as everything else, drifting the lanes of the drowned town like its last inhabitants — some say exactly that. People keep a few in sunken cisterns and read their health for omens. High above, somewhere in the cloud cap, lives a white bird that is almost never seen; its call, when it comes, is the only wild voice in the basin, and the whole town goes still to hear it. Mostly the water is empty in a way that listens back.

Quiet is the basin's medium. The mist muffles; the water carries. Voices cross a whole terrace with no effort, so everyone speaks low, and the town sounds like one long held conversation. Every house arranges its eaves over reeds and hollow stones so that the roof's slow dripping makes a private music — you can know a house by its dripping, the way you know a person by their step. Messages to the far settlements go by knock-code, struck with copper rods on the drowned masonry, traveling through stone and water: three blows, a pause, an answer an hour later, and no one finds the delay strange.

And rarely — a few times in a life — a knock comes up from below the deepest known course. Three slow blows, or five. From the throats of the springs, perhaps, or from somewhere no rope has reached. The elders say it is the mountain growing, and this is true in general, whatever it explains: the peaks are still lifting, the cairns on the rim stand higher every generation, and the surveyors — the rope-and-plumb people — keep rival counts of whether the rim is outrunning the water or the water the rim. There is a heresy which holds that the water will find the Gap first: the one notch in the rim where the mist pours endlessly outward, where, a few times a year when the cap thins, you can hear a far white noise beyond — the basin spilling, or somewhere dry. Once in a while a thread of genuinely cold air slides down from the Gap, and the mist races, and people pull their woven cloaks tight, because cold is the one thing nobody here is built for. Then the Gap closes. The orthodoxy says the springs are patient and the town has a hundred courses yet. Everyone builds the next stair either way.

Time is told in courses. A child is "four up," meaning born on the fourth terrace above the water. When your floor floods you move up; you are also said to have given a room to the water, which is correct. The dead go down, the living go up, the fire is carried. People's teeth are flecked white from the water; their hair dries stiff and pale after swimming and is combed out with combs of pod-wood. Day and night are read off the tending of the glow-jars and the slow shifting of the mist, there being no sun to consult.

At night, from the water, the town exists twice. Once above the waterline, in its dim strung lights; and once below, inverted, where the glow-moss has gone feral down the old stairwells and the drowned courses smolder faintly green all the way to the edge of sight. Between the two towns the waterline slides upward at its finger's width a year. A diver comes up a rope, slowly, because everything here is slow, and breaks the surface with a small soft sound, chalk-white to the shoulders, and rests a hand on the gunwale and listens. Drips. A far low voice. The tick of the adze at the top of the town, where someone is working late by jar-light. Somewhere below, three knocks — or a hull being scraped early — and then the basin holds its breath and is quiet again.

The water rises. The mountains rise. Between the two slownesses, the town builds its stair.
