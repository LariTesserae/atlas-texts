**The Roof**

The plateau does not rise so much as arrive — a continent lifted bodily, a second ground held above the weather of the world below. From its rim, on a clear morning, you can watch a storm whole: a grey curtain with edges, walking across the lower country two days away, trailing its rain like hem-fringe. Up here the air is too thin and too cold for that kind of weather. The Roof makes its own. A dome of still, heavy cold sits over it for weeks at a time, and storms slide off the dome's shoulders and go around.

It is a young land, and it shows. The rock has not had time to soften. Scarps stand raw where the earth's skin tore; their scree fans are still bare, grey, unlichened, sharp enough to cut a hide. The glaciers left recently — in the span of the long memory — and their moraines lie across the valleys like fresh-plowed furrows, the boulders in them still angular, still bearing the scratches of their ride. The rivers are young and undecided, braiding and rebraiding across gravel flats, changing course after every melt season as if they haven't settled on what they want to be. At the plateau's torn edges there are flows of black volcanic glass, flaking into blades. Everything here is either freshly broken or in the act of breaking.

Because the cold is absolute. Not winter-cold, not a season — cold as the ground state of the world. Spit cracks before it lands. Leather left out overnight will shatter like pastry. And yet water is everywhere, in the way a rumor is everywhere: never in your hand, always nearby. It lives as rime, furred thick as a thumb on the lee side of every stone each dawn. It lives as fog that pools in the hollows at night when the heavy air drains downhill, and condenses on everything, and is gone by midmorning. It lives as brine slush under the crust of the great salt pan the people call the White. Once a day, when the sun stands over the south-facing rock, melt happens — an hour of trickling, water running in black threads down warm stone — and then dusk takes it back and every thread stiffens into a glass beard.

Liquid water is not a substance here. It is an event, with a time of day.

The ground itself is patterned by the marriage of damp and cold. Frost works the soil all year, heaving, sorting, and it has arranged the whole plateau into rings and polygons of stone — nets of cobbles drawn into cell-shapes, five paces across, ten, laid out to every horizon as if the land had paved itself and then pulled the paving apart at the seams. It is the first architecture anyone sees up here, and there is good reason to believe it was the teacher of the second.

The living skin is thin. Lichen mostly — crusts of orange and black and grey-green so old and so slow that a hand's breadth of growth is a century. Cushion plants hard as wood, hugging the rock out of the wind. Sedge in the melt-hollows, a green that lasts a few weeks and is grazed the moment it shows. In sheltered gullies, dwarf willow lies flat as spilled paint; its thumb-thick stems, older than the city, are the only timber the Roof has ever grown, and they are cut with the ceremony due to treasure. There are no trees. There has never been a tree.

And yet the place throngs. The graybacks come in herds that take days to pass — shaggy, humped, wide-hoofed animals, bearded, breathing smoke, almost silent by nature, so that a herd of ten thousand announces itself only as a sound like rain: the tick of hooves, the tear of lichen. They cross the Roof on routes older than any wall, following the slow green, and everything else in the world arranges itself around their passage. The gray cat follows them, low and fat-tailed. The wolves follow the cat's leavings. Bonebreaker birds ride the thermals above the herds and drop the long bones onto certain flat anvil-stones to get at the marrow — some of those anvils have been in use so long they are cupped like mortars, and the people know them by name. Marmots whistle, rarely, one note. Snowfinches boil up from the herd's footsteps. In the deeper ponds there are sluggish fish that have never seen a current. Nothing decays up here, so the work of rot falls to beaks and teeth, and what they won't take, the cold keeps.

**The Thaw**

Where a pass crosses the rim, where the herd-routes pinch together above the White, the city stands. They call it the Thaw, after the one hour of the day it was built to catch.

It is made of stone the way the ground is made of stone — selected, not cut. The frost does the quarrying, riving the bedrock along its bedding into flat slabs; the builders only choose, shim, and seat. Walls rise without mortar, stone breathed onto stone, the joints chinked with turf and wool, and the finest of them are fitted polygon to polygon in a style any child can tell you was copied from the frost-nets of the open ground. The lanes are narrow enough to touch both walls. Roofs are corbelled slabs, turfed, daubed with dung-plaster that freezes harder than brick. Doorways are knee-height and shoulder-wide — heat is a flock and you do not let it stray. Windows are panes of clear ice cut from the ponds in the deep season, set in bone frames; they frost over nightly and are scraped each morning, and the scraping is one of the dawn sounds of the world.

The Thaw is dense the way a seed is dense. Everything is stored, stacked, hung, layered. Dung-bricks in silos like brown masonry — the only fuel of a woodless world, gathered from the herd-routes in summer and dried on every flat roof. Frozen meat hanging in open-sided stone towers, quarters and flanks, because the air itself is the larder and it never fails. Salt in slabs from the White. Wool in bales. Obsidian blades from the rim-flows, traded down off the plateau for things the Roof cannot make. Tubers by the ton, processed by the weather itself: laid out on the frost at night, trodden dry by day, frozen and squeezed and frozen again until they become pale, light, everlasting stones of food. Bone is a building material, a tool stock, a fuel of last resort. The streets run with black channels that carry the noon melt down to stone cisterns, and the channels are ice by dusk, and in deep winter the drifts roof the lanes entirely and the city goes half-underground, moving through snow tunnels lit by ice panes, smoke escaping through hidden holes.

And below everything: the city stands on itself. Nothing rots, so nothing is thrown away in any sense that matters — it is buried, and frozen, and kept. The Thaw rises on its own midden, four thousand years deep, and the oldest rooms are cellars now, and below the cellars are older rooms. Dig a grain pit and you cut through a stratigraphy of meals: bones, cord, broken blades, a child's shoe, all of it preserved as if dropped last winter. The dead do not leave, either. They are seated in niches in the walls of the dead-house terraces, dried by the wind and kept by the cold, generation on generation, facing the herd-routes. The density of the Thaw is not only goods. It is presences. No one who ever lived here is entirely gone.

**The rules of the world**

Water is an event. Everything is arranged around the noon hour of melt: the frost-harvest at dawn, when the rime is scraped from the stones into skin bags; the fog-nets of woven grayback hair strung along the ridge, dripping into gutters; the runnels opened and the cisterns blessed; the gardens — south-facing, stone-walled, manured, black-soiled to drink the sun — watered by hand in the short trickle. The stones are the warm-blooded citizens of the Thaw; they soak the day's light and give it back all night, and a wall is a hearth you can lean on.

Sound is expensive. The baseline of the world is a silence so complete that small noises carry meaning: the ping of the salt pan contracting at dusk, the squeak of cold snow underfoot, the click — all night, every night — of frost re-seating the stones of the walls. The dogs of the Thaw are bred over centuries to whine rather than bark. Voices are low. There is one sanctioned loudness: each dawn, at the cisterns, a stone chime is struck as the ice-skin is broken, and it rings off every wall in the city and nobody complains. Seasonally, twice a year, there is the herd-week, when the graybacks funnel through the pass below the walls and the rain-sound of hooves does not stop for days, and the whole city smells of animal, and the people speak of it afterward the way others speak of great weather. And rarely — a few times a winter — the ground booms: a deep, door-slam concussion underfoot, water freezing fast somewhere in the cracks below, and the old people nod and name the season by it.

Nothing rots; everything keeps. Carelessness is permanent. A dropped blade is found by your granddaughter. This makes the people careful in a way that visitors mistake for slowness.

Everything arrives slowly, and the slowness is the wealth. A good house takes three generations to finish, and everyone knows which stones of it were whose. Walls are dated by the lichen on them — a crust this wide, a century — so that every building wears its age like a tree, outward. Water boils at a temperature that barely scalds, so cooking is a long patience and most food is frozen, dried, or fermented instead. The glaciers at the rim advance a toe's length in a lifetime, and the shoreline of the White shifts a hand's width a year, and the terraces of the Thaw creep upslope a course of stone per decade, and the trade routes adjust over decades to a moraine collapse, and no one calls any of this change. Change is what the herds do. The rest is the world breathing.

**Dusk**

In the still cold the smoke does not rise. It leaves the roof-holes and flattens, pooling under the lid of the inversion, and by evening the Thaw wears its smoke in strata — level grey beds, house-height, lane-height, so that the city looks like a thing sedimented rather than built, a deposit of itself. Which, of course, it is.

The light goes long and level across the Roof. The frost-nets of stone throw shadows like thrown nets. On the far slope, darkening now, a herd is a weather system of its own — a slow grey front of bodies, steam rising off it like a low cloud. The runnels in the streets click and tick as they freeze, lane by lane, a sound like a great knitting. The ice windows glow from inside, the color of tallow. Somewhere the gray cat screams once — the one loud thing it ever does — and the snowfinches settle into the eaves by the thousand, into the joints of the fitted stone, into the warm breath of the walls.

The first stars come out dense as frost on a pane.

Nothing here happens. It never stops happening.
