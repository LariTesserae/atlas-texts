# The Anvil

The first thing is the light.

Stand on the rim and it takes your eyes a full minute to stop watering. The sun here is too large and too white, and the air — for all its altitude — is dense and still, a great cold pressure-cap the plateau holds over itself like a bell jar, so the light comes down undiffused and then comes back up off the rime and hits you again from below. Shadows are black blades. Nothing glows; everything glares. You will learn, if you stay, that nobody on the Anvil navigates by sight. The world is too bright to see. It is known by ear and by warmth.

Below the rim — and it is kilometers below — a cloud sea turns over itself in slow gray weather that belongs to a different world entirely. The Anvil rises out of that weather and ignores it. The plateau runs past every horizon, and it is still rising: a finger's width a year, patient as a nail growing, which is why everything up here is young and will stay young. The rock is fresh basalt and obsidian, frost-shattered into fins and blades and talus with edges that cut glove leather. Nothing is rounded. Water never gets the time to round anything here; the cold shatters and the cold preserves. Sharpness is conserved on the Anvil — it is almost a law of physics.

The cold is total. At high noon, in the full white blast of the day, the open air stands forty degrees below the freezing of water, and it never rises above that, not ever. And yet the plateau is not a desert of ice. It is black with life, and it is loud.

You have to understand the water, because the water explains everything else. Water never falls here. It is carried. There is no rain, no river that finishes forming — only rime growing in feathery gray thickets on every lee surface, only steam, only breath, only the brief bright drip where something black or something warm has made a private climate. Melt-pockets gather in the lee of the dark vegetation and around the bodies of animals, and each one is ringed with an explosion of small life, and each one refreezes within meters of its source. The entire hydrology of a continent passes through bark and lung and fur. Dampness is biological. The wet places are the alive places.

The black is the blackfelt: a mat of low, dense, ink-dark growth that carpets every surface the wind allows, centimeters thick at the edges of valleys, meters thick in the sheltered basins where it has lain down its own dead for centuries. Those deep layers do not rot — nothing rots here, rotting requires liquid water's permission — they compress and warm and smolder without flame, so that threads of sweet coal-smoke rise everywhere from the ground, thin as hairs, and the whole plateau idles like a banked furnace. The felt is black because black is a technology: it drinks the murderous light, melts its own microclimate, keeps a film of liquid at its growing surface even while the air above it could freeze exposed skin in seconds. This is a cold-powered ecology. The gradient between the white sky and the black ground is the dynamo everything runs on.

And then there are the valvewoods.

They stand in forests trunk-to-trunk, dark columned things thirty meters tall, and they are engines — unbuilt, grown, but engines beyond argument. Each trunk is a stack of chambers. The tree draws meltwater up through the felt, admits it to a chamber, seals the valve — an actual valve, a ring of lignified tissue that closes like a sphincter — and then deliberately lets the chamber freeze. Ice expansion is the stroke. The water becomes the piston; the tree lifts its sap and its mineral slurry a stage higher on the pressure of freezing, vents, opens the next intake, begins again. A valley of valvewoods at work sounds like nothing so much as a factory floor: a million slow knocks and chugs and exhalations, phase-locked in patches so that whole hillsides pulse together, syncopate, drift apart, lock again. When dawn returns to a valley that has been frozen through the long night, the startup takes days and it is one of the great sounds of the world — the ice in ten million chambers cracking loose at once, boom after boom rolling across the basin like artillery walking closer, and then the chugging beginning, ragged, then smooth, the way a vast machine is coaxed into rhythm.

The valvewoods bear fruit, of a kind. Their roots crack the fresh basalt and strain it, and what they excrete at their branch-ends are blooms — dense nodules of refined iron and copper, botanical in form, metallurgical in fact, hanging like dark fruit. Everything with teeth here has teeth of grown metal. Everything with a resonating chamber in its skull — and most things have one, because sound is the only long-distance sense that works — has bones alloyed from bloom-fruit. The herds crack the blooms off the low branches as they pass, and the trees do not resist. That is the transaction. The fruit is wages.

Now: the herds. Because the fauna of the Anvil is not an element of the landscape. The fauna is the landscape, moving.

The world turns slowly here — a day lasts the better part of a year — and dawn is not an event but a place: a band of melt and noise perhaps a hundred kilometers wide, crawling across the plateau at three kilometers an hour, a walking pace. It is called the Walking Morning, and it is forty thousand kilometers long, and it never ends, because the Anvil girdles the world and the Morning simply goes around it, forever, once per long year. Ahead of the Morning: months of frozen night, black, silent, locked. On it: thaw. And at the thaw line, everything that lives has gathered to receive it — because the felt that grew lush through the previous day froze at dusk and stood through the night as standing black hay, an entire continent's harvest stored in the cold, and the Morning is when it is collected.

So the herds walk with the morning, at the speed of the morning, in numbers that hide the ground for weeks-deep columns. Mill-herds, they are called, because of how they sound and how they move: slab-sided, many-legged, their leg-phases rippling down the column in waves like power traveling a line-shaft, individuals as interchangeable as teeth on a gear. Their hooves strike sparks off the flinty scree — invisible in the glare, but audible, a dry ticking like a textile mill, continuous, continental. Their lowing is a single note sustained across thousands of kilometers, a contact call, a mapping of the world in echo. Their breath merges into a fog bank that walks with them and can be seen from the rim as the white line that marks the Morning from horizon to horizon. In the dry cold their fur loads with static, and the column crawls with blue light, and when the grazing is heavy the whole front crackles like a fire that has decided to be electricity instead.

Above them the sky is full. This is the thing no visitor believes until they stand in it: there is no empty space. The gauze — the winged trillions who drink the steam-condensate off the valvewood crowns and pollinate everything and strip a thawed carcass in minutes — rises off the Morning in a shimmering haze that reaches to the ceiling of the flyable air, so that the light itself seems to grain and shimmer, and its wingbeat hum is the bass note under everything, the sound you only notice when, months later, it stops. The herds pack the ground. The gauze packs the sky. The valvewoods pack the valleys. And the night side — the night side is the most densely occupied of all, because the dark half of the Anvil is not empty. It is full. Every crevice, every pocket in the felt, every crack in the frozen rock is crammed with sleepers: eggs, spores, larvae, and folded animals stacked like inventory in a warehouse, frozen solid, waiting their turn at the thaw. The Anvil packs its presences in time as well as space. There is no room between them anywhere.

Not everything walks with the morning. At the trailing edge of the day, where dusk creeps after it at the same patient pace and the light begins its weeks-long failure, the stills are waiting. A still is the coldest thing alive: a low, motionless animal with blood that runs near ambient and vanes of black metal-bone it can raise like a sail. It lies in the herd's path, heat-invisible, for days, and it kills by being cold — it drinks warmth through whatever touches it, and the drained animal simply stops, frost feathering out across its flanks in seconds. A still that has fed must raise its vanes and dump the stolen heat, and the plume of steam rising off a still in the half-light is the only steam on the dusk side of the world, and the sight of it empties a hillside faster than any alarm call. The stills take the late, the old, the slow — the ones who failed to keep pace with the morning. Then the dark comes, and the stills freeze where they stand with their reserves banked in chemistry, and the ones that fed poorly do not wake again.

Here is what you will keep noticing, and keep failing to get used to: there are no artifacts. None. There has never been a wall, a tool, a road, a ruin, a scrap of worked material anywhere on the plateau — and yet the Anvil smells of hot metal and ozone and lubricant (the valvewood resins are oils; the tenders — small darting animals who live in the valve-ports and pick the rime from the seals, paid in sap-warmth — groom themselves slick with it), and it sounds like a mill, and it produces at continental scale to a schedule, and every part of it is maintained, fueled, harvested, and replaced. Nothing here is built. Everything is manufactured. The industry has no industry behind it; there was never a maker and there is no missing one. Evolution was the engineer, and the factory is simply what wilderness looks like when the gradient between fierce light and deep cold is the fuel, and there has been time enough to burn it well.

Time, here, is the one thing in generous supply. The herds circle the world once a long year and graze the same valleys on the same date, cycle after cycle. A valvewood takes a century to claim a hillside; the felt takes ten thousand years to lay its coke-beds; the plateau rises its finger's width and is in no hurry about it. The fast things — hoof, wing, the cracking bloom of a thaw — all move inside a patience so large it reads as stillness from outside. Nothing rots. Nothing rounds. The cycles turn like the slow shafts of a machine that was built to run for geological ages and is only now, perhaps, warming to its work.

The rules of the Anvil, as near as they can be written:

Water never falls. It is carried, pumped, breathed — and it is spent.

Heat is the only currency. Every partnership is a heat contract.

Sharpness is conserved.

There is no empty space, on the ground, in the air, in the ice, or in the calendar.

Walk with the morning, or not at all.

Stand on the rim long enough and you will hear it before you see anything: the hum of the gauze under the knock of the valvewoods under the tick of hoof-sparks under the one sustained note of the herds, a chord the size of a horizon, coming closer at three kilometers an hour. Then the white breath-line resolves out of the glare, and the frozen valley at your feet begins to tick and drip and crack awake, and the blackfelt steams, and the sleepers stir in their millions beneath the ice. The Walking Morning does not stop for anything, and it has never stopped, and the choice it offers is the only choice the Anvil has ever offered anything alive: step into the light and move, or let the dark have you until it comes around again.
