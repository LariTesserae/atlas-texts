You hear it before you see it. Hours below the tarn, crossing the high bare shoulder of the mountain, you feel it through the soles of your boots: a single low stroke, somewhere between a drumbeat and a landslide, arriving up through the rock rather than through the thin cold air. Then nothing, for a long while. Then another. Your pulse, without asking, begins to slow toward that tempo. By the time you crest the rim you are walking in time with the place.

The Ram Tarn sits just below the skyline, in the altitude where weather is manufactured. It is small — you could throw a stone across it on a good day, swim it in ten minutes — a single bowl of water brimming in a crater of shattered stone. The walls are the color of broken flint and they are *sharp*. Nothing here has been rounded. Three hundred years ago something deep met something wet and the mountain's shoulder blew open in a steam blast, and the rubble of that hour still stands exactly as it fell: glassy scarps, splintered ledges, talus blocks with edges that cut. Snowfields lean against the upper rim and never melt all the way. There is no soil. There is nothing green anywhere — not a blade, not a cushion of moss, not a stain of lichen on the rock. The mountain simply ends at a fracture line, and the water begins.

The water is cold in a serious way, a few degrees off ice, snowmelt and deep aquifer mixed, and it is *dense* — dense with dissolved mineral, dense with fine suspended bloom, dense with bodies, dense with sound. It is saturated to the edge of precipitation; everything it touches it slowly rimes with stone. It is also, faintly and undeniably, electrified. Lean over the brim and your hair prickles. Put a wet finger to your tongue and you taste pennies. The piezoelectric quartz veining the crater walls, squeezed day and night by the machine below, bleeds a small patient charge into the whole basin. Compasses are useless here. The animals are not.

Because the floor of the tarn is a machine. This is the thing the place is *about*: no hand built it, and it is industrial all the same. The blast crater punched down into a throat of fractured volcanic plug, and that plug — columnar, jointed, standing in sockets of its own debris — is now a field of stone rams. A hundred or more, each the width of a church bell, standing vertical in the dark water. Beneath them, deep chambers charge with gas and heated groundwater, slowly, over days, until pressure overcomes weight — and then a ram fires.

Watch one stroke from above and it is almost tender in its slowness. The water over a particular crown begins to dimple and boil with fine bubbles. Then the column rises — a finger's width, a hand, sometimes a full arm's length — driven up its socket on a cushion of steam and pressure, and the tarn answers with a boom that you feel in your sternum, your teeth, the marrow of the long bones. A plume of warmth and grey bloom billows up around the crown. For a moment the piston's head nearly breaks the surface, close enough to touch, streaming, and then the chamber vents, the pressure sighs away through a thousand fissures, and the ram settles slowly home into its socket with a deep mineral *thock* that rings the crater like a bell foundry. Around the floor, other chambers are charging. A boom every few minutes, somewhere in the basin, around the clock, around the year. The tarn is a hydraulic ram the size of a cathedral, and it has been running since the hour of its own birth.

The engine produces. That is the plainest way to say it. The strokes drive a circulation — a great slow gyre that takes a full week to turn over, dragging surface water down and bottom water up, spreading the vent-warmth and the mineral bloom through every stratum. On the rams' crowns and flanks grows a thin grey-gold felt, chemosynthetic, bacterial, the closest thing to a plant this world will ever grow, and the pistons cultivate it the way a piston cultivates nothing: by moving, by stirring, by bringing the chemistry to the film. Everything in the tarn eats from that felt, directly or at one remove.

And there is *so much* here to eat it. The fauna are not abundant so much as layered, stacked, near-touching. The surface carries a dimpling fleet of skaters, pale insects that ride the meniscus and flee each stroke's ring of waves. The midwater is thick with glass krill — thumb-length, transparent, their organs visible as copper filaments — in swarms so dense they turn the water to milk, so dense that at noon, when the sun drives clean white shafts down to the working floor, the swarms write moving shadow-script across the rams. Rind-snails graze every submerged stone, wearing the mineral crust as they make it. Socket eels, long as a whip and narrow as a ribbon, live *inside* the piston sockets, in the pressure chambers themselves, and when a ram fires they ride the stroke upward like passengers in an elevator, mouths open, straining the fresh bloom from the plume, then pour back down the settling column to wait days for the next one. Dog-sized armored things walk the floor on many deliberate legs, polishing it, unhurried, deaf to everything but the boom.

The boom is the clock of this world, and the biology has wound itself around it. The krill cannot molt without it — their shells grow rigid in the mineral-saturated water, and only the shockwave of a stroke, felt through the body like a hammer tap, cracks the old casing cleanly so the animal can back out of it. When a ram fires, a soft crackle runs through the whole swarm at once, thousands of shells splitting in a single second, like static, like applause in another room. Spawning, hunting, settling, hatching: all of it is timed to percussion. Stay an hour at the brim and your own heartbeat slows to the tarn's tempo. This is not a metaphor. It just happens.

Between strokes, the basin is not quiet. It is reloading. There is the constant fine crackle of bubbles rising off the floor, the tick and ping of warm stone cooling in cold water, the clicking of the krill, the low hum that the water's charge lays against the eardrum, and occasionally — once in a week, perhaps — the calving of a slab from the sharp young walls, which is the loudest sound the tarn knows how to make, a concussion followed by a long roil of standing waves slapping the far rim. The crater's shape focuses all of it. Shout across the water and your voice comes back to you seconds later, deepened, lengthened, as if the tarn had considered your words and answered in its own register.

The light is ordinary, which somehow makes it stranger. This is no lightless deep: the sky sits right on top of the water, and by midday the sun reaches all the way down, so the whole engine works in plain view — rams rising and settling in slow columns of bubbles, swarm-shadows sliding, the socket eels pouring up and down like quicksilver. At dusk the basin goes the color of lead and the booms come up out of nothing visible. In winter a collar of ice forms around the rim, thick enough to walk on, but the center never closes; the engine keeps its throat open, black and steaming faintly into air so cold it hurts to breathe, the warm heart of the mountain refusing to be sealed.

Everything here is patient. The water level does not change — rain for a month and the brim does not rise; drought for a year and it does not fall. The engine drinks exactly what the sky gives, and what it does not drink it sends out through a single notch in the rim, one white thread of overflow that falls away down the mountain's flank, the tarn's only export. Sediment settles a grain at a time. The rams wear their sockets wider by less than a hair in a decade. And roughly once in a dozen years, by slow drift of timing, the chambers come into agreement, and every ram in the floor fires within the same few minutes — the full stroke. The surface of the tarn domes bodily upward. The boom rolls off the rim and is heard in valleys a day's walk away. In that hour every socket eel feeds at once, every swarm cracks its shell at once, and the next generation of the basin begins.

It will not last forever. The chambers are silting at their own geological pace; in some thousands of years the rams will seize in their sockets one by one, the gyre will stall, the charge will fade, the ice will close over the middle for good, and the Ram Tarn will become an ordinary cold lake on a bare mountain, and then a bogless stone basin, and then a strange round ruin of sediment that puzzles whatever comes later. But that ending is so slow it hardly counts as motion.

For now it runs, unwitnessed and unbothered. Nothing made by hands persists here — drop a coin and the swarms will worry it apart, or the water will rime it over, and in five years you would find only a stone with a coin's ghost inside it. The tarn permits no rival structures, no second machines. It is the one engine this mountain needs, a factory with no walls, an industry of pressure and cold and percussion, manufacturing warmth, food, sound, and time — patient, deafening, brimming at the top of the world, beating.
