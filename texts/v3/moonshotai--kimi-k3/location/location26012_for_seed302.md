You hear it three days before you see it. Not through the air, at first — through the ground. A pressure in the soles of your feet at night, a hum in whatever stone you sleep against, as though the earth were a held bell. By the second day it is in the air: a roar with no weather in it, no rise, no fall, a sound like surf that has forgotten how to break. Then you climb above the cloud line, and there it is.

A massif of white stone, blazing. From below it reads as a single frozen cataract — a waterfall the size of a mountain range, stopped mid-plunge. Closer, it resolves into columns. Closer still, the columns resolve into more columns, and you understand the first true thing about the place: there is no space in it. It is packed. Take a cavern the size of a province, give it a hundred million years to fill itself with its own drippings — stalagmites, flowstone, draperies, straws — then peel the roof away and hold the whole thing up to the sun. That is this. A cave turned inside out. Ridges of it run to the horizon, colonnade after colonnade, every one of them wet, every one of them roaring.

The stone is travertine, tufa, flowstone — banded cream and ivory and pale rust, laid down in layers thin as leaves. Nothing here is sharp. Time has taken every edge the way tongues take the edges of candy; pillars swell and sag like candle wax, like bone, like something once liquid that agreed to stop. In places the stone is porous as hard sponge; in places it is dense and glassy, banded like onyx, and where the water sheets over it, it shines with a second, liquid glass. It looks built. That is the strangest thing to hold in your mind — it looks more made than any city, terraces stacked like amphitheaters, pillars ranked like organ pipes, slots between fused walls straight as streets, and none of it was ever built. Nothing here was made. Everything here is architecture.

And everywhere, water. It comes from two directions at once. The air itself is saturated, heavy, and the high stone is cool — cool enough that the sky condenses on it, so the massif sweats continuously, every surface beading and weeping like a cold wall in a warm room. And from within: the crown of the massif is a field of low stone domes, each one weeping from its apex, springs under deep pressure pushing up through the rock they themselves are building. Water rises and water descends and meets in the stone, and then it all goes down — down the flutes of the pillars, one thread per groove, each thread in a groove it has worn and sealed and worn again for longer than language. The pools along the way sit exactly at their brims, rimmed with rimstone dams like the edges of stacked plates, each pool releasing one thread at its lip. No more, no less. Nothing floods. Nothing is ever dry. In the thin fins and blades at the escarpment's edge, water climbs a hand's breadth upward through capillary stone before it lets go and falls — the one small disobedience the place allows itself.

At the rim you can see the whole economy. The overflow combs over the edge in a lace of a billion threads, a continuous fringe of falling water going over into the cloud sea below — and dissolving. The drops fray to mist on the way down. No drop completes its fall. The mist sinks into the cloud, the cloud leans against the cool mountain, the crown drinks, the springs rise. You are standing inside a closed loop, a circuit of water with the sun for a heart, and it has been running at exactly this rate for longer than anything with a name for rates has existed.

Now the sound. Up close it separates into layers, the way a fabric separates into threads when you hold it to the light. The top layer is a hiss — seepage, ten million pores exhaling water. Beneath that, a clatter: the threads striking stone, the drips from every ledge and curtain and straw, each one tiny, each one distinct if you fix your attention on it, all of them summing to something like applause. Applause that has never stopped. And beneath that, the bass, which you do not hear so much as receive: water falling inside the hollow pillars, the ones with wet throats, booming in the bone of the mountain. It comes up through your feet and rearranges your breathing.

It is deafening. That word is too small. The reason is simple: there is nothing soft here. No moss, no soil, no leaf — nothing to drink a sound. Bare wet stone is a mirror for noise, and the massif is a hall of mirrors, millions of hard faces throwing every drip back and forth until it joins the sum. You shout and feel your throat work and hear nothing at all. You learn, if you travel with anyone, to speak palm-flat against a pillar, through the stone. There are no echoes — an echo needs silence to come home to, and silence has never once occurred here. And then, after a day, two days, the strangest accommodation: because the chord never varies — no attack, no decay, no phrase — the listening mind begins to erase it. Unvarying sound carries no information, and the brain trades it away. The loudest place in the world becomes, hour by hour, a kind of silence. You live inside a roar the way fish live inside water.

Moving through the interior is not walking; it is a negotiation conducted with your whole body. You squeeze sideways between pillars slick as poured glass. You wade shin-deep in runnels milky with dissolved stone, cold enough to ache. You climb rimstone steps, your shoulder always against one wet flank, your palm on another. Visibility is a few pillars deep; beyond that, white stone and white mist and white light layered to opacity. The air itself is spray — you become wet from breathing. Your breath fogs, because it is always just cold enough for breath to fog, and the fog rises into light that has not moved.

Because it is always just past noon. This is the fact you resist, and then stop resisting. The sun stands where it stands. The glare off the wet stone does not shift; the shadows of the pillars lie where they have always lain, part of the architecture, painted in place. Mist-halos hang at fixed angles. The pools, brimming and still, hold a second sky, so the floor shines like the firmament and the light comes at you from below as well as above. The brightness has weight the way the sound has weight. Every sense is filled to its ceiling — sight by glare, hearing by the chord, touch by the stone that is always against you, taste by the iron-and-coins flavor of the air. Only smell is quiet. Smell has one note here: cold wet mineral, faint sulfur, the smell of the inside of a stone.

Where the packing loosens — on the shoulders of the massif, in basins between ridges — the terraces spread: fields of rimstone, amphitheaters of opal water, pool above pool above pool in long descending staircases, each dam a curled lip of stone built by its own overflow. Up on the crown, among the weeping domes, the stone is newest and softest, almost yielding under the thumb, still warm from nothing, still being laid down. This is where the mountain is happening, molecule by molecule.

What is absent: green. Your eye searches for it the way a tongue searches a gap — no leaf, no blade, no needle, nothing that waves, nothing that turns toward the light. The light here is taken by stone. And yet the seams are not lifeless. In the sheltered angles, out of the worst of the glare and the scour, there is a film: rust-orange, ochre, sulfur-yellow, a grey so deep it reads as green until you understand it isn't, a black manganese sheen like oil on water. It is life that drinks dissolved stone — iron-eaters, sulfur-eaters, a chemistry from before the invention of leaves. It covers everything it can reach, a micron thick, and it is the pasture of the only common animal.

They are called, by no one, the skaters: finger-length, translucent, more water than flesh, a lens of water carried over a many-legged body like a droplet that decided to walk. They graze the film. Each leaves behind it a trail of clean pale stone, and the film recolonizes the trail within weeks, and that is the most change you will ever witness here — a line of handwriting erasing itself. Lift one from the runnel and it sags, unmade, unable to hold its shape outside its element. Put it back and it resumes, unhurried, at the pace of the world.

And then there is the other one, which you will not see. There may be a few dozen in the entire massif. It stands tall as a person on four long splayed legs built for slick stone; it is pale as the pillars and blind as the deep cave it resembles; its head is mostly ear — a flared bowl of thin bone, a listening dish. One forelimb ends in a single stout finger tipped with mineral. It walks a territory of ten thousand pillars, and it knows them the way you know a voice. Because each hollow pillar has a note — the water falling inside it tunes it — and the massif, to the listener, is not a maze but a chord-map, a geography of pitches, and it navigates by the architecture of a sound you cannot even hear as separate. Sometimes it stops and touches a pillar with that one finger.

A single clear note, struck out of the roar. The only deliberate sound in the world. The roar closes over it like water over a stone, and the chord goes on, and the listener cups its whole head against the pillar and drinks the note's return, reading the pillar's depth, its fullness, its health. It moves between these moments at the pace of the place — a step a day, a stillness lasting a season. Mineral films its joints. The flowstone is slowly, courteously depositing on it, and it permits this. It wears the beginning of its own burial the way the pillars wear their water. Even the rarest animal here aspires to the condition of stone.

Because that is the law, once you have stayed long enough to read it: nothing changes; only matter moves. For every gram the water takes from a pillar's face, it gives a gram back on the far side. Every column is being dissolved and rebuilt in place, atom by atom, forever — which means the pillars are not objects. They are habits. They are events repeated so faithfully that they have come to look like things, the way a standing wave in a river looks like a thing. The shape abides; the substance passes through. The mountain you see is not the mountain that was here; it is the mountain's memory of itself, made mineral, kept current.

Noon does not end. The chord does not modulate. The film creeps back over every trail. The overflow frays to mist and the crown drinks the mist. An event — a true event, a thing happening that has not been happening — is the rarest substance in this world, rarer than the listener, rarer than dryness, and almost the only events there are come from that one mineral fingertip, somewhere in the white labyrinth, once in an hour or a day.

You stand in the roar you can no longer hear, in the glare that no longer moves, wet through from the breathing air, and if you stayed — and you would have to mean it — the loop would take you in without noticing: your breath's moisture rising to the cloud, your small heat accepted by the cool stone, your voice long since surrendered to the chord. The place would not change for you. The place does not change for anything. That is what it is for, perhaps, in the scheme of landscapes: to be the one that was finished — finished the way a chord held forever is finished — and to go on being exactly, deafeningly, glitteringly itself.
