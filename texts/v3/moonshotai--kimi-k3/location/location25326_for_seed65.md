# The Sounding Plateau

You hear it before you see it. The climb up out of the cloud-sea takes the better part of a day, and for the last hour of it the light arrives first — a fierce, white-blue brightness soaking down through the thinning mist — and then, as your head breaks the surface of the clouds, the sound arrives, all at once, whole, like stepping into the inside of a bell.

Then you stand on the rim and look across it, and the second strangest thing happens: for all that noise, the land is almost empty. A hundred miles of pale stone, and you can count the living things in view on one hand, each one the size of a punctuation mark. The plateau sounds crowded and looks abandoned. Both impressions are correct, and neither ever changes.

### Terrain

The plateau sits at an altitude where the air is thin enough to argue with. The sky overhead is deep violet, dark enough at the zenith to hold two stars in full daylight. Below the rim, on every side, the cloud-sea: white, featureless, permanent, out of which all the moisture and all the history of this place once rose. What lies beneath it, the plateau does not say.

The land itself is finished. Whatever violence shaped it — the old strong winds, the old wet — ended long ago, and what remains is the settled result: broad pavements of pale granite polished like bone, rounded tors, basins floored with ancient silt, scree slopes in which every stone has found its final angle and sits varnished and immovable. To kick a pebble loose here would be to cause the first landslide in a thousand years.

At the rim, where a faint upslope wind still rises off the cloud-sea exactly as it always has, the bedrock is carved into flute-fields — scalloped pipes and hollows, organ-like, some of them older than the species that nest in them. The stone is honeycombed. Strike it, and the answer comes back from somewhere beneath your feet rather than from where you struck.

### The Light

The sun does not move. It stands at a fixed height above the cloud-sea, white and unwavering, and if it travels at all, it travels more slowly than lichen grows — and lichen is the local unit of patience.

Because of this, everything on the plateau has a day side and a night side, and the night side has never once been lit. Every boulder, every tor, every flake of scree is two-toned: bleached pale on its lit face, near-black on its shadowed one. Shadows lie fixed at a single angle across the whole country, a hundred thousand stopped sundials. No one has ever seen the dark face of any stone here. You could carry a lamp. No one has.

The temperature is a cellar's, and it does not vary — except across the line. Lit stone is hand-warm; shadow is killing cold; and every shadowed hollow, radiating its cold into the thin air, works as a trap.

### The Water

There is no rain to speak of, no standing water, no stream that flows. What water the plateau owns is mined from the wind.

The air that rises off the cloud-sea arrives wrung nearly dry, but not quite. What little it carries freezes out, grain by grain, in the permanent cold traps of the shadow-hollows. Every shadow on the plateau therefore has the same anatomy: a white heart of hoarfrost at its center, and at its margin — where the fixed light just reaches the stone — a thin rim of perpetual melt, dark and damp. The frost accumulates at the heart and melts at the rim at exactly equal rates. The boundary between them has not moved in longer than anything here can remember.

This means the wet places of the world are drawn, precisely, as contour lines — and it is along those contours, along the damp hems of ten thousand unmoving shadows, that the sparse life of the plateau has gathered. Lichen in rust and black-green threads the melt-rims exactly. From above, the country looks embroidered: every shadow hemmed with a narrow band of growth. All the life of this place lives on a contour line.

### The Sound

The air is too thin to be trusted with sound, so the stone carries it instead. That is the first rule of the acoustics here: the ground is the medium. The chorus arrives through the soles of your boots, through the long bones of your legs, through the floor of your skull.

It has layers. At the bottom, the stone chord — the constant wind playing the flute-fields, deep and organ-like, different in every district, so that a traveler could navigate this country blindfold, by harmony. Over that, the hiss: millions of fret-crickets in the lichen-hems, rasping a noise floor so continuous that the ear edits it out within the hour. Over that, the drumming — knock-hares hammering their signal-stones, the pulses traveling for miles through bedrock. Over that, the gyre-swifts at the rim, thousands-strong columns of them, crying as they wheel. And above everything, at long intervals, from every direction and distance, the hornbacks: single notes held so long they seem less like calls than like the country itself bowing a string.

Individual voices come and go. The sum never changes. It is a standing wave the size of a country, and at the rim it is genuinely deafening — a physical pressure, painful, the kind of loudness that rearranges a visitor's breathing.

Yet the plateau keeps its silence the way it keeps its water: stored in specific geometry. The dry silt basins of the interior swallow sound whole. Walk down into one and the chorus falls away layer by layer until even the cricket-hiss dies, and the loudest thing left is your own pulse, which visitors find intolerable. These basins are the dormitories of the plateau. Everything that sleeps, sleeps there.

### The Living

The hornbacks are the large life — slate-colored, heavy as yaks, each one crowned with a hollow convoluted crest of gristle that is, functionally, a trumpet. They crop the lichen-hems and drink by licking the melt-rims, walking the ancient polished trails that run from shadow to shadow. They are spread one to a shadow, because a shadow feeds one. The herd has never gathered; it exists only as a chord sustained across a hundred miles, each animal a single held note, correctly spaced. Their loneliness is maintained by song, and their notes — this is the strange, settled elegance of it — sit in the gaps of the local stone chord. Every district's animals have learned to sing in the frequencies its rocks leave open.

The knock-hares drum with hind feet on particular adopted slabs, drum-stones polished by ten thousand generations of the same signal. To listen, they press their chins flat to the bedrock and go still, reading the pulses of hares miles away. The fret-crickets live in the hems and are the hems, effectively — half the lichen-margin's bulk is cricket. The gyre-swifts never land en masse; they ride the rim's rising air in permanent wheeling columns, feeding on the spores and gossamer insects the upflow carries out of the unseen world below, roosting in the ancient flute-holes.

And there is the hush. Leopard-sized, the pale color of a lit stone face, soft-footed, ear-valved — the only silent thing in the loudest place in the world. It dens in the dead basins, hunts in the full roar of the open pavement, and times its final rush to the swell of a hornback's note. Nothing here watches; everything listens; and the hush cannot be heard. It is the sole argument against the local philosophy that noise is life.

### The Traces

There are no structures. Nothing built, nothing arranged, no walls or graves or fire-rings. What exists, scattered at perhaps a dozen sites across the whole region, are knapping floors: patches of struck flakes around a boulder, hand-axes and rough blades of the same pale granite.

They are old beyond any local measure. The plateau has varnished them to the same finish as its own skin, and the lichen hems them exactly as it hems every other stone, which is the country's way of saying *these are mine now*. Whoever made them came up out of the cloud with stone in their hands and left with less of it, and did not stay, and the plateau does not explain them. The only tool-users currently in residence are the hares, with their adopted drum-stones, who have been at it far longer.

### The Laws

The sun does not move.

Every shadow has a white heart and a hem of life, and the boundary between them is fixed.

The ground remembers every sound. Step on bare stone and the whole country knows it; the lichen mats and the silt basins are the only quiet footing.

The loud are the livestock. The silent is the lion.

The wind that carved the flutes still blows. It will never finish, and it will never change its mind.

There is no word here for *day*. Visitors bring the word with them, and wear it out. Time is measured in lichen-growth and held notes. Events happen — a call, a kill, a dislodged pebble — but nothing changes.

Stand at the rim, then, with the cloud-sea below and a hundred miles of half-lit stone ahead, four animals the size of punctuation marks and a sound like the inside of a bell. It has always been exactly like this. That is the strangest thing of all: whenever you come back, it will still be true.
