# The Keld Pot

The loudest place for a day's walk in any direction is a hole in the ground that no one can find unless they already know where it is.

The country above it is a low moor of whaleback limestone fells, the rock the color of wet ash, the elevation nothing — a long, slow heave of land under a cold sky. Rain comes in squalls and is gone within minutes; the rock drinks it whole. Between squalls the moor is a dry country pretending to be a wet one — dew on the bent grass, damp in the moss, mist standing in the hollows like something left out overnight. The hollows are everywhere: shakeholes, dimples in the fell, as if the land had been pressed with a thumb while it was still soft. After hard rain a few of them hold ponds the size of dinner tables. By morning the ponds are gone, drunk down through the cracks. This is a land with water in it but almost none on it. It keeps its moisture the way a miser keeps coins: hidden, in pockets, underground.

You can walk it dawn to dusk and meet nothing with a spine. Once, in spring, a snipe drumming over a peat hollow. A sheep's skull in a grike, clean as porcelain. Half a mile from the hole, the foundation stones of a shieling, robbed down to knee height, unconnected to anything anymore except by the dead who once used both. The emptiness is the moor's main quality — vast space between presences, so that every presence, when it comes, lands like an event.

## The finding

One shakehole among hundreds is the one. Those who know, know it by small signs: nettles in the funnel bottom, growing rank on the droppings of whatever shelters at the lip; a ring of greener grass around the slot in summer, where the cave's exhaled cold makes dew out of the night air; and on the coldest winter mornings, a plume of steam rising from the ground itself, the cave's breath condensing, the hole announcing itself to an empty fell like a sleeper under snow.

The slot is body-width, under an overhang, floored with flowstone slick as poured tallow. Three small cups are pecked into the lintel stone in a row, the width of a thumb apart — old work, softened by centuries of wet.

## The descent

The passage drops at a steep wriggle, and the stone remembers feet. There are dished hollows at the right intervals for heels, polished by wool and boot and bare skin, and a handhold at the squeeze worn to the shape of a grasping hand. Everything loose has already fallen. The floor is sediment and blocks glued down by calcite. This is an old wound in the rock, long healed over, settled into itself.

The sound arrives before anything else — before light, before the smell of wet stone, before the room itself. First it comes through the bone of a hip braced against the passage wall. Then it is in the teeth. Then it is a pressure more than a pitch, and then, rounding the last bend, it stops being something heard and becomes something stood inside of.

## The room

The chamber is a bell of dark, eight paces across, rising high overhead into a chimney of blackness. From a crack in that chimney falls the water: not a waterfall — a thread, wrist-thick, a wire of water drilling down out of the unseen and striking a basin of stone in the floor. Most of the year this is all it is. In drought it thins to drops that fall the whole height and shatter. After rain it thickens — but three days late, because the moor above filters its weather down through forty feet of rock, and the room reports the sky's news on a long delay, a memory of rain falling on no one.

## The sound

It is very nearly the loudest thing a person can stand inside without harm. It does not hurt, quite. It fills.

There is a standing note at the bottom of it, the room's own note, its resonant frequency — felt in the sternum, in the fillings of teeth, in the palms laid flat on the floor. Over that, a mid hiss like a furnace drawing air. Over that, the high fizz of spray, fine as escaping steam. There is no direction to it; it comes from the walls as much as from the water. Speech dies at the lips. A shout comes back off the stone shredded, unrecognizable, a voice passed through a torn drum. Two people in the chamber communicate by touch, by sign, by mouth pressed to ear.

After an hour the ear gives up. The roar becomes a silence with texture. The brain begins to hear inside it the way eyes adjust to dark — and then the smallest sound that penetrates, a pebble shifting underfoot, a change in the drip's rhythm, arrives enormous, like a word spoken in a library.

This is the room's privacy and its law: nothing that happens here can be overheard, even by oneself. A person could weep, rage, confess, go briefly mad at the top of their voice, and the room would keep none of it and give none of it away. People did, perhaps. That may be what the room was for.

Coming back out, the moor feels padded, stuffed with wool, and the roar persists as a ghost in the ears for hours — the cave's voice staying in the body after the body has left the cave.

## The light

There is almost none. The chimney that admits the water admits a grey seep of sky — a paleness, a suggestion — enough, at midday, to gleam along the falling thread and make it visible: a bright, flickering line in the dark, the only thing in the room that catches light. Breath steams faintly in it. For a few weeks on either side of the year's height, at the right hour, the sun itself threads the shaft, and then the wire of water goes incandescent and a small column of gold stands in the room, and its foot lands precisely on the lip of the stone trough that was built, long ago, exactly where the light comes down.

The alignment is drifting. Centuries from now the beam will miss the trough entirely. The room is patient about this.

## The green

The room spends its entire allowance of vegetation in one patch, and spends it lavishly. Where the grey light falls and the spray drifts, there is a garden the size of a thrown cloak: moss in cushions deep enough to lose a finger, sodden, jeweled, layered green on green; hart's-tongue ferns unstraping their glossy tongues; liverwort spread in snakeskin platters across the wet rock; a skin of emerald algae on every surface the mist reaches. It is lush the way a relic is rich — all the wealth gathered into one small object. Ten paces away the walls are bare black stone and nothing.

And in the dark niches just past the light's edge, the goblin's gold: a moss that lives by catching stray light and returning it, so that the darkness near the shaft is faintly, genuinely starred with green-gold points — the room's own dim constellation, grown rather than hung.

## The stone

The walls are wet, black, waxy with old flowstone — pours that finished pouring long ago and now simply gleam. In a side alcove there is moonmilk, white calcite paste soft as cheese, that appears and dries across decades on some schedule of chemistry no one watches. The falling thread has built its own furniture: rimstone dams, low dikes of stone in the shape of the water's impatience, and a gourd at the basin's edge where drips keep a nest of cave pearls turning, each grown a grain a decade, round as eggs, pale as teeth.

## The people

They came for the water, one guesses — the one unfailing water in a thirsty land. They left: the worn footholds; the cup-marks at the lintel; a trough of slabs, waist-long, joints once luted with clay, set under the fall to catch it, its rim now grown over with calcite — the stone is slowly digesting it. A half-circle of dry-stone wall against the chamber's side, slumped to knee height, mossed over, sheltering a flat rock the size of a bed or an altar. A faint charcoal lens in the floor sediment, where a fire was, rarely, long ago.

And in the clear shallows of the pool, crusted into the siltstone: flint flakes, a bone pin, clay beads, one bead of amber, and — newest and still older than anyone's memory of the place — a plain bronze ring. They are not objects anymore, exactly. They are inclusions. To take one now you would have to break rock. The offerings are becoming geology, which is perhaps what offerings hope for.

Then the people stopped coming, centuries back, and the cave folded even that era into its own timescale without pausing.

## The living

Almost nothing lives here, and what does is specific.

In the pool: a few dozen white amphipods, eyeless, the length of rice grains, grazing films too thin to see. At the entrance's twilight: a single orb-weaving spider that hangs its egg sacs like white beads on a thread — a necklace in the dark. Two or three herald moths, wings closed, dead-leaf, waiting out the winter on the wall. Some winters, one bat, folded like a shut umbrella into a ceiling crack.

And the room's one citizen: a dipper. It comes through the black without hesitation, lands on the trough, bows — the bird bows; it is what dippers do — and then walks down the stones into the water and along the bottom, alive inside the roar's own element. Its call is a flint-struck, ringing note, shaped by a million years of whitewater to be audible in exactly this: the only voice the room does not erase.

## The slow clock

Nothing hurries here. The flowstone thickens by a page a century. The moss advances across the old wall a handspan a lifetime. The thread of water is the only fast thing in the room, and it is the same fast forever, which is a kind of stillness.

Once in a decade, or a generation, the outlet chokes on its own sediment and the pool rises. As the air shrinks, the room's standing note climbs — the great voice tightening, pitch by pitch — and anyone inside would hear the flood announced musically before they saw it. Then it drains, and leaves a new line of silt at the wall, knee-high, waist-high: the room's diary, one entry a generation, written in mud.

## The rules

The water never stops. It thins; it does not stop. That is the room's one promise.

The cold never changes: five degrees, wet, constant, in every season, sucking the heat out of any hand, any seat of stone. Weather enters only as sound, and three days late.

Light is an appointment, not a condition. It comes at its season, briefly, and the garden lives the whole year on the ration.

Speech is useless. The room keeps a short inventory — one water, one light, one garden, one wall, one trough, one bird, some days — and whatever enters is counted. A thrown bead enters the record. A person, for the length of a visit, is the largest thing the room contains, and the deafest.

And the roar goes on whether or not there is an ear to refuse it: the room talking to itself, in the dark, under an empty moor, at the top of its voice, forever.
