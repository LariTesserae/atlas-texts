The country is not finished. That is the first fact of it and the last. Everything else — the cold, the height, the dark, the tremendous noise — follows from this: you are standing in a region of the world that is still being invented, and the invention is proceeding patiently, across centuries, and it is not going well or badly, simply *going*, the way stone goes.

Begin with the sound, because the sound comes first here. Sometimes literally.

**The Chord.** The mountains are rising — a young range the locals of no locality call the Teeth, eight kilometers of raw, sheer, unweathered stone — and their rising is not a matter of earthquakes but of a sustained, infrasonic tone, a root note too low for ears that you nonetheless hear in the sternum, the teeth, the jelly of the eyes. It swells and fades across decades. In loud years the dust lifts off the plains in sheets and stands in the air, undecided. In quiet years it settles, and the settling is the only precipitation this world has: there is no water, has never been water, the word *rain* has no referent. What water does elsewhere, sound does here. It pools in resonant basins. It flows along prepared channels. It is drunk. It erodes — the cliffs shed vast thin spall-sheets, shaken loose by their own singing, and this acoustic weathering is the only erosion the region has yet learned. The plains are dust and bedrock with nothing between. There is no gravel. The world has not had time to make small pieces of itself; the middle sizes have not been invented. Somewhere beneath a shrieking scarp, the first pebble is almost, almost finished.

**The light** is dim because most of it hasn't arrived. Light is slow here — slow in a way that is not a constant but a local habit, still being negotiated — and the sun, such as it is, a dull ember low in a pewter haze, switched on recently enough that its first light is still en route. On the far plain you can see it coming: a brightening on the ground, a gray-gold stain creeping across the dust at walking pace, due to arrive as true sunrise in a few hundred years. Meanwhile light does other unfinished things. It pools in hollows like a liquid — visible lakes of pale luminance you can walk down into and stand in, lit from below. And shadows do not vanish when their occasion passes. They settle. They deposit. The valleys are floored with shadow-sediment, dark strata of every shadow every stone has ever cast, a legible archive. You can read, in a cliff's shadow-bank, the silhouette of a pinnacle that spalled away four thousand years ago. Nothing here is ever erased. The world keeps every draft.

**The flora** is lush and contains not a drop of water. Where the Chord folds against itself and stands still — at the nodes of the world's own resonance — sound condenses, and there the Choir Groves grow. They are the oases of this country: forests of hollow quartz reeds forty meters tall, tuned like organ pipes, played by the thin cold wind and by the Chord itself; pillar-trees of selenite and columnar basalt that accrete at the tip a finger's width a century and ring, when struck, for years; felt-fields of gray mineral wool that spread a meter a hundred years, drinking vibration straight out of the ground; and under everything, films of charged dust-lichen crawling across the stone in slow calligraphy. The groves glow — piezoluminescence, light squeezed out of crystal by the pressure of the Chord — milk-blue, pale green, the only bright things in a dim world, and cold to the touch, all of it, a cold garden. Within a grove the density is extravagant, almost oppressive: sound braided into standing waves thick enough to lean on, growth upon growth, ringing within ringing. But the nodes are far apart. Nine groves, perhaps twelve, in a region you could cross in a season — nine days' walk of humming gray steppe between one and the next, the felt-mats thinning, the reeds failing, until there is only dust, the drone in the chest, and the far glow of the next oasis like a cold campfire over the horizon.

**The artifacts** are the strangest thing here, because there is no one to have made them. There is no animal life — none. No burrows, no tracks, no bones, and the shadow-archive, which forgets nothing, holds no silhouette of anything that ever walked. And yet: the Lines. Single courses of hand-fitted stone running dead straight for tens of kilometers across the plains, humming. They are waveguides — aqueducts, in a country where sound is water — carrying the Chord's overflow from the loud roots of the Teeth out to the groves, irrigating them, and they do a second job as well: they anchor geometry. Walk a Line and distance behaves; the far spire is as far as it looks. Leave the Line and the walk to that same spire takes as long as it takes. Then there are the cairns — tuned stacks of resonant stone, one every few days of travel — inside whose radius the local laws hold firm: echoes follow their sounds in proper order, shadows stay attached to their objects, pebbles stay down. Outside the cairn-fields, in the deep canyons of the Teeth, echoes arrive *before* the strikes that make them, and you learn to listen for the ring of a stone you have not yet dropped. There are listening cells, too: corbelled domes of fitted slab, doorless, each with a stone ear-trumpet set in the wall and a seat facing it, where someone once sat, or will sit, attending to the world being born. And knapping floors, where stone was flaked not for edges but for pitch — the rejects scattered in a glittering halo, each flake ringing faintly, wrongly. Fewer than forty sites in the whole region. All of them un-eroding. The cairns are sharper-edged each century; the cells' slabs fit tighter every decade; the knapping debris is less weathered than the day it — will be? — dropped. The ruins are condensing out of the future, arriving early, the way echoes do here. The builders cast no shadows in the sediment record because they have not yet stood in the light. Perhaps the structures are instructions the world is following in order to invent its makers.

**The Edge.** Walk far enough in any direction the Lines don't go and the world simply declines to continue. The horizon granulates: a standing gray static from sky to ground, like weather that never arrives, giving off a soft white-noise hiss beneath the Chord's deep note. It is not a wall and not a void; it is the unwritten. Two of the Lines run toward it and stop at facing cairns, as if surveying it. One Line goes in. Nothing has returned from it, because *return* is one of the concepts that hasn't been built yet on that side.

**The pace** of all of this is geological, liturgical. Time thickens with altitude — the highest Teeth are so new that duration itself hasn't properly started up there, and events smear: a struck stone's ring lasts a generation; a falling spall-sheet hangs in the air across a season, descending by degrees. The felt-fields creep. The dust-lichen writes its one long sentence. The groves tune themselves over millennia, and when a great swell of the Chord peaks — once a century, twice — the reeds put out ringing node-flanges all at once, a flowering that is also a chord, cold light and cold sound braiding up into the dark, and the Lines hum it away across the plains to groves not yet grown, past cairns not yet built by builders not yet born, toward a sunrise that has been on its way since the world began and will get here when it gets here.

Stand in a grove at the peak of a swell and you will understand the ecology of the place in your body: the pressure in the bones that is the rain; the glow that is the only warmth of color; the cold thin air; the nine-days' silence of the steppe waiting beyond the last reed; the shadow-strata underfoot keeping every version of the world that has ever stood in this spot. It is loud. It is nearly empty. It is dim, and cold, and rising, and young — young in a way that has nothing to do with time and everything to do with the fact that the laws here are still being practiced, still being sketched in dust and tuned stone and patient sound, and may yet be revised.

Somewhere below the Teeth, in the spall, the first pebble finishes becoming small. The world takes note, in shadow, and begins on the second.
