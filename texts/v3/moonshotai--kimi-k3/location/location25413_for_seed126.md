They call it the Anneal — the world that finished becoming.

From far off it is the brightest object in its sky: a planet with the albedo of a pearl, cloudless, unwet, returning its star's light almost undiminished. Closer in, the brightness resolves into flatness — a flatness that occurs nowhere in nature and here is total. The whole surface has been planed, lapped, worn down to a single continuous plane, and what the plane exposes is the cross-section of everything that was ever built here. The planet is a book sliced through one line of text, and you walk on the edges of its pages.

The ground is marquetry. Every square meter of it is made — nowhere is there soil, nowhere a stone that was not manufactured — and every step crosses a hundred thousand years of buried intention, cut and polished to a tolerance of microns. Here a braid of conduits runs exposed as parallel lines of blackened copper, waist-wide, vanishing into the shimmer. Here a structural foam has been worn to a lace of bronze that crunches faintly underfoot. Here a band of black glass, kilometers long, that was once a layer that thought. Here a seam of bone-white ceramic. Here — and you learn to look for these — strata of close, regular marks, dense as script, because they are script: archives, compressed and fossilized, sliced at the level of a single line, readable now only the way tree rings are readable. Whole libraries pass under your feet as bands of slightly darker gray.

Relief is measured in tens of meters over hundreds of kilometers. The tallest thing in a day's walk may be your own morning shadow. There are shallow basins where some buried district subsided an eon ago, and long low swells, days apart, that are not geology at all but convection — because the crust floats. Beneath the plane, meters down or kilometers, it varies, lies the world's one fluid. Not water; there has never been water here. A salt, melted at the world's making and never allowed to freeze: warm, dense, faintly luminous, circulating in cells the size of continents. The plain rides on it the way pack ice rides a sea, and the plain's slow breathing — a heave of centimeters over years, felt only as a change in the horizon — is its circulation. Where a seam fails, the salt rises: a spring of soft amber light that crusts to glass within moments of meeting the air, and around each spring a garden grows, the only crowded places on the planet.

And there are the verticals. A dozen on the whole world, perhaps two dozen — stumps. Things that once went up and no longer do. One is a tether anchor, a mountain-scale fitting sheared off thirty meters above the plane, and from it a fallen guy-line runs dead straight for over a thousand kilometers, a carbon filament thick as a river valley, half-buried, still bleeding a slow charge into the ground. Along it grows the only linear thing in the ecology: an orchard of antenna-trees, fractal, black, drinking the leakage. You can see a stump from weeks away. Travelers steer by them.

The air is thin, bone-dry, and carries almost nothing. It has never held a cloud; weather here is made of light, dust, and electricity. The brightness has three sources and you learn them in your first day. The star, white and large. The ground, which is pale ceramic and glass lichen and gives the light back. And the haze — a permanent high veil of charged dust that the plain's residual fields will not let fall, so that the sky is a luminous cream even at noon, and dusk lasts three hours while the suspended dust goes on catching the sun long after the ground has lost it. Noon is a white totality: the lichen saturates and re-radiates, shadows go thin and blue, and you walk on the surface of a lamp. The heat is moderate, managed — the plain keeps its temperature the way a room is kept for instruments — and the nights are mild, warmed from below by stored day seeping back out of the tiles.

From any slight rise you can watch dawn arrive: a line of light a thousand kilometers wide, sweeping toward you across the marquetry faster than sound travels in this air. Once a season the dust comes properly — walls of it on the horizon, bright as cliffs, lit from inside by the static of their own friction, taking a week to pass. Dust devils wander the plain at dusk, glowing faintly, discharging in small blue ticks between ground and sky. And there are mirages, though not the kind you know: here it is not hot air that bends the light but the plain's own latent fields, lifting the strata of distant country into the sky, so that sometimes you walk toward a phantom circuit diagram hanging above the horizon — a map of a place drawn in its own reflection.

The silence is the thing no one is ready for. The air is too thin to carry much, and what it carries it muffles. You hear your own breath inside whatever you are wearing. You hear the grit of your steps, and you learn that ground this dense is an instrument: hollow drum-fields where a cavity underlies the tiles give back a dull note you feel in the heel more than hear. Under everything, through the bones of your feet, runs the world's one steady sound, which is not a sound: a pulse of about one beat a minute, the deep salt going its rounds. And occasionally — once an hour, once a day — a crack too far away to hear arrives seconds later as a single bell-note through the soles of your feet, struck from an expansion seam a hundred kilometers off, one of the valley-sized joints that open in the day's heat and close at night. When that note comes, the wiregrass around you leans toward it. Almost imperceptibly. You will decide, each time, that you imagined it.

The vegetation is sparse in the way everything living here is sparse: kilometers between individuals, and yet everywhere, given time. Glass lichen crusts the ceramic in pale rosettes the size of dinner plates, a hand's span of growth a century. Wiregrass stands in wiry copper tufts along the seams, heliotropic, swinging its filaments across the sky at a pace too slow to see; at dusk it folds, and the folding is the only sound the flora makes — a dry tick, soft as a fingernail on a table, if you are lucky enough to be standing in a tuft of it when the light goes. The antenna-trees along the fallen guy-line. The salt-gardens at the springs: filaments that drill the glassy scabs to sip the warmth beneath, tended — though no one tends them — into spiral beds.

The animals you may count on one hand, per continent. The smelter-grazers are the likeliest encounter: things the size of a small room, moving a body-length a day, eating the conductive dust and leaving behind a trail of small pure ingots — copper, silver, now and then a bead of gold — like the world's most patient mint. The seam-walker you will see first as a bridge; then it moves, and recalibrates your sense of what is allowed to be alive. High in the bright haze ride the motes, translucent gas-bags on the thermals, the sky's only fauna, visible at dusk as slow drifting sparks. And the basins — learn to read basins. Most subsided honestly. Some are mouths. The difference is in the lichen, which will not grow on a mouth, and in the dust, which on a mouth lies faintly sorted, faintly trembling, as though recently breathed. A mouth opens perhaps once a decade. What it eats is whatever is standing on it. This is the food web: dust, grazer, basin. It runs at the pace of the salt below, and it has never been in a hurry.

Once in a century, give or take a decade, a region wakes. No one has predicted one successfully; the plain keeps its own calendar. A country a thousand kilometers across lights from below, warms, hums at the edge of hearing, remembers some task and performs it — the antenna-trees there bloom into a brief metallic flower, the nights stay warm, the springs run bright — and everything that walks, walks there, from a month's travel in every direction. Then it lets go. The lights subside. The fauna disperse. The lichen creeps back over everything. The leading guess is that these are maintenance cycles, the world noticing itself. The other guess is dreams.

Because the ground is not dead. It is annealed — and there is a difference between dumb matter and matter that has forgotten. Camp on the plain and the plain notices: the tiles beneath your bedroll warm by a degree. Dust does not settle on you. In the morning your footprints have softened at the edges — not erased; forgiven. Pile stones to mark a cache and come back in a year: the pile will be flat. Not scattered. Flat. Somewhere under every region, repair and erosion are still at their long negotiation, and the plane is their truce. Flatness here is not a fact but an activity. The world is kept.

So: a finished place, but not an ended one. Whoever made it made it to last, and then — as far as the record shows, and the record has been planed to one line's thickness — left, or stopped, or became it. What remains runs: the salt, the seams, the dust, the grass turning its face across the sky. Stand on the plain at dusk, in the long amber twilight, warm from below, in a silence so complete you can hear your own heart answer the slow one in the ground. The grass folds. Far off, something the size of a valley exhales and closes. A bell-note arrives through your feet from a hundred kilometers away, and everything around you leans, almost imperceptibly, toward it.
