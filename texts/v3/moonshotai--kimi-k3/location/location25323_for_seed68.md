Come over the rim in the morning, and you hear it before you properly see it: a low, continuous hum with a heartbeat inside it, rising out of a basin that should not be making any sound at all.

The basin is small — you could throw a stone across its narrow end, walk its length in twenty minutes. It is bounded on every side by the old high-water line, a bathtub ring of bleached snags, mineral-white silt, and matted dead reeds that runs along the slope like chalk. Everything below that line is the Bed. Everything above it is the world. The hamlet — nine houses, a coal shed, the Engine House, a scatter of pipe-yards — lies down in the Bed, spaced so far apart that each house seems to have been dropped rather than built, and the emptiness between them is as much a part of the settlement as the buildings.

Fifty years ago this was a reservoir. Then they built the lifting station at the lower end, opened the gates, and began pumping the water up through the rising main and over the rim to feed a canal on the far ridge. The water went away and mostly stayed away. What is left is young land — some of the youngest you will ever stand on. The silt underfoot has barely had time to become soil. It lies in great polygonal plates, cracked and curled at the edges like pottery sherds, still crisp, still sharp enough to flake between your fingers. The gravel bars are angular, unrounded. The concrete apron below the old boat ramp is unweathered, its board-marks legible, and the ramp itself ends in midair, six feet above the bed it once served. Dead trees still stand where they were drowned, silver and branchless, and the wind moves through them with a thin, reedy clicking.

And here and there, out of the silt, the drowned village is coming back. It was sacrificed to fill the reservoir, and now the reservoir is gone and the village is surfacing again — slowly, a few centimeters a decade, as each year's one or two real storms shuffle the silt around. Lanes of setts. Doorsteps at ankle height. Cellars open like dry wells, their stairs going down into darkness that drips. The hamlet has built itself partly out of this exhumation: lintels and walling stone robbed from the older foundations, so that the new houses wear the old village like inherited coats. It is a place of layers, and the layers happen to be standing at the same level.

The original stream — the Sike, a cold, peat-stained thread — still runs through it all, faithfully following its pre-drown course across the open bed, and the Engine House drinks it. That is the whole industry here: the hum you heard from the rim is the sound of water being taken away. Electric pumps, big as carriages, with a diesel standby shouldered alongside; brass gauges; a switchboard that ticks; an intake pool where the Sike is gathered behind a grill, and where a kingfisher sits, astonishingly, on the very pipe that is stealing its river.

Because the water is pumped away, the Bed is dry. That is the central fact and the small strangeness of the place: a waterworks that manufactures dryness. But no system this size holds itself perfectly. The pumps lift deep, cold groundwater, colder than the air above the bed, and so the great pipes sweat. Condensation beads on every flange and runs down every standpipe. Glands weep. Valve pits flood. The rising main ticks and drips along its whole climbing length. And inside the Engine House, on still days, it rains — a slow interior weather, vapor condensing on the cold roof trusses and falling in fat drops that ring on the casings and the floor plates, so that a garden of moss and fern has established itself indoors, in the gloom, around the feet of the machines. The tenders walk their rounds in oilskins under a roof that does not leak, exactly — it precipitates.

Everywhere the system bleeds, the basin turns violently green. This is a lush place the way a circuit board is busy: not everywhere, but intensely along the traces. Each leak grows a garden. Below a weeping valve, within a space no bigger than a room, the vegetation packs itself shoulder to shoulder — willow and alder suckering overhead, water mint and brooklime and yellow monkeyflower crowding the wet seam, horsetails standing in the silt like a memory of coal forests, cress skinning the slow water, moss upholstering every north-facing pipe. Duckweed paints the still sumps an enamel green broken only by the heron's footprints. Between the gardens, the dry bed stretches tan and grey and nearly bare, tufted with thistle and fireweed and the first pioneering birches — so the whole basin reads from the rim as green threads and green knots on grey cloth, stitched along the pipe runs, densest at the nine houses, because each house has its leak.

That is how you get a house here. You take on a weep. The leak-book in the Engine House lists them all by name — the Lych Gate Weep, Kettle Gland, the Long Cry — and each is somebody's garden, somebody's watercress, somebody's mint patch, somebody's responsibility. The rule is simple and absolute: no leak is ever fixed without another being opened. The tenders speak of "tuning" the system rather than repairing it, and a house whose weep fails in a dry year is pitied the way a failed harvest is pitied elsewhere. The drinking water is stranger and lovelier: troughs set beneath the coldest pipe runs catch the condensate — "new water," they call it, water that has been nowhere and touched nothing — and every kitchen in the hamlet has its drip-barrel, and everyone will tell you, correctly, that it is the sweetest water in the district, and everyone will omit that it is the sweat of the machine they serve.

The coolness helps. The bed is a frost hollow; cold air drains into it at night and pools, so even in summer the mornings come with breath visible and dew heavy on every flange, and the last spring frosts lie on the silt when the rim is already soft. But the light is ordinary light — this is no mist-drowned hollow. The sun comes over the rim at a reasonable hour, the shadows are the right length, the days are the usual length, and at night the lamps on their poles along the pipe-track burn with plain electric yellow. It is only the air that is cool, and the water that is colder.

What the basin lacks in water it makes up in life, because everything alive here is crowded into the same few wet places. The frog population of what was once a whole reservoir now owns perhaps a dozen sumps, and at dusk the chorus is not a chorus but a wall — a percussive, deafening braid of croak and trill that you feel in your teeth. Toads walk the pipe-track in the cool evenings. Dragonflies work the gardens in dozens; swifts and swallows hawk the insect haze above them and nest in the Engine House rafters, shrieking through the indoor rain. Water voles plop off the garden edges. A heron stands at each sump like a grey custodian. Foxes come down at dusk to drink at the seeps, deer step over the ring at dawn, and out on the dry plates the slow-worms and hares hold the emptiness. The abundance is real but concentrated — life compressed into oases, and loud in proportion to its compression.

And it is loud. That needs saying plainly, because the hamlet is small and sparse and could be mistaken for a quiet place. It is not. The Engine House hum never stops; it has a low note you feel in the breastbone and a rhythm inside it like a great slow heart, and every so often a valve seats somewhere in the system and a water-hammer goes down the rising main like a struck bell, once, rolling across the bed. Drips echo in the open cellars of the drowned village. The pipes tick as they cool. The snags click in the wind. The swallows scream. And the frogs — the frogs have learned the machines. The pumps rest at set intervals, and in the first seconds of each scheduled pause the frog chorus surges up into the silence as if released, and the tenders, checking their watches, say the second shift has clocked in.

They tell time here by the plant, not the sky. The pumps lift through the small hours when the power is cheap; the sumps rise and fall on a weekly tide that has nothing to do with weather; the gardens are planted and thinned by the lift schedule. Everything else moves slowly. Rain comes seldom — an occasional cool frontal drizzle that darkens the silt plates for a day and is gone, wicked down and pumped away. Real change arrives about once a year, as a single storm that re-cuts the Sike's channel, shifts a bar, exposes another doorstep of the drowned village. The tenders measure their careers in such events: two doorstep years, one year the church foundation showed its corner. The white ring on the slope fades by degrees nobody can see happening. The young land ages the way young land does — imperceptibly, then all at once, then imperceptibly again.

The rules, in the end, are few and everyone knows them. Water is accounted: first water to the canal, second water to the gardens, new water to the kitchen. Never stop a weep without leave of the leak-book. Drink the condensate; it has been nowhere and touched nothing. Leave the deep cellar by the intake alone — the one whose stairs go down further than the light does, where the Sike slides into the grill and the cold comes up the steps like something breathing; there is no rule written about it, but no one goes down, and lintels taken from the old village are never taken from its threshold. And when the hum stops — not the scheduled pause, which has a name and a feeling all its own, a held-breath strangeness they call the Still, when even the frogs seem to listen and everyone unconsciously lowers their voice — but the unscheduled silence, the wrong silence: come running. From anywhere in the basin, from the furthest garden, come running. Silence here is not peace. Silence is the alarm.

By full dark the lamps are lit along the pipe-track, widely spaced, each with its own faint halo of moths, and the hum has settled into its night register, and the second shift of frogs is in full voice from every sump at once. The whole basin holds its noise the way a held breath is held — cupped inside the old white shoreline, bounded, small, young, working. From the rim you can see every light it has, count its houses on your fingers, and hear it anyway.
