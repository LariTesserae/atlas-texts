High on the young mountain, just below the summit ridge where the wind does its ordinary work, there is a slot in the rock that you would walk past nine times in ten. The peak around it has never weathered. The ridgelines are sharp as broken pottery, the scree all angles, the gullies raw — a mountain still in its first draft, and it has been in its first draft longer than mountains should be. Out here the air is thin and bright and cold. But the slot exhales. A slow warmth leaks from it, wet as breath, and in cold weather it steams faintly, the only soft thing on the whole ridge.

You go in sideways. Ten paces of crawl, stone close on both shoulders, and the warmth rises degree by degree until the rock against your palms sits slightly above the temperature of your own skin — so that touching it feels, unmistakably, like being touched. The light does not fade so much as surrender. Somewhere behind your shoulders it gives up, and what replaces it is not the dark of a moonless night or a shuttered room. This dark has grain. It has weight. After an hour even your eyes' private static — the soft lightning that plays behind closed lids — runs down and goes out, quieted, as if the dark here were a solvent for seeing itself.

At the inner mouth of the crawl, at hand height, there is a spiral carved into the stone, the size of a palm. It is the first thing the fingers find. It says, in the only language that works here: you have arrived, now go slowly.

The room beyond is small — forty paces of connected hollow, one main chamber with alcoves and a crawl to a second cell the size of a coffin stood on end — and it is full the way a crowd is full. Nothing is more than an arm's length from anything else. And everything in it is young. The stalactites are the beginnings of stalactites, nipples and straws of stone no longer than a finger. The flowstone lies on the walls like the first ice on a pond. Columns reach for each other from floor and ceiling and have not met. Rimstone lips on the floor are only just learning to hold water. Every form in the room is an almost — almost a curtain, almost a bridge, almost a bowl. Nothing here has had time. Nothing here will take time. The chamber has been beginning for a very long while, and it shows no intention of proceeding.

If you brought a light — though the room discourages this; flames burn low and sullen in the dense wet air, and the dark presses in close around them, pooling the light at the wick — you would see pale stone the color of new bone, streaks of ochre at the seep line, white root-hair hanging in one alcove, and everywhere, on every tip and edge, water standing in small bright skins. Then the flame would gutter, and the dark would take it back.

The water comes from two directions, and this is the room's first secret. From above: the summit's rain and mist, percolating down through the fissures of the young peak, arriving drop by drop through the ceiling. From below: a warm deep seep that rises along the back wall, carrying the mountain's interior heat and a mineral sweetness, a faint whisper of a struck match long ago. The two waters meet in the room and they balance exactly. The film on the walls never thickens and never dries. The basin under the ceiling's one active drip never rises and never falls — what enters leaves by a groove worn into its lip, no, not worn, *cut*, and from there by a channel under the floor to some hidden accounting. At the tip of every young stalactite a drop stands at its fullest, trembling at the edge of its own skin, and does not fall. Break one with your finger and over the following days another swells to precisely the same fullness and stops. The room keeps its levels the way a held note keeps its pitch.

The drip is the room's only event. Every few hundred breaths the ceiling releases one drop into the basin, and the basin — this was someone's work, long ago, the fall smoothed to the right height, the depth shaped to the right resonance — answers with a single low note, soft and round, more felt in the sternum than heard. It is not a clock. A clock implies. The note simply happens, the same note it has always been, the room's one word spoken at its own unhurried interval.

Otherwise: quiet of an uncommon depth. The wet soft surfaces swallow sound whole. Your voice falls at your feet. Two arm-lengths apart, you must touch a companion to be sure of them. The scrape of your own entry is eaten before it reaches the walls. What remains, once you have been still long enough, is the loudness of your own body — heart, breath, the small machinery of the gut — returned to you as though the room were saying: this is the instrument you brought. And rarely, when the wind on the summit ridge shifts, the room breathes once: a long slow exchange of air through the slot, felt on the skin rather than heard, the mountain turning over in its sleep.

Life comes here sparsely and stays rare. In one alcove, the summit's thin gardens — the sedge mat, the dwarf shrubs clinging to the ridge above — have sent their roots down through the fissures like letters into a house: pale, hair-fine, etiolated, drinking the wet air, each thread ending in a bead of water. Nothing green grows in the room; there is nothing for green to do here. At the warm seep line, a dark felt of something between moss and mold spreads no wider than a cloak, faintly warm to the touch, feeding on whatever the deep water carries up. In the basin lives a fish — eyeless, the length of a finger, pale as a root — circling its small world, grazing the film. The knotted records suggest there has always been a fish. Whether it is the same fish is a question the room declines to answer. There are crickets, too, pale and patient, that have forgotten song — song is for finding each other across distance, and here there is no distance, only touch — and small thread-legged things that skate the water-film on the walls. You may visit and meet none of them. The room keeps its fauna the way it keeps its water: at the exact edge of enough.

People have been coming for a very long time. Whoever they were — valley people, coming up in the warm season, crawling in with clay and cord and harder stones for carving — they understood the room's law immediately, or learned it the hard way: nothing here is ever erased. No erosion, no fading, no rot finishing its work. A groove cut a thousand years ago is as fresh as one cut yesterday. So touch became their speech, and they made it deliberate. A path of smoothed handholds runs from the threshold spiral to the basin to the far wall — a small liturgy: enter, wash, speak. Before that wall stand a few shaped kneeling stones. The wall itself is the densest thing in the room, and the densest thing in the room is its past: grooves over grooves, spirals, tallies, statements in a lost grammar, every visit still present, every hand still here, layered until the stone is more mark than stone. In another alcove hangs a loom of knotted cords, dozens of them, a library meant to be read by pinch and slide — and along the cords, strangely, water climbs, against the world's habit, keeping the knots dark and swollen, so that the texts are also irrigation and the library drinks. There is a stack of small clay cups, one for each visitor who ever came, each crusting at the rim with mineral and never finished crusting. There is a bench of flat stacked stones. There is, once, metal: a small copper ring on a cord, simple work, and it hangs forever at the edge of tarnish, bright, arrested, the way everything here is arrested.

Their rules were few and they are still the right ones. Enter clean. Move slowly. Add little, and only with intention, because the room forgets nothing and forgives everything into permanence. Do not mark idly — your hesitations are kept as faithfully as your certainties. Expect no echo; the room returns nothing but itself. And if you sleep here, know that you will be folded, gently, into the equilibrium: you will wake beaded with the room's water, your clothes warm on your body, your eyelashes salted with the first faint ring of mineral — the room beginning, without malice and without haste, to include you.

Because that is the deep rule, the one the builders only suspected and the water knows entirely: this place does not change. Events happen — a knot is added, a cup is set down, a fish circles — but the great state returns to itself, always. Warmth reclaims cooled stone. Levels restore. The drop regathers. The room is not a ruin and not a construction site, though it looks like both; it is an equilibrium, a held breath, a place that found its balance at the very first moment of its existence and has kept it ever since. The people who knelt here understood, finally, what they had found. They called it the First Room. There is no second.

Stand at the center of it — you will have to stand by memory, by the map your hands have made — and feel the crowded young stone around you, the warm saturated air, the whole unerased past leaning close on every side, the fish turning in its basin, the roots drinking, the note gathering itself somewhere in the ceiling. At every stone tip in the dark above you, a drop stands full at the edge of falling. It does not fall. It has never fallen. It is not waiting; waiting implies.

The room is simply, perfectly, and forever about to.
