Long before the ground shows you anything, you hear it: a chord with a heartbeat.

From a mile off across the limestone plateau it is only a pressure on the ear, a lowness, the way a large building is audible before it is visible. Closer in, the chord separates into parts — a bass note that never stops, a slow thud in threes, water speaking in many registers at once, and at long intervals a clank of metal that arrives twice: once from the thing itself and once, a breath later, thrown back from the far wall of somewhere. The plateau gives no other warning. It is thin-soiled sheep country without sheep, swallow-holed, its few streams all practicing disappearance. A lane runs out onto it and gives up at a stile. The sign on the stile says LOWER WORKS NO. 4. Past the stile the land dimples like a held breath and falls away.

The bowl is two hundred paces across at the lip and narrows as it drops — a collapsed eye in the karst, a hundred and fifty feet deep, the lowest ground for forty miles. Two-thirds of the way down, the air has a ceiling: a fog deck, flat as a lake surface, that never burns off and never rises past the rim, and everything below it is the town. From above you see roofs first — slate gone black and green, terraced rows stepping down the slope, all of them leaning slightly inward as if listening — and among the roofs, the works. A long hall with a clerestory. A squat roundhouse. Pipe-racks running overhead like a second, busier street. Bridges crossing the lanes at three heights, so a person could walk the main lane at ground level, at the level of the first floors, or along the rooftops. One chimney, dead, a relic of the brief coal century, wearing a green plume of growth at its crown. The crew called it the Candle. Nothing here has smoked in a hundred years.

The stair down is iron riveted to the rock, bolted over the line of an older wooden stair, which itself covers a stair cut into the stone, its treads dimpled — not by boots. By drips. The wall weeps the whole way down, each stratum at its own rate: the hard beds seep, the soft beds run, and every weep-line is planted thick with maidenhair. The water main climbs beside you, riveted iron, warm as a wrist, faintly trembling with the strokes of whatever is pushing it. Halfway down, the air warms and thickens and the light goes green, and then you pass through the fog deck — through the ceiling of Loudwater's sky — and the sound, which the fog had been pressing down all these years, opens its mouth.

The floor of the bowl is either pavement under moss or the moss itself; there is no bare earth anywhere, and there are puddles with longer tenure than the newest terrace. The lanes are stairs. The houses stand shoulder to shoulder and lean across the lanes until a person can touch both walls at once, and the pipes run over everything in racks, and the ferns come down over the pipes, so that the whole town reads as one woven object rather than many. The warmth is a one-cardigan warmth, and the air is always one breath from rain, and frequently pays it. Nothing dries here. Nothing is meant to.

At the bottom lies the pool the crews called the Baptistery — the spring itself, the reason for all of it. It rises in a visible dome, a boil at the pace of a heartbeat, tepid as bathwater, faintly sulfur-sweet, and so clear you could read the date on a coin at two fathoms. Nothing moves in it on its own. At its edges the stones wear an algal velvet, and over its skin sits its own small weather, a private fog, breathing.

Above the pool stands the hall — the Choir — and inside the Choir are the three. Their nameplates say PATIENCE, DILIGENCE, PERSEVERANCE, and they are engines of a kind that exists almost nowhere else: low-gradient engines, working the difference between the spring's warmth and the shade's cool, a matter of a few degrees. They have almost nothing to work with, so they are built enormous to catch it, the way a great sail is built for a small wind. Their flywheels are the size of millwheels and turn once while you count twenty. Their beams rock the height of the clerestory. The stroke is a rising groan; then the beam hangs at the top and the whole bowl seems to listen; then the downstroke, and the cushioned thud of water delivered. The three run out of phase — a round sung very slowly by three iron voices. Their governors spin with brass balls wide and unhurried. Their brass is polished to the height a hand could reach, and below that it is green, and the green has stopped there, as everything here stops at its own settled line. The engines sweat. Every cool surface in the hall sweats. Condensation stands on the beams in beads and runs off the noses of the valves, and the oil cups weep steadily, and the woven belts slap their pulleys as softly as a sleeper turning over.

What the Choir pumps, the Rosary carries. The bucket chain leaves the pool's outflow and climbs the wall in a long slant, each bucket filling, rising, tipping at the top into a wooden trough that exits through a notch in the lip — a clank every nine seconds, and its echo a breath behind. The main takes the rest, driven up the rock by the engines' stroke. Both leave the bowl and neither comes back, and what waits for the water up there — a reservoir, a canal summit, a city's cisterns, nothing at all — the town does not ask. Loudwater's only product is height. It lifts the spring over the edge of the world, and in doing so it holds the sump level to a chalked line on the pool wall, and the line never varies by the width of a finger, because the town pumps or it drowns, and it has chosen, every nine seconds for longer than anyone can say, to pump.

You should know that it rains under things here. The weather arrives from below, not above: the warm water exhales, the fog deck cools the breath, and the moisture comes back down off the undersides — off the bridges, off the eaves, off the rim's overhang — in a slow, local, indoor rain that never consults the sky. Umbrellas hang by every door, for crossing yards. The drip-points have been falling in the same spots for three hundred years and the flagstones have the dimples to show for it; the stone has been cried on, and has worn its crying-places smooth. The gutters and flumes take all of it up again in chords. The sky's weather, whatever it is doing, is a rumor that stops at the fog deck. Loudwater manufactures its own.

The crew is gone — left, or finished, or never quite arrived; the paperwork is calm about it. The terraced rows stand furnished. In the mess, forty bowls are racked above forty name-hooks, and the range is plumbed directly to the spring and stays blood-warm to the touch; nothing in Loudwater has ever boiled. In the office the chart recorders still ink their drums, one line a day, every line identical, and the spent paper has slumped off the table and softened on the floor into sedimentary felt — the autobiography of the water, written by the water. The rota board under glass lists M. DUVALL — ROSARY, A. OKONKWO — CHOIR, NIGHTS, the glass fogged from within. Every clock in town is driven by its own water feed, and every feed runs at a slightly different pressure, so Loudwater keeps eleven times at once, all of them slow. On its pole above the hall, the shift bell is struck by a cam on PERSEVERANCE, and when it rings, the lamps in the Choir come on, and the shifts change, and no one clocks in.

The green is one green. There are no pollinators here, so this is the old world, the spore world: ferns in the splash-belts, horsetails at the sump edge, liverwort in black-green felt on the north faces, selaginella spread like coral over anything that holds still. Everything clones; everything creeps; the whole bowl may be a single patient body wearing many shapes. Some things still flower — small, pale, self-married — out of an older world's habit, in the window boxes, for no one. The dead are not eaten so much as absorbed: rot here is fungal, and it glows. Foxfire runs through the fallen timbers and the wheel-pits, a cold green burning, so the works' undersides glimmer like something holding its breath. The hot pipes carry their own gardens — every standpipe wears a beard graded by temperature, bottle-green at the cuff, rust and cream at the warm collar. And the vegetation has long since become structural. The vine that killed the Candle now holds it up. Walls lean and are caught by root-mats. Rust thickens to a skin and stops. Nothing in Loudwater finishes falling.

Nothing with a heartbeat lives here. That is the rule, and it has no exception and no explanation. The arc lamp in the yard hisses in the middle of its own light and gathers no moths. The eaves are perfect for nests and hold none. The clear water should be thick with larvae, snails, small silver things, and there is nothing in it that moves on its own; the mist should be strung with webs, and it is bare. Hawks ride the updraught above the bowl all afternoon and shear off at the lip, every one of them, as if the air down there had changed its mind. And because of this, the loudness of Loudwater has a silence threaded through it: no wings, no chitter, no small feet in the ceilings. The chord is enormous and it is all one kind of voice — water, iron, breath — and it never once says anything alive.

Night, when it comes, is only a deepening of the green. The fog deck sinks a few feet and the upper stair stands in clearer dark. The few living lamps come up — a swaying catenary of caged bulbs over the main lane, half of them dead, the rest burning at midday too, because noon here is a rumor the rim tells the far wall — and the foxfire strengthens in the pits, and the Baptistery keeps a low lamp lit under itself, a faint algal glow, so that the town at night is lit from below, by its own well. The moon, when there is one, crosses the small bright pond of sky in an hour and is gone. The chord does not change. It seems louder, the way a house seems louder at night.

The clocks of Loudwater are these: the drip, which counts the seconds. The wheel, which counts the minutes. The bell, which counts the shifts for no one. The light, which counts the days by a shade. The chalked line on the pool wall, which counts the seasons by a hand's width. The moss, which counts the decades by the flagstone. A pipe retired, a terrace caught mid-lean: those count the generations. And the rim itself, dissolving grain by grain into the water that will be pumped over it, counts the longest time of all — the bowl grows wider by the width of a leaf per lifetime, and is in no hurry about it, because nothing here is in any hurry about anything. There is no gradient steep enough to make a thing rush. The warmth is nearly even, the water is nearly level, the light is nearly constant, and the whole town runs, patiently and at full volume, on the nearly.

Stand on the bridge over the Baptistery at the center of the bowl, at night, and you are at the mixing desk of all of it: the bass of the spring, the round of the three engines, the nine-second clank and its echo, the flume-chords, the drip-percussion off tin and leaf and stone and water, the hum of the dynamo house, the hiss of the arc lamp gathering nothing. Now and then, by some long coincidence of phase, all three beams hang at top dead center at once, and the whole bowl listens to its own held breath — and then the strokes resume, in threes, out of step, forever.

A drop forms on the underside of the bridge above you. It swells past the size a drop should be, lets go, and strikes the pool: the smallest sound Loudwater makes, answered by the largest.

No one is coming, and it does not matter. The spring rises, the engines answer, the water goes up and out over the lip of the world. Loudwater keeps the water down. Loudwater keeps the water moving. Loudwater keeps eleven times, all of them slow.

Loudwater keeps.
