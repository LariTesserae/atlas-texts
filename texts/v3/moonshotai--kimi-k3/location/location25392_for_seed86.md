The plateau comes first, because you have to cross it to get there: a long, wind-combed upland of grey grass and low thorn scrub, cool even at midday, the kind of country where rain falls perhaps a dozen times a year and vanishes so quickly it might never have happened. The vegetation never thickens. Between the grass clumps is bare, pale earth, and beneath the earth — you learn this slowly, over days — the ground is thin. A footstep rings differently here than there. Cracks breathe. On still mornings, standing over a fissure no wider than a hand, you feel cold air moving up past your face, steady and mineral-smelling, and you hear, very far below, water falling: a thin, patient sound with a long echo folded into it.

Then the plateau simply opens. Nine wells are scattered across the region, places where the ceiling of the world underneath has failed and dropped away. They are young collapses — geologically fresh, the fracture faces on their walls still clean and pale, still sharp enough to catch a sleeve, unsoftened by weather or root. Sheer cylinders of rock, thirty, fifty meters deep, and down each one falls a column of dim daylight. Ferns band the upper walls in the damp breath of the shaft; moss takes over lower, where the light thins; a few birches lean over the rims but nothing much grows down in the wells themselves — a scatter of saplings on the talus cone at the bottom, roots dangling from ledges, algae filming the wet stone. Damp, never wet. The wells gather what little rain the plateau gets, and after a storm, threads of water fall straight down the shafts for a few hours, unspooling into mist before they land.

Below the wells lies the reason for all of it: a labyrinth of limestone galleries that runs for tens of kilometers under the plateau, on several levels, halls the size of cathedrals connected by passages like streets, by crawls, by vertical joints choked with fresh breakdown. It was made quickly and then abandoned by its maker. A river cut this system and was captured away — diverted to some lower course when a distant valley breached — and the water left mid-gesture. The walls are still fluted in sharp scallops, every ripple of the old flow preserved like a fingerprint. The rimstone dams stand in terraced rows along the sloping floors, but their pools are empty or hold only a skin of water behind a dry lip. Soda-straw stalactites hang no longer than a finger, just beginning, as if the cave only recently remembered it could grow. Nothing here has a patina. Nothing is rounded. The whole underworld has the unfinished, gleaming sharpness of a thing that was just unwrapped.

What water remains is a matter of pockets and habits. Drip lines fall from the same points in the ceiling they have always fallen from. In the deepest halls, where the air stillest and coolest, condensation beads on the rock and runs in slow threads. A few clear pools persist in the lower levels, cold enough to ache, and one small stream still threads a canyon-passage for a kilometer before sinking politely into gravel and ceasing. Cracked mud floors ring faintly underfoot, plates of dried silt from the last time anything flooded, which was long ago and will not come again. The cave is damp the way a held breath is damp — residual, interior, going nowhere.

The light never rises above a kind of dusk. Near the wells, at noon, you can read the grain of the stone; the rest of the day is twilight grading into dark. Away from the shafts, the only light is alive: along the damp walls near the sinking stream, colonies of gnat larvae hang their fishing lines — hundreds of tiny blue-green points, each with a curtain of sticky threads below it, glimmering in the black like a sky someone spilled. It is beautiful and it is a trap, and it is the brightest thing in the deep cave. Your eyes adjust until that is enough.

You navigate by ear. The acoustics are total: limestone conducts sound like a rumor through a village, and every hall has its own delay, its own way of answering. A single drip falls into a pool and the echo takes four seconds to finish telling you about it. And the cave is loud — not everywhere, but where it is loud, it is overwhelming. Follow the cricket chorus that thickens near every well, then go deeper, toward a sound like distant surf, like rain that never arrives, and you will come to the colony halls.

The ceilings there are dark with bodies. Bats in the hundreds of thousands, layered so densely the rock itself seems to breathe, to ripple. At the dim cues of dusk and dawn — the wells' faint brightening and failing — they pour through the passages in rivers, and the sound of it is a roar, air physically moving, wing-beats building to a weather system inside the stone. The floors of those halls are dunes of guano, meters deep, and the guano is not inert: beetles work it in shifting sheets, flies cloud above it, mites and springtails thread it, fungi bloom across it in pale pelts. The ammonia of it sharpens the cool air. On ledges above the bats, cave swifts nest in rows, clicking their way through the dark with small, precise sounds, adding their own chatter to the din. In the twilight zones the walls shimmer — and you realize, looking closer, that they are *covered*: crickets shoulder to shoulder, a living, singing skin of stone.

The pools are full. Pale, eyeless fish crowd them, hanging in the cold clear water; amphipods swarm any morsel that falls; isopods trundle the pool margins. At the wells, snakes drape the ledges and harvest bats from the air as the evening exodus streams past. Everything eats everything, and the base of it all is the two rainfalls: the guano falling in the deep halls, and whatever the wells drop in from the world above — leaves, insects, the occasional stunned bird. Life poured into this place when the ceilings opened, and it has been packed in, teeming, ever since.

Among the anvil stones, you find the only artifacts that exist anywhere in this world. A stocky, heavy-billed bird — a knocker-bird, grey as the rock — hunts snails along the well walls and the cricket-thick twilight zones, and it has a habit: it wedges a snail into a crevice, selects a stone from the floor, and strikes. The crack of it carries through three halls. Along certain passages, every few hundred meters, there is an anvil: a flat-topped block with a lightly worn hollow, a scatter of selected cobbles around it, a midden of broken shells beneath. Some stones have split from use into rough edges. That is the entire technology of the place — handheld, unstacked, uncemented. Nothing is built. Not here, not anywhere: no wall, no nest platform beyond mud and spit, no mark of any hand that makes things to keep. The tools lie where they drop, and because nothing disturbs them, they lie there a very long time.

And between all of this — this is the thing the sound disguises — is emptiness. Most of the labyrinth is vacant. Life here is archipelago, not sea: the colony halls, the well gardens, the pools, the anvil passages, and between them, kilometers of gallery where nothing lives at all, where you walk for an hour hearing your own steps and one drip every twenty seconds, where the cool air sits motionless and the dark is complete. The loudness is real, but it is islanded, and the stone between the islands is silent, and the silence is most of the map.

Because nothing changes. That is the deep rule of this place, the one everything else obeys. The temperature holds at the same cool degree, winter and summer, day and night; there is no weather below the wells, no season, no frost, no thaw. The pools sit at the levels their calcite rims recorded, and the rims record no variation. The drips fall at the rates they fell at a century ago. The bats leave and return on a cycle so exact it functions less like behavior than like a clock that always shows the same time. A knocker-bird's footprints pressed into silt decades ago are still crisp at the edges. Nothing accretes — the dust does not deepen, the sediment does not shift, the young rock does not weather, because there is nothing here to weather it. The cave was made in a rush and then the making stopped, and it has kept its youth the way a held breath keeps the air: perfectly, and without effort.

Stand in one of the empty halls, far from any well, and let your eyes go dark-adapted to nothing. You will hear the distant colonies through the rock — that surf-sound, that weather that never arrives — and beneath it, close by, one drip, then another, each one landing exactly where the last one landed. The place is full to bursting and almost entirely empty, loud as a storm and still as stone, brand new and ancient, and it intends, so far as a place can intend anything, to stay exactly this way.
