The range it hides in has no water in its memory. These are mountains that never learned mud, never learned ice — raw mineral all the way down, scree that has been falling the same way since before falling was a process with rules. High enough that the sky stops being blue and becomes enamel. The climb is hot and loud with your own breathing, and then the scree does a thing it shouldn't: it folds. A gyre in the talus, a winding that goes nowhere twice and then, if you keep faith with it, once more — and you are standing on a lip, looking down into a bowl the size of a village square, entire at a glance, the way you take in a pocket watch opened on a palm.

And like the watch, it is working.

The light is the first wrong thing, because it isn't wrong at all. It is ordinary noon. Honest sun, short black shadows, the whole scene exposed like a photograph properly taken. At this altitude the air should be thin and cold; instead the bowl holds a dense dry heat, a held-breath heat, heat that shimmers over the center of the floor and turns the far rim liquid-edged. The altitude says cold. The work says otherwise. Above, the enameled sky is utterly cloudless — there is no water here to make weather from — and at the zenith, through the whole afternoon, one star holds steady as a rivet.

Crossing the lip is like entering a room. The wind stops at the boundary as if it had been a noise in another part of the building. Your shadow hesitates on the threshold, once, and then catches up with you, and after that it behaves, mostly.

The trees — call them trees; the word will have to do, and so will *engine*, and so will *ore* — stand well apart, each alone in its own clearing of new stone, nine or ten of them around the bowl. Their trunks are iron built up in laminations, layers thin as pastry leaves, the way a transformer's core is built and for the same reason: to keep the eddies out. Nothing here has ever rusted, rust requiring water, so nothing looks old — the iron is bright and dark at once, warm to the touch, faintly oily with its own freshness. Count the growth rings and you would read not the tree's age but its voltage. The leaves are mica, thousands of them per tree, each a loose flake of smoked glass, and they are the lushness — not coverage but opulence, extravagance, foliage to the point of excess on bodies spaced like figures in a frieze. The leaves turn by ones and twos in the rising thermals with a faint dry ticking, as if the trees were thinking. Put your ear to a trunk and you will hear the hum. You have been hearing it already, in your teeth, since the lip: a low held organ note, the sound of a line on shift.

Between the trees, the blooms. Buds like the porcelain insulators that pin wire to poles — stacked skirts, fluted petticoats — rising on dark stalks from the felt. A bloom opens over thirty years, and when one is ready, which happens every decade or so, it does not send scent or seed. It sends its charge. The arc leans across the gap to the next bloom slowly, almost courteously; the receiving bloom leans into it; for a moment the two are a single machine. There is a tick you feel in the fillings of your teeth, and then the smell of ozone — the smell of a storm that considered happening and was filed instead. The distances are tuned. A step closer between any two blooms and the bowl would short itself into one bright event; a step farther and the arc could not be persuaded. The emptiness is load-bearing. You walk through it as through the halls of a sparse and careful museum, and the placards are the silences.

Underfoot, in patches the size of rooms, grows the filament felt: wire-grass gone feral into verdigris and bright copper, soft as any moss, giving under your step and springing back behind you. Where you press it, a faint light stands in the blades for a breath and then lets you go. By evening the felt has forgotten your footprints entirely. It is the fastest thing in the bowl. Your footfalls are the loudest thing that has happened here in some time.

There is no water. Not hidden, not deep, not once: none. The trees keep a sap anyway — gallium, or something gallium remembers being — and on the hot afternoons it beads on the laminations and runs, quick and mirror-bright, the way water flees a hot pan. The floor is seamed with channels, dry now, but in the long hot decades — the wet season here is a temperature, not a weather — those channels run full and glowing the orange of sodium lamps, and the whole bowl hums a half-step lower, and this, locally, is abundance. If you could get below the floor you would find the water table. It is a voltage. Wells here would fill, slowly, with light. And in the newest stone there are gutters — channels cut with the profiles of rain-thinking, installed by whatever specifies such things. No fluid has ever used them. They read like a rumor of water that hasn't reached this world yet. At the warm vents, dry vents breathing heat without steam, sulfur sublimes straight out of the air and sets as flowers — flowers of sulfur, the old chemists called them, and here the name is literal: pale yellow rosettes blooming on black glass, blooming in the foundry sense and the garden sense at once.

The bowl is not finished. This is what you understand last and then cannot stop seeing. The rim is a work site with no workers. There the ground is newest: bright cleavage faces, unweathered, sharp enough to cut the light. A boulder near the edge is specified on one side only — the near face is granite with intent, granular, decided — and the far face is a low gray suggestion, a placeholder, geology in draft. Your eye slides off such things. You forget them while you are looking at them; attention has no purchase on the unfinished. At the newest margin the ground is busy making more ground: forms half-sunk in fresh rock that turn as slowly as hour hands, extruding crystal, laying down stratum the way a loom lays weft. They are not machines that build the world. They are the world's way of building, caught in the act, kept on view because the bowl hides so well. Past them the slope is drawn in outline; a fault line stops mid-word. The rim advances perhaps a hand's width in a century. Nothing in the bowl is worn. Wear has not been installed here yet — every edge is a birth edge.

And the floor beneath you, the finished part, is made of retired versions: earlier drafts of the trees, deprecated engines, compacted gently over ages into ore. Geology as an archive of prior builds. The place buries its own history, tenderly, and stands on it.

If it has a name, call it the Draft. Every sense of the word is load-bearing: a preliminary version; the slight taper that lets a pattern leave its mold; a current of warm air; a conscription — of stone, here, into world.

A few things are true here that are not true elsewhere, or not admittedly. Nothing was built; everything is manufactured. No hand has touched this place because hands are what it is for. The categories leak — machine, plant, mineral, the bowl declines to sort them, and after an hour, so do you; your warmth reads as fuel, your pulse as a small unreliable clock brought in from outside. The crop is ground, and the word is exact in all its senses: the floor, the electrical reference, the basis that things stand on. And the resemblance runs only one way. Nothing here imitates anything. Every dynamo ever wound, every insulator ever cast, is a dim recollection of this bowl; somewhere in the design of all our engines is a memory of mica turning in a thermal. This is not nature copying the machine age. This is the original of which the machine age is the echo.

The strangest thing may be the shadows. They are ordinary — noon-short, black, correct — except that there are more of them than there are things. The floor carries the shadows of a denser garden: blooms not yet placed, trees not yet risen, a sparse shading cast backward from some fuller shift. The garden's shadow is lusher than the garden. Stand still at noon and you can feel it — the finished version leaning over your shoulder, reading the draft.

There are no animals. Nothing here eats; nothing decides; nothing wants. Seeds — wound coils the size of a fist — cross the gaps on a slow drift of charge, or in ballistic arcs thrown years ago, landing with a tick the felt forgives. The quiet is not the quiet of sleep. It is the quiet of a shop floor between operations, of a kiln holding temperature, of a greenhouse at noon. Work quiet. A human visit is not a unit of time the bowl keeps. A bloom opens in thirty years; the rim takes a century per hand's width; the felt forgets you by dark and it is the hasty one. Your whole stay is a single tick of cooling metal at dusk. If you returned in ten years, nothing would be where you left it, and nothing would have moved.

Where the finished ground goes is the last mystery and the plainest. The floor completes itself quietly and then, without seam or ceremony, stops being here. You cannot find the join. But the world below — the plains you crossed, the scree you climbed — carries, once you have stood in the bowl, the unmistakable feel of product. Of output. Every step you have ever taken was on ground that left here finished.

Leaving is not hard because of the climb but because of the finding: the fold in the scree resists being seen twice, and the bowl resists being believed once. At the lip the wind resumes like noise from another room. Behind you the felt has already forgotten your feet. The hum goes on at the pitch of a held breath; the mica turns in the thermals; somewhere below, the world is by a hand's width more real.
