**The Green Well**

The country above is dry and bright — grass gone the color of old straw, limestone that ticks as it cools in the evenings, a sky with nothing in it. You could cross it for days and not find water. And then the ground opens.

The hole is easy to miss: a gap no wider than a courtyard, set in a shallow saddle of rock, its rim furred all around with ferns that have no business growing in this province. Heat comes up out of it — damp heat, slow and steady, like a breath. Stand at the edge and the air rising past you is blood-warm and smells of wet stone and greenness. The herders who graze the dry hills call the place the Hush, or the Green Well, and they water their flocks elsewhere.

A stair goes down. It was cut once into the wall of the shaft, a spiral of narrow steps, but so long ago and so worn by feet and seeping water that it is now more a ripple in the rock than a staircase — a suggestion of descent. You follow it down through the light, and the light thins, and the heat does not thin. It thickens. By the third turn the air is warm cloth pressed against the skin. By the fifth, sound begins to fail.

The chamber at the bottom is forty paces across, no more — a dome of limestone, thirty meters from floor to the open eye of the oculus. Chapel-scale, not cave-scale: bounded on every side, all of it visible at once once your eyes adjust. From the oculus falls a single column of daylight, green where it threads the leaves of the fig at the lip, narrowing as it descends, landing on the floor as one oval of gold about the size of a doorway. Everything else lives in a deep amber-green twilight that never quite changes except in where the oval falls. The oval is the chamber's hearth, its clock, its altar.

A dome like this should ring. It does not. Sound dies here within an arm's length — swallowed by wet moss, by soft flowstone, by air too warm and heavy to carry it, and by something beyond all that, some preference of the place itself. Speak, and your voice comes out flat and close, as if uttered inside a cupboard. There is no echo. You hear your own heart. You hear your joints. The loudest thing in the Green Well is the drip: one slow tick of water from the ceiling into a basin, spaced like a clock that has forgotten what hours are for. Visitors whisper here without ever deciding to.

There is no stream. Water arrives the way weather arrives in this room — from the walls. At dusk the rock exhales: a warm damp breath that beads on every cool surface, runs in rivulets down the flowstone, gathers in the fern-throats. By midmorning the stone drinks it back. Condensation is the rain of this world, and each hollow of the floor has its appointed drip. Along the seep-line, rimstone basins step down the wall in terraces no bigger than cupped hands, each brimming, each spilling a thread into the next. At the floor's lowest point lies a pool the size of a doorway, black, so still it seems skinned over with membrane — water that has not been disturbed in years and shows it.

Below the main drip stands the basin. It was carved, once — a rough bowl chopped from the floor stone — but generations of hands packed clay around its rim to raise it, and the lime-rich drip has hardened that clay into stone, and the stone has grown a lip like a shell's, accreting a fraction of a grain a year. It is half made, half grown. That is true of nearly everything down here.

The vegetation arranges itself by its distance from the light. At the far edges, where the dim is deepest, there is only a green-black lacquer of algae glazing the wet stone. Closer in: moss, in zones you could map by the deepening of its green. Then ferns — maidenhair trembling in the seep-lines, hart's-tongue ferns unfurling from every ledge and niche of the walls. And where the oval of sunlight lands: a forest the size of a blanket. Seedlings, vines, moss hummocks, fungi, all crushed into a thicket you could not put a foot into without crushing a hundred lives, every stem leaning, every leaf turned to catch the light as it sweeps. An entire woodland compressed to the size of a rug, fighting its whole slow war over one doorway of gold.

Above it all hangs the root. The fig at the lip sent a root down thirty years or three hundred ago, and it reached the floor, and now it is not one root but a curtain, a cable, a braided column as thick around as a man's chest, fusing where it touches into something like a trunk. It is the chamber's highway. Everything that travels between the two worlds travels the fig-root: lizards, ants in both directions, a snake longer than the stair, moths at night, beetles at noon.

The census of the place is long and almost entirely silent. Crickets work in the moss — you do not hear them; you feel them, a faint continuous shiver in the sternum, in the teeth, song pitched below or above the ear's reach but not the body's. Isopods the size of thumbprints trundle through the red clay. Springtails drift like spilled pepper across the pool's skin. In the carved basin live small frogs the color of weak tea, motionless for hours, eyes like wet seeds. The black pool holds a pale fish, two, three — no one has counted them — that cruise in slow ovals and rise to anything that touches the surface. A hunting spider, big as a spread hand, keeps its web across a fold of the root-curtain and takes moths all night without a sound.

What falls, the floor takes. A beetle blunders through the oculus and is met within the hour. A lizard drops from the lip and by morning there are only isopods, conferring. Once — the bones are in the clay now — something the size of a hare came down, and the floor fed on it for a week, and the ferns around the spot grew darker for a season.

The commuters keep the schedule. In ledges of the dim, swifts nest in cups of mud and spit; at dusk and dawn they pour through the oculus in silence, wings making no more sound than turning pages. Under the dome sleep the bats, a rustling dark against the stone, and their leaving is the chamber's great daily event: hundreds of bodies rising in a spiral up the light-shaft and out into the evening, a fountain of life you feel as a cool moving wind on your face and hear as nothing at all, their cries pitched far above the ear. The silence holds even as the ceiling empties. The owl that roosts at the lip drops its pellets through the hole all night, and the springtails are grateful. Everything here eats what the upper world discards.

Of the people who cut the stair, almost nothing is left, and what is left is being digested. Near the entrance ledge, on a smoothed panel of flowstone, are tally marks — hundreds of them, grouped in fours-and-ones, worn to ghosts. Counts of what: jars lowered? days survived? Below, a low wall of stacked stones stands entirely inside the fig's roots now, gripped like something the tree refuses to give back. And in the flowstone beside the basin, fused and mineralized, hangs a petrified rope — a braid of stone hemp, preserved at the moment the drip took it. Nothing fired, nothing forged. They came with stone and clay and fiber and their hands. Perhaps they came for the water. Perhaps they came for the silence; there is nowhere else in this country to get it.

The day turns. The oval of light climbs the wall and crosses the floor and dies, and the life of the chamber rotates after it like a sunflower too slow to see. At dusk the rock exhales, the swifts go out, the bats rise, the snake comes down the root-column hunting by heat. At dawn the bats return in their soundless hundreds, the stone drinks, the frogs shift an inch. The seasons move the light-shaft across the floor like the beam of a slow lantern, and for a few weeks each year it strikes the black pool directly — and then the pool answers: an algae bloom like green smoke, the pale fish rising to spawn, the frogs gathering at its edge.

And sometimes, rarely, it rains in the world above. Then the oculus becomes a waterfall for an hour, and the chamber takes its one deep drink of the year, and on that night — only that night — the frogs find their voices. The whole dome fills with calling, the single loud night of the Green Well, a festival of sound in a place built of silence. By morning the hush has resealed itself, as if nothing had been said.

Nothing here is new and nothing is finished. The roof sheds a stone a century; the floor accepts it and begins the long work of dissolving it. Guano becomes clay, clay becomes fern, fern becomes cricket, cricket becomes snake, snake becomes the owl's pellet, the pellet becomes the springtail's bread. The flowstone welds everything slowly to everything else. The chamber holds the temperature of blood and the patience of geology, and it has been in this state — not approaching, not declining, in it — for longer than the tally-marks can say.

The rules are simple, and everything here keeps them.

What falls, stays. What stays is changed.

The light decides.

The hush is kept.
