You feel it before you hear it, and hear it before you see it. Half a mile out across the marsh, walking the plank causeway, the thudding comes up through the soles of your boots — slow, regular, a heart's gait. Then the hum gathers around it, a braiding of drones and hisses. Then you climb the Ring Bank and look down into the bowl, and there it is: a shallow pocket of peat a quarter-mile across, packed brim-full with green and steam, roofs and willow crowns braided so tightly together you can't tell which is holding which up, and the sound of it rising like heat off a stove.

The map calls it Withymill. Everyone who works there calls it the Works.

Inside the bank, the ground is black peat and it is not really ground. Water stands a finger's breadth beneath it; a footprint fills in thirty seconds and is a pond by evening. So nobody walks on earth here. You walk on planks — bowed, mossed, renewed a section at a time, generation after generation — or on brick, or on iron grating that rings underfoot. Straight black drains seam the bowl, their surfaces sealed under duckweed and red water-fern, all of them converging, by long-established gravity, on the Sump beneath the engine house. The water is not under the place. The water is in it, among it, in ongoing negotiation with it.

Everything else is plants. Not decoratively — structurally. The drain margins are solid with yellow iris and bur-reed. Every fence, pipe run, and pylon has been converted by bindweed, clematis, and hop-vine into a green cord of its former self. Willow and alder close overhead; the sky is available only in strips. The plank walks grow a pile of moss like carpet underlay; the roofs carry turf, stonecrop, and in one guttering a fully self-satisfied miniature reed bed. Seedlings stand in the mortar of every wall. Ferns have colonized the damp corner of the dynamo room, three feet from a spinning armature, and nobody has the heart to wipe them. There is no surface in the Works that is not either growing something or being wiped so that it won't — and the wiping is understood to be temporary.

Shed Three has a willow growing through its roof. It has been there forty years. At some point the roof began, structurally, to belong to the tree, so the collar of lead flashing was fitted around the trunk and the tree was entered in the inventory alongside the machinery: *Tree, one, in service.* It is inspected on the same rounds as the pumps.

This is the equilibrium the whole place runs on, and it was not the plan. The founders came, a hundred and twenty years ago, to drain the bowl for pasture. The fen answered drainage with reed and willow in quantities no grazing could justify, and the Works — slowly, over decades, with the patience that is its defining trait — learned to take the fen's answer instead of its own. Now the reed and sedge come in from the outer beds on the narrow-gauge tram, the osier withies come in bundles, the pollard tops come down on their nine-year rotation, and all of it goes to the mill. The beaters pound it to pulp. The presses squeeze it into fenboard — stiff grey board that goes out by barge down the lode to the river, and comes back as money. The waste is pressed into mill-cake and burned in the boilers that drive the beaters. The ash goes onto the bank and the paths. The outfall water, warm and rich, is spilled into the outer beds to fatten next year's reed. It is a wheel, and it is green at every point of its turning, and it has turned without pause longer than anyone alive.

The heart of it is the engine house: two boilers, coal when the barge has been and mill-cake always, and a pair of duplex steam pumps whose beat you felt through the causeway planks — *thump... thump-thump*, *thump... thump-thump* — the actual heartbeat of the bowl. Off the same plant: a dynamo, whining a high descant over everything, feeding the caged bulbs that hang under the leaves; line-shafting out to the mill; and the rising main, a cast-iron arm that climbs the Ring Bank to the Spout, where the pumped water of the entire Works pours, night and day, in a smooth arched jet, down into the outer marsh it was seeping from. The circle is closed over the dike. You can stand at the Spout and watch the bowl being emptied uphill forever.

The sound never stops, and it is layered like the vegetation. Base: the pumps and the boiler-room hum. Middle: the beaters, faster, a felt thudding in the plank floors; water hissing at sluice gates; belts slapping; the tram tubs screeching on curves. Top: the dynamo whine, the steam chuffs, the drip of condensation off every beam onto every roof. Over it all, at shift change, the whistle — the one voice louder than the Works itself, the clock of the place. When it blows, birds rise from the marsh outside the bank. Inside, nothing rises. Inside, the loudness has winnowed the fauna to almost nothing.

That is one of the strangest things about the bowl: how empty it is of animals, how total the green. A heron stands on the outfall apron most days, deaf or philosophical, and the men take their breaks by it. There are eels in the Sump — they arrived as elvers, up the very pipes, from an ocean no one here has seen — and sometimes they must be lifted gently off the suction screens. Midges at dusk. Spiders in the rafters. The works cat, stone deaf from birth, asleep on the warm dynamo casing. In summer a pair of swifts nests in the dead flue of the old chimney and screams through the din, and this is noted, and remembered. A kingfisher came once, decades ago. It is still spoken of by year, the way other places speak of floods. Birdsong is an outside thing; you hear it only from the top of the Ring Bank, facing away.

So the Works has evolved its own acoustics. Everyone speaks in *mill voice*, a shout-register they carry, slightly embarrassing, into the outside world. Children learn to read lips without being taught. No one attempts conversation through rain on corrugated iron — you wait out the burst, and this is so ingrained it has the force of etiquette. The night keeper walks his rounds with a lantern and a brass listening rod, pressing it to bearings, reading the health of the place through his jawbone. The rule under all rules: *if it sounds wrong, it is wrong.* Sound is health. The drone is safety.

Which makes the weekly event — they call it the Hearing — the strangest ritual of the place. At dawn on the seventh day the boilers are banked and, one by one, the engines stop. For a few hours the bowl is silent, and the silence is enormous. Cooling iron ticks like a hundred kettles. The marsh filters in over the bank — wind in reed, far-off water — sounds that belong to another world. People whisper out of something older than habit. And the men watch the Telltale wall in the gauge house, the brass float-dials that are the place's nervous system, because during the Hearing the drains rise. A finger's breadth. Two. You can watch the bowl begin, patiently, to fill. Every child knows the numbers: untended, the water is at the doorstep in three days and in the clock case in five. Then by dusk the pumps are run back up, the water is drawn down, the drone reasserts itself, and everyone exhales. It is a rehearsal of the drowning, performed weekly, calmly, with tea.

The light is ordinary — soft grey daylight, glare off black water — but it arrives in the bowl filtered, green-dimmed, broken by willow and steam. At dusk the louvered drying sheds glow from within like lanterns exhaling. At dawn, mist lies in the bowl in strata and the sheds and chimneys stand in it like masts. The air is warm and stays warm: the climate is mild, and where it isn't, the Works corrects it — compost heat off the pulp stacks, the steep pits steaming on cold mornings with their sweet-foul reek of tannin and fermentation, and the condenser loop discharging into a little steaming lagoon behind the boiler house that everyone calls the Engine's Garden. Gunnera there, leaves like tabletops. Ferns. A fig against the boiler-house wall. It is the engine's own garden; the engine grows it as a byproduct of being alive, the way the whole fen grows the Works.

Everything is old and nothing is ruined. The brick is soft as soap at the corners and patched with blue engineering brick; the iron carries strata of paint you could count like rings; the brasses are bright from a century of wiping; the stair treads are hollowed by boots; the elm sluice gates are silvered and sound. Flood marks from the one time it all went wrong are kept, unpainted-over, on the engine-house wall, as instruction. And in the yard stands the Post — a cast-iron column driven down through the peat when the Works was founded, flush with the turf then, and now standing taller than a man. That is the deepest fact of the place, deeper than the pumps: the bowl is sinking. Drained peat shrinks. The land surface lets itself down, an inch a year, and the pumps lift the water a little higher, and the rising main gets another section every generation, and each year a nail is driven at the doorframe to mark the settlement. The Works cannot win. It can only continue. Continuing is the whole technology.

Forty-odd people live inside the Ring, in the cottage row and over the stores, and their time is long time. The revolution counter in the office reads in the billions — that is the age of the Works, counted in heartbeats. Rotations run in years: osiers one, reed two, pollards nine, apprenticeships seven. Growth equals cutting. Silt equals dredging. Leak equals patch. Nothing is sudden here except the whistle and the rain-bursts, and both of those are scheduled by nature if not by the office.

At night, from the top of the Ring Bank, you can take the whole scale of it in a single turn of the head, because it is small and it is bounded and it is complete. Outward: the grey marsh to the world's edge, silent, the lode running straight to the river, a barge lantern moving on it like a slow star. Inward: the lit green knot of the bowl, steaming, beating, audible for miles — a heart the size of a hamlet, sunk in the middle of water, pumping itself dry and feeding itself green and burning its own excess to keep the whole wheel turning, as it has turned, beat on beat, for a hundred and twenty years. The sound it makes is not the sound of a struggle. It is the sound of a place that has come, at great length and by great patience, into full agreement with itself.
