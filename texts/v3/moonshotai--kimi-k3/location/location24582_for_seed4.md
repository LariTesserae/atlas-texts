The land up here gives no warning. It is a frozen dark — low barrens of black gravel and wind-polished ice under a night that, this season, has lasted longer than anyone's memory of day. The cold is absolute and dry, and the wind is the only inhabitant. Then the ground simply ends: a rim of raw, ice-cored gravel, freshly slumped, still shedding pebbles that click down into nothing. The edge is sharp because it is new. Within living memory — within the memory of the oldest house-layers below — this was a buried block of dead ice the size of a hill, stranded in the till. It melted. It let go. What it left is a kettle: a steep-sided room in the earth, a few hundred paces across, hidden, self-contained, and — by the standards of this world — warm.

Descend. The air changes in layers as you drop below the rim. The wind stops first, cut off cleanly. Then the cold eases — not much, but enough — because somewhere under the floor a deep seep rises, mineral-heavy and faintly warm, and the hollow traps what little heat it makes. Mist hangs at knee height and does not move. Condensation beads on everything and freezes in place, so the whole room is sugared with rime. The dark down here is not the dark of the barrens. It is thicker, wetter, close against the face — and it is inhabited.

The floor is not ground. It is a single living skin: a mat of pale, waxen growth, ankle-deep and warm to the hand in places, that has never in its existence been touched by light and does not want any. It feeds on the seep — on the chemistry of the water, the iron and sulfur and faint luminescent bloom that rides it — and it has colonized every square handspan of the kettle's floor and crept partway up the walls. Out of this felt rise the columns: hollow stems, pale as tallow, some thin as a wrist, some thick as a body, spaced wide apart with long empty intervals of mat between them. That is the character of the room — total life, sparsely stood. You are always touching plant and rarely near one. The columns carry the wet cold upward and drip. They flex. They are the timber, the thatch, the rope, the fuel, the architecture. Everything here is either the mat, the columns, or something made from them.

And then there are the others.

You will feel one before you know it: a change in the air, a warmth on one cheek, a slow pressure in the chest that is not quite sound — breathing, vast and unhurried, pitched below hearing. The great ones. There are never many in the kettle at once. Three, perhaps five. Each the size of a house, each radiating heat like a banked oven, each surrounded by a weather of its own: a steaming halo of melt, a crushed ellipse of mat where it lies, a slow ecology of small pale things grooming its hide. They are the reason the room is what it is. They come over the rim from the barrens because the kettle is the only open, wet, growing place in the dark, and everything in the room has arranged itself around their warmth, their bulk, their long patience. You do not see them so much as navigate by them. In total darkness, in a cold that kills, the compass here is temperature: the gradient of warmth that says *that way, a flank; that way, the seep; that way, home.*

The settlement is scattered across the intervals between them, and it is a real town — organized, layered, governed — but it is a constellation, not a cluster. A hut here against the lee flank of one great sleeper. Two huts there, a hundred paces of empty dark mat away, beside another. Nothing on the cold side of anything; everything tucked into heat-shadows and warm seams. The people build from the columns: frames of bundled hollow stems lashed with sinew and rawhide, panels of woven mat-felt, floors raised on bone and gravel against the wet. Their tools are stone, bone, antler, fish leather, and a little native copper, cold-hammered, reserved for needles and small blades and always wrapped, because metal rings, and ringing is forbidden.

Because the first law of the kettle is quiet. The room absorbs sound the way snow does, and the people have made a discipline of cooperating with it. Paths are felted. Mallets are bone. Children learn stillness before speech, and speech stays low — below the breath of your host, as they say. The loudest regular thing in the kettle is the breathing of the great ones, and it is felt more than heard. The loudest events — one of them rising, ice ticking on the rim, a kill somewhere out on the mat — arrive rarely, and are discussed for days in whispers. What hunting happens here happens mostly unheard. You find it afterward: a swathe of crushed felt, a warmth already fading, the small lives of the mat closing over the site like slow water.

The people do not kill the great ones. This is the second law, and it is older than the town. A living flank is worth more than any carcass: shelter, warmth, the melt-halo where the mat grows fastest, the grooming-rights to its hide. The people tend them, pick the ice from between the plates of their skin, and the great ones tolerate this the way a hillside tolerates weather. When one does die — of age, of the rare hunter that follows them over the rim from the barrens — the whole scattered constellation of huts converges, and it is the only time the town exists as a single body. The butchering takes days and is the calendar. Bone beams for a generation of houses. Hide for a meeting-roof. Fat rendered into the oil that feeds the lamps.

The lamps are the only light, and they are rationed like grain. Small clay dishes of fish oil with felt wicks, one to a hut, doused at the first sign of the bright water — because the seep has moods, and when it pulses, the water rising through the mat carries a faint cold luminescence, and for a night or two the entire floor of the kettle glows dimly blue, the columns standing in it like reeds in moonlight that has forgotten the moon. On those nights no one burns oil. On those nights the great ones shift and grunt in their subsonic register, and the people lie on their hut floors and watch the only weather of light their world produces.

Everything here moves, slowly and then suddenly. The mat grows fast enough to find your footprints gone in a week; it swallows dropped tools, old paths, dead huts, and the town rises on the compost of its own past — dig an arm's length through the oldest midden and your fingers strike dirty ice, the floor of the world, still there, still waiting. The seep swells and slackens. The rime thickens and weeps and thickens. The small fauna of the mat comes in pulses — a season when the water threads with blind white fish and everyone fishes by feel in the dark, a night when the whole felt floor seems to crawl — and then long sparse intervals of almost nothing, emptiness, the wide dark between columns. And over it all the great breathing of the year: the great ones arriving over the rim, and the town swelling with warmth and work and low voices; the great ones departing, and the huts dousing down to ember-life, the people drawing into the deepest warm seams to wait out the thin season half-asleep.

From the barrens above, all of this is nothing — a cold dark hollow, a breath of mist at the rim. You could walk past it within twenty paces and never know. That is the nature of the kettle: a single room, complete, hidden, young, already old with its own layers. The people's maps end at the rim. Their architecture is a plant; their streets are a temperature gradient; their cathedral is a sleeping animal; their sky is a dark that ends, on the best nights, in the dim blue glow of the floor. Stand anywhere in it and there is likely no one within a stone's throw — only the mat underfoot, the wide-spaced columns, the faint mineral smell of the seep and the animal smell of the nearest flank, the pressure of enormous slow breathing against your sternum, and the warmth on one side of your face telling you, the way it tells everyone here, exactly where you are.
