From the outside there is almost nothing to find. A dim flat waste under a low sky, and standing from it a single black pinnacle — four kilometers tall, sharp as a chip struck from obsidian, which is close to what it is. No ridge leads to it. It is not a mountain so much as a mountain's shard. From any distance it reads as geology, and this is wrong only in detail: the spire is spoil. Something drilled the ground and stacked its tailings upward around its own borehole, the way a worm carries its castings. It is the youngest high place in the world. Nothing has weathered it. The rain has not yet decided what to do with it.

At the summit the spire opens — a jagged mouth, partly veiled by a crown of filament and lens that the builders left behind, a mesh that catches mist and rations the little daylight there is. Beneath the crown stands a permanent ring of fog, where the thin air of the waste meets the heat rising out of the throat. Passing through it is the only door the place has.

The bore does not go straight down. It turns. A slow helix, one full revolution every few hundred meters of depth, its walls fluted with the regular scallops of the drill's passage — like rifling, like the thread of a screw eight kilometers long, because that is what it is. The turn is the first rule of the place, and the source of everything else: you can never see more than one coil at a time. The far wall is always near, two or three hundred meters across the void, and everything above or below your coil is rumor. The bore takes a vastness and folds it into intimacy. Every room of this city faces a wall, and the whole of it is never visible from anywhere, and so the place feels less like a canyon than like a house with too many corridors.

The light comes from below. At the bottom of the bore there is no floor — there is a diaphragm, a lens of dull red light set across the throat, and it pulses on a period of about a minute, brightening and dimming like something breathing with the lamps on. Everything in the city is lit from underneath. Every ledge casts its shadow upward onto the ceiling of the coil above it, and the eyes never stop finding this wrong. The other light is the rationed grey that filters down past the crown, bouncing from coil to coil, paying a toll at every wall, arriving at the middle depths as little more than a direction. Between these two dimnesses float the city's own lights — sparse amber and cyan points, guide-lines running along the ledge-roads like streets lit for pedestrians who have not yet been born.

Descend, and the place zones itself like altitude, but the zones are a machine's.

First, just under the fog ring, the dewline. The heat rising from below carries steam, and somewhere in the upper coils the walls are cool enough — barely — to take it back. Here the flutes run with film-water. Everything has a sheen. Acres of condenser fins stand out from the wall in banks, thin as blades, swaying almost imperceptibly, harvesting; they look like a meadow in a light wind, and they are not a meadow, and nothing about them is alive, but they behave. Along thin guide-wires the collected droplets climb — this is the one place in the world where water flows upward, beads walking taut filaments into the ducts, pulled by fields the fins are laying down. Lidded basins of black construction-glass hold the take: hot, still water, steaming faintly, dark as the waste's sky. The wet here is clean in a way wet stone never is. No green film, no moss, nothing colonizing. The dewline glitters and is sterile, and it is the brightest part of the city, because it is the only part that reflects everything.

Below the dewline, the middle coils — the city proper. Ledge-roads spiral along the flutes. Rail-spines run the long way down, and occasionally a carrier-sled glides past on one, a soft whisk dopplering up or down the helix, the only thing in the place that ever goes by. The buildings cluster in loose districts with wide bare wall between them — it is a city of negative space, a few structures standing small against the fluted dark, the void doing most of the architecture. And the structures are not buildings, exactly, but organs: separator stacks, valve galleries, tank farms, duct-trees, things whose functions can be guessed the way organ function is guessed — flow, exchange, storage, waiting. Among them, disconcertingly, are the parlors. Small dry rooms, human-sized. A bench. A shelf. A spigot that gives hot clean water. In one of them, on the shelf, a single cup, cast from the same glass as everything else, set out as if for a guest. Doors at walking height open onto tool-chambers. Stairs with even risers connect ledges that nothing climbs. The city is not ruined — nothing here has ever been used enough to ruin. It reads instead as pre-inhabited: a city built before its people, or instead of them. A city in the subjunctive mood.

Below the built band the coils go bare — just the drill's scalloped flutes descending through kiln heat, dry now, sweat gone before it beads. At the weep-points where brine sweats from the rock, the only natural ornament in the place accretes: salt fans, white halite feathers and gypsum blades, delicate, ephemeral, dissolved to nothing whenever a rim-rain sends its overflow this far down and grown back in the weeks after. Sparse survey lights. The occasional dormant construction head folded against the wall like a tucked wing.

And then the shimmer zone, where the air stands in visible columns, and below that the diaphragm, the red lens, breathing. The bore does not end there. The diaphragm is not a floor but an eyelid. Below it continues the apparatus proper, of which all of this — the bore, the city, the spire itself — is only the exposed part: a throat, a gill, the flange of something buried much deeper that trades heat and vapor with the sky. The pinnacle is the planet breathing through a new mouth.

The processes layer. The water loop never stops: steam rises from the depths, gives itself up at the dewline, climbs the wires, fills the basins, and what the basins overflow falls off the ledges into the void — interior rain that rarely lands, shrinking as it drops, flashing back to vapor in the kiln zone with a long faint hiss the whole shaft carries, and rising again as part of the warm breath of the place. The rising column rotates, slowly; the helix turns it, and fog-bands barber-pole upward through the coils, and falling drips drift in slow spirals. Construction happens in bursts — days of stillness, then a far tick-tick across the void, and a fresh extrusion of foamed glass-ceramic growing out of the wall, the rock itself the feedstock. Finished parts are indistinguishable from new ones, because nothing here ages. Ledges migrate. The map is a rumor of last month. Nothing is old; the oldest object in the city is the bore itself, the wound, a few decades young at most. And rarely — this is the place's only true weather — rain comes to the waste outside, and the crown weeps, and for an hour real water falls into the coils and the upper city stands in its own storm. The reservoirs brim. The salt fans dissolve. The condenser fields sway in the drip-wind. The hour of steam, and then the long drying, and the loop resumes.

The sound is mostly the sound of a held breath. Single drips strike with echoes seconds long, returning from three directions, bent and pitch-shifted by the helix. The young spire ticks as it settles, thermal pings from the walls, a glass woodpecker across a valley. The sleds whisk past. In the dewline the film-water makes a sheen-sound like a page being turned very slowly. And when the wind crosses the rim at the correct angle, the whole bore — an organ flue the size of a mine — sounds a single infrasonic note for minutes at a time, felt in the sternum and the teeth rather than heard, played for no one, answering a sky it cannot see.

No birds cross the void, though it is shaped like an aviary. Nothing roosts but maintenance drones folded in rows on the high ledges, still as birds the way a shadow is like a bird. If the city notices a visitor, it does so the way a thermostat notices — truly, and without opinion. The parlors' lights come on as you approach. They are always off. They are always on for you.

The rules of the place are few and absolute:

The center is never built across. Water may cross the void; structure may not. In all its history the city has never once obstructed its own throat.

Light comes from below. Shadows fall upward.

Water falls but rarely lands.

Nothing is old, and nothing is finished.

You cannot see the whole of it from anywhere. To know the city you walk it, coil by coil, and the walking is the only map.

Somewhere above, in the dewline, a droplet lets go of a fin and falls into the void, drifting with the slow rotation, dwindling, gone to vapor before the kiln zone, rising past you an hour later as part of the warm breath of the place — to condense, to climb a wire, to fill a basin, to fall again. On its shelf the cup stays dry, and waits. The red light pulses. Slowly, in the dim, through its new throat, the planet breathes, and the city — satisfied is not the word; correct — continues.
