From the col you can see the whole of it at once, framed between two cairns — a bowl in the mountain's shoulder, small enough that a strong arm could throw a stone from rim to floor, or near enough. That is the first thing the place does to you: it lets you take it in with a single look, and then it spends the rest of the day refusing to feel small. A walker could cross it in an hour. No one does. Something about the distances inside it — the emptiness between things, the silence, the light — stretches the hour until it behaves like a day.

The herders in the valley below call it the Cup. Their saying is that the Cup keeps the sky.

The headwall at its back still carries an apron of ice, and the ice is leaving. That is the fact everything else hangs from. Within living memory — within the memory, the marks suggest, of people whose cairns still stand on the rim — the glacier filled this bowl to overflowing, and when it pulled back it left the mountain's new skin exposed: pale gneiss, polished to a dull gloss, scored with striations that run like comb-marks down the slabs, the rock the raw gray-bone color of something never before touched by air. The moraine it dumped at the bowl's lip is still a fresh scar, sharp-crested, unstable, its boulders angular and unrounded, its surfaces bare of the black and orange lichen that crusts every older stone. The whole place is young. You can read its age in lichen the way you'd read tree rings — and the story it tells is a few centuries, no more. A landform in its first morning.

And it is still being made, audibly, though you have to learn to hear it. The frost works every night. Water seeps into the new cracks by day and wedges them wider by dark, and somewhere on the walls a stone splits with a flat crack, startling in the quiet, and a small fan of scree runs down and stops. The talus field below the headwall hides the last of the glacier: a core of dead, banded ice under the rockfall, sweating. You hear it long before you see it — a trickle with no visible source, water talking to itself under the stones, the ground ticking faintly, thermally, like a slow clock. On a still afternoon you can stand on that talus and understand that you are standing on the mountain's icebox, on the last of what filled this bowl.

At the lowest point sits the tarn, and the tarn is where the place stops pretending to be ordinary.

It is small — a stone's throw across, two — and it is black with rock flour and depth, and it does not ripple. Wind that is moving your sleeve moves nothing on that surface. Only touch raises rings: a drinking animal, a dropped stone, a finch's foot. The rest of the time the tarn keeps a perfect second sky, held upside down in still water, and at night it keeps the stars — keeps them better than the sky does, because down there they don't tremble. The melt threads that feed it run milky turquoise for an hour at midday, braiding down the polished slabs, and the tarn's level rises a finger's width, and by dusk the threads have thinned and stopped and the whole plumbing of the bowl freezes shut again. Water here is not a feature. It is an event, on a schedule, and everything living knows the schedule.

That schedule is the bowl's heartbeat, and it runs fast. Most of the year the Cup is locked — snow, silence, nothing moving. Then comes the brief window, a few weeks of high summer, and the place compresses a whole ecology into the four hours around noon. The light arrives early and means it: at this height the sky goes dark, almost violet, and the sun comes over the col like a blade, bright the way a blade is bright, and about as warm. That is the first wrongness you notice. The light here illuminates without heating. It doubles itself off the snow patches and the pale polished rock until the world is almost too bright to look at, colors gone saturated and strange — and the moment you step into shadow the cold takes you like well water. Warmth exists, but only in specific places: on the black slabs that hold the sun, in the lee of the moraine, on the flat stone the pikas use. You learn the warm spots the way you learn furniture in a dark room.

At midmorning the melt begins. Threads appear on the slabs. The buried ice ticks faster. And then, all at once, the bowl is awake.

The green of it is not spread evenly — nothing here is. The bowl reads from the rim as gray and bone-white, and the life sits in it like a wound of color: a flush of sedge and moss below the tarn's outlet, ankle-deep and dense as felt; seams of green following each melt thread; and everywhere the cushions. Moss campion, purple saxifrage, glacier crowfoot blooming at the very edge of the melting snow, each plant pressed flat against the stone, each cushion no bigger than a fist and older than you are, decades of an inch a year. A garden you could carry in two hands, that cannot be carried, that is the accumulated patience of centuries. In the flowers, for those four hours, bumblebees work — the only hum in the bowl, fat and unlikely in the cold, flying the warm hours and vanishing when the light tilts. On the old snow patches the algae bloom faintly red, a rust-colored stain, the closest thing to warmth the snow ever shows.

The animals are abundant and almost never visible at the same time. This is the bowl's arithmetic: the emptiness is full of sign. Tracks, droppings, burrows, shed feathers, scraped-out resting hollows — every surface is inscribed — but the bodies themselves are sparse, far apart, each one alone in its acreage. One pika works the whole boulder field below the headwall, and you hear its sharp *eet* arrive from across the talus as if it were at your elbow — the bowl does that with sound, swallowing what is near and delivering certain far things whole, so that you stop trusting your ears on about the second day. The pika spends the noon hours hauling mouthfuls of sedge to its drying stone, laying out hay in the sun, because winter is the default here and summer is the errand. Marmots whistle from opposite ends of the moraine, long intervals apart. A herd of ibex stands on the far slabs as gray dots, and in early summer they come down to the cliffs with kids, because nothing with teeth wants to climb here. Something with teeth comes anyway, rarely: a cat, seen once a season if that, known mostly by its scat and by the way the whistling stops. Overhead, once every few hours, a lammergeier crosses the bowl on wings you can hear — the air is so quiet that wingbeats register — and below its usual route sits a flat boulder white with splinters, its anvil, where it has dropped bones for longer than the cairns have stood. Snow finches work the melt edge. Ptarmigan tick in the willow scrub — a knee-high forest you step over, a whole woodland in miniature. At dusk, briefly, midges rise over the tarn, and the finches rise to them, and for ten minutes the bowl is almost busy.

Then the light goes, and everything stops at once, like a shop closing.

The people below have never lived here. They visit — a few days a year, in the window — and their marks are as sparse as the animals and as patient as the cushions. The cairns on the col, some tumbled and black with lichen, some fresh-headed: everyone who crosses adds a stone, and no one takes one, and that has been true for a very long time. A low ring of stones against the moraine, a shelter or the memory of one, its roof slabs stacked beside it as if waiting, its hearth stones blackened by fires that burned a lifetime apart. A stone-lidded cache pit, empty. And on the polished rock below the old ice line, pecked marks — tallies, a spiral, the stiff-sided outline of an ibex — some of them on slabs that were under the glacier within living memory. Someone stood on rock just born from the ice and counted something, or claimed something, or thanked something.

By the tarn there is a single boulder, and on it the offerings: bone beads, antler tines, a disc of cold-hammered copper gone dark. Nothing is built near the water but this. Whatever rule that is — and the herders only shrug and say the Cup keeps the sky, as if that explains it — it has held.

Those are the bowl's terms, and you learn them by staying. The light will not warm you; find the black stone, find the lee. Water runs at noon and not otherwise; drink when the mountain drinks. Move slowly, because the place has decided to be quiet and your noise will simply be swallowed, and you will be left with the feeling of having shouted into a held breath. Touch nothing on the tarn-side boulder. Add a stone at the col. And understand that what you are standing in is an interval — a few bright centuries between ices, a young land still sharp from its making, already inhabited, already named, already loved in the spare way that hard places are loved.

At night the cold locks down and the bowl ticks as the stones contract, and the buried ice answers, faintly, from under the talus. The sky goes very dark and very full. And the tarn, which has kept the sky all day, keeps it all night too — the only still water for a day's walk in any direction, holding the stars without a tremor, a black cup brimful of light, while everything that lives here waits, frozen into its burrow and its schedule, for the four warm hours to come around again.
