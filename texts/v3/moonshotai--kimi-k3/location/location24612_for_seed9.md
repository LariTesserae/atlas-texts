**The Condensary**

The lift cage comes up through the last of the cloud and stops at a bell. From the landing you can take in the whole of the works in a single turn of the head — which is the first strange thing about it, because everything here behaves like a watershed, and watersheds are not supposed to fit inside the eye.

You can walk the perimeter rail in ten minutes if you don't stop, and you will stop. Below the rail the poured apron falls away in a clean, steep batter, and below that is air, and below the air, most days, is the top of the weather. Whatever peak the builders started from is buried now, irrelevant. Nothing native shows. Every surface you can see — every wall, basin, stair, flume, and cliff face — was mixed, cast, riveted, glazed, or grown here. The nearest thing to wilderness is the cloud, and the cloud is cargo.

The air is warm and full. It is the warmth of a held breath, of sun on wet stone and the waste heat of the condensers braided together, and it never quite dries. Normal daylight comes and goes as the clouds pass: bright, grey, bright again. By early afternoon the fog tide climbs the slope and the works goes inside its own weather, and that is when the place does its real work, because this is a machine for drinking cloud.

It drinks through the condenser rows — tall fins of cold alloy standing in ranks along the upper terraces, beaded and streaming — and through the fog harps, frames of taut wire that hum faintly when the wind is right and weep steadily either way. It drinks through the Cold Main, a pipe the diameter of a tree trunk that runs the length of the works at shoulder height, refrigerated from the pump hall, sweating so hard in the warm air that it rains underneath it on schedule. The crew calls it the six o'clock rain, though it runs all day; six is when they walk beneath it to cool off, bareheaded, laughing, in the middle of a clear sky. The water gathers in weep channels, drops into the Long Basin, and from there enters the circulation, which is the closest thing this place has to blood.

The circulation does not respect gravity. Pumps in the hall send the water uphill as often as down, so a stream can pass you twice — once at ankle height in a glazed runnel, once overhead in a flume, going the other way. The channels lattice everything: under the walkways in grates, along the stair risers, across the terrace walls in sheets the thickness of a fingernail. Every weir has a number and a gauge glass and a pencil mark. The water is distilled-clean and stays that way for about a day, which is how long it takes the algae to find it.

And the ground. The ground is the thing you will tell people about.
