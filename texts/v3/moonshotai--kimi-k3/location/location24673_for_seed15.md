From above, there is almost nothing to find. A low mound in a hollow at the foot of a slope, turfed over, warm underfoot if you happen to lie down on it — which no one does. A clay chimney no wider than a wellhead rises from its crest, and if you put your ear to that opening you feel the sound before you hear it: a dense, busy roar, a hum in the bone of the hill. The hill is a lie told in turf. It is heaped earth over a masonry vault, and under the vault is a room, and the room is nearly full of water.

It was finished a few weeks ago. That matters. Everything about it still carries the hand.

You enter by the light well, because the door is drowned. The stair that descends to the door is drowned too — you can see it through the water, eleven steps going down into their own reflection, the bottom ones wavering out of sight into jade. The chamber is small, perhaps ten paces across, square, with a shallow barrel vault, and the water has risen to within an arm's length of the crown. It was never meant to. The pool was designed chest-deep, with dry walkways and benches at its rim, but the wooden sluice on the outflow jammed shut at the first filling, and the inflow — a clay pipe running down from a spring cistern higher on the slope — was not stopped in time. The water climbed past the benches, past the coping, past the door lintel, until it found the emergency overflow slot cut high in the far wall. Now inflow equals outflow and the level holds there, constant, one body-length of warm air trapped between water and vault. The builders went for the sluice-key, the mason, the smith. They have not yet come back.

The light is the first thing, always. At midday the sun stands over the wellhead and drops a single column straight down through the shaft into the pool, and the whole body of water becomes a lamp. It is spring water, mineral-clear, faintly sweet with dissolved lime, and it carries the light all the way to the floor — two and a half body-lengths down — where hand-glazed tiles in turquoise and pale cobalt receive it and throw it back upward. You can read the floor from the surface: the tile-setter's pattern, a border of interlocking waves, and in the center a sun disc whose rays wobble and shiver with every disturbance above. Caustic nets crawl ceaselessly across the vault, bright ripples of reflected light sliding over lime concrete that still bears the grain of its timber formwork, every board-mark crisp. The wet upper walls are burnished lime plaster, polished with soap until they gleam, and they glare like wet bone. When a cloud crosses, the room falls for a moment into silver dusk; then the sun returns and everything blazes again.

And it is loud. The room is a bell that water rings. At the east wall the bronze spout — cast in a sand mold, still pink-gold, not yet green — pours a wrist-thick stream in an unbroken arc, and where it strikes the surface it drives a deep, hollow drumming down into the water. The overflow slot at the west drinks with a continuous gurgle, pulling a slow current across the pool's skin. Because the nights are cooler than the water, the vault breathes out its own weather: vapor rises all day, condenses on the cool stone crown, and by morning it is raining inside — a hundred drips at a hundred periods, some striking water with a deep plink, some ticking against the one emergent step of the drowned stair, some falling on the coping ledge. All of it multiplied by the hard wet surfaces into a bright, crowded roar that never stops. The builders knew what they were making. Set into the vault in three rows are ceramic resonator jars, mouths flush with the plaster, and they take the water-noise and hum with it, a low chord under everything, tuned — the deepest answering the inflow's drum, the highest alive with the drip-rain. If you float with your ears at the surface you hear two rooms at once: the clattering bright one above the waterline, and below it a dense, dark hum that comes through the bone, the spout's bass note filling your skull.

The warmth is a residue and a gift at once. Under the tiled floor run the flues of a hypocaust, and the furnace in its service slot went cold days ago — the smell of wet ash still ghosts up from its mouth — but the great mass of brick and concrete remembers the fire and gives it back slowly. The water holds at the temperature of a wrist, of bath-water an hour after the servants stopped feeding the flame. The equatorial sun through the shaft does the rest. Steam lifts off the surface in slow coils and climbs the light column, so the sunbeam is visible for its whole length, a solid thing, full of turning vapor.

Almost nothing lives here yet, and that too is the newness. The water has not had time to learn mosquitoes. No fish were ever stocked; the stocking would have come after the dedication. No swallows have found the vault. A single moth mistook the light shaft a week ago and lies on the surface near the coping, pale as a pressed petal, and it is the only animal in the room. The green has begun, though, because green needs no invitation. A veil of algae is just forming where the light strikes deepest, turning the upper water faintly jade, and a dusting of duckweed dots the sheltered corner behind the spout's splash. Moss — blown in as spore, or carried on the masons' trowels — has taken the mortar joints in a bright green thread-work near the wellhead, and on the ledge of the shaft itself one fern has rooted, its fronds hanging into the steam. Along the south wall, sunk to their rims in the floor, sit four clay pots where lotus tubers were planted at the filling. One has sent up a stem. A single leaf has reached the surface and is beginning, just now, to unroll.

The objects are few and stand far apart in all that water and light: the spout; the stair; a wooden offering bowl that floats its slow circuit from spout to weir and back, going around and around like a thought the room can't finish; an oil lamp in a dry niche, unlit, its wick still white; and through the drowned doorway, if you dive and look, the dim service passage with its neat stack of firewood, waiting for hands.

The young stone is beginning its long conversation with the water. At a hairline crack in the vault, lime is already precipitating into a stalactite no longer than a grain of rice. Down one wall, faint white streaks mark where the drips have chosen their paths. At the waterline, the barest scum-ring is whitening the coping — the first line of a record that will, in years, become a band of mineral crust telling every flood and ebb. The chisel tracks on the coping stones are still sharp enough to catch a fingernail.

The place runs itself by plain rules. What pours in must pour out; the weir holds the level. What the sun gives by day, the night air takes back as rain from the ceiling. When the afternoon storms come over the slope — and they come most days, this season — a wall of rain drops straight down the shaft, the roar doubles, the surface boils, the overflow drinks hard, and then the sun returns and the steam rises thicker than before. The room has a pulse and it is the day's.

Nothing about it is strange. It is only new, and warm, and full of light, and playing itself like an instrument whose musicians have stepped out — the water arriving on schedule, the vault humming its chord, the one lotus leaf opening in the bright drowned air, ahead of everyone.
