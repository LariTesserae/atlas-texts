The climb should cool you. It does the opposite. You rise through a belt of cold gray cloud, and somewhere inside it the air turns—first mild, then warm, then wet as breath—and you come out onto a rim of black, freshly broken rock into heat like a greenhouse with the door shut. Below you, weather. Above you, a hard bright sky. Between them, stretching forty kilometers to a pale far edge: the tableland, steaming.

It reads as flat from the rim, flat as a held breath, a vast plane of bottle-green and bronze and near-black red, sheeted with water that mirrors the haze. What breaks the flatness breaks it at long intervals—a tower here, another a haze-softened hour's walk beyond it, each one smoking faintly at the crown. There is nothing modest to look at. No middle distances full of things. It is emptiness and presences, and the emptiness is alive.

Because the first thing you learn on the ground is that there is no ground. No soil, no stone underfoot. The plateau is upholstered in a single continuous skin—a mat, knee-deep, a braiding of root and stolon and flattened leaf, saturated through, warm as blood. It flexes under your weight and slowly rises back. Lay a hand flat on it and wait: there is a pulse. Slow, deep, irregular. Water being moved. The whole floor is a muscle doing hydrological work, and it smells of crushed green and hot mineral, of a wrung sponge, of pepper.

You can walk an hour across it with nothing taller than your shin. The openness is enormous and oddly quiet-feeling, even though quiet is the wrong word for this place. Shallow cells of water hold the sky like held breath. Sedges stand in sparse tufts, widely spaced, as if placed. Then, on the horizon of your walk, a presence resolves.

The standpipes are the plateau's monuments. Ribbed columns, a hundred meters and more, grown as bundles of vascular pipe around a hollow core, flaring slightly at the base like a tree that decided to be a cooling tower instead. They draw the hot water up from the mat—up and up, against sense—and vent it from the crown as mist, so each one wears a slow white plume that the wind tears sideways. They sway. They groan as they sway, a deep timber-note, a ship's hull note. Put your ear to one and you hear the interior: knocking, glugging, the long swallow of rising water. Plumbing in a building that was never built.

Between the standpipes, the other architecture. Channels cross the flats in raised vaults of grown fiber, arch after arch, and the water inside them runs on a gradient so true across kilometers that the mind refuses it, then accepts it, then is quietly frightened by it. There are basins—vast cupped leaves the size of lakes, stepped one below another, spilling rim to rim in stepped cascades. There are dikes, long living levees, that wall the plateau into cells which flood and drain and flood again on a rhythm you have not yet identified. Nothing here has a joint. Nothing has a cut edge. There are no tools and never have been, no hands, and yet it is all made—constructed, engineered, deliberate in every proportion except the proportion of a maker. The plateau practices civil engineering as a metabolism. That is the wrongness at its heart, the thing you keep circling: intention without anyone to intend it.

The heat comes from below. The whole table is young, still rising on its fault, and its bones are close to the surface and hot. Springs break through the mat in steaming wounds; geysers pulse on their own slow clocks; fresh scarps open where the land tilts, spilling a lake sideways into a new cell, and the gray ash of the wound is closed over by creeping green within days. The rim where you entered is sheer and unnotched—nothing has had time to weather. The waterfalls that fall off every edge of the plateau haven't carved so much as a groove. They just spill, white cords dropping into the cloud belt, and the clouds below rain on other, colder countries.

Every afternoon the storms come. Convection builds all morning in the saturated air until the sky goes bronze and then the rain arrives, hot as bathwater, drumming. And this is when you understand that the plateau is loud. Rain on the hollow vaults is a thousand drums. Geysers hiss without pause. The standpipes knock and gulp. The towers groan in the wind, and the wind itself plays the hollow cut stalks of old growth like organ flues, a chord that wanders. At the rim the falls make a white noise that never leaves the edge of hearing. And under everything, in the pauses, there is a sound you will spend a long time refusing to believe: a faint, pervasive crackle, like a fire that never catches, like static—growth. Millions of cells dividing at once. The place is audible at the scale of its own tissue. Loud, and entirely voiceless. No call, no wingbeat, no buzz. Nothing that has a throat uses it. There are no throats.

No animal has ever stood here. That fact organizes everything. Nothing grazes, so nothing is trimmed; growth is bounded only by water, heat, and gravity, and it collides with gravity constantly. The turbulence is the plateau's true nature. Near the hot seeps you can watch it happen—press your palm beside a new shoot and hold still, and the shoot lengthens against your hand; a leaf unscrolls like time-lapse made real. The standpipes live for weeks. They rise, they flower at the crown—enormous pale blooms, sweet-scented, opening to no one, pollinated by wind and water and their own devices, perfume and color poured out for no eyes and no noses—and then some noon the fiber fails and the tower folds with a bass boom you feel in your sternum, its stored water bursting out in a single geyser. By dusk the mat is already crawling over the wreck. In three days the fall is a green mound. In a week you could not find it. Nothing on this plateau is old—except, perhaps, the mat itself, which may be one organism, may be the only ancient thing here, may be the plateau proper, everything else merely its temporary organs: a regional being fruiting towers the way a mold fruits spores.

The light is ordinary light, which makes it all stranger. A normal sun, a normal day, gold through the steam haze, rainbows standing in the mist-crowns after the storms. At night it darkens honestly, and only the water in the channels carries a faint green luminescence, and the knocking in the pipes goes on, and the groans, and the crackle. You sleep—if you sleep—on a warm floor with a heartbeat.

From the rim at evening you can stand with one foot in steam and one in thin cold mountain air. Far off, beyond the cloud belt, other ranges hold honest snow, proper altitude, landscape that obeys the rules. This one doesn't. It sits up here like a cistern in the sky, feeding the rivers of lower worlds—a watershed that built itself, a waterworks with no masons, hot as a bath where it should be frozen.

Leave, and come back in a month. The cells will have shifted. The tower you knew will be a mound; three new ones will be smoking on a horizon that was empty. The map redraws itself continuously, and the plateau is not a place that holds a shape. It is a place that keeps making one.
