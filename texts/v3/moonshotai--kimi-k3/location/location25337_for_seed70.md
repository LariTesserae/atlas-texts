The mountains did not rise into the air. They came up through the sea, and most of them are still beneath it — a cordillera of new rock climbing from a deep floor toward a bright ceiling, so young that the water has not yet had time to forgive them their edges.

Geologically, this happened a breath ago: thousands of years, not millions. The range runs for several hundred kilometers, dozens of peaks strung along a fault that is still open and still working. The tallest summits break the surface as skerries of black glass — the only dry land in the region, unwalkable, soilless, rinsed by warm rain. Everything else is drowned, and lit, and high. Altitude is inverted here: the high places are the bright ones. To stand on a submerged summit is to stand in alpine glare; to descend the flank is to go from glare into green dusk and then, kilometers down, into a night that never fully lifts from the basins.

The rock is basalt that chilled against water, which is to say it is largely glass. Obsidian walls, columnar cliffs whose organ-pipe joints are the size of towers, spires, shards, ridges thin enough to seem deliberate. Nothing is rounded. There is no sand anywhere, no mud, no gravel — nothing has existed long enough to be ground down and carried off, and so the basins between ridges are bare dark stone, and every ledge ends the way the fracture left it. Where the glass is clean and thick, daylight does something it does nowhere else: it enters the rock and travels. The ridges are slow light-pipes. Their flanks carry dull amber seams down into the dimness, veins of captured noon, and the deep rock is never wholly dark where a clean mass of glass connects it to the sunlit shoulders above.

Into this new country the plants came first, and so far they have come almost alone. They solved the problem of height with buoyancy. Every species here carries bladders — gas-filled chambers of wax and membrane — and anchors itself to the glass by holdfast cables, so that the forest stands vertical in the water column, fronds straining gently upward, a whole flora hanging between its roots and the sky. And it is tiered, strictly, by light.

The gold tier lives just under the ceiling: broad buoyant blades the color of late afternoon, packed so densely over each summit that from above a peak reads as a halo. Below it, the emerald tier — the thicket — where the density of the place becomes a physical fact. Curtains of frond near-touching frond, every cubic meter occupied, sightlines of a few meters at best, the water itself turned to green dim weather, saturated with drifting seeds, shed bladders, slow debris. In the spore months, a green snow falls upward and downward and hangs, and the thicket becomes something like the inside of a shaken globe settling at half speed. Below the thicket, the rust tier: wide, thin, crimson-brown leaves spread flat to catch whatever light filters through, everything slower, sparser, more patient.

And below the rust tier, the light gives out — and there the forest's past stands in stone.

Because the water here is young-rock water, warm and heavy with dissolved minerals, what dies does not rot so much as set. Silica and pale carbonate bloom over dead tissue, and within a few decades a fallen tier of forest is a replica of itself in stone, still standing, still anchored. Every mountain wears its whole history in section: the living crown, then the petrified forests marking where the light-line used to be — pale ghost-groves in the dark, tier below tier, going down — and at last the bare black root where nothing has ever lived. The peaks grow a handspan in a good year. The forest climbs to keep its place in the light. The stone diary thickens underneath. The rules of the place are simple and strange: what dies turns to stone; what lives knows its depth and keeps it; nothing is rounded; nothing hurries.

The new rock also weeps brine, and brine is heavier than the sea. It gathers in the caldera bowls and crater basins as true underwater lakes — lakes with shorelines you could stand on, lakes with surfaces. Their tops shiver when the long swell passes far overhead; otherwise they are mirror-still and nearly lifeless, since nothing rooted can survive in them and drifting bladders that sink to the boundary crumple there or lie on the halocline as if on glass. A caldera takes decades to fill. Then a rim overtops, and a fall begins: a rope of heavy water spilling down the flank, too slow and too coherent to be believed, bending around ridges, pooling in some lower bowl — a waterfall that may take a year to finish and makes no sound at all except a faint granular hiss where the two waters shear against each other.

The ceiling, from within, is hammered light. Rain comes often and warm, and from below it is silent — a dimpling of the sky, a soft static at the surface. After heavy rain a skin of freshwater lies briefly on the denser sea, and the light coming through goes doubled and thin and strange, and the tallest bladders of the gold tier push up through the ceiling into open air and rest there like the backs of sleeping animals. Fronds tear free on their own slow schedules and raft together into golden meadows that drift the currents around the range, a decade to a lap, voyaging gardens with no destination.

What is missing, always, is the animals. A sea this crowded, and no flicker of fish, no clicks, no chorus, nothing darting at the corner of the eye — the fullness of the place and the emptiness of it sit side by side and never resolve. There are the lens-grazers: discs of near-perfectly clear tissue, meters across, perceptible only as a bending in the light, a lensing of the forest behind them. They come through on circuits measured in years to sift the spore-snow, and the thicket parts around them without breaking and closes behind without marking their passage. In the holdfast tangles there are small glassy lives, few and easily missed. And once in a long while something from the deep basins wanders up into the light and dies there, and its arrival is an event that nothing witnesses, and the slow stone takes it in time like everything else.

The quiet is nearly total. Cooling glass ticks, irregularly, the way a house settles but at geological tempo. Frond rasps against frond when the long swell leans through the forest. Rain whispers at the ceiling. And years apart, the range adjusts itself — a low groan felt in the chest more than heard, the whole country shifting in its sleep.

From a saddle between two summits, in the bright shallow water, the scale of it opens. Ridge after ridge runs off in both directions, each one crowned with its halo of gold, the color grading from gold to emerald to blue to a haze that is not mist but simply distance through dense lit water. Between the ridges, gaps of deep blue where the basins drop away. Dozens of peaks, days of travel from one end of the province to the other, and beyond the farthest visible crown the strong sense of more — a whole drowned country, young and saturated and nearly silent, extending past any vantage.

Nothing here is finished. A single frond may live a century; a saddle becomes a ridge within a lifetime; a new spire splits an old forest from below in the slowest violence there is, and any map of the place is a rumor with a date on it. Everything is early. The sea is still deciding what this landscape will become. And in the evenings, when the open water has gone fully dark, the glass ridges hold the day a little longer — amber seams dimming by degrees along the flanks, the mountains letting go of the light as slowly as they do everything else.
