The climb takes the better part of a day, most of it inside the cloud. You walk up through gray wool, through dripping thickets that thin and shrink as the air thins, and then — somewhere around the point where your breath starts to come short — you break the surface. The cloud sea falls away beneath you, a second, softer country, and above it lies the bench: a broad tilted table of pale stone and green pelt, standing in clear cool light, with a town scattered across it so low against the ground that you understand only slowly that it is a town at all.

The mountains are young here, and they show it. The ridges rise like torn cloth, blade-thin, unsoftened; the scree slopes are still loose enough to clink underfoot; the rock breaks white and sharp. Nothing has been rained on long enough to round. The soil is new, thin, black, full of stone shards. And the water is young too — it has not yet learned to make rivers. It sheets over bare rock after rain, pools in natural basins the size of a cup or a bath, each with its own sprig of moss, its own fingernail-sized frog, and then slips into the joints of the stone and vanishes. There are seeps where the tilted strata daylight — long dark stripes of wet green across the pale slope — and there is frost every night, feathering the shaded faces of the stones, ticking faintly as it melts each morning. The sun has some warmth. The shade has none.

What water the bench has arrives on a schedule. Each evening the cloud sea rises like a tide, tops the rim, and delivers perhaps a quarter-hour of fine rain — no more, never more, however dry the year below. All night the mist stands in the folds of the land, and all night the town drinks. At dawn the cloud sinks again, and the day comes up clear. This happens every evening. This happens every morning. A traveler who stays a week begins to suspect it; a traveler who stays two stops suspecting.

The vegetation is lush in the way of high, cold places — not a canopy but a pelt, knee-deep and springy, laid over the whole bench. Cushion plants firm as ottomans. Cup-shaped rosettes with silvered, felted leaves that comb the fog and hold what they catch. Lupine spires, bunchgrass on the dry steps, a low herb that gives off a garlic smell when crushed. In the sheltered folds stand groves of small gnarled trees with red bark peeling away in scrolls, hung with lichen beards that drip at first light. Moss lies deep over every shaded stone — deep, and exactly bounded. On the walls of the town the moss holds its borders like a garrison. It does not advance. It has not advanced in living memory, or in the memory of the valleys below, which is longer.

The town itself: a few hundred buildings spread across kilometers of bench, nowhere crowded, nowhere quite absent. From the rim it reads as meadow — the roofs are sod, laid over stone flags, green with the same turf as the ground, so that only gables and doorways and the thin threads from the hearths betray it. Each house is fitted pale stone without mortar, low-doored, small deep windows, lintels of black wood, and a stone stair built into the gable end leading up onto the roof — once for tending the turf, now used mostly by the mottlebacks, the small fine-wooled grazers who stand on the rooftops against the sky like figures on a frieze. Beside every path runs a runnel: a knee-deep channel of dressed stone, lidded in stretches, with settling basins and carved overflow-lips at each junction. The runnels are the town's second skeleton. They run full at night and after the dusk rain, thin to a shine by midday, and feed the cisterns that lie under the floors — stone-roofed, reached by steps, the water cold and tasting of rock, always at the same level. A dark wet line marks that level on every cistern wall. It is never higher. It is never lower.

At the head of the bench lies the reservoir, a long rectangular pool rimmed with fitted blocks, still as poured glass, long as a held note. The herds drink at the stepped end. Frogs chime there in the dark.

At the bench's far edge is the quarry, and the quarry is where the place tells on itself. The bite in the living rock is fresh. Blocks lie in every stage of making — one half-detached, propped on stone wedges; a stack squared and waiting; a sledge of poles with its ropes coiled beside it, the fiber sound as the day it was laid. A hide roll of bronze chisels, bright. Walls around the town stand waist-high with the next course of stones laid out on the ground in the order they were to be placed, the mason's plan still legible. Scaffolds of lashed poles lean where they were left. Nothing sags. Nothing rots. The tool marks on every dressed face are as crisp as the morning they were cut, and the steps of the town, for all the hooves that pass over them daily, are unworn. The work stopped one morning, and the morning never ended.

The animals have inherited everything. Mottlebacks graze the old terrace-plots, where grain and tubers still sow themselves and bear, and are cropped, and bear again, and are never diminished. Wrens own the lintels — every third doorway has a nest. Small dark ducks stand in the runnels after rain, absurd and dear. Whistlers call from the scree at dawn; hummingbirds, improbable in the cold, work the rosettes and quarrel over them. A wide, smoke-colored cat is sometimes seen at dusk and mostly not seen. Foxes sit on the meeting-house steps like doorkeepers. In every third house, something is sleeping. The herds neither grow nor shrink; calves and bones enter the grass at the same quiet rate, and carcasses do not last the night — the foxes, the beetles, the patient fungi are part of the housekeeping. Nothing is allowed to accumulate. Nothing is allowed to be different tomorrow.

The meeting-house is the largest building, many-benched, smelling faintly of cold smoke. In its hearth a fire burns low and blue-hearted, and has never been fed, and the wood basket beside it is full. Travelers sleep here by custom. In the storehouses the jars are sealed with clay and full of grain, and you may open one and eat, and the grain is sweet — and in the morning the jar stands sealed again, and full. This is the most frightening thing about the bench, until you decide it is the kindest.

The rules, as travelers have pieced them together, are simple and total. The day repeats: the same mist standing in the same fold at dawn, the same sun clearing the same notch, the same quarter-hour of rain at dusk. Footprints pressed into the wet path are gone by morning; the grass you bent has risen; the turf you cut is whole. What you take is returned. What you leave remains — that is the one seam. A tin cup on a windowsill, rusting honestly. A scarf faded to a ghost of stripes. A boot gone soft. Names scratched into lintels by the rare visitors who found the place, a dozen across the centuries, the oldest in alphabets no one below can read. The bench cannot digest what it did not make. The only decay in this world is imported.

And the sky, it turns out, did not agree to the arrangement. The moon waxes. The stars wheel. The heavens keep their own time here — the only time there is to keep — and travelers who check the moon against their calendars feel the floor of the world tilt a little beneath them.

It is very quiet. The altitude edits sound down to its essentials: water, mostly — drips echoing in the cisterns, the runnels' night-voice, the ticking of melting frost. Wings. Hoof-scuff. The whistle of the rock-whistlers at first light. At the evening wind-shift, when the cloud-tide tops the rim, the rising air moves the clapper of the bronze bell in the tallest of the three towers — bronze still the color of the hour it was poured — and it gives one note, sometimes two, and the condor that nests on the tower's shoulder shifts and resettles. A shout comes back off the cirque from impossibly far away. Travelers try it once, and not twice.

Small wounds close overnight here. Old travelers say softer and stranger things, and by old custom no one stays past the seventh morning, and no one says why. The people of the valleys below, when they speak of the bench at all, say only that it keeps what it finishes.

At night the reservoir holds the stars and gives back stillness. The herds lie down in the lee of walls that have never weathered. The frost returns to the same stones in the same feathers; the mist returns to the same fold; the runnels fill and whisper. The town stands the way a held breath stands — scaffolded, half-made, tool marks fresh, fire lit, jars full, every doorway open.

It is always the first morning on the bench. It has been the first morning for a very long time.
