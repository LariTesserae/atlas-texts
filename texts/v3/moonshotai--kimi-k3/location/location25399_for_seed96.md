From the rim, in the first hour of light, the valley below looks like a fingerprint — whorls and terraces packed so tightly against one another that the eye can't separate them at distance, only read the whole as texture. It takes a moment to understand what you're seeing. The cirque is high, cupped by peaks still carrying last winter's snow, and everything inside that cup, from the snowline down to the outfall notch, is made. Not terraced around, not nestled into — *made*. Every slope is faced with fitted stone. The floor is pavement. The streambed is a stone throat. Even the snowfield's lower edge ends in a masonry lip, a clean gray line where the built world begins and never, anywhere within the valley, stops.

There is no bedrock here. Or rather, the bedrock was cut and relaid until it stopped being bedrock.

You descend, because there is nothing to do at the rim but look, and the looking raises questions that only the stairs can answer. Everything is stairs. The stair treads are dressed stone, knee-high, and their surfaces are pecked — thousands of overlapping hammer-strikes, each one legible, each one *fresh*, as if the dressing happened last season. Sharp arrises everywhere. No rounding, no patina, no polish of use. Your boots are the loudest thing in the valley.

At the snowline, the intake. A grille of fitted lintels — the Comb — strains the meltwater, holding back ice and gravel, admitting the flow through a row of slots cut with obvious care. Behind it lies a stilling pool, black with reflected sky, and beside it the first of the gauge stones: a pillar set in the water, notched up its length in regular increments. The water stands at the fourth notch today. Someone once cared, very much, exactly where it stood.

Then the Work performs its first act, which is division. The stilling pool empties onto a single carved block where the channel splits into three, and each of those into three again — the cuts proportioned so precisely that the water apportions itself without argument, the way a careful mind divides an inheritance. From there the channels multiply and take on separate lives. Some run open at ankle height, napped in shadow. Some dive into the pavement and travel dark. Some climb onto stone bridglets and cross overhead. At one junction you can stand with your arms out and touch three waters moving at three different heights, none touching, none wasted. The valley is packed in every dimension it has: basins shoulder to shoulder across the terraces, channels stacked above channels, cisterns hollowed beneath the floors you walk on. No ledge is idle. No interval is left unshaped.

The main descent is a water stair — several hundred weirs, each a hand-span drop, each lip cut true. The flow folds over them without splashing; the receiving pools are deep-lipped and swallow the fall. The sound is a whisper you feel more than hear. Beside it runs the dry stair, and you climb down counting treads and lose count somewhere past two hundred, your breath short in the thin air, the sun warm on your shoulders and the shade of every wall instantly cold.

The terraces between are fields of basins. The settling ponds are the strangest: their floors carry fine gray sediment laid down in laminae so regular they look ruled — one skin of rock flour per season, the valley writing its own archive in silt, a record no one is left to read. The mirror ponds are stilled behind lips that bleed off every ripple, and they hold the sky so perfectly that the water becomes a lie. It is snowmelt-cold and utterly clear — no algae, no film, no life of any kind — and so the surfaces are invisible, betrayed only when a breath of wind touches them. You learn to test each step. When you put your hand in, the cold is a clean pain.

Beneath the pavement, the cisterns. Stone caps with hand-holes sit flush in the flags; lift one and cold air breathes up at you, smelling of wet cut stone and nothing else — no mold, no decay, nothing organic at all. The vaults below are corbelled, ring on ring of stone closing over the dark, and down there the Work makes its quiet music: single drips striking still water at long intervals, each vault tuned differently by accident or intent. In the deepest cistern a slot in the shaft admits a blade of direct sun for a few minutes on the right days — the only light that has ever reached that floor, arriving on schedule, patient as everything else here. In another vault a drip falls every few heartbeats onto a growing pillar of pale stone. The pillar rises perhaps a hair in a year. In some unhurried millennium it will meet the drop, the circuit will close, and that one small sound will stop. The Work has planned, in its mineral way, for its own silence.

Partway down you find the dressing floor, and this is where the place stops being merely strange. A block lies half-pecked, the pattern of its finishing interrupted mid-surface. A hammerstone rests on it — *rests*, placed flat, the way a person sets a tool down meaning to pick it up again. In a wet seam nearby a chisel of simple bronze has crusted green where it was dropped. Wooden wedges stand in a split block, swollen with water, still doing their work. The wood is sound. Nothing has rotted, because nothing here rots things: there are no fungi, no beetles, no bacteria working the grain. The cold and the sterility keep everything. It is not a ruin. It is an interruption.

And there is nothing else of them. No hearth, no charcoal, no graves, no dwellings — the valley is packed end to end and yet contains not one room meant for living in. Only, near the rim, a few low windbreaks where watchers might have crouched, and beside them a slab covered edge to edge in grouped tally marks, thousands of them, the last row stopping halfway across. Whatever was being counted — the melt, the years, the slow retreat of the snow above the Comb — the counting ended mid-entry, and the instrument outlived its readers.

The absence of life is total and it changes everything you thought you knew about water and stone. No moss in the joints. No lichen freckling the faces. No birds at dawn, no midges dimpling the ponds, no marmots whistling from the walls. The rain that builds in white towers most afternoons and breaks briefly, cold, over the cirque, falls on a world it cannot seed. The Work will not be colonized. Its sharpness is not young despite the weather; it is young because there is nothing alive to soften it, and time here runs on geology alone.

By midday the light becomes the valley's second occupant. The air up here is thin and offers no resistance; the sun strikes the wet stone and the stone throws it back, black where saturated, near-white where dry, mica flashing in the fit of the joints. Every pond holds a piece of sky. Under the channel-bridges, reflected light weaves restless nets on the stone. And at dusk the valley performs its quietest trick: the air above it darkens first, while the hundreds of still basins go on holding the sky, so that for a few minutes the ground is brighter than the heavens — the Work keeping light the way it keeps water, a little past its time.

Nothing here hurries. A drop of meltwater that enters the Comb takes the better part of four days to cross the valley, resting in stilling pools, waiting in cisterns, edging through the long laminar whisper of the stairs. The quick events are few: the afternoon rain, the ticking of the night freeze working its fingers into the joints, once in a cold snap a deep boom from a vault below, like a struck drum, once, then silence again. Against the frost — the valley's only vandal — stands a slow mason: the water itself is mineral-heavy, and wherever it slips its intended edges it lays down pale stone, extending spouts a hair a year, sealing joints, healing cracks. The accretions follow the lines of the design so faithfully that it is hard, looking at them, to remember they are only chemistry. The Work is held between two patients — ice that unmakes it, stone that regrows it — and for now the building is ahead.

At the bottom, the channels are gathered again, the Divider run in reverse, and the whole valley's water is given to a last stone groove at the outfall notch, where it falls free for the first time — the one loud thing in the place, its voice spent entirely outside itself. Standing there, looking back up at the packed terraces climbing into the bright thin air, you understand that you have not walked through a city, or a temple, or a machine exactly. You have walked through a measurement still being taken. The gauges stand in their pools. The varves thicken by one skin a season. The tally waits, mid-row, for a hand that set down its hammerstone and meant to come back.

Below, in the dark of the deepest vault: a drip. A drip. The pillar rising to meet it.
