Two days below the rim, while the trail is still switching back through hot scrub and the air smells of dust and crushed thyme, the traveler first mistakes the sound for an illness — a pressure behind the ears, a faint organ note that will not resolve. By the second day it has harmonics. By the time the path crests the rim and drops into the high basin the locals call the Bowl, the note has become the size of weather, and it is obvious that it has been going on for a very long time and intends to continue.

The Bowl is an old volcanic basin, warm and high, cupped by a rim of welded tuff gone soft as old brick. It is shaped like a trap, and what it traps is cloud. Every afternoon the warm air climbing from the lowlands spills over the rim, condenses against the cooler heights, and fills the basin with a slow, bright fog that moves through the streets like a tide that has forgotten to leave. Rain comes most evenings, warm as bathwater. Nothing here ever freezes. Nothing here is ever quite dry.

This is why the town exists. Its name is Rocío — dew — and it is not so much a town as a machine for drinking the sky, with a town attached.

The works spread across the whole basin, loosely, with great tracts of wet gravel and shallow ditch between one structure and the next — a city scaled for tens of thousands, inhabited by a few hundred. The largest structures are the Harps: fields of tall screens, hundreds of meters of tensioned mesh and filament, set in slow-turning frames that face the wind the way sunflowers face the sun. The fog combs itself out on them. Water beads on the filaments, gathers, and runs off in ropes, into gutters, into flumes, into the system. The Harps are the strangest things in Rocío and they are entirely silent. Stand under one in the afternoon fog and you hear nothing but the roar of everything else; the harvest itself makes no sound at all. The screens drip. That is all. Acres of cathedral-sized instrument, and the music comes from elsewhere.

The music comes from the water once it is caught. The flumes of Rocío are its streets — raised concrete channels, green with algae, white with lime, carrying the condensate down through the basin at walking pace. At the low point of the Bowl stands the Drop Hall, where the flumes arrive and the water falls through penstocks into the great turbines, and the turbines turn the dynamos, and the dynamos send current back out along catwalks of copper gone the color of old bronze — current to run the pumps, the fans, the slow drives that turn the Harps into the wind. The town runs on the difference between cloud and valley floor. It is a near-closed loop, and the surplus — water and power both — goes over the rim.

Over the rim: that is the direction everything tends and no one goes. On the basin's south edge the flumes step down out of sight in a cascade of switchbacks, descending into the cloud band, and beside them runs the cableway — the Basket, they call it — a wire line that comes up out of the white three times a week, dripping, carrying salt, dried beans, cloth, machine parts, letters. The contracts governing this exchange are centuries old and written in a hand no one uses anymore. Notes sent down are answered, eventually, in different hands. Whether the lowlands are one town or many, thriving or ruinous, no one in Rocío can say with confidence. The water goes down. The Basket comes up. It is enough. It has always been enough, slowly.

Now, the sound. It must be described in layers, the way the tenders learn it. At the bottom is the fundamental: the Drop Hall turbines, a note so low it is felt in the sternum and the soles of the feet, the note the traveler heard two days out. Above that, the penstocks — a chord of rushing, pitched differently in each pipe, tuned at the founding so the notes reinforce rather than beat against one another. Above that, the fans and condensers, a broad hiss like a held breath. Threaded through it all, the flumes: the bright roar of moving water, everywhere, because the flumes are everywhere. And beneath and between the engineered layers, the groans of the structures themselves — metal expanding in the warm afternoons, concrete settling, the Harps' cables singing faintly when the wind gusts, each cable a different pitch, so that in a blow the whole basin strums.

The founding engineers tuned the town. This is the fact that organizes everything else. Rocío was designed as a chord, a single standing consonance the size of a watershed, and for two hundred years the tenders have kept it in tune — adjusting penstock gates by handspan and by ear, re-tensioning harp cables, retiring a fan whose bearings have gone sharp. Every engine-house keeps its own family of tenders, hereditary mostly, and each house is responsible for its note in the chord. The children learn the tuning before they learn letters. In a dry vault near the Drop Hall, in the only quiet room in the basin — the Dry Room, felt-lined, where a visitor hears, appallingly, their own blood — the founding Standard is kept: a rank of bronze tuning plates, struck once a decade, against which the whole town is checked.

Because the chord is sinking. This is the slow thing, the patient thing, the process on which the whole place turns. The turbines wear. The water table under the basin settles. The fog belt itself, the tenders believe, is descending the mountain by some small distance each generation. Whatever the causes — and they are debated in the slow, intermittent way of people who measure their arguments in decades — the result is measured precisely: Rocío sinks through its registers. A semitone in a lifetime, roughly. When a house can no longer hold its note, the whole town is retuned downward together, a ceremony that takes most of a year. The old tuning plates in the Dry Room no longer match the town they govern; they are kept as a record of a higher, younger Rocío, and the eldest tenders speak — with an emotion no outsider can quite identify, grief or hope or both — of the Last Semitone: the day the fundamental falls out of human hearing altogether, and the loudest town in the world becomes, by its own definition, silent.

To live in it meanwhile, the townspeople have adapted in ways that read as strange anywhere else. Speech happens in a narrow band, a high flute-like register that slots into a gap in the machine spectrum — everyone in Rocío speaks slightly above their natural voice, all the time, and a conversation across a flume looks like two people calling to one another on a mountainside. In the Drop Hall no one speaks at all; the tenders there work in sign and touch, and their earplugs are wax and wool, replaced daily, and even so they go gently deaf by forty and consider it a kind of retirement. When an engine is stopped for its long maintenance — each house rests once in a generation, for a season — the partial chord that remains is, everyone agrees, far worse than the full one. The town goes thin and wrong. People sleep badly, weep without cause, and the eels rise in the cisterns, disturbed by the change in the pressure of the world.

The eels are one of the few wild things. The basin is nearly empty of life, and what it holds is specialized past recognition. The cistern eels are pale, slow, thick as a wrist, spawning once a decade in the warm sumps; the tenders count them as colleagues. A small dark swift nests in the flume spillways, behind the curtains of falling water, in the loudest real estate in the world — safe there from everything that hunts by ear. Midges dance at dusk in the fog. That is close to the whole census. Vegetation is nearly as spare: the soil is thin over the tuff, waterlogged and metal-salted, and what grows is moss in orange and black, a wiry sedge along the ditches, lichen on the windward face of everything. And the flume trees — a single species, a small crooked water-loving tree planted along the channels at the founding, now self-sown in long green lines that map the water system from any height. The trees are the town's map of itself, drawn in the only color that isn't gray, white, or copper-green.

White: the lime. This is the other slow thing, and the one that will outlast the chord. The works leak — everything here leaks, has always leaked — and the water is rich, and where it leaks it deposits. Every flume wears a white apron of travertine. Stalactites of lime hang in doorways and under catwalks. Down at the basin's edge, a decommissioned engine-house has been flooding for sixty years and has become a grotto: terraces of white stone cascading through the broken roof, and somewhere inside, the tenders say, the old turbine, entombed, accreting, turning slowly into a pearl the size of a house. Rocío is precipitating itself. The built and the weathered stopped being distinguishable generations ago; the town is not decaying, exactly, nor being maintained, exactly. It is fossilizing in place, at the rate of a fingernail's growth per year, and the tenders regard this with the same calm they bring to everything — it is simply another of the long cycles, like the retuning, like the eels, like the Basket.

The light, it should be said, is the most ordinary thing in the basin. No eternal gloom, no eerie glow — plain daylight, pearl-gray through the fog, bright and glaring off wet concrete when the sun burns through, a brief gold hour most evenings when the rim catches the low sun and the whole wet city shines like something just washed. Rainbows are unremarkable. They happen indoors, in the Drop Hall, when a sun shaft crosses the mist above the turbines; the tenders glance up and glance away.

At dusk the fog comes in earnest, the Harps turn their faces to the wind with a sound like a sheet being shaken out, the warm rain starts, and the chord goes on — the same chord as yesterday, a hair flatter than last year, a semitone and a half below the plates in the Dry Room. The flume trees drip. A tender somewhere opens a gate by a handspan, listens, closes it by half a handspan. The water goes over the rim into the white, and the Basket will come up on Thursday, as it has for two hundred years, out of whatever the lowlands have become.
