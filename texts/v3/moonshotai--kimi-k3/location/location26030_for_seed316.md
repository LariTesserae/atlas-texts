The sun has been setting for a long time. It hangs at the edge of the sky, enormous and dark red, dim enough to look at directly, warm as a held breath, and it has been in roughly the same place for longer than anything alive can remember. The light it gives is the color of old bruises, of wine held to a lamp. It never brightens. It never quite darkens. Under it, the world lies flat — worn flat, ground flat, a continent eroded to its own floor, where the horizon is so level it looks drawn with a straightedge and the tallest thing in most provinces is the rain.

There is no one left to name this place, so it keeps the name it makes. The sound. Call it the Seethe.

The water table is not beneath the ground here. It *is* the ground. What passes for land is a warm black colloid — silt ground so fine across so many ages that it has stopped deciding whether it is liquid or solid — and it is saturated to its skin, sweating, weeping, drowning in its own substance. The rain helps, if that's the word. There are several kinds. There is the ordinary rain, warm as the air it falls through. There is the sideways rain that rides out of the storm provinces and travels for weeks. There is, in certain districts, a rain that condenses directly out of the air onto the undersides of things, falling upward in slow beads. And there is the sweat — the atmosphere simply giving up, moisture appearing on every surface at once, as if the world had been startled. Dryness is a local and temporary rumor.

And the plain is busy. That is the thing that takes the longest to understand, because at first the eye reads the surface as empty — no trees, no grass, nothing a traveler would call a landscape feature. But look down, look close, look for one full minute, and the ground is *working*. Domes swell out of the slurry, skins of wet horn, each one budding smaller domes that bud smaller still, and the whole mass reaches toward some shape — almost a fern, almost a hand, almost a colonnade, once, memorably, almost a face — and then exhausts whatever impulse drove it and slumps back into slurry, and the slurry is taken up by the next generation of buds, which are already rising shoulder to shoulder with the last. There is no bare ground anywhere. Every square meter is occupied, not by things but by *events*, and the events crowd and overlap and grow over one another in a permanent scrum. This is the density of the place: not biomass, not bodies, but process, packed wall to wall, nothing allowed to finish.

The sound of it never stops. Each dome bursts with a note like a struck drum, and across the plain there are thousands of such notes every minute, merging into a single roar that behaves like surf if surf had no shore and no lull. Beneath the drumming runs the ground-tone, a planetary hum too low to hear with ears; you feel it in your teeth, in the long bones, in the water in your own body. Above the drumming, the rain hisses in its several voices. And out on the horizon, the standing storms add their thunder — the great wandering cyclones, centuries old each, each with its own temperament, its own itinerary, its own grudge. The cumulative effect is not merely loud. It erodes. Thought becomes difficult to hold; the self becomes difficult to hold. Travelers — and there are almost no travelers — learn to think in the spaces *between* the roar's surges, the way one learns to cross a street between carts.

There are quiet holes. They are rare and they are wrong. In the lee of certain ruins, in the brief stillness pressed into the earth by a walker's footfall, the roar drops away — and what replaces it is not silence but *other* sound, sound that does not belong to this moment. A storm from a thousand years ago, intact, released from the sediment that preserved it. A voice, perhaps. The plain keeps what happens to it. That may be the central fact of the place: it keeps everything, and gives it back later, changed.

The Seethe remembers in other ways too. A wave in the slurry will repeat its exact shape three times, like a phrase practiced. An echo occasionally arrives *before* the shout. At one particular location — there is no reason for it to be there, no stone, no mark — the plain builds a colonnade every day at the same dim hour, eleven arches of wet horn, and each day's colonnade is a little more resolved than the last, a little more certain of itself. It has been doing this for as long as anyone has watched. Whether it is remembering a real colonnade or rehearsing one that has not been built yet, there is no way to know, and the distinction may not mean anything here. Distance, likewise, is a custom rather than a law. The causeway is longer on the return journey. A walker's silhouette on the horizon may take three days to walk toward and remain precisely as far away, and on the fourth day be behind you. There are places where a second horizon appears beyond the first, a second ruled line, and things seen past that line behave by rules the world has otherwise forgotten. Reflections in standing water lag by a breath, and occasionally the water keeps your reflection when you leave, and walks it elsewhere.

The world is old enough that even its physics has worn soft at the edges. It is not coming apart. It is *settling*, the way very old houses settle, and the settling never ends.

The vegetation that survives is the vegetation of refuges. The most widespread plant is the float-mat — black-green, rubbery, leafless, a species that gave up on roots in favor of drift, riding the churn in rafts the size of fields, hosting nothing, feeding on the slurry directly, indifferent. On the rare swells of bedrock — and bedrock almost never shows itself now; the world's last hill is a rise of perhaps four meters — grow the glass reeds, hollow, brittle, black, and when the roar strikes them at the right angle they sing, one low organ note added to the world's chord. And sometimes the churn turns up an ancient seed from the deep sediment, some species from a harder era, and it germinates in an hour, flowers, fruits, and is digested by the unending dusk — an hour-tree, a whole life lived inside a single surge of the plain's metabolism.

The rest of the world's flora is on the walkers.

There are perhaps a few dozen walkers left, worldwide. Each is known by its silhouette. They are immense and immensely slow — gray, plated, their hides cracked like old leather with silt in the cracks, their many-jointed legs worn into a strange simplicity, as if age had eroded their anatomy the way it eroded the mountains. Their eyes are dim and red, like the sun. They wade the shallows of the Seethe, filtering the froth, and the plain cannot digest them because they are slower than its appetite — slowness is the only citizenship the world recognizes — and on their backs, in the accumulated silt of centuries, grow forests. Real trees, rooted, patient, the last true forests in existence, carried at a walking pace through the rain. A walker's footfall stills the churn for a few minutes, pressing a quiet hole into the world, and behind each walker stretches a trail of footprints, and very occasionally — this is luck, this is an event — a footprint will contain a gardener: a palm-sized translucent creature, many-legged, that lives its entire life inside that single print, tending the crushed buds into a brief, doomed order. It is the only agriculture. Nobody planted it.

The ruins are not what they seem, or rather they are exactly what they seem and the seeming is strange. Scattered across the provinces, rare and partial: a causeway of dark glassy blocks running from nowhere to nowhere, the only dry walking in the world; staircases descending into open water with no final step; single walls standing in the marsh, visibly settling, tilting at rates you can watch across a week; rings of foundation stones worn to anklets. The stone is denser than anything the plain makes now, and the plain cannot bud around it, only pile against it like water against a pier. Here is the thing: you will find the same wall in different provinces. The *same* wall. Either the plain has learned the shape and re-expresses it, or the wall is one wall and distance is lying, or — the reading that fits best, that the oldest walker seems to confirm by carrying a ring of foundation stones fused into the silt forest of its back like an inherited barnacle — the ruins were never built by anyone. They are the bones of the world, from when the world was young enough to make bone. Structures it precipitated in its hard youth, before erosion softened it to slurry and froth. It is not surrounded by the works of some vanished maker. It is sinking into its own skeleton.

No devices. No tools. Nothing made of anything but stone-set-on-stone, which may be the oldest technology of all, or not technology at all — just the way the world used to hold itself up.

The rules here are few and they are not negotiable. Everything is wet. Nothing finishes; forms are events, and permanence is a style of motion too slow to see. Loud places bud faster — sound shapes matter here, the roar is not a byproduct of the churn but one of its organs. Slowness survives; speed is digested. And nothing leaves. That is what the erosion has really meant, across all this time: not loss, but accumulation. The mountains are still here, ground into the silt underfoot. The old species are here, sleeping as seeds in the sediment, waking for their hour. Every storm that ever blew is here, preserved in bubbles, waiting to be released. The world is not dying. The world is *full* — full to its skin, packed with its own history, everything it has ever been still present, still happening, all at once, at the top of its voice.

Stand on the causeway at the unending hour of sunset. The warm rain falls, some of it upward. The drumming of the buds rolls out to the doubled horizon, where a walker carries its forest through provinces it has not seen in centuries and will not see again. Near the block under your foot, the slurry swells, rehearses an arch, thinks better of it, begins again. Somewhere a bubble surfaces and pops, and for a moment, through the roar, you hear the sound of a storm that ended before the sun swelled — and then, a breath later, the echo of your next footstep arrives, politely, a little early, so you'll know where to put it.
