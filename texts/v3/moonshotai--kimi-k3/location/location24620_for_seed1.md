You hear the Coldworks long before you stand in it.

The sound arrives first as pressure, then as rhythm — a beat too slow for a heart and too regular for weather, rising out of the low country ahead as though the ground itself were doing something on purpose. By the time the fog thins enough to show you the basin, the beat has stopped coming in through your ears and started arriving through your sternum, your teeth, the small bones of your feet. This is the first law of the place, and you learn it before you learn its shape: here, sound has depth. It pools. It lies on low ground the way water lies on low ground everywhere else, and the basin — being the lowest ground for a day's walk in any direction — is full of it. On the slope down you wade into the noise the way you'd wade into a lake. Ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then to the chest. Behind the broad swells of worn rock there are lees of hush, calm pockets where the pressure lets up, and you find yourself steering for them the way a swimmer steers for still water.

What little light there is comes up from below.

The sky gives almost nothing. A low lid of fog — the Ceiling — hangs grey and pearled and nearly motionless a few hundred feet overhead, and it is dark. The light comes out of the ground: a dim verdigris seep, the color of old copper in shade, rising from the black film that felts every stone and hollow. So the shadows fall upward. Yours stands on the Ceiling as you walk, a long grey figure gliding among the other long grey figures up there, and your eyes keep insisting the world is inverted, that you are walking on the lit underside of something, that up is down. Your inner ear votes to leave. Your feet learn otherwise.

The basin is wide enough that its far side is not a sight but a rumor. It was old before the word for old had anywhere to be used. Whatever mountains stood here were ground down so long ago that their dust has forgotten mountains; the swells that remain are the ghost of terrain, a landscape worn so far past its features that it came out the far side into something else. Because here is the thing no one will believe until they see it: the wearing went all the way through roughness and into precision. The channels that braid the floor carry slurries of brine and stone, and the stones are spheres. Not rounded stones — spheres, sorted by diameter with a fidelity that looks machined, lying in long graded runs like stock on a factory floor. There are fields of them, acres of identical iron-dark ball bearings, and when the wind of the place moves across the fields they tick and settle, tick and settle, a sound like an abacus the size of a valley. The channels themselves run in loops. They carry their cargo downhill at walking pace and deliver it, eventually, back to where it began, and the stones go around for millennia, getting rounder. Nothing here was built. There is no wall, no tool, no ruin, no mark of a hand anywhere in the basin. And everything here is manufactured.

The water is brine, and it is cold past the edge of freezing.

It stays liquid through salt and pressure and sheer unremitting motion, a chemistry of almost-ice, and it is everywhere — seeping, standing, running, condensing. The saturated air beads it onto every surface; the rocks wear it like wet fur, rime and sheen at once, the impossible texture of a cold that is somehow soaked. The weepstones are the worst and the strangest: broad outcrops that leak brine in slow tears down their faces, leaving beards of salt that glitter faintly in the ground-light. The weeping worsens when the water table falls, and the water table rises and falls on a period of days — the whole marsh breathing, the slowest stroke of all the strokes. At low breath the fens exhale their moisture through the stones. At high breath the flats drown shin-deep in liquid that burns like fire and will not freeze until you touch it. That is the other thing about the still pools: they are supercooled, holding liquid by tension alone, one excuse away from ice. Drag a finger through one and it snaps solid around your skin with a sizzle and a crack, flash-ice racing outward in fern patterns, and the sound of it carries for a long way in the pooled noise.

The beat has sources. Ten thousand of them.

They stand in rows and clusters across the fens: pressure vents, each one a cylinder of worn stone throat, each one cycling — a slow intake, a driven stroke of brine and vapor, a rest — with the patient rhythm of pistons. No two are in phase. The compounded overlapping of ten thousand strokes is the beat, and the beat is the floor of all the sound in the world. Walk among the vent rows and you can feel individual strokes lean against you as they fire. The throats are the only warm places in the basin, and so they are occupied — everything warm here is occupied — by creatures clamped over the openings like living caps, their bodies riding each stroke, swelling on the uptake, venting on the down. Watch one long enough and you will lose the ability to say whether the vent moves the creature or the creature works the vent. That distinction, like most distinctions here, turns out to be a temporary kindness you do for yourself.

The product of all of this — and it is a product; the place is unmistakably making something — is cold.

Near the vent rows the cold is fresh: sharp, clean, newly minted, with a bite that the old cold lacks. Farther out it goes stale, secondhand, worked. Somewhere beneath the basin lie the deep aquifers where the finished cold is stored, a reserve older than the surface, and the whole engine — the pistons, the conveyor channels, the sorting runs, the weeping stones — exists to keep that reserve full. Which is to say: the machine refrigerates itself. It holds its own brine at the brink of ice because that is the temperature its processes require. Its output is the condition of its own continuance. It is the oldest stable state of this region of the world, and the reason it is stable is that it never stops maintaining itself, and the reason it looks like a factory is that a factory is what age becomes when it goes all the way down and keeps going.

There is no day and night. There are shifts.

On a cycle longer than the water's breathing, the beat quickens. The verdigris glow of the ground brightens almost imperceptibly. The slurry channels run faster, the bearings in the fields tick more urgently, and the herds move. You learn to tell time by the stroke the way other places tell it by the sun.

The herds are slicks: wide, soft, many-mouthed animals that crawl the black film underfoot, flank to flank in their thousands, sipping charge. The film — the Felt — is the base of everything. It is not plant; nothing here is plant. It is a fungal-or-simpler quilt, a single felting of electrotrophic life spread over the entire basin floor, eating the chemistry of the brine and banking the difference, and its dim green-copper glow is simply its metabolism leaking upward as light. Where the charge runs thick the Felt blooms — slow green fire spreading under the stone — and the slicks find the blooms the way herds everywhere find pasture, and where they have grazed they leave dark spent strips that take days to recharge. A grazing herd hums. Each slick discharges softly as it feeds, and ten thousand of them together hum like a substation, and when a herd crosses the bearing fields the spheres roll beneath their bodies in long traveling waves, a clicking surf you can hear for miles. You always hear the herds before you see them. Weather announces itself the same way.

The condensers stand above it all, and it rains only beneath them.

They are tall, slow, columnar things — you will spend a long time not knowing whether to call them animals — straining the fog through bodies ribbed like cooling fins, harvesting the saturated air and letting it run off them in streams. Each condenser stands in its own small permanent rain, a circle of darker wet around its feet, and these circles are the wettest places in a wet world, and the slicks cluster at their edges to drink. From below, on the Ceiling, the condensers' upward shadows stand like black towers among the drifting grey shadows of the herds.

And then there are the silences.

Everything in the Coldworks makes noise; noise is the cost of presence. So silence is the tell. The hush-hunters move through the pooled sound the way sharks move through water, and you do not hear them coming — you hear a hole coming, a moving absence, a quiet with teeth, your ears emptying as it nears. The herds know it and sing louder, knocking stones, discharging in cracks, because prey that goes quiet is prey that is found. You will learn to do the same. Singing badly and continuously is, in the Coldworks, a survival skill.

Nowhere, anywhere, is there room between presences. The Felt lies over stone that lies over older stone; the slicks lie over the Felt; the condensers stand over everything; the Ceiling presses down from above; and the air itself is occupied — freighted with mist, with sound, with the mats' motile cells drifting like slow plankton, so that every breath you take is a migration, a small ecology entering and leaving your body. Nothing falls unclaimed. Death here is not an event but a hand-off, a station change: a body goes down and is already being processed by the time it reaches the ground, sorted, routed, taken up into the line. The works do not pause for it. The works do not pause for anything.

These are the rules, gathered: Sound has depth, and pools in low places, and hush gathers in the lee of large things the way calm gathers in a lee everywhere. Light comes from below, and shadows rise. Nothing is only one thing — mineral, machine, and animal are a single gradient here, and any name you give a thing is a loan, not a fact. In a world with no slack in it, every event leans on every other, and effects arrive pressed hard against their causes — sometimes slightly ahead. You will remember a piston's stroke a half-breath before it falls. The memory is how the body braces. And the world ends where the beat fades. There is no visible rim to the Coldworks; the basin simply extends, open and low and full, past anything you could see even in clear air. But walk far enough in any direction and the stroke thins, and the pooled sound shallows, and the light from below dims to nothing, and that acoustic edge — the place where the beat stops — is the only border the Coldworks has ever needed.

Stand still long enough and you will feel it: the cold has noticed you. Warmth is a resource here, and you are a vent that has not yet been assigned a station. The slicks begin, politely, to graze the heat off the ground where you stand. A condenser turns — slowly, with the unhurried certainty of something that has never once been hurried — to include you in its rain. Nothing about it is hostile. Nothing about it is welcome. The works are simply total, and presence is employment, and already your breathing has found the beat, and already your shadow, up there on the Ceiling among the towers and the drifting herds, looks less like something you are casting and more like something the Coldworks has been casting all along — as if the factory, patient and ancient and loud, has one more part now than it did before you arrived, and is running, in its way, slightly smoother.
