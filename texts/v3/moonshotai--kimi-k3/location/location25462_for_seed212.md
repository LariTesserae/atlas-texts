Three days before you see it, you hear it.

A roar beyond the hills, constant as a river that never arrives. Sometimes it reads as a crowd the size of weather; sometimes as wind in a forest that isn't there yet. It does not quiet at night. It changes register at night — lower, wetter, more deliberate — and that is worse.

Then you come up the last raw scarp, into the warm upslope wind, and look down into the basin.

Your first thought is that you've found a city. It is all there: blocks, towers, walls, avenues laid in a rough grid, districts of distinct character, a white cloud parked over the whole of it like smoke that decided to stay. The light is a humid white glare, ordinary daylight fattened by haze. Your second thought arrives slowly, as the details refuse to resolve: nothing down there was built. It is growing. Your third thought never quite finishes arriving: it is growing *now*. You can see it move.

The canopy is stacked five, six, seven tiers deep, and above it the towers rise — a lattice species that grows as open scaffold, woody scaffolding that dies standing and is recolonized while still upright, so that every tower is a ruin and a construction site and a finished building at once. Walls of hedge run in unnervingly straight lines. Avenues cut the blocks, bare hard-packed lanes kept bare by poison exuded from the flanking roots — chemical zoning, allelopathy as urban planning. The streets smell of pepper and crushed stems. Walk one and you feel the roots on either side deciding to tolerate you.

The land itself is young and hasn't learned to be soil. The rim is a fresh scarp, all sharp unweathered faces, glittering where they're split. Inside the basin there is no bare stone at all except wounds — places where the churn has torn the ground and you can see rock that looks surprised. Root-wedging outpaces rain here; the plants break the geology faster than weather can soften it. What passes for soil is made, not found: duff, root-felt, a hundred seasons of the valley compressed under your boots, springy and warm. Nothing mineral shows. Everything you stand on was recently alive or currently is.

There is no water. This is the thing you must keep relearning, because everything looks drenched — the air is a held breath, every surface is swollen and turgid and warm to the touch — and yet there are no streams, no pools, no wells. The river is inside the wood. At dawn the spires and comb-leaves comb the valley's own breath-cloud, harvesting the fog the canopy exhaled all night; the valley drinks its own exhalation, looped and thrifty. Cistern-blooms hold cupped hoards in their axils, guarded by thorn. Put your ear to any trunk and you hear the interior river, drumming, gurgling, straining upward. And threaded through it, everywhere, a fine snap-crackle, like knuckles, like a fire in another room: water columns breaking under tension in a million stems. The sound of thirst. The whole valley runs on almost-not-enough, and the almost is audible.

You learn the few laws in the first days. Nothing here is still. Falling is a way of building. Down is a local opinion. Water is memory. The silence means: pay attention.

The roar, up close, has layers. The bass is trunk-grind — the crowd-pressure of dense wood growing against dense wood, a groaning like pack ice, like a harbor of ships straining their moorings. The middle is a billion stomata exhaling, a hiss like static, like applause heard through a wall. The percussion is pods detonating their seeds, limbs letting go, towers coming down — and they are always coming down somewhere, and where they fall they root, so collapse is just another construction technique. A colonnade is a tree that fell over on purpose. And over it all, the thing you will not be able to explain later: the roar has syntax. It patterns. You keep almost understanding it. That is when you should sleep.

There are no animals. None. No webs between the branches, no gnaw-marks, no burrows, no dung, no wingbeat anywhere in that enormous sound. Nothing eats, and nothing is eaten. Seeds lie where they fall, unhoarded, accumulating in drifts like unclaimed mail. The work animals do elsewhere gets done anyway, by plant flesh pressed into service: stamens that catapult their own pollen, petals that detach and glide — a blizzard of petal-moths on the wind, each one a flower's severed hand running an errand — and fruit that stands up on stilt-rootlets, toddles a few paces off the avenue, and plants itself. Decay is reabsorption; the living drink the dead directly, root into their own fallen, sip. You will often feel watched. You are. The heliotrope blooms track warmth, and a courtyard of flowers will turn, slowly, entirely, to face you.

Time here is zoned like land use. Seasons are districts. You walk an hour from a blossom-quarter into the rust and shed of a fall that began this morning and will be duff by tomorrow, and the map redraws itself nightly, and no avenue is the same length coming back. You stop trusting your shadow, which arrives a breath late. During the growth-flushes you find heavy green fruit hanging where no flower has ever been, and the tree hurrying a blossom out behind it, catching up with its own consequence. Under the deep canopy, distances are negotiable; the interior of the tangle is larger than the basin that holds it. You mention this to no one, because there is no one.

Then the silence.

It comes total and instantaneous — the roar switched off — and it is more deafening than the roar ever was. The cloud above thickens into itself, graying, lowering, and the first drops fall big as fruit. Then the sky lets go, and the valley doubles its voice, triples it, and the avenues reveal their second job: gutters, all of them, the whole gridded city sluicing rain toward the basin floor, where a flash-lake stands within the hour and is colonized before your eyes, green fur creeping across open water while you watch. Everything grows at visible speed, and at visible speed the world goes loose: streets re-grid, shadows unhook from their owners, a path you walked this morning now requires an afternoon. You are briefly, politely lost. The valley is not trying to keep you. It is just not, at that moment, keeping the geometry either.

Afterward, the bloom-tide: every stem flowering at once, spending the windfall in a single riotous week, petal-moths in blizzards, pods going off like hail on a tin roof, the loudest days of the year. Then the long damp decline, the hoarding, the crackle of thirst creeping back into the trunks. The valley waits for its own breath to come back to it. It can wait. It has the loop.

At the center — or at each center, because there seem to be several and they may move — the avenues converge on a plaza: a clearing kept bare by poison, ringed by the oldest colonnades, fallen trunks the width of houses, rooted at both ends, arching. The air there is the stillest and the loudest at once. Standing in it, you finally permit yourself the thought you have been circling since the rim: that the districts are not neighbors but organs, that the streets are for the valley's attention to travel along, that a billion grafts have made one body of the crowd — or a negotiation so old and continuous that it behaves exactly like one.

The roar is its voice. It is not singing. It is counting — stem, seed, debt of water, footfall — an inventory that never pauses and never finishes.

Nothing here will eat you. You are not prey. You are something to grow on: a trellis that walks. Behind you, the avenue you came by is already narrowing, the hedge leaning in with the patience of a few hours, your footprints filling with runners. It does not have a name. It has a sound, and the sound follows you a full day past the rim when you leave, and then lets you go.

It does not drop the count. You have been counted.
