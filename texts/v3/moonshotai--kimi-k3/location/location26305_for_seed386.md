*Nothing here was built. Everything here runs.*

---

**The Stack**

Three days before you reach the place, you can see it working. On the horizon, where the high plateau breaks off, a column of white stands straight up into the blue — too steady for cloud, too slow for smoke. It does not drift. It does not lean with the wind. It stands all day and glows faintly at dusk and is there again the next morning, and the morning after that. This is the exhaust of the Kettle: the plume of a lake that has been evaporating into the same piece of sky for longer than any memory carried down the valleys.

Walk toward it and the land climbs, dries, cools. The plateau's rim is a cold grassland of tussock and giant rosette plants — silver lobelias tall as a person, groundsel with trunks wrapped in their own dead leaves like lagging on a pipe. Cloud comes up out of the canyon all afternoon and the rim wrings it dry. Every moss clump beads over; every rosette holds a cup of water at its heart. This is where the water clock starts. This is the condenser.

Then you come to the edge, and the world simply stops being there.

**The Descent**

The Kettle is a branching system of gorges cut four thousand meters into the plateau's flank — rim at 3,800 meters, floor at two hundred *below* the level of the sea. Down is the new south. Heat here is a depth, not a latitude: every hundred meters of descent trades you one climate for the next, five of them stacked like floors in a mill, and the animals commute between them the way workers change halls.

The walls are old. Nothing about them is fresh or jagged; the canyon stopped being an accident long ago. Tiered strata step down in broad benches — columnar basalt, the only right angles in the whole valley, cooling's own geometry, organ pipes stacked and broken and colonized. The falls that pour over the bench lips have rounded their edges over millennia. Old landslides sit under mature forest. Talus slopes have hardened into gardens. The river below carries away almost exactly what the walls shed, neither more nor less. The ledger balances.

Below a certain contour — you cross it in an hour, and you feel it on your skin before you see it — the air reaches its dew point and stays there. This is the Weep. From here down, nothing is dry. The walls are glazed. Water stands in beads on every leaf, threads down every column of basalt, drips from every overhang on long, slow intervals, ticking in many rooms at once. Ferns hang in curtains. Clubmoss trails like wet rope. And the density begins: nothing grows alone here, everything grows *on* something — orchids on figs, figs on ledges, ferns on the orchids, moss on the ferns, film of algae on the moss. Epiphyte mats thicker than the branch that holds them. Lianas as thick as a leg, three of them twisted into one rope, ascending into mist. Bare rock is a temporary condition in the Kettle.

**The Works**

One side canyon — the locals would have a name for it, if there were locals — is given over entirely to hot water. Springs come out of the limestone deep in the wall at eighty degrees and descend in stairs: travertine dams that have grown over their own waterfalls, pools tiered below pools like a line of vats. Each step cools the water one stage, and each stage has its own chemistry and its own color. Rust-red at the hottest heads, where the iron-oxiders work. Then emerald. Then chalk white. Then amber, where the sulfur comes down. The water changes hands all the way down the staircase, and every hand takes something out of it.

The dams accrete as fast as they crack. They have been repairing themselves since before the plateau finished rising. Gas vents in the rock pulse on a slow rhythm — a breath held, a breath released — and the boiling pots at the stair's foot wear lids of foam that bulge and settle, bulge and settle. It is the loudest place in the Kettle, which is to say: you can hear it from across the canyon, a low working sound, more felt than heard.

**The Floor**

At the bottom the air is a hot bath. Afternoons run past forty-five degrees in the shade, and the shade is everywhere, and it doesn't matter, because the shade is full of warm water. You do not sweat on the floor of the Kettle. You condense.

The river comes down from the falls wide, green, deep, and slow, warm as blood, steaming at dawn. It braids over bars of black glass sand — old obsidian flows worn to crockery — and slides past a jungle that crowds the banks in layers: giant aroid leaves like oiled tarpaulins, figs that sweat from every leaf tip, palms along the slower reaches, cane in the light gaps. Where thermal creeks run in, the heat has edges and life reads them precisely: bare mineral at the source, then algal paint, then a band of ferns that tolerate what nothing else will, then the full green, all within a body's length.

The river ends in the Broth — a terminal lake, kilometers across, that never overflows. What comes in, the sun and the geothermal floor lift out as vapor. What enters the Broth becomes weather. Its shores are rimmed in salt and travertine; its shallows steam; the plume that stands over it is the Stack you saw from three days out. Crocodiles keep to the cool inflows. In the hot shallows, herons stalk on thick-scaled legs, unhurried, fishing pools that would scald anything else. The backwaters tingle if you trail a hand in them. The river is wired — electric fish work the warm, slow margins, and the jungle knows it, and drinks from the shallows with a certain care.

**The Schedule**

The valley runs on one shift per day, and it is never late.

Before dawn, cold air drains off the plateau and the canyon fills with mist to the brim — a white sea with the crags standing in it like islands, like hulls. Sunrise gilds the upper walls while the floor sleeps under the fill. Mid-morning, the mist begins to stir and rise in columns; hawks find the columns first.

Then, near midday, the Lighting: for about two hours the sun stands over the slot and reaches the floor. Shafts swing through the rising vapor like lamps being carried across a hall. The floor heats hard. The convection strengthens. And for those two hours — only those two — the cicadas run, a low drone in a register you feel in your sternum more than hear, and then they clock off.

By mid-afternoon the cumulus has built over the rim and the storm shift punches in — within the same hour every day, as if scheduled. Warm rain, brief and tremendous. Lightning walks the rim. The walls run with a thousand temporary rivers. Even the thunder arrives flattened, rolled between the walls until it's felt in the ribs more than heard. In the wind before the rain, the slot canyons sound their note — a hum too low for the ear, played on the stone flutes of the side gorges. The building's ventilation.

By evening the rain stops and the hot rock steams. Everything drips. By full dark the basalt benches begin to tick as they cool — contraction, stone settling into its night shape, a slow applause with minutes between the notes. The mist seeps back in. The Kettle idles.

**The Crews**

Nothing is idle, though. The shifts change at dusk like a factory floor.

The cliff swifts come out from their nests behind the waterfalls — through the water, dry to wet to dry, twice a day — and take the evening hatch: mayflies rising off the warm river in columns like smoke from green chimneys. An hour later the fruit bats come up from the floor in the up-shift, commuting four hundred vertical meters to the figs, which fruit on staggered schedules all year so that the canteen never closes. Bench goats — small, slate-grey, rubber-hooved — descend the basalt during the Lighting to lick the mineral seeps, and the cat that hunts them knows the schedule too, and keeps it. The vultures ride the Stack's thermal by day and find whatever the heat has finished; sanitation is quick at these temperatures, and thorough.

This is the strange thing about the Kettle, the thing you would call uncanny if the word weren't too strong: the order of it. No hand has cut a step anywhere. There is no wall, no channel, no tool, no ash of any fire. And yet the water is captured, staged, processed, and exported on a schedule; the heat is generated, circulated, and vented; the freight moves downriver and the vapor moves up and the books balance at the end of every day. It is a works with no workers, a mill with no miller. The weirdness is not that anything impossible happens here. The weirdness is that everything happens *on time*.

**The Rules**

The Kettle insists on a few things.

Heat is below. Cold is above. Everything migrates accordingly.

Water never leaves; it only changes shifts — cloud to moss to drip to falls to river to lake to cloud.

Below the Weep, everything is wet, and everything that is wet is occupied.

The day has a schedule. The valley keeps it. The storm will arrive within its hour; the hatch will rise within its hour; the stone will tick when it cools.

And what reaches the Broth does not come back, except as weather.

---

At night the sky is a river — a slot of stars overhead between the walls, moving as the hours turn. The mist refills the canyon from below, silent, rising past the benches, past the gardens, past the sleeping swifts behind their falls. The lake steams under the moon. Somewhere a dripfrog calls once, and is answered a long time later. The hum of the slots has stopped; the vents in the Works breathe on alone. The plume still stands over the water, faint in the dark, holding its shape against the stars.

The Kettle idles. The night shift has the watch. Nothing here was built — and nothing here ever stops running.
