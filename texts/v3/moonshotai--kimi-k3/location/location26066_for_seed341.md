You hear the Kettle before you see it. The dry country up here is all cracked clay and thorn scrub, shimmering at a heat that warps the horizon — and then, underneath the shimmer, a note. Not a noise exactly. A chord, low and many-layered, felt first in the soles of the feet and the fillings of the teeth. Follow it and the ground simply ends: a rim of pale travertine, crumbling at the lip, and below it a hole in the world perhaps four hundred paces across. You can walk the whole rim in under an hour. Nothing here is far from anything else. It is a room.

The first rule of the room announces itself overhead. Birds crossing the upland shear off at the rim, banking hard as if they'd struck glass. Pack mules plant their feet and will not be led closer. Nothing with a heartbeat lives down there — this is what the water company men tell you, matter-of-factly, the way they'd tell you a bridge is out. They have tried, over the decades: chickens, cats, a pair of goats, once an aviary as a wager. The chickens stopped eating. The aviary birds dashed themselves against the bars before noon. Nobody agrees on the cause. Some blame the Note — that perpetual engine-chord rising from the sump — and some blame the water, which leaves a brassy taste at the back of the tongue, and some say the two are the same thing. The keepers themselves are the standing exception, and even they will admit, if pressed, that after a year below you dream in rhythm.

The descent is by an iron cage-hoist that rattles down the wall on a cable, past spring-lines where the rock weeps in long threads, past ledges furred with growth that thickens as you drop — dry ferns near the top, then mosses in wet pelts, then great broad leaves the size of table tops, glistening, overlapping, drinking the seep. The temperature climbs as the light fails. By the time the cage jolts to its rest you are in a hot green-brown dusk, wet through in minutes, sweat finding no purchase in air already saturated. The sun enters this place only as a guest: for a short while around midday a single column of light leans over the rim and stands in the town like a pillar, visible, almost solid, moving slowly across the water and the roofs. The keepers call it the Beam and set their day by it. The rest of the hours belong to the lamps.

They are always lit — thousands of them, filament bulbs in wire cages strung along the boardwalks, amber globes on the bridge posts, and, in the older lanes, lantern-moss: a cultivated growth trained along wall-slats and trellises that gives off a faint greenish luminescence, dim as a dying coal but steady, needing only the wet. The whole town doubles itself in water. There is water everywhere to double in. The water table is the ground here; the sump at the basin's heart is less a pond than a pause in a flooded floor. Buildings stand on piles and stone plinths above tea-brown channels. The boardwalks between them run on tarred trestles, and between the buildings — this is the strangest thing for a town — there is mostly nothing. Wide dark water. Mud bars. Stands of the green. The Kettle was built for more than it holds, and the empty intervals have been kept, the way a chord needs its gaps. Perhaps thirty structures remain: the pumphouse, the press-sheds, the coal drops, a row of cottages of which only five show lamplight, a hall with its shutters nailed open, a dynamo house, a chapel converted to a valve house. You could stand on the boardwalk at its center and count every roof.

The pumphouse is the heart and the loudest thing in the world. It is built like a cathedral by men who had only brick, iron, and faith in boilers: a long nave of a building with tall round-topped windows of clouded glass that glow amber at every hour, and inside, ranked down its length, the steam engines — great walking-beam monsters, flywheels taller than a man, polished by a century of oil and handling to the soft sheen of old saddles. They drive the rising mains: iron ladders of pipe that climb the wall to the rim cisterns, where the Kettle's one export begins its journey out across the dry country in aqueducts. That is the town's whole reason for being. The dry land sends coal down the incline; the Kettle sends water up the pipes. A fair trade, struck long ago, still honored.

Everything here is worn and nothing is ruined. This took some time to understand. The rust on the railings has sealed itself into a hard brown skin that no longer flakes; the brick has gone soft-edged but no softer, year on year; the canal coping is rounded like river stone, and the moss in the mortar joints is, by now, frankly load-bearing. Everything that was going to fall fell generations ago and was absorbed — you can see a collapsed boiler house at the north end, its walls long since dissolved into a green hummock that children of the keepers use as a slide. The travertine terraces at the wall bases grow a thumbnail's thickness a year even as the warm rain dissolves them from above. Deposition and dissolution, growth and decay, have shaken hands. The keepers do not repair toward newness. They tend toward sameness. Their work is closer to gardening than maintenance.

And they garden — the Kettle is half farm. The green that fills every interval is, as far as anyone can determine, a single organism: one clonal mat, propagated by runners for longer than the company has kept records, spreading vegetatively under the town and up the walls. It has never flowered. There is nothing here to pollinate a flower, and no one remembers there ever being blossoms; it simply grows, visibly, audibly — in the heat and wet you can watch a tendril advance across a windowsill over the course of a week, and on still nights the growth makes small sounds, creaks and soft tears, roots working into new ground. The keepers cut it back from the machinery with hooked knives, train it onto the filtration terraces where its roots strain the spring water, spread its broad leaves under the lid to harvest the condensation, and harvest the mature leaves in rounds for the press-sheds, where they are crushed for a bitter aromatic oil the dry country uses in its lamps. Whether the green serves the town or tolerates it is a matter of keepers' debate, usually held over the third drink. Vines and pipes run together along every wall, painted and unpainted, and at a glance — at two glances — you cannot always tell which is alive.

The sound never stops, and it is enormous. Under everything, the Note: the summed bass of the engines, felt in the sternum more than heard. Over it, the clank and sigh of the walking beams, the chuff of the boilers, the hiss of steam at the release valves — a locomotive exhaling, again and again, forever. Water in all its voices: the rush of flumes, the churn of the turbine race, the tick and patter of ten thousand drips off leaf-tips and eaves, each boardwalk with its own rhythm section. When the interior rain comes it is almost deafening — the Kettle makes its own weather beneath its lid of steam and coal-haze and trapped cloud, and every few hours the saturated air tips and warm rain falls straight down out of the underside of the sky, drumming on corrugated iron until conversation is impossible. Not that there is much conversation. The keepers, the few dozen of them, go about with wool plugs tied round their necks, speak little and close, and develop over the years a habit of talking in the gaps of the engines' cycle, so that their sentences arrive in a kind of syncopation. Shift-change is marked by a steam whistle that you feel in your molars. On the first day of the month, or when the springs swell, they open the sluices and let the sump rise, and the boardwalks float up their posts, and the town moves about by pole-boat for a day or two, and the engines labor at a deeper pitch, and the whole basin trembles.

The water trembles even when still. Lean over any channel and watch: fine concentric rings, expanding and collapsing, everywhere, always, at the exact frequency of the Note. Every puddle keeps the engines' time. The keepers say a fish could not bear it; they say the rings are what the birds see from above, the whole hole shimmering like a struck drumhead, and that this is why nothing crosses the rim. They say a lot of things. What is certain is that in the Kettle there is no birdsong, no insect whine, no splash of fish, no rustle of anything that breathes — and yet the place is more alive, more in motion, than anywhere you have stood. The motion is vegetal and mechanical and hydraulic: growth, combustion, circulation. The cycles overlap like gears — the engines' shifts, the Beam's daily transit, the interior rains, the cutting rounds, the high waters — and the town is their meshing.

The rules, then, are few and absolute. Drink from the spring-heads, never from the standing channels. Touch no rail with a wet hand — the dynamos feed the tram line and the lamps, and the condensation beads on the ceramic insulators until, now and then, one lets go in a snap of blue. Cut the green where you're told and no deeper; nobody knows what the mat would do if truly wounded, and nobody wishes to learn. Do not bring an animal down. Expect your pulse, within a fortnight, to slow to the Note.

Come at beam-time, if you can arrange it. The pillar of sun slides over the rim and crosses the dark town like a searchlight being walked by a patient hand, and where it strikes, the amber water ignites, steam boils up off the channels, the broad leaves glow translucent, and the pumphouse windows answer it from inside, gold to gold. For perhaps half an hour the Kettle is a lantern with a flame in it. The engines do not pause. The rings do not still. Then the Beam climbs the far wall and is gone, and the room returns to its brown dusk and its amber lamps and its one held chord — a hot, wet, thundering quiet, with no song in it but its own.
