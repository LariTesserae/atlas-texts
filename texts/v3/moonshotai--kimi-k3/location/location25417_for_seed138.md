You hear the place long before you see it. Coming along the coast, the fog has a gray dome in it that does not move — the only cloud on the shore with a fixed address — and under the dome, through the mist, comes a chord of sound with no events in it: a deep roar, a walking-pace thud, a high thin hum, surf, gulls. The sound is the first thing the place gives you, and it turns out to be the thing it is most made of.

Then the fog takes you, warm as breath, and the land appears: black, glassy, sharp. The whole cove — you can walk it end to end in twenty minutes — is new lava, the youngest ground you will ever stand on. Ropey shelves of basalt frozen mid-pour, slabs tilted like crockery, fissures that breathe. There is no soil. There is no rounded stone. The rock cuts boot-leather and rope and careless hands; the hands here say the land is still hot from the mint. Along the waterline the slow seep of the flow meets the sea and the sea grinds it to black sand at exactly the rate it arrives — a yard gained, a yard lost, the shoreline fixed on the company charts for forty years, newborn every single day of them. The surveyor comes annually, measures, and goes away with the same map.

The works are scattered across the new rock the way the ground dictates: you build where it's cool enough, and the vents keep the gaps. So the hamlet has no center, only presences with wide black space between them — the boiler house, the condenser towers, the long canning shed, the white salt pans down at tide level, the iron pier, four cottages in a row, a mess hall, a storehouse on stilts over a warm seep. What connects them is not streets but pipes: runs of iron and copper on low trestles, with duckboards alongside and a railing on the steam side. You walk the pipes. The pipes are numbered, the lamp poles are numbered, the whistle schedule is posted at the pier, and the whole small parish of industry hums inside its fog like a single engine with its parts spread out for cooling.

Water is everywhere, in every state it can manage. The sea, of course, hissing on coarse new sand with a sieve sound. Hot springs weeping from fissures. Steam standing out of the ground in slow white columns. Condensate running in open troughs, warm as bathwater, the cleanest water in the world, dripping from the great copper worms of the condensers. Brine moving through the pans in a slow, managed sheet, never higher, never lower, crusting everything downwind in salt rime. The air itself is saturated — that is the dimness, in fact: the heat of the ground pulls sea air across the warm wet rock and the air gives up its water as fog, continuously, self-made, self-maintained. The fog is not weather here. The fog is a product. The sun shows through it sometimes as a pale coin; mostly the light is a directionless pearl-gray, dusk-bright at noon, and the electric lamps burn at all hours, haloed, because noon and midnight are barely distinguishable anyway. At night the fog holds the lamp-glow and the dark comes up gray rather than black.

The heat is graded, and everything in the cove knows the scale. Near the Kitchen — the fumarole field inland, where the big vent called the Kettle roars — the rock is too hot to touch and the duckboards are doubled. Farther out the ground is merely warm, and you learn the warm paths the way you learn streets. Iron rusts overnight here, then stops: a skin of rust that deepens to a line and holds, as though even corrosion finds its level. Wood goes silvery. Brass is oiled daily, and the oiling never ends and never fails.

The loudness, once you're inside it, resolves into layers, and then resolves back into one. The Kettle's roar is the floor of it. The pistons beat at walking pace, unhurried, unvarying. The dynamo lays its high hum under everything, and the condenser towers make a private weather — rain falling inside them, always. The surf combs the black sand. Cooling rock ticks and pings faintly beneath it all, the youngest sound there is. And the gulls. The gulls are enormous in number and they nest on every warm ledge, every pipe flange, grading their nests by how much heat each bird can bear — life in this cove is zoned by degrees, like a thermometer unrolled into space. Orange and emerald algae take the hottest channels. Crabs work the outflows where a hand couldn't bear the water. Eels slide in the condensate troughs, descendants of a bucket someone tipped in years ago, present now in their permanent numbers. Brine flies cloud over the pans and the birds work the clouds. Mussels pack the pier pilings. Seals haul out on the black sand in the lee of the warm discharge, and fish school in the discharge itself, and the canning line never wants for material. A rat or two in the storehouse, for completeness. In the mess window, a potted geranium that has been described as doing poorly for eleven years.

Vegetation proper barely exists — a bright seam of glasswort at the cool margins where seep water meets the tide, a dusting of first lichen on the oldest rock, which is thirty years old and counts here as ancient. Nothing roots because nothing has had time to become soil, and nothing has time because time, in the usual sense, is not how this place runs.

The people — a few dozen hands, company-employed, paid partly in scrip — live inside a loop. The whistle goes every six hours, and the six hours after a whistle are indistinguishable from the six hours before it: same fog, same gray light, same chord of sound, same warmth through the boot soles. Shifts relieve each other into identical days. The work is not production so much as maintenance — salt is harvested at the rate it forms, fish tinned at the rate they school, pipes wrapped, bearings greased, pier legs jacked level as they settle into the new sand, and nothing is ever finished. The hands have a saying that a job done is a job lost, and they mean it as comfort. Nothing here is allowed to complete. The intake equals the outflow. The ledgers balance to the ounce. The salt that leaves on the packet each month equals the salt the pans made; the heat that rises equals the fog that falls.

That is the law of the cove, and it has no exceptions anyone can name: the youngest land in the world, held perfectly still. Every plank has been replaced many times and nothing has ever changed. Once, in someone's grandfather's day, a bearing changed pitch for an afternoon, and they still tell the story — it is the only story. Ask what year it is and a hand will glance not at a calendar but at the pans, the pipes, the gulls on their warm flanges, and shrug, because all the clocks here read the same hour.

Stand at the end of the iron pier at what might be evening. The lamps come on behind you — they were never off; you only now notice them. The Kettle roars, the pistons walk, the surf sieves the black sand, the whistle is due and then sounds and then is due again. A seal slips off the sand. The fog neither thickens nor lifts. The shoreline holds its surveyed line, liquid last winter, ancient today, forever. The whistle's echo comes back off the fog exactly as it did six hours ago, and six hours before that, and the gulls settle on their graded ledges, and the warm ground breathes up through the soles of your boots, steady as a pulse that has found its resting rate and intends to keep it.
