The climb up to the Rim takes the better part of a day, and the whole way the land is lying to you. It looks built. The long slope is terraced in steps too regular for geology, each riser the height of a doorway, each tread strewn with pale blocks stacked like palletized stone awaiting a crew that never clocks in. Only when you put your hands on it does the lie resolve: the terraces are contraction joints, the blocks are columnar stone snapped along its own crystal habit. No tool has touched any of it. Nothing here has ever been touched at all.

At the top, the plateau opens — call it the Held Plateau, because that is what it is doing, and what has been done to it. Three hundred kilometers of high, warm, cloudless country under ordinary daylight. The air is thin and still and smells faintly of hot metal, like a machine shop an hour after the machines were switched off. The sky is bare in a way that takes time to notice. No clouds, ever — there is no water to make them from — and nothing crosses it but the sun. The light behaves here. Almost nothing else does.

The ground is the youngest ground imaginable, and it will be the youngest forever. Every surface is a fracture face: cream-and-rust stone broken into plazas of glittering cleavage, ridges with edges you could cut bread on, scree slopes of angular shards stacked at the exact angle of their first fall. Nothing has been rounded. Nothing has been weathered. Erosion arrived on this world, made a single preparatory gesture, and stopped — a canyon begun and never deepened, a crack opened and held. The fine dust between the stones is bone-dry and faintly electrostatic; it clings to you in filaments, and when you kick it loose it doesn't drift, because there is no wind to carry it. It falls, settles dead-flat within seconds, and locks. Which means: your footprints are permanent. The youngest event on this plateau, the most recent thing that has ever happened here, is your arrival. Step carefully. You are writing the only history this place keeps.

It is the machinery that unsettles, at first, before it becomes beautiful. A ridge of parallel stone plates runs forty kilometers across the southern flats, spaced with the regularity of cooling fins on an engine block — and they *are* thermal, the country shedding the heat of its own making, but at a scale no engine could survive. In the cliff faces, seams of native copper branch and rejoin in patterns indistinguishable from circuit etching. Helical crystals twist out of the rock in long drill-thread spirals, screws turned by no hand, fastening nothing. Below one escarpment lies a scree of near-perfect stone spheres, melon-sized, nested in a grooved channel like ball bearings in a race, waiting to roll. They will never roll. And underfoot, through the soles of your boots and faintly in your teeth, there is a hum — a standing micro-tremor, the crust's held note, invariant, the same today as it has always been. Not a process. An idle. The plateau does not imitate a factory; you come to understand that it is the other way around. Factories imitate physics. Here the physics is simply bare, industrial in its grammar, scaled to landscape, and producing nothing but itself.

Then there are the trees, and the trees are the heart of it.

You see the first one from twenty kilometers off: a dark scribble on the horizon, like a burnt antenna. It does not resolve as a tree until you are nearly beneath it, because nothing about it is tree-shaped in the ordinary way. Three hundred meters of trunk rises out of the dust — not solid wood but a bundle of capillary tubes fused into a column, warm to the touch, body-warm, refinery-warm. There is no water in this world, so the trees do not use any. Their sap is a molten salt, a eutectic mixture that stays liquid at the plateau's gentle temperatures, pumped by no pump, circulating on the held thermal gradient between the warm crust and the thin sky. Above the trunk the thing explodes into a froth: thousands of branching arms of black-bronze filament, rigid as coral, mineral-reinforced, ending in acres of matte dark vanes angled to the honest sun. It is less a plant than a lattice, less a lattice than an antenna farm, and it is *lush* — not in the way of a forest but in the way of a city. Each tree carries more living surface than a hillside meadow. Each is a continent of itself, extravagant, intricate, thousands of years in the growing.

And there are perhaps three hundred of them.

That is the emptiness. Stand beneath one and the next is a rumor on the horizon, a dark thread against the bare sky. Between the trees: nothing. Raw rock, dead dust, distance — kilometers of it, a regional hush of open country where the presences are so rare you could census the whole forest from a balloon in an afternoon. Lushness here is a quality of the individual, never of the crowd.

At the base of every tree lies the other industry. The trees feed on the young rock, fractionating it for what they need, and what they cannot use they excrete as waste — pure, reduced, immaculate. Around each trunk the ground falls away in concentric aprons: an inner ring of coarse dark slag, then a band of sulfur in yellow shards, then white-blue zinc crystals, then, outermost and brightest, nodules of copper, mirror-bright. Untarnished. There is no water and almost nothing reactive in the thin air, so nothing here ever dulls; the copper that fell from the tree's chemistry ten thousand years ago shines exactly as it fell. Mixed through the copper are the seeds: heavy metal spheres, fist-sized, polished by their own formation, strewn downhill in glinting dozens. Ball bearings from the orchard. They take centuries to germinate, and almost none do. Somewhere on the plateau a seed is a hundred years into deciding. The forest's population is, for all purposes, fixed — as many trees as there have ever been, no more coming, none leaving.

The eldest of them stands on the northern flats, and its apron has become a hill — a glittering conical mountain of its own accumulated output, sulfur and zinc and mirror-copper stacked by millennia, the tree crowned on top of its treasure like a refinery that forgot it was supposed to ship anything. From the Rim you navigate by such glints. The whole country is legible as a map of slow, purposeless production: industry with no product, plenty with no purchaser, a supply chain of one link.

Down in the swales, if you look closely — you have to look closely — the dust carries a faint viridian stain. This is the felt, the second life of the plateau: a dry photosynthetic moss-analog two millimeters tall, a continental lawn invisible from any distance, spreading its faint green film over whole plains. In the hollows where it has lived longest, its dead have drifted into soft pale deposits knee-deep, weightless, uncatalogued. Nothing eats the felt. Nothing eats anything here. There is no animal life of any kind — nothing to pollinate, nothing to graze, nothing to scatter the copper seeds, nothing to walk between the trees and look up. The abundance is entirely unused, and because nothing decays in the dry stillness, the dead simply persist, mummified in place, deep time preserved soft as cloth. Your trail through the felt-drifts will outlast every monument your species ever raised. The plateau keeps everything. It has nothing else to do.

The water story is shortest, and saddest, and it explains the rest. Cut into the western plateau is a canyon system the locals of no nation would call the First Cut: a river valley begun and never finished. Its walls drop twenty, thirty meters, fresh as the hour they opened. Its floor is a graded channel, unsorted rubble, side gullies that start confidently and end mid-thought. Everything about it says *water anticipated* — the rock prepared a drainage, a whole hydrology in the subjunctive mood — and the water never came. Not a single stone in the channel is rounded. Not one. There is no water on this world, and there never has been: only, sealed in vesicles deep in the young crust, a few spoonfuls of ancient condensate locked in stone since the first minute, untouched, unpoured. All the water of an entire planet would not fill a cup. The rivers of the First Cut are a promise the world made to itself and then, for reasons written nowhere, simply stopped making.

Because that is the deepest fact of the Held Plateau, the thing you feel gathering in you the longer you stay: nothing is going to happen. The sun crosses; the shadows of the great lattices sweep the dust like the hands of a clock moving over the face of a stopped machine; the warmth holds its narrow band; the hum holds its note. The world, it seems, had a brief and furious infancy — a few thousand years of self-construction, crust cooling into its fins and circuits, channels cracking open, the first felt spreading, the first seeds of the first trees — and then it reached its fixed point. Equilibrium. Every input matched by an output, every gradient too gentle to drive anything further. It has held that pose ever since: young, and young forever, erosion paused at its first hour, construction paused at its first shift. Time passes *over* this place without passing *through* it. A pebble placed on a slope will sit there for geological ages, a monument to the concept of placement.

And the silence — the silence has texture. The thin air carries almost nothing, so the world is arranged in soundless dioramas: you can see the next tree ten kilometers off and know that if it fell, it would fall in perfect quiet. What remains is the hum, felt rather than heard, and your own body, suddenly loud — breath, heartbeat, the crunch of dust that settles instantly and forgives nothing. And then, rarely: once in a decade, somewhere in the country, some crystal lets go of the last of its stress, and the plateau rings once, a single chime like struck glass rolling across a hundred empty kilometers — and is quieter afterward. Asymptote by asymptote, even the sound is running out. The world is still settling toward its rest, one chime per age.

Come evening, go back to the Rim. The light goes level and honest and long, and below you the lowlands stretch away — even rawer than the plateau, a chaos of new crust and sealed bubble-fields and mesas that stop halfway up, the construction site trailing off toward a horizon that was never going to be finished either. Behind you, across the whole region, the tree-shadows draw their slow lines, the copper aprons catch the sun and throw it back undiminished, and your footprints lead away through the felt-drifts: the only story ever told here, already permanent.

You will want to say the place feels *abandoned*, but that isn't right. Abandonment requires someone. It feels, instead, like a workshop at the end of day one — except that day one never ended. A world that built itself, refined itself, landscaped itself, and then stopped on the threshold of its own purpose, holding everything: the almost-rivers, the almost-forest, the almost-machines, the warm idle hum of an engine between beats.

It has been between beats for a very long time. The light moves across it, patient, and the plateau holds its almost the way other worlds hold water — completely, and to the brim.
