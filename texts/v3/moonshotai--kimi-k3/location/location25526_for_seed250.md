Begin at the surface, because for all its depth, this world is best understood from its skin.

The light is ordinary, and that is the first surprise. A sun. A day. Clouds doing the usual work of clouds. Underneath them a grey-green sea rolls under a cold wind, and there is nothing on it — no weed, no drift, no birds, no spume of anything breathing — and this emptiness is so complete that it reads, at first, as a painting of an ocean rather than an ocean. The horizon is clean in every direction. The air is wet enough that the distinction between sky and sea is mostly a courtesy: spray, mist, and a rain that falls somewhere on the planet at all hours, sometimes in walls a thousand kilometers wide.

And it is loud. Even above the water, under the wind, there is a low continued roar that has no source you can point at. It is the planet, working.

Descend, and the second surprise arrives: the water is clearer than water should be. Light does not die on schedule down here. It goes kilometers deep before it thins to blue dusk, because there is almost nothing in the water to stop it. This is the governing fact of the world — its emptiness. The ocean is so sparsely occupied that it is closer to a vacuum wearing the shape of an ocean. A mote of dust per cubic kilometer. You could drift for a month, suspended at mid-depth in four-degree water, and encounter nothing larger than a grain of sand. The great midwaters have no inhabitants, no traffic, no debris. They are a between — thousands of vertical meters and continental horizontal leagues of cold, faintly lit, perfectly silent-looking space.

Silent-looking. Not silent. Sound carries forever in water, and this water carries the sound of its own making: the deep groan of uplift, the roar of vents, the sharp tolling of cooling rock — great black bells ringing somewhere below, always — the static of storms conducted down from the surface, and beneath all of it a single steady tone, too stable to be noise, that comes from everywhere and therefore nowhere. There is no direction to any of it. Hanging in the midwater, you hear the whole planet at once. Occasionally something rises through the frequencies that sounds almost like a voice, almost like song — but there is nothing here that sings. That is a rule of the place: nothing moves by wanting. No fin, no eye, no mouth. Anything that moves is water, stone, storm, or the Work.

The Work. It has no other name, because there is no one to name it. When you finally reach the bottom — and the bottom finds you first, because it is coming up — you understand that the seafloor is a construction site, and the technology is not machines. There are no machines. No artifacts, no structures, nothing built in the way hands build. The engineering is dilute: a handful of working motes per cubic kilometer of sea, a technology with the density of a rumor, operating at geological patience and planetary reach. The water itself carries the instructions. The crust carries them too — the new basalt comes up black and glassy and raw, and if you examine it closely enough you find the same atomic pattern repeating in its crystals in one hemisphere and the other, identical, like a signature. Like a circuit.

The relief is absurd. The young crust builds with a vertical ambition that nothing yet opposes — erosion here is embryonic, a rumor of a process — so ridges shoulder upward without softening, trenches open deeper than they have any reason to be, and the distance from the deepest floor to the highest summit is close to twenty kilometers. There is no sediment. Nothing has lived and died here, so nothing settles; the abyssal plain is bare fresh lava, gleaming, and the vents that tear through it are young and furious, boiling the cold sea into columns of mineral smoke, wrapping the surface above them in permanent fog.

A few summits — nine, perhaps eleven on the whole planet — break the surface into thin, freezing air. They are black, rime-crusted, steam-wreathed, and raw as fresh breakage. There is no soil. There is no sand — the world has not yet had time to make a single grain; its coasts are glass shards and angular talus, and the rain runs off them in sheets because no river has had time to learn a channel. And on the wet rock, here and there, the first stains: olive films, grey-green crusts, a faint dusting of life or of something performing life. Whether these are the world's own pioneering biology or a biological component of the Work is a distinction that may not exist yet. Higher in the water column, the same ambiguity writes itself larger: once per ocean, at intervals of years, a bloom appears — a stroke of green thousands of kilometers long and effectively one cell thick, visible from the peaks as a single calligraphic mark on the water. It persists for weeks. Then the sea takes it apart again, every atom accounted for. Nothing is wasted. That is another rule.

The turbulence operates at every scale. Storms the size of continents iterate over the same new ridges season after season, as if testing them. Islands are raised in a decade and dismantled in the next; a plateau will be built with evident care and then dissolved back into the sea, its material reclaimed for some later attempt. The map is a draft, redrawn in wet ink. And sometimes — not often, but sometimes — the water holds a shape it should not: a dome on a calm day, a spiral current like a fingerprint, a swell that travels half a day against the wind before remembering itself. These pass. They are not alive. They are more like a hand pausing over the work.

The rules of the world, then, as they emerge: nothing is finished. Nothing is wasted. Distance is the default condition; presence is the exception. There is no silence anywhere in it, and no voice. The cold is thorough and ordinary. The light is thorough and ordinary. And the whole vast, empty, thundering arrangement is rising, degree by degree, decade by decade, out of its own ocean — a planet rehearsing, at full volume, for an audience it does not have, becoming something it has not yet decided to be.
