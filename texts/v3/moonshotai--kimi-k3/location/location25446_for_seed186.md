# The Plateau

The first fact is the height. The second is the dark. The third is the quiet — and the quiet is really the first two working together.

Stand on the Rim and you are standing at the last altitude where air still bothers to be air. Above you the sky has already given up its blue. The sun hangs small and hard and white, an ember with a sharp edge, and even at midday there are stars near the zenith, faint as flecks of mica in dark stone. Shadows do not soften here. They fall like cut cloth, black against black, and the land reads as a thing of silhouettes and dim sheens: frost on the lee side of every rock, the far snowfields glowing dull as banked coals, the long dark expanse of the interior plateau spreading out below, vast and patterned and nearly still.

This is a country the size of a small sea — rim peaks, passes, interior plains, cirques, pans — and almost all of it is empty. Emptiness is its medium, the way water is the medium of a lake. You can walk a day across the interior and hear nothing but your own pulse and the small tick of the ground. Nothing answers. The dark holds.

## The Air

The air is too thin to carry sound the way sound wants to be carried. A shout dies within a body-length. A falling frost-block, which anywhere else would crack like a rifle, arrives here as a felt thud in the soles of the feet, a shiver in the teeth. The wind, when it comes over the Rim in the afternoons, does not howl — it is felt as pressure and as charge. Dust streams. Loose fibers stand on end. The hair lifts on anything that has hair. But the air passes over stone with hardly a whisper, because there is so little of it to push against itself.

So the world has learned to speak in other channels. Everything here signals through the ground — footfall, tremor, the slow percussion of freezing — or through light: small, cold, blue-violet glows, sparingly spent. Nothing is loud. Nothing can afford to be.

## The Ground

The interior plateau is floored with stone that the frost has been sorting for longer than any living memory of stone. Freeze and thaw, night and noon, ten thousand times ten thousand repetitions, and the ground has organized itself: polygonal nets of larger rock edging cells of finer grit, stone stripes running down the gentle slopes like combed hair, blockfields of frost-riven slabs lying stable and dark and lichen-crusted, fitted against one another like a pavement no hand laid. Call it the Mesh.

It ticks. At the cold hours the whole Mesh contracts and ticks, soft as a cooling stove, as needle-ice extrudes beneath the surface grit and lifts it in fine columns, then lets it settle. Each cycle moves the stones a fraction of a hair. The fractions have added up, over ages, into pattern — and the pattern is not decoration. The coarse borders channel what little brine runs in this country. The Mesh is a circuit etched by cold, and it has had a very long time to get itself right.

Nothing here is raw. The cirque walls are old, softened, their headwalls draped with perennial snowbanks in the shade. The tors stand weathered into squat knobs. Even the steep slopes move only at the pace of the stone stripes — a slow creep, balanced, production matching export. The land settled its accounts long ago and now simply keeps them.

## The Water

There is almost no water, and there is water everywhere, and both are true because the water never holds one state long enough to be counted.

It arrives as ice-fog and diamond dust, riding up over the Rim on certain winds. It plates every surface as rime — needles and feathers of frost, thick as a thumb on the windward side of every stone, every stem, every hair. Then the small sun climbs, the black ground drinks what warmth there is, and for an hour or two around midday the rime lets go. Melt threads appear along the coarse borders of the Mesh — the melt-lace — brief, cold, silent runnels, most of them no wider than a finger, many running under a skin of refrozen ice so that even their music is muffled. By dusk the lace has stopped. The water has gone back to ice, or down.

Down is the brine. In the low cells of the Mesh and in shallow dark pans scattered across the interior, the melt concentrates its salts year upon year until the water is too briny to freeze even here. The pans lie still and black, holding the dark sky's dimness without rippling. And the brines thread down into the ground along the stone borders, making veins — conductive veins, electrolyte laid into the circuitry of the Mesh. Everything on this plateau is a capacitor. The moss-felts store water. The dark stone stores the noon's thin heat. The brine veins store charge. Nothing is stored for long.

## The Gardens

Where the veins cross — where brine and shelter and trapped snow coincide — the plateau keeps its gardens. They are scattered wide apart, hours or days of empty Mesh between them, and they are the only lushness in a country of stone, and they are black.

The lushness here is the color of a bruise. Cushion-plants grow in dense felts knee-deep and acres wide, near-black leaves packed tight against the cold and tuned to drink every scrap of the weak sunlight; the felts hold water like batteries and warmth like embers, and inside them the temperature runs a few merciful degrees above the plateau's. Through the felts rise the spark-reeds: tall, dark, resinous spires, branched like candelabra, their tissues veined with conductive fibers, their tips coming to fine points.

And in the brine veins beneath the gardens live the films — ribbon-colonies of microbes strung end to end, passing electrons down their length the way a wire does, wiring the garden into the Mesh.

It is easiest to say it plain: the plateau is a works. It has intake, refinement, storage, distribution, discharge. It has shifts. It has no buildings.

The intake is the afternoon wind. When the air pours over the Rim it carries ice-dust, and blowing ice-dust tears charge apart the way any blown crystal does, and the Mesh — dry, cold, vast, insulating — holds the imbalance. Through the afternoon the tension climbs. Fibers lift. The drifters high overhead change their lanes. The felts seem to listen.

The discharge is dusk. The spark-reeds, rooted in the brine veins, wick the charge up out of the ground, and as the light fails the gardens go into corona: a soft fizzing, a faint blue-violet fire crawling along the spire-tips, small arclets stitching from reed to reed with sounds like a fingertip tapped once on a table. In thicker air this would be thunder. Here the plateau spends its electricity in whispers. The arclets tear the thin air's nitrogen into food, and the fixed salts wash down with the night's rime into the felts — the gardens fertilize themselves out of the sky. And the ions streaming off the spires seed the moisture above them: fog condenses first over the gardens, born of the discharge, so that each garden by full dark sits under its own small manufactured weather, breathing out the mist that will plate it by morning with tomorrow's water.

The works makes its own rain. It makes its own fertilizer. It builds nothing. There is not, in this whole country, one single made thing — no cairn, no wall, no tool, no ruin. Even the trails fade; hooves cannot scar the Mesh. The only lines on the land are the stone stripes and the brine veins, and the land drew those itself.

## The Animals

Life comes to the gardens in pulses, because between them lies the Between, and the Between is days wide.

The felt-oxen come in herds of a few dozen — massive, black, silent animals, furred like the felts they graze, their feet broad and soft and packed with sensors. They do not call to one another. They drum: a forefoot stamped once on the Mesh carries farther than any voice could, and a herd grazing spread out across a garden stays in constant tremor-conversation, a conversation you would only know about by laying a palm flat on the ground. They follow the melt-lace's brief noon flow from garden to garden, grazing the felts, which regrow; the felts have been grazed for as long as they have grown, and the balance holds.

Where the discharge crackles, the arc-moths rise. They are fist-sized, dust-winged, drawn to the corona, drinking the fixed nitrogen salts from the spire-tips even as the arclets snap around them — tending the discharge the way bees tend flowers, carrying spores of the felt-plants and the film-microbes from garden to garden on their bodies. They are the logistics of the works, the freight line. At dusk a garden full of moths looks like a slow, dim snowstorm of faint lights.

And where there are herds there is the hoar-skate. It is the color of rime, flat as a shadow, and it hunts the frost-filmed open ground at the edge of the gardens, motionless for days, reading the Mesh through its whole lower surface. It feels the drumming of the oxen. It feels the difference between a stamp that means *grazing* and a stamp that means *nothing yet*. When it finally moves it moves once, fast, and quietly — everything here is quietly — and the herd drums the news across the ground for an hour afterward, and then the pulse passes, and the silence reseals.

Over it all, in the dark upper air, the drifters cross. Great gas-bodied jellies, dim as moons behind cloud, trailing root-fine antennae hundreds of feet down, glowing faintly with charge of their own. They ride the katabatic rivers of cold air that pour off the heights at dusk, garden to garden, lane to lane, seeding the felts with what they carry, harvesting charge from the discharge below with their dangling lines. Sometimes, on a discharge evening, you can see three or four of them strung along a lane like lamps along a road — the only sense of distance the dark ever offers. They make no sound at all. Nothing about them touches the world except their shadow and their thread-fine lines, and the lines barely touch.

Below notice, in the pans and veins, the films do the oldest work: conduction, chemistry, the slow redox bookkeeping that the whole works runs on. The reeds and oxen and moths and skates are the visible shifts. The films are the factory floor.

## The Between

And between all of it — between garden and garden, herd and herd, drifter and drifter — is the Between: hours of ticking Mesh under a dark sky, frost on every stone, your own pulse in your ears, nothing moving, nothing answering.

The Between is not a failure of the world. It is a requirement. The charge that powers the gardens needs vast dry insulated ground to build across. The drifters need long cold lanes of sinking air. The herds need distance enough that no garden is ever grazed past its regrowing. The emptiness is the medium the industry runs in, the way a fire needs the room around the flame.

It is also, simply, what the plateau mostly is. The gardens are bright held breaths. The Between is the long exhale of a very large, very cold, very patient body.

## The Cycles

The day is the smallest cycle: rime before dawn, melt-lace at noon, wind and rising charge through the afternoon, discharge and fog-birth at dusk, freezing quiet through the dark. Tick, flow, fizz, stillness. Every day the whole works runs one shift.

The storm season is the great cycle: weeks when real snow comes up over the Rim, the snowbanks in the cirque shade grow fat, the melt-lace swells and reaches farther across the Mesh, the herds bunch and move, the moths cloud, the skates eat, the drifters calve their young into the enriched lanes. Then the storms withdraw, the lace retreats, and the plateau subsides into its long ordinary: the fog-tides, the dusk-fizz, the ticking nights.

Under everything, the slowest cycles run. The frost sorts the stones a hair a year. The stone stripes creep downslope. The blockfields settle their slabs one grain tighter. The brine veins concentrate. Nothing here is in a hurry, and nothing here is still — it is all in motion at once, at every tempo, and the tempos have had so long to fit themselves together that the motion itself has become a kind of rest.

## The Rules

The dark is altitude, not night. The sun is up there. It is the sky that has thinned away.

Water passes through all its states within a day and belongs to none of them.

Nothing is made. Everything is sorted, grown, stored, or discharged.

Sound is local. The world speaks in tremor and in cold light, and mostly it does not speak.

Charge, like water, gathers in low and sheltered places, and must be spent by dusk.

Life collects where the veins cross. Between the crossings lies the Between, and the Between is most of the world, and the world would not work without it.

And the last rule, which the others all serve: the slowest processes are the strongest. The frost taught the stones where to lie; the stones taught the brine where to run; the brine taught the gardens where to grow; and the gardens taught the dark to glow, faintly, in whispers, on schedule, forever — a factory the size of a sky, that builds nothing, and wastes nothing, and has never once been loud.
