From the rim, in the ordinary grey light of midday, the valley looks at first like weather that has lain down and refused to move. It fills a high bowl on the mountain's shoulder — a half-day's walk across, rim to rim — and it has districts the way a town does, though nothing here was ever drawn or decided. Below you, the pale Sump Flats steam faintly against the cold. Beyond them, dark mast-fells rise in bristling slopes, and between the slopes run fold-lines, deep creases where the ground seems to have been pinched together by a slow hand. The light falls on all of it the way light falls on a road. It explains nothing.

Then you notice that the valley is moving. Not in one way, not at one speed — everywhere, at many rhythms, none of them agreeing. The mast-fells rock. The flats breathe. Something on the near slope rises, holds, and sinks. It looks like a crowd, like a harbor, like machinery glimpsed through heat-shimmer.

And there is no sound. That is the second wrongness, and it is the one you will carry home.

Descending, your footfalls die in the ground. The floor of the valley — the felt, as the few who have crossed the rim call it — is a knee-deep mat of layered, scale-like growth, springy and dense, furred with rime on its upper faces and warm two feet down. It swallows impact the way fresh snow does, but completely, greedily. A shout travels the length of your arm and stops. Nothing here works by striking; everything works by pressure, by swelling, by the slow hydraulics of sap and ice, and the felt absorbs what little vibration escapes. The valley eats its own voice. What motion there is, you feel through your soles: a deep, slow stroke far below, regular as a heartbeat, more organ than ear.

The felt is alive, all of it. There is no soil in the valley — only plant, and the compacted bodies of former plant. Dig down through the living crust and you pass through fiber-peat, layer upon layer of it, warm and wet and faintly sweet, and deeper still the warmth gathers into something like a low fever. The deep bed. The valley's firebox. Down there, in the dark, the dead of ten thousand seasons are being burned without flame, digested into heat, and the heat is hoarded. Pipes of root carry it upward. The whole valley floor is a furnace with the door shut.

Above the bed the damp is constant and strange. The air is bitter — cold enough to ache the teeth, cold enough that your breath hangs in front of you like a private fog — but the ground breathes too, and where the ground's warm breath meets the sky's cold, water happens. It condenses on every stem, beads on every fiber, drips upward-born from below even under a clear sky. There is almost no open water: only the sumps, steep-sided black ponds on the flats, half-roofed with living membrane, steaming at their rims where rime grows thick as masonry. Everything else is condensation, ice-lens, fog-drip, sleet that arrives fast over the rim and melts into the felt before it can lie. The valley is damp the way a machine room is damp. It sweats.

Stand beside a pumpwood and watch it work. It is jointed like bamboo, thick as a barrel, ringed with old ice-collars, and it does nothing for a long while — and then a shudder climbs it, and the whole column rises an arm's length, slow and deliberate, and a collar of rime cracks without a whisper, and a plume of white breath pours from its crown and is taken by the cold. Then it settles. Somewhere below it, in chambers warmed by the bed, meltwater waits; somewhere in its upper throat, a plug of ice has just taken its stroke, swelling as it froze, driving the column up, hauling the valley's blood a few feet higher against the cold. Freeze is the piston. The gradient is the engine: warm decay below, hard cold above, and every living thing here straddles the difference and feeds on it.

Which means the valley's economy is perverse, and it is the first rule you learn: cold is fuel. The worse the weather, the harder the work.

There are hundreds of pumpwoods in any view, thousands on the slopes, and not two of them in step. That is the valley's peculiar energy — a bazaar of cycles, ten thousand merchants who have never agreed on the hour. Masts sway and nod at their own counts. Vents inhale and exhale on private schedules. Sheets of fiber creep out of the slotted flanks of the spinner-plants and accumulate in pale heaps that slump, slowly, under their own weight, like snow settling off a roof — except that what slumps here is product. The valley manufactures. Sheets, cables, coils of spun fiber tougher than rope; resin that hardens into pale ingots; extruded piping that newer growth climbs and old growth abandons. Nothing was built here, and everything is produced.

And nothing is kept. That is the second rule. Watch a stand of mast-reeds exhaust its patch of bed: the whole stand shudders, the felt beneath it heaves once, and the stand goes down — not falling, subsiding, swallowed over the course of minutes as the bed opens and takes it. It happens a hundred times a day across the valley, and every time it arrives the same way: a pressure in the soles of your feet, a deep soundless tone you feel in your chest, and a gap in the slope where a grove used to be. By the next day the wreck is green. Growth and collapse are exactly equal. The floor neither rises nor sinks. Cores drawn from the sumps go down through forty feet of layered fiber, and every layer is the same season, repeated: the valley has been precisely this, neither more nor less, for longer than the surrounding ridges have worn their present shapes. It is a storm that has kept one shape for ten thousand years.

Even the mountains are being eaten on schedule. At the rim, the millroots work: great ridged plates of tissue that turn on the bare stone, slow as the hour hand, grinding the bedrock to a grey meal that the melt carries down to the beds. The bowl the valley sits in is a bowl it made. The grooves in the rim rock are polished by millennia of the same slow jaws, and they will be polished deeper, at the same rate, forever. Everything here is ancient in form and frantic in motion, an anthill inside a cathedral, and the two facts do not disturb each other at all.

Come evening, if a true cold pulse crosses the rim, you can watch the valley answer. The freeze-front is visible: a white line running over the fells faster than a man can walk, rime blooming on every stem in the space of a breath, the whole grey-green sea paling as it crosses. And the valley deepens its work. The vents breathe thicker. The strokes of the pumpwoods lengthen. Fog pours upward off the flats and hangs over the bowl in a ceiling that glows faintly at the edges where the ordinary moonlight catches it. Seen from the rim on the coldest nights, the valley steams like a city's works, stacks breathing by the thousand — and it does all of it in total silence, under totally ordinary stars.

On the driest, bitterest nights, the static comes. The fiber-heaps and the swaying masts build charge the way dry looms do, and it bleeds off the tallest tips as a cold violet corona, soundless, a fire with no crackle. There is a small dark bird — you may see one, you may not — that lands on the glowing mast-tips at dusk and sits there while the light crawls over it, drinking the charge off the plant, and departs dull and ordinary into the dark. It does not sing. Nothing here sings.

Animals are rare enough that their rarity becomes a quality of the place. You can walk a full day and see nothing that is not plant, only the tracks at a sump's edge: broad, plate-like prints pressed into the rime, already softening as the felt reclaims them. The feltdeer makes them — the only large animal in the valley, grey-brown, its coat textured exactly like the floor, and if you startle one it does not run. It stands, and your eye slides off it, and there is only floor where a moment ago there was an animal. In the warm throats of the vent-stacks live the pale sleepers, larval and slow, never seen whole — you see a glisten, a deliberate withdrawal into the dark, nothing more. The valley barely needs animals. It pollinates itself, plows itself, buries itself. It is nearly a closed body, and it exports nothing but fog.

The last rule you learn by staying still. Warmth draws attention. Stop to rest and within the hour the nearest pumpwoods will have leaned, fractionally, toward you; the vents nearest your camp will breathe a little faster; the felt beneath you will have risen, gently, to cup your shape. The valley is not hostile. It is only interested, the way soil is interested in a seed. Do not sleep uncovered on a warm night, and do not expect anything you carry to remain yours: a rope left on the felt is frayed by morning and gone by the next night, digested fiber by fiber. There are no paths, no cairns, no marks. Nothing made can stay made here. The valley tolerates no straight line older than a day.

And when you climb back to the rim and look down at the whole of it — the breathing flats, the rocking fells, the slow white plumes rising by the hundred into the cold — the strangest thing is not the motion, not the industry, not the age of it. It is that the loudest thing in the valley, the entire time you were in it, was the inside of your own skull.
