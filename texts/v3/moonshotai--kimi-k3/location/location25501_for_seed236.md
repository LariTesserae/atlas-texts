## The Still

The basin below is a sea that finished dying a long time ago. Its floor is a pavement of salt polygons, white and faintly pink, fissured into plates that fit like the back of a turtle's shell, and the heat above it stands in visible columns. Nothing moves down there. Nothing has moved down there for longer than anything remembers.

Out of that floor rises the Rise — a dome of buried salt, shouldering up through the crust of its own dead ocean. It is young, as mountains go, and still being born: the deep salt below never forgot it was once liquid, and it presses upward a finger's width a year, extruding fresh faces of halite — glassy, sharp, amber and milk-white and blush — that nothing has weathered, because weather requires water, and there is none. A slow tongue of salt glacier spills from the dome's shoulder and creeps downslope at the pace of a growing nail, its surface crevassed into blades.

And on the crest, small enough to walk across in an hour, sits the Still.

From the pan below, it looks like a drop of honey on the rim of the world. A candle-mound. Something warm-colored and faintly glossy against the hard glitter of the salt. It is the only lush thing in the basin, and it contains not one free drop of water.

---

**The first law of the Still: the green is inside.**

The city's outer skin is wax and horn and bone-pale laminate, sealed and double-sealed, every surface polished to a soft gloss by the tenders. It reads as amber, cream, old ivory. Only when a wall is wounded — rare, and quickly mended — does the truth flash out: wet, saturated green, vivid as anything in any watered world, before the amber scab grows over it. The verdancy of the Still is subdermal. Its lushness is a kept secret, held under wax the way other places hold water under ground.

Because water here is not found. It is *inherited*. The Still runs on an endowment — a fixed quantity of moisture, sealed into bladder-tissues clear as tears along the walls, circled endlessly through the city's body and the bodies of everything that lives in it. To drink is to borrow from a closed account. Nothing evaporates; nothing is spent; even the dead are rendered back into the ledger. At night, cool condenser-fins along the tower flanks reclaim the city's own breath. The Still is a still in the distiller's sense: a sealed vessel, turning the same precious moisture over and over, forever.

---

**Nothing here is built. Everything is grown.**

The city is one organism — or several wearing one another so long that the question has stopped mattering. It has no builders, no tools, no hands that wanted anything. And yet it is unmistakably a town: dense, organized, quartered, infrastructural. The urbanism is anatomy.

The **streets** are channels between fused wall-lobes, floored in petal-felt that gives softly underfoot and swallows every footfall. They are narrow — near-touching — and layered three deep: root-crypts below, where the city's slow acids work the salt of the dome; the street level; and a canopy of arched bridges overhead, grown vaults lacing the lanes into dim, golden tunnels. Nothing is dark, though. The walls themselves are light-pipes, translucent horn fibers that drink the daylight on the surface and conduct it downward, so that even the deepest crypt glows amber-green at noon. At night the fibers release what they stored, faintly. The Still's lamps are its own skin.

Beneath glassy resin flags in the plazas, you can stand on the city's bloodstream: sap-canals, sealed, the fluid within moving slow as honey, carrying mineral salts up from the roots and sugar down. The **towers** are the respiratory crown — a dozen spires, cathedral-scaled, their tips furled into calyx fists during the day. They are warm to the touch. The whole city is faintly warm, compost-warm, alive-warm, and the warmth of its breathing draws the still air through the streets in a drift so gentle it is indistinguishable from no wind at all.

There are quarters, like any town. The nursery-vaults, where the city buds hollow cyst-chambers that its animals rent with service. The render-yards, where husks and dead are taken down into the canals. The exchange-floor — a broad plaza where the economy happens, silent and constant: animals arriving with mineral dusts, fallen wax-scales, their own dung, and leaving with nectar, salt-licks, alcove-rights. At the plaza's center is the tithe-bowl, a shallow digestive basin, the one carnivorous organ of the city, where the elderly and the dying of every species eventually go to lie down. The Still eats occasionally. It prefers to be fed.

---

**The animals are many, and none of them make a sound.**

The streets are never empty — this is a crowded place, saturated, every surface occupied — and yet you could stand in the thickest of it and hear your own heartbeat as the loudest thing present.

The **feltbacks** are the large presences: slow browsers, velvet-shod, cropping the sound-dampening moss-mats that upholster every ledge and bridge. They take an hour to cross the plaza. Their grazing keeps the velvet healthy; their breath feeds the city; their bodies are walking water-banks, and they know it, and move with the unhurried dignity of the wealthy.

The **darters** are the only fast things in the Still — small moth-winged couriers, their wings fringed and muffled, flying in perfect silence. They carry pollen, and they carry the mail: scent, dabbed from gland-wall to gland-wall, messages delivered on the body. At dusk they rise in soft swarms that look like ash falling upward, reversed.

The **tenders** are crablike things descended, perhaps, from some brine-shrimp of the dead sea below. They polish the wax seals, pick at blemishes, harvest scale, and tend the seams where the rising dome splits the streets. The **renderers** are slow slug-things that find every husk, every shed, every corpse not given to the bowl, and take it home to the canals. And in the deeper lanes there is the predator the animals simply do not name: the **patience**, a thing that holds still for weeks in an alcove, indistinguishable from the city's own tissue, until the arithmetic of hunger changes.

There is no birdsong. There is no stridulation, no call, no howl. Loudness, here, is a wound — sound means damage, means a wall split by the rising salt, and the tenders come to noise the way blood comes to a cut. Over the generations, everything that made noise was noticed, and everything that was noticed was sealed over. Silence is not an absence in the Still. It is the immune system. It is the sound of health.

---

What passes for sound, catalogued entire:

At dawn and at noon, the salt ticks. Tiny mineral clicks as the dome warms and expands — the mountain's quiet fidget, the closest thing the place has to a clock. Rarely, a seed-pod cracks somewhere in the canopy, and at that single sharp report every feltback freezes mid-step, every darter holds in the air, and the whole city listens to the echo of it for minutes afterward. Below hearing, there is the pulse of sap in the canals — felt through the soles, not heard, a slow deep throb like the place's held heartbeat.

And once in some years, the wind comes up off the pan. That is the Still's only weather and its only catastrophe. The calyx towers slam shut. The animals file into their cysts. The wax seals tighten. And the city's other archive — the one written in the air itself — is erased.

For the windless air of the Still holds scent the way other towns hold noise: layered, persistent, legible. Every lane carries hours of conversation in it — an alarm-musk near the tithe-bowl, a bloom-signal along the nursery vault, the address-scents of a hundred cyst-doors, the darters' delivered correspondence hanging unopened in the stillness. The animals read the streets by nose the way you'd read a morning's broadsheets. When the wind comes, all of it is torn away, and afterward the city is amnesiac for a season, re-laying its trails, re-introducing its streets to themselves.

---

**Time moves here the way the salt moves: upward, and slowly.**

The dome is still rising, and the city rides it like an elevator going nowhere. Streets split a finger's width a year along the pressure-seams and the tenders heal them; you can date a lane by its width, and the maps of the Still are calendars. The cycles are long and they are trusted. Nightly, the towers open. Seasonally, the spore-flush comes — a soft, soundless snow of spores drifting through the lanes, the Still's version of spring, settling on the feltbacks' backs and carried everywhere. And once in a generation, a crosser arrives: a great tortoise-like pilgrim that has spent years walking the dead pan from one of the other domes on the horizon — because the basin is not empty. There are other Stills out there, candle-mounds at the edge of sight, and at dusk the calyx-tips of every city flush slow colors, green to amber to deep rose, talking across the dead sea in light. A single exchange takes an evening. A conversation takes a decade. The basin, which looks like the end of the world, is in fact mid-sentence.

Dusk is the rush hour. The towers unfurl; the secret green comes out into the open, lush and unabashed, drinking the cool; the stored daylight wakes faintly in the walls; the darters rise; the exchange-floor fills. The day-city is amber and asleep. The night-city is the true one — and by midnight, seen from the pan below, the crest of the dome glows softly like a lantern with the flame turned all the way down.

---

Stand at the rim at dawn, then, with the dead sea below and the still air above and the young sharp salt underfoot, and listen to the only voice the Still permits itself: the small ticks of warming wax and warming crystal, scattered and patient, like a great book turning one page. A city with no builders. A lushness sealed in wax. A fortune of water, endlessly kept. News without sound, wealth without spending, a mountain that is still arriving.

The whole place is one long held breath — and it has no intention of exhaling.
