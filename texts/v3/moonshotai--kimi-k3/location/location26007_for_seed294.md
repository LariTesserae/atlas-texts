The water is the first fact. It is total, and it is cold — the kind of cold that slows every process it touches without ever quite stopping one. Light comes down from somewhere far above, a gray-blue seep with no sun in it, directionless, strained through a ceiling of ancient ice that is too distant to see. You could hang in the middle of this and turn slowly and believe, for a while, that you were in a flooded canyon, a drowned cave system, some natural basin.

You would be wrong. Look at any surface closely enough and the truth arrives: every stone here was placed. The walls are masonry — fine-grained, pale, built of sand grains and shell-shard and a secreted cement that outlasts its makers by geological ages. The floor, if you dig into it, is older work fused under the weight of newer work, strata of intentions compressed into something like bedrock. There is no natural substrate anywhere in the Cistern. Dig forever and you find only deeper architecture. There is no geology here. There is only archaeology, all the way down.

The water itself has texture and taste. Iron and lime, mostly. In the still districts it is clear enough to see across a hall the size of a valley; elsewhere it carries veils — silt lifted off the rubble plains, white mineral floc rising from below, rust-red curtains of oxidized iron hanging in doorways like cloth. The red veils mark the cold seeps, where deep water wells up through the oldest masonry carrying dissolved minerals, feeding the films. The films are the only producers here: microbial, simpler than plants, spread as a dull sheen over every tended surface. There is no vegetation. There never has been. What passes for a garden in this world — and the spire-tips do look, from a distance, like gardens in bloom — is entirely animal: fans, nets, lacy tubes, all of them alive, all of them straining the water.

Because the water is always bringing food, from two directions at once.

From above comes the fall: a fine, endless snow of detritus drifting down from the bright ceiling, the leavings of an outside world that enters through the Gate in the Rim and is never seen directly. From below comes the rise: the seeps blow out slow columns of white floc, mineral smoke ascending, rust-stained near the iron-rich springs. Between them, in mid-water, the two snows cross — and that crossing layer is where everything gathers to feed. It hangs in the dimness like a visible weather, a faintly glittering band, and the whole sparse population of the Cistern orients on it the way surface creatures orient on a shore.

The builders work everywhere, alone. The masons come in many lineages: grain-carriers no bigger than a thumbnail, ferrying one sand grain at a time across distances that must feel continental to them; secretion-masons extruding slow ribbons of mortar; tunnel-keepers reaming the old passages to keep them open. They never hurry. The cold forbids hurry. You can hang motionless and watch one mason work for an hour and see the wall grow by almost nothing — and understand that this same wall, by this same patience, has been growing for longer than most mountain ranges last.

Against them work the unbuilders: rasp-mouthed, patient in their own way, prying mortar apart for its minerals. They are the decomposers of architecture, and they are the reason things fall. They are also, in a sense, the reason things stand — because every arch you see is a survivor of ten thousand arches the unbuilders took. Nothing here was designed. Every form is what remained.

The fauna are abundant in kind and sparse in number. Grazers wide as a hand creep across the films, leaving trails of cleaned stone behind them — wandering glyphs, a script that writes itself and is never read. Filterers crowd the spire-tips where the current runs. Pillar elders, enormous and centuries old, sit fused into walls they have filtered for so long that the masonry grew around them; they are load-bearing lives now, and when one dies the district shudders. Long pale hunters cross the open halls on errands of their own; you may watch one traverse a whole nave in a minute and then not see another for days. Bioluminescence is rare and faint — three small lights in a dark hall might be a lure, a courtship pulse, a mason's marking-glow. The darkness is mostly unlit, and mostly unbothered by that.

The shape of the Cistern, in broad strokes: deepest are the Fossil Works, foundation-halls half-filled with silt, where the oldest masonry has gone to true stone and the seeps breathe and the red veils hang and the cold is stillest. Above them rise the Needles, spire-fields straining toward the bright, young and contested and visibly growing, bristling with gardens. Between and around them lie the Fallen — rubble plains where towers came down, which serve as quarries, nurseries, and proving grounds; the grain-carriers mine the Fallen, and young masons apprentice on rubble before they are trusted near a standing wall. At the basin's heart runs the Long Weir, a dam older than any living lineage, over which the water pours in a slow, wide, nearly silent underwater fall, raising a permanent mist of silt in the plunge pools below. At the edge of everything, the Rim: a curved wall rising beyond sight, crusted with abandoned districts. And at one point in the Rim, the Gate, where the outside sends its river in — the source of the falling snow, the driver of everything.

The driver, and the drummer. Because for all its stillness, the Cistern is never at rest. It breathes. As the Gate-flow swells and slackens on its cycle of days, the whole basin inhales and exhales through its arches, and when the Breath runs strong, the structure flutes — a tone below hearing, felt in the bones, that opens every filter-fan at once and stands the silt up off the plains in veils. Towers fall: undermined by generations of unbuilders, a spire leans for days, shedding gravel, and then goes — slow, silent from a distance, the boom arriving late, the dust-bloom rising like a slow fire. Within hours the scavengers converge from across the district. A tower-fall is a whale-fall of stone. For a week the sparse world is crowded; then the feast thins, the masons arrive to quarry the carcass, and the plain resumes its long emptiness. When the cold deepens, brine rejected by the ceiling ice runs down the spires and freezes as it descends — ice fingers reaching down the towers, sealing the gardens, holding everything in suspension — until the thaw-surge comes down from the Gate and shatters them, and the shards fall like the slowest glass rain, and rebuilding begins before the last shard lands.

Nothing stops. Nothing hurries. The Cistern is the oldest thing and the newest; its plan was settled ages ago and its substance is never twice the same. It eroded long ago into its final form, and its final form is turbulence — a standing wave of masonry, held up by the very motion that wears it down.

It is mostly quiet. The near sounds are small: the tick of a grain set in mortar, the rasp of a grazer, the click of a mason's limbs. The far sounds are large and always late — the groan of a strained wall deciding something, the boom of a fall arriving long after the dust. The snows make no sound. The silt makes no sound. The fluting of the Breath is felt more than heard. The loudest regular presence in the Cistern is your own movement through it.

The rules, as the place teaches them:

Every stone was carried or secreted. There is no bedrock, only older work. Nothing is waste — the fallen are quarries, the dead are mortar, silt is tomorrow's wall. The snow falls from both directions; feed where they meet. Light never strengthens; those who need it climb, those who need minerals descend. Every wall is either being built or being eaten. Slow is not still.

And one more, which you learn last: this is not a ruin. A ruin implies that someone stopped. Nobody here has stopped — not the masons, not the unbuilders, not the seeps, not the snow. Hang in some great dim hall at the meeting layer, with one mason ticking away at a wall it will never finish, three faint lights burning in the dark, the two snows crossing all around you, and somewhere far below the long groan of stone deciding whether to stand a little longer — and you understand that the Cistern does not persist despite its collapse. It persists by means of it.
