From the rim road, the basin looks like a held breath. The cloud deck sits on it low and seamless, the color of old tin, and it has sat there so long that the difference between morning and afternoon is mostly opinion. At noon a brighter coin of gray appears somewhere overhead, slides a little, and dissolves. That is the sun here. The light that reaches the streets is the light of an hour after sunset, all day, every day, and it has been that way long enough that the whole place has reorganized itself around the dim.

The heat meets you at the rim like a wall leaning out. Down in the basin the air sits at forty-five degrees and near total saturation, and breathing it is a kind of work — thick, warm, tasting faintly of rust and ammonia and the sweet mineral breath of standing water. Nothing dries. Sweat doesn't evaporate; it joins the general wetness, which is the defining fact of the place: it almost never rains, but water is everywhere, because the air itself is a slow leak. Cold pipes carry rivulets down their length like veins. Every undersurface in the city wears a bead-line of drops, growing, merging, letting go. The water cycle here doesn't fall from the sky. It condenses out of the air onto anything that will hold still long enough.

Which is the whole point of the city. This is the Well — the region's name for it, though no region's people live here anymore. The Well is a dehumidifier the size of a valley. Its districts are organized around the fin fields: ranks on ranks of vertical black condenser fins, each two or three meters tall, kilometers of them in long rows like a forest with the idea of leaves removed. Refrigerant runs through them and keeps them the coldest things in the basin, and the warm saturated air, pulled through the ranks by slow fans, gives up its water onto the metal. It runs down the fins in threads, into gutters, into canals of green-black water moving warm and unhurried as blood through the lower districts, into sumps the size of ballrooms, where pumps — big, patient pistons, the city's only heartbeat — push it up the rim in stages, lift station to lift station, until it goes over the edge in a covered aqueduct and vanishes into the haze toward wherever the thirsty are. They stopped living here generations ago, when the wet heat crossed the line a body can't survive. But the plant was built to run unattended, and it does. The water still ships. Presumably someone still takes delivery.

Between the fin fields and the canal grid lies the built fabric: avenues a kilometer straight, plazas wide enough to hold weather, warehouses with the interior volume of cathedrals. And here is the strange thing about the Well — it is enormous and it is almost empty. A plaza may hold a single lamp, a single bench, a single sleeping cat. A warehouse may contain, in ten thousand square meters, one dripping pipe and nothing else. The residential blocks stand with their doors open and their interiors skeletonized — the wood long since eaten or rotted, the paint surviving only on protected undersides, everything organic reduced to its mineral remainder. A driverless freight crawler still runs its route on a buried schedule, headlights on in the permanent dusk, hauling condensate cisterns to the rim station with no one at the rim to wave at it. You can walk an hour down the central avenue and your only company is your own footsteps, and even those come back wrong — muffled, swallowed, deadened.

The deadening is the felt. This is what grows here instead of anything green: a low fungal-and-bacterial upholstery that lines every damp surface, a few millimeters thick on walls, a few centimeters in corners and along canal edges, in colors of rust, charcoal, bone, amber, and a violet so dark it reads as black. No green survives the dim — there isn't enough light in the light to feed a leaf — but the felt needs no light at all. It lives on grease and old hydrocarbon, on the organics the condensate picks up, on the city's slow shedding, on itself. It is faintly warm to the touch, metabolically warm, like a compost heap's patience. And it drinks sound. A city upholstered in the stuff is a city with no echo, a natural anechoic chamber the size of a valley.

The felt is the meadow, and the meadows are grazed. This is where the emptiness reveals itself as a lie of scale. Get close to any stretch of felt along a canal and you find it moving: isopods the size of your thumb, grazing in slow herds like sheep; springtails in their drifting millions; silverfish pouring away from your shadow like mercury; cockroaches of a dozen species, some small as sequins, some heavy enough to hear. The warehouse that seemed to contain nothing contains, on inspection, a floor that ripples. Moth larvae work the felt in lace. Slugs cross the plazas at night in caravans of slime that dry to silver calligraphy by morning. Over every sump, midges dance in dense balls, a meter across, humming just at the edge of audibility.

And everything that grazes is hunted. Ceilings across the whole city are freckled with geckos — every ceiling, every one, a speckled sky of them. Sheet-web spiders occupy every right angle ever built. Centipedes long as a hand patrol the gutters. Dragonflies quarter the canals, and toads sit the sump edges in rows, pulsing. In the black water, pale catfish surface to gulp air with soft, deliberate kisses, and there are more of them than the water should hold; drop anything edible in a canal and the surface boils. At the dimming of dusk — the deck going from gray to black, the only real event the sky offers — the bats come out of the hollow stack flues and the hollow fins in columns like smoke running backward, rivers of them, tens of thousands, carving through the moth storms that boil up around every burning lamp.

For the grid is still alive. Sodium lamps burn day-round across the wired districts, half of them dead and half alive in no pattern anyone chose, and each living lamp wears a cone of moths, hundreds deep, wings ticking against the housings like fingertips on paper. The bats work the cones. The cats work the rats. And the rats — the rats are the Well's true citizens, its inheriting class, moving at night in quiet streams along runs so old that the stone is polished with generations of fur and sebum, glossy baseboards of rat-traffic running the length of every wall. Lean feral cats hunt them, and an owl nests in Stack Four, and the whole enormous food web, from film to felt to roach to gecko to rat to cat, runs hot and continuous in the warm dark, more animal life per square kilometer than any forest ever managed — assembled entirely from the city's leftovers and the water it pulls from the air.

Yet for all that multitude, the Well is a quiet place, and the quiet is not an accident. It is a rule, enforced. The hunters here hunt by ear. The cats listen. The owl listens. The centipedes taste vibration. Generations of selection in this felt-upholstered dark have produced a city of soft feet: even the toads here don't call — they hum, low, a throb felt in the soles more than heard. The dogs, when there were dogs, barked themselves out of existence. The loud get eaten. What remains is a soundscape of near-nothing: the subsonic thud of the pumps, felt in the ribs before it's heard; drips at a dozen pitches in every enclosed space, an accidental music that never repeats; the tick of cooling metal; a far-off steam release like a long sigh; the hush of a rat-stream passing, like rain remembered.

There is weather, of a kind. Every few weeks the deck thickens and drops and it truly rains, warm and straight down, and the city flushes itself — gutters roaring, sumps rising, the pumps quickening their heartbeat until you can feel the change through your shoes. The felt blooms in a rush. Moth emergences follow in waves. The catfish go into a frenzy that can be heard two streets away. Then the rain stops, the deck seals, and the Well settles back into its long dripping equilibrium, which it has held, essentially unchanged, for longer than anyone's records run. Nothing is falling down anymore. What was going to fall has fallen. The concrete has weathered to rounded softness, calthemite stalactites hang under every bridge like a cave's beginning, the rust has stabilized into banded patinas that will hold another century, and the silt lies fine and sorted in every corner by the patient splash of drips. The place is not decaying. It is settled — a ruin that reached its final form and found it durable.

If there is a tension in the Well, it is only this: the pumps. As long as they thud, the water stays at ankle height in the low districts and the arrangement holds — machine, felt, swarm, cat, lamp, drip. If they ever stop, the basin takes the city back within a season, and the felt and the catfish inherit everything between the rim and the rooflines. Nobody watches for this. A control room in the pump district holds a wall of dead consoles and one live screen, updating faithfully for no one.

Stand on the rim road at dusk and look back. The lamps make a dim constellation across the valley floor, each one crowned with its boiling halo of moths. Steam and warm exhaust rise from the stacks in slow columns that flatten against the deck. A bat river bends overhead, soundless. The pump-thud comes up through the roadbed into your ribs, steady as a sleeping animal. Down there a cat sits on a warm rail over black water, watching the surface breathe. The Well is not dead, and it was never really abandoned. The emptiness turned out to be human-shaped — a vacancy exactly the size of one species — and everything else, everything quiet, moved in.
