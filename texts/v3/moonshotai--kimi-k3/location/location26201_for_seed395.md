From the rim, the first thing is how sharp everything is.

The old shorelines step down the inner slope in benches and strandlines — wave-cut notches, gravel bars, bleached shell-hash — and they look as though the sea drained last season. Nothing has softened them. There is no rain here to soften anything, no rivers to grade the slopes, no frost to split the stone. The basin below is not ancient-and-worn; it is raw, a floor that has not yet been touched by the processes that will someday make it a landscape. It is embryonic in the way a cut is embryonic: fresh-edged, unfinished, still deciding what to become.

The basin is continental. From the rim the far side is not a place but a condition of the light — a paling, a haze-line where salt and dust and distance become indistinguishable. The floor lies low, lower than the sea ever filled it, a sump of dense warm air. Out across it the pans gleam white and bone-grey, and rising from them, scattered at first and then gathered into a dark continuous mass at the basin's heart, are the districts — the Stand — shimmering in the afternoon heat like something half-imagined. Which it is. It is still being imagined. It has only been at this for a few thousand years.

The rule of the place is simple: the water left, and the life did not.

Some of it went up, long ago, into a sky that gives nothing back. Most of it went down — drawn into the fractured basement rock as the crust flexed under the sea's shifting weight, and there it remains, a deep cold brine threaded through cracks hundreds of meters below the pans. The surface gets none of it. No dew forms. No storm has ever crossed the rim. What the living world uses, it must lift itself, meter by meter, and what it lifts it never releases. Water here moves only from root to tissue to mouth to tissue again. It does not fall. A drop spilled on open ground is an event — things arrive for it within seconds, from under stones, out of cracks, on wings.

Cross the pans and the youth of the world is underfoot. Desiccation polygons, hexagonal plates of salt crust, their edges still crisp and curling, ticking and crackling faintly as the day's heat leaves them. Mirages make water out of distance — the only standing water you will ever see here, and it is made of light. Out on the largest flats walk the pan-walkers: tall, long-striding animals, sparse as ships, their humps and bladders swollen with hoarded water, crossing between districts over days. They are the long-distance arteries of the basin — carriers of seed, of salt, of chance. And they are how the Stand grows. When a pan-walker dies on the open salt, its body is a founding. Its stored water feeds a pioneer garden of salt-loving seedlings; its bones become scaffolding; within a decade the skeleton is furred over with young growth, within a century it is a thicket-island, within a millennium it has grafted itself onto a district or become the seed of a new one. Even their dung falls like seed-bombs — each one a wet nucleus around which the dry organizes itself. The city colonizes the desert by being carried across it, one body at a time.

Enter the Stand and the light steps down in green stages.

It is built of two materials, and both are alive. The welltrees are the first: not single trunks but fused colonial stands, thousands of stems merged into fluted columns with waxy, pleated rinds, their barrel cores swollen with stored water, their root-plinths diving through the cracked basin floor like foundations — down and down through the dark to the buried brine. They are hydraulic engines before they are trees. They drink the deep water, and everything else in the district drinks them. What they pull up is brackish, so they sweat the salt out through their rinds: beards and scurf and weeping crusts of it on the south faces, sloughing off in plates. Salt is the city's exhaust. The pans are, in part, its accumulated exhale.

The second material is the work of the builders — colonial animals who mix silt, salt, and their own mineral excretions into a ceramic harder than the basin's native stone. Their stilltowers rise between the welltrees, fluted and flued, hollow-chambered, grown rather than made. Termite-logic at the scale of architecture: stacks that draw the day's heat up and out, shafts that catch the cold night air sliding down from the rim and pull it through the deep levels. Bridges of fused root and woven fiber cross from crown to crown. Districts have grown into one another until there are no gaps, only gradations — canopy, mid-loft, under-gallery, tunnel-warren, root-cellar — and every surface at every level is occupied, layered, claimed. The density is total. There is no bare wall. There is no unused ledge. The understory is a cave-system with its own dimmer green daylight, its own citizens, its own weather of slow cold air.

And it is, unmistakably, a city. It has infrastructure. The grafted root-lines that run between districts are aqueducts — districts share water across them, trade it, in the oldest groves perhaps have shared it so long that the whole Stand is functionally one plumbing system with many mouths. It has reservoirs: cistern-chambers deep in the cool levels where a caste of animals hangs from the ceilings, swollen to translucence, living storage tanks tended and tapped by keeper-guilds and drawn down in lean seasons. It has wells — literally: chisel-beaked animals gnaw tap-holes into the water-wood of the welltrees, and at dawn the queues form, species standing in patient sequence at a weeping hole in a trunk, each drinking its fill and moving aside. The truce mostly holds. Mostly. The patient hunters know the wells too, and wait in the stillness nearby, and the wells are where the basin's quiet predator-prey arithmetic is worked out, one hesitant drinker at a time.

It even has rain — but only indoors, and only a few beads at a time. At night the flue-stone of the stilltowers cools to the black sky, and the humid air of the inhabited chambers yields its moisture against that cold stone, drop by drop, and the keeper-guilds make their rounds and drink it. The only precipitation this world has ever known falls inside its towers, before dawn, in the dark, and is gone by morning.

Nothing here is wasted, because waste is water leaving the system. A carcass is a vessel of water before it is meat, and it is opened accordingly — a relay of reclaimers arrives in a sequence so precise it reads like a timetable, each guild adapted to exactly one stage of the unmaking, until the bones themselves are gnawed for their marrow-moisture and the site is bare salt again. Nothing rots in the open. Rot is too expensive.

The sound of all this is almost none.

The basin is the quietest inhabited place imaginable. Afternoons: the hush of wind on salt, the faint crackle of the crusts cooling, heat-shimmer silence. The hunt, when it happens, happens in stillness — ambush, patience, a single brief scuffle muffled by the close-packed growth. Night: wing-hum of the big moths, the soft sipping at the wells, the slow creak of towers growing, the tick of stone, and under everything the long low sigh of the flues breathing — the city inhaling the cold air that pools down from the rim. Somewhere far off, now and then, a deep thump: the basin floor settling, the crust still rebounding from the weight of the sea it lost, the ground literally not finished yet. And if you press your ear to a welltree's root-plinth you can just catch it — the faintest seep-pulse, water climbing through wood in the dark. The loudest water in the world, and it is inaudible.

The days run on a strict breath. Heat builds over the pans; dust devils wander; the Stand seals itself shut, every pore closed, and waits. Evening brings the cold drainage off the rim, and the city opens — stomata flung wide, flues drawing, the whole mass of it inhaling at once. Seasons turn on the wind. There are dust storms that scour the outer walls and are answered with thicker salt-armor. And every few years — never on schedule — the wind shifts and crosses some far remnant of moisture beyond the rim, and arrives bearing air that merely remembers water. Not rain. There is never rain. But for one night, or a handful, the humidity rises a fraction, and the entire Stand responds: felt-crowns unfurling on the towers to harvest the passing damp, flowers opening by the million, mating flights filling the dark, wells weeping freely, cisterns swelling. The city calls it nothing, being a city of plants and animals, but everything in it behaves as though it were a festival. Then the wind swings back, and the sealing resumes, and the long dry reasserts its law.

Districts age. A stilltower fails, leans on the tilting floor, comes down — and the fall is a feast and a founding both, the rubble colonized before the dust settles, succession running in fast-forward because nothing here can afford to wait. Walk from a pan's edge to a district's heart and you walk through time: pioneer salt-garden, adolescent thicket, the high old groves, a collapse being eaten. All the stages side by side, none of them finished. The map is not done being drawn. The ground is not done moving. The experiment is young enough that you can feel its wager everywhere — that a world can lose its sea and refuse to follow it.

Because the sea is not gone. That is the last thing to understand about the basin. It is only hidden, and held. It lies in the dark fractures under the floor; it climbs the root-plinths; it stands in the barrel-trunks and hangs in the cistern-chambers and walks the pans in the bodies of the walkers; it beads, one drop at a time, on cold flue-stone before dawn. The city is the shape the sea makes now that it can no longer make waves. Once the water wore the living things within it. Now the living things wear the water, and carry it, and do not let it go.
