You hear the Stack long before you feel its warmth, and you feel its warmth long before you see anything at all — because, strictly speaking, you never see it.

The approach is across the Glacier-dark: high frozen plains where the only sound is your own breathing and the occasional deep, tectonic ping of ice contracting in the cold. Hours of that silence. Then, faint underfoot, a tremor — not yet sound, more like the memory of sound. Then the air, which has been dry as paper for days, begins to carry something: a low roar stitched through with a fine electrical crackle, like surf heard through a wall. Then the sting-fog finds you — supercooled droplets, liquid only because nothing has yet dared them to freeze, and they freeze on you gladly, feathering your lashes and the hairs in your nose with rime. You are crossing the breathline. A hundred paces later the fog is merely wet. Then it is drizzle. The rime falls off you in slabs. The black ground begins to weep, then to run. The roar resolves into ten thousand channels of moving water, and the crackle resolves into the graze, and you understand that both sounds are the same sound — the city — and that you have been inside it for some time.

There is no sun here, or none that matters. The old archives hold images, disputed. What the sky actually holds is the Lid: a low, permanent ceiling of black cloud, the Thaw's own exhalation condensed and returned, hanging a few hundred meters up like a sounding board. It keeps the warmth in, the drizzle cycling, the noise reflected lovingly back down onto the noise-makers. Very rarely — once in a generation — the Lid parts. This is called an Opening, and it is not celebrated. Stars mean the blanket is off. The temperature falls out of the sky, the breathline lurches inward, frost fur crawls across districts at a walking pace, and everyone in the Stack goes quiet and listens to the world's teeth grind until the Lid knits itself shut again.

Because of the graze, there is the Dark Law. The graze — the blind, finger-length swimmers that fill every channel in their uncounted billions — navigate partly by light, and an unshielded lamp draws them the way blood draws the rest of the food web: a boiling, a frenzy, a living eclipse. Clothwings batter themselves against brightness. So light is licensed, shuttered, ember-red, and rare. The people of the Stack are not crippled by this. They are adapted the way the whole Thaw is adapted: children learn echo before speech, tongues clicking off walls; maps are kept as songs, each street a melody of intervals and returns; privacy is achieved with sign-cant, or with whisper-hooks pressed to the jawbone. A visitor is lent a pair of clack-stones and a guide's elbow and told, kindly, to stop trying to look.

The plateau itself is barely born. A young uplift of black basalt and volcanic glass, tilted, joint-blocked, utterly raw — no soil anywhere, no rounded stone, nothing green ever, not even the idea of green. Frost-shatter has only just begun its patient work; the rivers have not carved valleys so much as scribbled across the surface and frozen mid-sentence. Into this unfinished geology the Thaw sits like a coin pressed into ice: a warm basin some twelve kilometers across, zoned in rings. A steaming core of open water. A ring of tepid canals under perpetual drizzle. A ring of rime-fur, where every surface wears white mineral feathers, harvested for water and insulation. A ring of sting-fog. The breathline. Then the ice, going on and on.

The water is the world's blood and it is everywhere near the skin. The plateau weeps — artesian seeps bleed from every scarp, warm with depth, brackish with dissolved stone. Walls condense; ceilings drip; the city sweats. At the rim, water performs its slow tricks: channels freeze into dams, pressure builds for years, the dam bursts, the flood runs a kilometer and freezes again mid-cascade, so that the outer rings are terraced with icefalls that take a decade to finish falling. Under the Lid the loop closes — what the canals exhale, the sky returns as drizzle. The Thaw drinks itself.

And the city: the Stack is not built so much as precipitated. Its material is growstone, a basaltoid mineral tuned out of the brine by lattice-song — shaped resonance, horn-choirs at the Growth Front singing hexagonal columns up out of the wet at a meter a decade. The columns rise near-touching, bridged and buttressed and carved within, cells stacked on cells, streets that are canals with narrow ledge-walks, districts layered over districts like music over music. Nothing stops accreting. Scraping crews work every facade in rotation; an abandoned structure becomes a reef within decades, film-choked, graze-thick, colonized. The saying goes: *the city eats the inattentive.*

At the center, the Firsthearth. Down a shaft older than the city, wrapped in field-works no living engineer fully parses, sits the Kept Warmth — an ember-kernel of something current physics has no polite name for, bleeding heat into the aquifer for longer than records run. Whoever kept it first is unknown; the Keepers now maintain it liturgically, by inherited procedure, and it works. It breathes. Across decades its output swells and shallows, and the breathline advances and retreats, and the city's calendar is kept in breath-decades. The shallow centuries have left their mark inside the living city: the Cold Galleries, whole districts entombed in clear ice when the warmth withdrew, street-fronts frozen mid-life, walked now by lamplight-of-special-license — archaeology of one's own great-grandparents. Beneath everything, always, the hearth's fundamental hum, felt in the bones of the inner ear, in the soles, in the teeth.

The ecology begins with film. On every warm wet surface: chemosynthetic mats in rust-red, sulfur-yellow, tar-black, the only primary producers this world has ever needed. The graze eats the film. Midges rise off the canals and the clothwings — tarpaper-winged, echo-netting, roosting in chattering thousands under every ledge — eat the midges, and their guano is scraped and fed back to the film-farms, because nothing here is wasted. Sump-kine, huge slow filterers half-domesticated, are walked through the deep channels by herders, lowing like foghorns. Bell-eels hum in the culverts, electrosensitive, prized and dangerous. And over it all the sheer *quantity*: you are never out of reach of something alive. Every mouthful of water is filtered first. Crossings crunch. Sleep happens in sealed cells behind silt-screens. When the graze phase-locks — the choir, they call it — whole districts ring like struck bells, and the Answerers go up the towers and sing it back out of tune.

Then there are the menders. Knee-high, many-legged, carrying the only commonplace lights in the city — dim ember status-dots no graze cares about. They are the feral descendants of the original construction swarms, and the distinction between animal and machine has been politely ignored by everyone for centuries. They graze film and re-deposit it as mineral. Half the unplanned architecture of the Stack is mender-work; they will re-point your house or wall it up entirely, depending on whether you keep your salt obligations. The Salt Tax is the one tax nobody evades.

The people themselves are heat-shaped. Warmth is rationed by ring and inherited like land; the inner districts are old, dense, layered, rich in every sense including temperature. Voices run low — the thin cold air up here carries bass better — and the language is freighted with click phonemes that double as sonar. Professions: soundwrights who grow the stone, scrapers who fight it, film-farmers, rime-harvesters, eel-hunters, breath-watchers who read the hearth, core-readers who drill the columns and date history by accretion rings, drummers, lamp-wardens. The Dark Law has its wardens; sound has its licenses, since in a city under a sounding board, your voice is a shared resource. Once a decade, by special dispensation, comes the Kindle: one great lamp lit above the core canal, and the whole city turns out to watch the swarm storm rise and smother it — a living eclipse, light devoured by life — until the wardens douse it and the roaring dark closes back over.

Time does not pass here so much as accumulate. Without days, the city keeps the tick — district bells marking shifts. Without years that mean anything, it keeps breath-decades. Buildings grow around their occupants; decisions are made on decadal scales; patience is not a virtue but a load-bearing structure. Core-readers say the Stack is less a place than a process, one long exhalation of the planet through the Firsthearth, and that nothing here is finished, including the world.

From the breathline at tick-change you can feel the whole of it. Behind you: hum, roar, click-floor, chatter, lowing, horn-choirs seeding a new column at the Growth Front, the market's haggled thunder, all of it pressed back down by the Lid into your skin. Ahead of you: the Glacier-dark, the only true silence. And underfoot, arriving hours late through the basalt, a drum-pulse — stone-speech, the network that stitches the Thaw to the other warm islands scattered across the frozen night. Some of those stations have been silent for centuries. Three turns ago, one that had been quiet for two hundred years began drumming again, in a dialect nobody recognizes. The Stack has been debating its answer ever since. There is time. There is always time here. The hearth breathes, the Lid drips, the graze clicks on, and the city holds its note.
