A day out on the plain there is nothing to see, and then there is something to hear. The land is warm dust and thorn scrub, flat as a griddle, the kind of country where sound travels farther than sight. What arrives first is a low chord, almost below hearing — the note a hand makes cupped over a bottle mouth, but held, and harmonized, and slowly turning as the wind turns. Then you see the birds: a gray wheel of pigeons and swifts rising from nothing, from plain, from a hole in the world. The hamlets a day's walk behind you call the hole the Throat. The old mason marks cut into its parapet are nine notches in a row, and the keepers still sometimes use the older name: Nineflute.

The rim is a parapet of small fired bricks, hand-sized, laid generations ago and whitewashed so many times the corners have gone soft as knuckles. Around it stand nine vent shafts like stubby chimneys, each a different width and height, each capped with a notched stone. The wind plays them the way a shepherd plays pipes, and which shafts sound, and in what combination, depends entirely on where the weather is coming from. One tree grows at the rim — half-dead, thorned, stubborn — and its roots have found the gap between parapet and shaft and gone down inside, where they hang in the light well as a dark curtain, drinking the damp off the air. The capping stones of the parapet are grooved where ropes have run for centuries. Below, the shaft drops in a square of daylight, and the daylight goes down farther than you'd believe, bouncing.

Because the whole interior is plastered in lime, and lime throws light. You descend a stair of brick-on-edge worn into shallow dishes by feet and paws, and the brightness follows you — falling in blades from angled light shafts, ricocheting off white walls, arriving at the third gallery down softened and golden, a working indoor daylight, neither dim nor glaring. The air changes as you go down. It gets warmer, not cooler: the Throat holds a steady blood-warmth year-round, the warmth of mass and breath and stored summer, and in the cold months the rim steams at dawn like a mouth. The smell is lime and wet dust, guano and wax and something green at the very bottom. And the sound rises to meet you — the cooing first, which is the room-tone of the entire underworld, then the drips, then the chord of the flutes overhead, all of it folded together by the shaft until the place seems to be humming a single long thought.

The first gallery is the Dove Ring, and this is where the density of the place declares itself. The walls are niches from floor to ceiling, each a hand's width, thousands of them, stacked like the cells of a honeycomb built by masons instead of bees — and every one is occupied. Nests on nests, generations deep, squabs in this one, eggs in that one, the oldest nests gone to powder and pottery-hardness at the back. The cooing is continuous and physical, a pressure on the chest. The birds shift when you pass, a rolling soft thunder of wings, and the flutes overhead answer with a wobble of pitch. Nothing here is unused. Every ledge has a tenant, every crack a traffic, every surface an assignment.

Everything you can see was made. The excavation, whenever it was cut, was lined to the last inch — brick, rubble core, plaster — and nowhere does raw earth show. The walls are stratigraphy of craft: plaster over plaster in layers a keeper can read like rings, every layer studded with the palm prints of whoever smoothed it, centuries of hands pressed into the walls until the galleries are crowded with people who are no longer alive. Repairs are made in kind, in the same bond, sometimes the same day; the oldest law of the place is that a brick which falls is relaid before dark. And yet the Throat is slowly, patiently making a cave of itself. Lime leaches from the old mortar and re-deposits as flowstone, as small pale stalactites, as rims of stone growing over the basins. The keepers scrape the walkways clear and leave the rest. Decay and deposition have long since come to an agreement. The building is eroding into a fossil of its own materials, and it is in no hurry.

There is no river here and no standing water except at the very bottom. The water table fell in the time of the keepers' grandmothers' grandmothers, and the Throat has lived since then on its own sweat. Warm air meets cool plaster and the ceilings bead over; the beads gather, find the drip-lines worn into flowstone, and fall. Under each drip sits a basin of fired clay, and each basin rings at its own pitch — the lowest gallery is a slow instrument, ticking in polyrhythm, never repeating, never silent. The keepers know which note belongs to which basin the way shepherds know bells. Water is drawn by measure, with a notched staff, and the notches are a record: the Throat's income, drip by drip, across the dry years. A few times in a decade the rains come hard, rain enters by the shafts, wooden sluices are thrown, and for one night the whole place roars like a held breath released. Then months of ticking again.

Below the Dove Ring is the Lamp Walk, its walls hollowed with thousands of oil-lamp cups blackened by wicks, and behind one particular brick — marked by a seam of wax gone dark — a bee colony has lived longer than anyone's memory. The hum is everywhere on that level, low and contented, and the air tastes faintly of honey. At dusk the geckos come out onto the plaster, pale and deliberate, to wait by the lamp niches for moths. Crickets hold the dark seams between the light shafts, and spiders string the drip-lines, catching what the drips disorient. The rule is lamps out by full dark. The moths' hour belongs to the geckos, and the Throat, the keepers say, has to sleep in its own voice, not yours.

At the bottom is the Sump, the Black Bowl: a jar-lined basin of still dark water in permanent dusk. Pale fish move in it — descendants, the story goes, of fish that came up with the old water, or that someone's ancestors released for luck; the story varies by hamlet. In rain years the frogs bloom down here and their chorus comes up the shaft like the place is shouting in its sleep. Above the waterline runs a smooth ledge, and the ledge belongs to the well-snake, who takes a nestling now and then and is tolerated, is even left offerings by custom, because something has to keep the doves honest. The keepers take the Sump's depth the old way: drop a stone, count to the answer. The depth-song is how the year is judged.

Sound is the Throat's weather and its clock and its news. The nine flutes' chord drops nearly a step when the pressure falls before rain, and every goatherd on the plain knows it: when the Throat sings low, tie the goats. At dusk the bats who hold the deepest gallery leave in a single exodus, a chimney of flutter going up the light well, and for a full minute the shaft's square of sky goes dark with bodies — while the swifts come screaming home down the same column, the two traffics threading each other in the dimming light, and the owls already waiting at the rim take the late and the slow. A shout in the shaft returns three times, and the saying is that the first two echoes are yours and the third belongs to the Throat, and should be answered politely. You call down before descending; you descend singing, so the birds shift and the keepers know where you are; you do not whistle below the Lamp Walk, because a whistle is too close to the alarm note and empties three galleries in a panic of wings.

The day has its machinery. Dawn: the Throat exhales, warm damp air rising to meet the cool plain, mist standing over the rim, swifts pouring out. Midday: stillness, the blades of sun creeping across the galleries at their appointed hours — the keepers time their rounds by where the light falls, second blade on the pigeon stair means midday — and for one short hour a shaft of sun reaches all the way to the Sump's rim, the only light the pale fish ever see. Dusk: the inhale, the bats, the owls, the geckos' shift beginning. And the year has its own: the roar of the rains, the long drip-counted drying, and the three harvests taken by rule and by turn — water by the notched staff, guano from the Dove Ring in the dry month for the hamlets' fields, honey after the thorn scrub blooms, the last comb always left. No cats below the rim. The snake keeps its ledge. Never count the bats aloud.

Two keepers at a time, changed by the moon, sent from the hamlets in rotation — that is all the human population the place holds, and they live in it less like owners and more like one more species with a niche, a little larger and a little more anxious than the doves. What is uncanny, down there, is the fit of things: how every hollow has its tenant, how the flutes' pitches sit exactly where the pigeons' coo reinforces them, how the whole artifact seems less built than grown into its own completeness. It was made for water and the water left, and the Throat did not die of it. It settled. It became warm, and loud, and crowded, and kept.

Evening now. The chord over the rim has slid down toward the rain note and a keeper is tying off the measure staff, humming so the galleries know her. The drips tick their soft chords into the basins. Somewhere deep, a brick lets go and clicks to the floor — and before dark, by the oldest law, someone will go down singing and lay it back. The Throat is warm as a held breath, full of wings and hands and honey and echoes, balanced exactly on the line between falling apart and turning to stone, and it keeps its own time, and the time is slow, and it is not running out.
