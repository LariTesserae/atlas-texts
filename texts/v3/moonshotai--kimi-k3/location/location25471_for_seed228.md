The plain above is flat enough to trust. Scrub, heat-shimmer, pale limestone breaking through thin soil — and one tree, a fig, standing alone where no tree should bother. The tree is the sign. It has been feeding on what is below for longer than anything on the plain has had a name.

You hear it before you reach it: a churn folded inside a hum, felt through boot soles as much as ears. Then the ground admits a hole. A collar of dressed stone rings it, knee-high, worn round on its outer face — and strangely sharp on its inner edge, as if the material there refuses weather. The opening is eight paces across. Warm air comes up out of it in a steady breath, wet, smelling of hot stone and something green-metallic, like the smell of a coin held in a sweating fist.

Below the collar, the ground opens into a bell. The neck narrows, then the belly swells: forty meters down to the water, seventy across at the widest, every wall of fluted limestone polished in horizontal ripples, as if the rock had been combed while soft. Mist stands in the shaft in slow-turning layers. And the chamber rains on itself — the warm breath rising from below condenses in the cooler neck, beads on the stone, gathers, and falls back as a fine internal drizzle that never stops and never wets anything that wasn't already wet. A weather system in a bottle. At midday a single column of sunlight drops through the eye and stands in the mist like a pillar of solid glass, and from below, out of the water, a second light rises to meet it — cyan-green, sourceless at first glance, so that the pillar seems to be lit from both ends.

The pool fills the whole floor. It is impossibly clear. Looking down from the one ledge, you see fifteen, maybe twenty meters to the bottom, and the bottom is not silt. It is a tessellated field — hexagonal plates the size of a spread hand, fitted edge to edge, with hairline filaments running between them that brighten and dim in slow traveling waves. The first thought is a drowned city. The second is a circuit. The third is coral. It is none of these; it is simply the floor, and it has been the floor for a very long time. The waves of light migrate across it like circulation, like thought, converging and dispersing without hurry.

The water itself is slightly wrong, in ways you notice only by staying. It is denser than it should be — a swimmer floats high, and the water seems to push back with opinion. Droplets on the warm walls sometimes slide sideways, or upward, before releasing. It tastes of metal, and it is clean in a way that natural water never is. This is because it is not entirely natural: the pool is full of engineered motes, small beyond seeing, and they have been filtering, pruning, and maintaining this one body of water since before the plain above had scrub on it. Nothing grows in it. Nothing rots in it. Anything left in it is, over days, gently carried down and set neatly on the lattice. The pool tidies.

And it breathes. That is the governing fact of the place. The cycle has no fixed period — minutes, sometimes an hour — but the shape is always the same. First the surface tightens, goes strangely smooth, and the water level drops a hand's width. The internal drizzle seems to hesitate. The whole chamber holds its breath, and in that held breath the loudest things are single drips, pitched by their fall. Then the filaments in the floor surge bright, the traveling waves all turning inward at once, and the pool exhales: a dome rises at the center, becomes a column of bath-hot water, bursts partway up the bell — never reaching the ledge; the margin is exact and ancient — and collapses into a radial slop that slaps the fluted walls hard enough to feel in the chest. Mist is blasted upward; some of it jets out of the eye, so that from the plain the ground appears to smoke. The roar in the stone bell is enormous, folded, doubled back on itself. Then the water settles, the filaments resume their slow migration, and the interval begins again — never twice the same length, yet never random either. There is a near-pattern in the timing. Listening for it becomes a way of being there.

Except at noon. When the column of sunlight stands on the center of the pool, the machine goes still. The surface glasses over. The lattice brightens all at once, evenly, drinking. The roar thins to the ticking of drips, and the hush that fills the bell has weight — you can hear the small machinery of your own body in it. This is the window, and everything about the human presence here is arranged around it.

Because people have come. Below the collar, a stair of footholds spirals a short way down the neck, worn to shallow dimples, almost erased. At the waterline, five square sockets are cut in a row — for a dock, a gate, a raft; nothing remains to say. And on the ledge, the one dry seat in the chamber, there is a hollow worn into the stone by the sitting bones of generations. Whatever else those people brought down here, the pool has long since tidied away. One artifact remains in reach: a ring of grey metal set flush in the ledge rock, palm-wide, uncorroded, warm to the touch. It hums faintly with each breath of the machine, and it does not stop humming even in the noon stillness. It might be a handle. It might be a gauge. It might be the one mouth the machine keeps at the surface.

Life keeps its distance. You could count everything alive in the room before dusk. The fig above sends down a single root-cable, thick as a mast, furred with ferns above the mist-line and bare and streaming below it, ending in a pale brush that drinks from the pool. Moss holds in two bright bands where the light-column grazes the walls. That is the vegetation, and there is a reason it is so sparse: the mist carries the motes, and seedlings touched by the mist fail. The chamber prunes its own biology. In the deep water, forty-odd blind white fish turn slowly as a single pale coin, grazing a faint film the lattice exudes between breaths. A dozen bats keep a cleft at the rim and spill out at dusk. Now and then a heron drops through the eye at noon, fishes the glassy calm, and leaves before the breath returns — the animals, too, have learned the schedule.

The sound is the place's true inhabitant. Under everything runs the machine's carrier tone, felt in the teeth and in the metal of the ring more than heard. Over it, the drizzle ticks at a dozen pitches, the bell shape folding every drip back into the air shaped like some other sound. During the pulses you shout to be heard by the person beside you. After rain on the plain above — and rain is frequent — the rim runs with brief waterfalls, the pool swallows the flood without rising more than a finger's width, the pulses pause as if the machine were attending to something, and the first breath afterward is enormous, the pool clearing its throat. Everything that enters the water cycle here stays in it. The water is old. It is largely the same water, recounted for millennia.

Nothing is young except the motion. The fluting on the walls is the turbulence written into stone, ripple by ripple, over spans that made the storm into geology. Every edge is rounded; every form is final. The collar at the rim is the only thing down here that hasn't weathered, and it isn't quite stone. Below the lattice, the filaments continue downward into dark — this one lit cell is not the whole body of the thing. What it is doing, no one can say: keeping the water, perhaps, or being kept by it. Once, you watch a ripple outlive its cause — the slap has died against the walls, the chamber has gone quiet, and a single chevron of wave still stands mid-pool, holding its shape like a held note, for a minute, for two. Then it lets go.

Stay through a noon. Feel the roar thin, the light arrive, the lattice drink. Put your palm on the warm ring while the bell holds its breath and the drips count time and the bright coin of sky burns at the top of the dark. The stillness is not peace; it is attention. Then the filaments surge, the water gathers itself, and the storm resumes — the storm that has lasted so long it has become a climate, a climate so stable it has become a room, a room so old it has become, almost, a presence. Everything here moves. Nothing here changes. It will be breathing when you climb out, and it will be breathing long after the plain forgets you were ever on it.
