The lake lies in a high green bowl where the snow is never entirely gone. From the rim — a walk of scree and wind-flattened grass, a few pines crouching at the tree line — it looks like nothing: a tarn, three hundred paces across, the color of bottle glass, with a white stream falling into it from the snowfields and a rock lip at the far end where the water slides out and becomes a creek again. A heron stands in the shallows. The surface gives back sky.

Then you go under, and the sky becomes a ceiling of hammered silver, and you understand that the tarn is not empty. The tarn is occupied.

The old village lies across the bowl floor in green, readable light. A dozen roof-lines, no more — it was always a hamlet — spaced wide apart with meadows between them, because even before the water came these people liked room around their houses. The walls are dry-laid stone, waist-high and chest-high, rounded now, every edge softened by centuries of silt and slow current. The upper walls were timber and are mostly gone, but here and there a roof survives whole: stone-slab or waterlogged oak shingles gone black and hard as iron in the cold, furred an inch deep in moss, so that the houses look less like ruins than like hills that have decided to be rectangular. Doorways stand open and are used. Fish go in and out of them all day with the unhurried assurance of tenants.

Everything is plant. That is the first law of the place and the second and the third. The meadows between the houses are chara — stonewort — a crisp, jointed turf that crackles faintly under a bare foot and smells, even through the water, of something mineral and clean. On bright afternoons the whole meadow pearls over with oxygen, each leaf beaded, and the beads rise in strings and flatten against the ceiling and slide there like mercury. Along the old mill-leat, where the one fast thing in this slow world still runs — the inflow stream, entering the lake, remembers its channel and pushes through the village as a clear, cold current — the water-crowfoot grows in banners ten feet long, white-flowered, streaming and snapping in the flow like laundry in wind. The lanes are hedged. Pondweed, dense as boxwood, clipped flat on top and at the sides, marks every path and property line exactly where they ran when the lanes were mud and the clippings were taken by goat. The clipping is still done. That is the thing that takes a moment to settle: the hedges are maintained.

The willows are the strangeness, if there is one. They line the plaza — cobbles under the chara turf, a boundary stone at each corner — and they never broke the surface. They were saplings when the water rose, and instead of drowning they simply stayed, and now they leaf underwater, small translucent leaves the color of pale jade, and they are pollarded every few years by hands, their withies cut for weaving, so that they keep the gnarled, many-knuckled shape of worked trees. A willow takes a lifetime to train. The people who cut them are cutting trees their grandparents cut.

The people live at the shore, in stone huts above the waterline that they call, without poetry, the landing. The village below they call the lower village, and it is not a ruin to them, and it is not a grave, and it is not a mistake they intend to fix. Their grandparents' grandparents moved up the slope when the landslip dammed the creek and the water came up over seasons, slow enough to carry out the beams and the seed grain, and then, having moved, they simply — kept tending. Nobody decided this. It is the kind of thing that is never decided, only continued, and by now it has been continued for so long that the tending is the culture.

They dive on one breath. That is the unit of time down here: not the hour, the breath. A diver greases her skin with fat, takes the cold — and it is cold, snowmelt cold, a cold that narrows the mind to a point — and goes down through the green to do one thing: reweave a panel of fish weir, cut an armload of withies, thin a mussel bed, lift a net. Forty breaths across a morning finishes a weir panel. Each diver carries two flat stones at the belt, and the knock-code carries through the water farther than sight does: one knock, *here*; two knocks, *look at this*; three, *up, now*. In a place where you can see your neighbor as a pale flicker across the whole bowl, sound is how the village talks to itself, and it is a loud village. The inflow falls into the lake with a roar the water keeps, a low pressure hum you feel in your teeth. The crowfoot reaches hiss and bubble. In spawning dusk the big charr gather over the meadows and drum — a swim-bladder thrum, deep as a hand on a stretched hide, dozens of them, until the water itself seems to be a drumhead being tuned.

And there is the bell.

The chapel stands at the deep end, stone-built, roofed in slate that the cold has kept, its tower rising to within a fathom of the ceiling. The bell is bronze, the oldest made thing in the valley, older than the flood, and it hangs in the tower arch under the water. In the weeks of spate, when the snow comes off the high fields and the lake rises and the current pushes through the arch, the bell swings and speaks — a great green note, muffled and enormous, that the divers feel in the breastbone before they hear it, that the charr answer by drifting into the chapel windows as if called. By hand it is rung once a year, on the first dive of the season, a diver hauling the rope and letting the note roll up through the ceiling and out across the cirque. The rest of the year it hangs silent over the apse, where, under the altar slab, lives a charr the length of a man's leg, uncounted years old, which no one takes. That is a rule. The otters take their share of every weir and are not pursued. That is also a rule, called the otter's share. The mussel beds — and the beds are gardens, rotated, thinned, never stripped — are closed in spawning season, as are the meadows. Nothing is fought here. Things are scheduled.

The fauna is thick in a way the sparse building only sets off. Crayfish process across the chapel floor in the evening like a slow congregation. Dippers — the small dark birds of the snowfield streams — come down through the ceiling walking, and work the lanes on foot, flying underwater from wall to wall, the only other citizens who treat the boundary as no boundary at all. Grebes spear through like thrown javelins. The heron, seen from below, is a pair of stilts and then a lightning strike. Newts hang in the willows. Leeches, caddis in stone cases, snails grazing the moss-fur of the roofs into mown patterns — the whole layered, patient economy of cold clear water, established, in balance, so old in its arrangements that even the silt has found its level: the inflow delta advances a pace a year, no faster, and has swallowed one house in living memory and will take the lower lane in a century or two, and this is known, and the gardens are already being set a little upslope.

Above the gardens, small floats stand tethered on lines — dried bladders, gourds — bobbing a fathom down over the rows like patient balloons, marking whose is whose.

In winter the ceiling seals. The light goes gray-green and dim, and the village belongs to the fish and the slow ticking of the cold, except when the ice shifts and the whole lake booms — a cannonade rolling around the bowl, loud enough to hear at the landing through the soles of the feet. Then in the melt the ceiling breaks, the silver comes back, the spate rings the bell, and a diver comes down through the green light trailing bubbles, grease-shiny, numb-lipped, carrying two stones and a knife and one long breath, and knocks once against the chapel wall: *here.*

The hedges are clipped. The weirs hold. The old fish sleeps under the altar. The lake is not something that happened to the village. It is something the village is, now, and slowly — at the speed of willow growth, of silt, of a bell learning to ring itself — becoming more so.
