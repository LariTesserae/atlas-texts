From the low country, days away, it reads as weather that forgot to move: a white mass on the high horizon, cloud-capped, generating its own sky. Travelers who climb to it find that the white is not snow. It is stone — wet stone, an entire plateau of it, a continent's raised back, holding more standing water than the lands below hold rivers. They call it the Cisterns, and the name is wrong only in implying a maker.

The mind reads it as a city before it reads it as a land. That is the first thing. Basins nested against basins, near-touching, stacked in tiers like theater seating, descending in long flights; rims shared between pools the way walls are shared between houses; channels running rim to rim, some of them raised clear above the ground on levees of their own making, so that water crosses the land on elevated causeways like aqueducts; deep round cisterns where the flow rests; narrow slots between old terraces where water moves in shadow, alley-width. There are districts. There are plazas — broad, shallow sheets where the water stands ankle-deep and perfectly still. There are stairs, in a sense: spill-lips worn into smooth weirs, each pouring into the next basin down. The infrastructure is dense, layered, maintained. Nothing with hands built any of it.

The mason is water, and it has never stopped working. The seeps rise cool and mineral-heavy through the plateau's body, and where they reach the air they give up their dissolved stone. The giving-up is not purely chemical. At every waterline lives a fringe of moss and a film of life thinner than breath — the only real citizens here, countless and invisible — and these films do the laying-down, grain by grain, the way coral secretes a reef. The city is built by its inhabitants, biologically, at the pace of stone. Where a rim overflows, the lip grows, because lip is where the water is thinnest and the film busiest. The basins therefore build their own walls. The channels build their own banks higher. Nothing is designed and everything is organized, organized the way water insists on organizing: hierarchically, patiently, without appeal.

And it is finished. That is the strange, settled thing about it. This is not a young formation throwing itself upward, and it is not a ruin. Stone is laid down here as fast as stone is taken away; the forms hold their shape the way a flame holds its shape — by moving. The rims are rounded, the weirs worn smooth, the corners soft. A breach, when it comes, is repaired within years, the new deposit rounding over into the old like scar tissue. Walk the terraces and nothing looks fresh and nothing looks broken. It has been in its own state for longer than any memory below, and it means to stay.

The fabric of it, up close: stone the color of ivory, of cream, of old paper, going ochre and rust where iron touches it, going gray-green where shade keeps it damp. Underwater it looks like poured milk-glass. The pools themselves come in two tempers — the small ones clear to the bottom, every pebble on their floors lensed and trembling, and the great settling basins faintly milky, opalescent, the color of the sky diluted in stone. Because every pool is a mirror in still air, the plateau holds more sky than sky: tens of thousands of small skies, one per basin, so that crossing it on foot is walking through doubled light.

There are quarters that have gone dry. The water shifts its duties over centuries — a channel clogs with its own deposit, finds a lower course, abandons a flight of terraces — and what remains is not ruin but retirement: white, intact, silent districts where the basins hold dust and silt and thin lawns of sedge instead of water. These are the walkable streets. Cotton grass grows in the old pool floors, cushion plants sit tight as upholstery on the rims, lichen crusts the walls in rust and ash-green, very slowly, the only graffiti. There are no trees anywhere on the plateau — the ground is waterlogged mineral, the wind is unpromising, and roots drown in stone-water — so nothing interrupts the sightlines. You can stand on a dry rim and see weather arriving from two days away.

Vegetation stays sparse even in the wet quarters: the moss fringe at the waterline, which is the workforce; sedge in the silted shallows; nothing taller than a boot. Fauna is rarer than vegetation, and every sighting is an event. A dipper — a bird the size of a fist — working a spillway, walking underwater on the weir-stone as though the air down there were unnecessary. A heron standing in a cistern like a piece of abandoned furniture, once in a season. Far off on the dry tiers, a string of goat-like shapes, gray on white, gone before you finish counting them. Midges hatching over the warmest — least cold — pools in the brief afternoons. And prints, sometimes, in the silt of a basin floor: a broad, round track of something that travels alone and is never seen. The silence absorbs all of this without strain.

The silence is the medium here; it is not an absence. The water moves thinly, in sheets and seeps, and thin water makes almost no voice. What you hear, when you stop walking: a drip ticking from a rim-nose somewhere below, irregular, patient. The low hiss of a sheet-flow crossing a weir. Your own breath, visible. In the cold hours before dawn, ice skins form on the stillest basins and tick and knock as the light comes. A pebble, dislodged by nothing, clacking once down a dry flight. Wingbeats, occasionally, loud as a shout. And on certain winds, from very far off, a rumor — the Outfall, the one canyon where all of this finally gathers itself into a single river and goes over the edge of the world in earnest. It is said you can hear the Outfall from a day's walk away, and that near it, the noise is like standing inside a bell. Nowhere else on the plateau is loud. The loudest ordinary event here is a breach — a rim giving way under melt-pulse, a basin emptying through the gap for an hour in a brief local roar — and that happens perhaps once in a lifetime within earshot of any given place. Then the films mend it, and the quiet resumes.

The plateau is large enough to keep its own weather, and it does. All day the basins breathe moisture upward; by midday the evaporation rises in long streets aligned with the terrace grain, and by afternoon there is a cloud cap over the whole country, self-generated, which the lowlanders use as a landmark. The cap condenses on the rims at night, so heavily that even rainless mornings begin with dripping — every lip and nose of stone weeping its harvest back into the pools. Rain, when it comes, is instantly admitted to the plumbing: there is no runoff here in the wild sense, only a vast mild quickening, every channel taking its share, the whole system swelling by a finger's width and subsiding. In the cold season the high seeps slow, ice lenses the still water, and the plateau ticks and knocks at dawn like a house. In the melt season the pulse comes down from the rim-snows and every weir runs full and the milky basins go milkier. It is always, always in motion. The stillness is only the motion's patience.

The rules of the place are few and total. Water does all the work. Nothing stands that is not maintained; nothing dry is dead, only off duty. Everything here overflows, eventually, into everything else. And nothing is owned, because there is no one to own it — only the films, laying down their grain a day, indistinguishable from the stone they serve.

From the eastern edge, where the terraces end in an escarpment and the land below spreads out dim and enormous, you can watch evening arrive across the plateau's own weather: the cloud cap lowering, the light going level and gray-gold, each basin taking its last color from the sky and holding it after the sky has let it go. Then dark, ordinary dark, and stars doubled — one field above, tens of thousands of fragments below, one in every pool. Somewhere a drip. Somewhere, faintly, ice forming. The water moves all night at the pace of stone, building the city no one lives in, keeping it exactly, exactly as it is.
