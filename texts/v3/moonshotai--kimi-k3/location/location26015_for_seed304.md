The land here has only just stopped being a lake. You can read that on the way in. The basin walls carry pale strandlines, one above the next, like rings a bath leaves, and the flats below them are silt so young it has not yet decided to be ground. Rain has not learned this land — there are no gullies, no worn channels, only sheet and ooze and a shine of standing water that comes and goes with the weather. The one sharp thing in sight is the scarp: a raw step of broken rock along the basin's east side, unweathered, still shedding pebbles after every rain, its fractured face glinting with seams of chert. The ground here shifted within living memory, an old woman will tell you, or her grandmother's memory. Everything else about the place follows from that.

You cross the last of the flats on a causeway of woven hurdles that sways underfoot, and your footprints fill behind you with cloudy water. Then the ground whitens.

It begins as a crust — thin at first, cracking like ice over the silt, then thickening into solid apron, a broad low dome of pale stone that the water is building from underneath. Springs rise through it, spread, evaporate, and lay down their mineral freight, and the apron grows a hair's breadth a season, rim over rim, terrace over terrace, white as a bone and faintly, strangely warm to the palm. Where the water threads across it, films of rust-orange and bottle-green algae streak the white, the only saturated colors in the whole basin. The crust grows fastest where those films grow. The stone here is partly alive.

On the apron's crown, packed onto it wall to wall, is the Rime.

It is a town with no streets. Some hundreds of houses stand shoulder to shoulder in a single mass, sharing walls, roof touching roof, and the roofs are the streets — a rolling field of low pale domes and shallow pitches, guttered, ladder-poked, dotted with peat stacks, drying racks, and shallow trays of tended green. You can cross the whole town on foot in the time it takes to boil a pot, and you can see its every edge from any rooftop: the marsh below with its dark rush-beds, the bare flats beyond, the strandlines on the far wall. It is a small world, complete, bounded on all sides, and crammed full. Above the roof-field rises one taller thing: the Headhouse, a dome of accreted stone over the town's eldest spring, glistening, dripping, half grotto already, with a heron standing on its crest as often as not.

The houses are made of the place itself. The crust is soft when young, and the Rimers pound it, slake it into slurry, and trowel it back into walls — the spring's work done over by hand. Walls run thick and grow thicker, because the seep water wicks up through everything, evaporates, and leaves its stone behind. A doorway built to a man's height becomes, in twenty years, a tunnel four feet deep, its threshold rising, its lintel scarred where generations have chiseled it back open. A floor grows upward like a slow tide. Abandoned rooms do not decay; they fill — curtains of stone from the ceiling, rims around the puddles, the beginning of a cave. The town is a cave system being built instead of dissolved.

Which means the town's sound is the chisel. Every cool gray morning, most mornings of the year, the Rime ticks. Hundreds of chert chisels on hundreds of thresholds and gutters and spring-eyes, each at its own pitch, so the whole roof-field chatters faintly like a clockmaker's shop heard through a wall. They soak their hands in the runnels to keep them working — the stone-water rises a few degrees warmer than the air, hand-warm, and at dawn the whole town breathes mist off its wet surfaces. In high summer, when evaporation is strong and the crust grows fastest, comes the Round: weeks of communal cutting, called by a stone clapper, when every able hand owes days to the common works — the gutters, the cistern mouths, the spring-eyes — because the one law no one survives breaking is this: never dam the water. Give it a path. Seal a seep in your floor and it will come up through your neighbor's wall, or under the cistern, or into the grain pit. The town's true infrastructure is not its roofs but its water-paths: a negotiated lattice of runnels, drips, and gutters that everyone maintains and no one owns, and the people who map it — the Hearers, who go stooping with ears pressed to floors after every tremor — hold more authority than any chief.

For the ground still turns. Every few years a shiver runs through the basin, the young faults adjust, and the water chooses new ways. Three winters back it left the Low Row entirely and came up through the floor of the weaving-house in the night; the weavers keep their looms on stilts now and are counted fortunate, since a house that receives the water receives also its harvest — for the Rimers crop their stone like a crop. Slabs are pried off soft and cured hard in the air, stacked on the roofs, poled out through the marsh channels to the trade landing: paving, troughs, hearth-stones, and from the throats of the spring-eyes themselves the dense white eye-stone, precious, hard as tooth, which makes querns that distant villages prize. Out go the slabs; in come grain, timber, salt, ochre, and once in a while a copper awl that passes hand to hand for years. Timber is the dearer for the land's bareness — a roof beam may carry the mortises of three former lives — and nothing green grows wild on the crust at all.

The green of the Rime is in custody. It comes up in baskets, silt from the marsh edge, spread in roof trays: gray cress, a bitter sedge, onions no bigger than thumbs. Below, at the marsh, the only profusion the basin allows — dark rushes for thatch and basketry, sweet tubers that the children dig, peat cut in summer and stacked on the roofs to dry. Beyond the marsh, the flats lie bare as poured slip, tufted only here and there with sedge like iron filings.

And the animals are few enough to know by name. The heron on the Headhouse is an office more than a bird — when one dies, another comes, and takes the same name, and the town watches it the way other towns watch a flag: while the Heron stays, the water is sweet. Small whiskered fish come up the runnels from the marsh at night, into the town's very gutters, and children take them in cupped hands. In the buried rooms live pale spiders and little else. Twice a year the passage flocks come down on the marsh and the Rime is briefly, shockingly loud — nets, smoke, feathers, weeks of glut — and for two or three weeks in early summer the midge hatch fills the air with the year's only buzzing, and the Rimers sweep the midges up and press them into dark cakes that keep. The rest of the year is quiet.

It is a particular quiet, the quiet of wet things. Water absorbs; thick walls muffle; rain is frequent and soft, often falling straight out of bright gray sunlight, so that the commonest weather of the Rime is a shining wet, and a rainbow over white stone is no rare thing. Sounds carry oddly — a neighbor's whole life heard faintly through a shared wall, intimate and unintelligible — and the Rimers speak low by habit, since a raised voice means one of two things: a hurt child or a new spring. Beneath everything runs the ticking: drips into cisterns, the chisel mornings, and at night, from inside the walls themselves, a faint mineral tick as the water threads new veins through the stone. The wall is drinking, they say, and they mean it fondly, mostly. But sometimes, rarely, there comes a crack — a floor settling, a slab giving way — and at that sound the whole town stops. Chisels hang in the air. Everyone listens, and then everyone looks at the Hearers, because a crack is news: the water has moved.

They keep two waters, the Rimers, and the distinction runs through everything. Stone-water rises from the ground, warm, white-making, good for building, bathing, soaking rush and clay; it is everyone's and no one's and must be given its path. Sky-water falls, and is caught in gutters and cisterns, and is soft and sweet and drinkable, and goes first to infants and the ill. At dusk in the cool of the year, the terraced bathing-runnel below the Headhouse steams faintly and fills with quiet talk — the town's one warm public hour, since the Rime is a cold-handed place: wool always damp, breath visible at dawn, coughs and aching joints the common inheritance. Against which the Rimers hold one secret comfort. The floors never freeze. The warm seep keeps the deep rooms mild all winter, and so the people sleep low and live high, days on the windy roofs, nights below.

Below is where the town keeps the rest of itself. Under every house, down stairs that were roof-hatches once, are the down-rooms: older rooms, buried by their own rising floors, given over to cisterns, storage, winter sleep. And under those, older still. The Rime is a tell forming in real time; your roof stands higher than your grandmother's did, and her roof is now somebody's cellar. The lowest rooms of the oldest houses are the archives, and this is the strange wealth of the place: the floors remember. Whatever touches wet rime-stone is kept. In the lamplight the Keeper of Floors can show you a willow leaf pressed flat a century gone, from a tree that no longer grows anywhere near; the knotted-cord mark of some forgotten package; a baby's handprint from the town's third generation; the footprints of a child who grew up, grew old, and died in the rooms overhead, still walking across her grandmother's kitchen. Nothing here is demolished and nothing is erased. It is only buried, and whitened, and read.

And at the very bottom of the oldest houses are the coated. The dead of the Rime go down — there is no ground to spare for graves, and no need. A niche is cut in a deep wall, the dead are laid in, and the water does the rest; within a generation the rime has sealed them into the white, stone sarcophagi grown in place. For years after a death the family chips the face-plaque clear each winter, a small ceremony, a few minutes of the tick-tick that is the town's whole music. Then comes the year they let it close. Gone under, they say, and it is not a sad phrase: the town is made of its ancestors in a way that is literal, load-bearing, underfoot. The lowest stones of every wall are somebody.

The Rimers' year turns on all of this — the Hatch, the Round, the Passage, the long Quiet of winter when the deposition slackens and the stories come out in the warm down-rooms. Nothing here is finished. At the marshward edge, ghost houses stand silted to their roof-holes, surrendered, filling slowly, tenanted by spiders; at the crown, new rooms are being troweled onto stone that was air a decade past. The whole town climbs itself the way the apron climbs the basin, a slow fountain of white, a colony of a thousand people living packed and hushed on the back of a spring — farming stone, harvesting quiet, chipping their doorways open against the patient rise of the ground.

Stand on the Headhouse roof at night and you will understand the arrangement. It is very dark — lamps are few, oil is dear — and the cool air smells of wet stone and peat smoke and, faintly, of the eggy breath of the spring-eyes. Somewhere below, a drip keeps time in a cistern. The Heron shifts on the dome and croaks once. Inside the wall beside your hand, so faint you feel it more than hear it, the stone ticks as the water threads it, and the whole packed mass of roofs and rooms and sleepers and ancestors settles infinitesimally and grows, and remembers. If rain begins — and it usually does — the people below do not wake. They only listen, half in sleep, the way you listen to a house you have lived in all your life, to be sure it is the sky that is speaking, and not the ground.
