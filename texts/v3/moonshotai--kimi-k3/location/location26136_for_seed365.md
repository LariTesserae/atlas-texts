Up on the plateau, the world is ordinary — wind, grass, a hard bright sky, herds moving like weather along the horizon. The wind is the loudest thing for miles, and it is very loud. Then the land simply opens. A seam, a darker seam exhaling mist, no wider than a street in places, dropping eighty meters into green dimness. The people who live down there call it the Hush, and everything up here — the wind, the herds, the screaming kites, the whole sky — they call the Loud. You stand on the lip a long time before you find the trail, because the trail is not built to be found. It is notched into the fluted wall and padded with bark-felt, and the first thing you learn, before the first step down, is to place your feet as if the rock were asleep.

It isn't asleep. It's young. That's the first strangeness: the slot is a fresh wound in old limestone, a few thousand years bitten out of the plateau edge, and everything about it is still sharp. The walls stand in pale fluted columns, bone-gray, unweathered, organ-pipe straight, weeping at their bedding planes. Where the seeps spill, the rock is busy rebuilding itself — tufa aprons growing a finger's width in a decade, crusting over ferns, swallowing old stair-posts. Scree lies where it fell, angles unsoftened. Above the falls at the head of the gorge is the New Cut, the raw amphitheater where the knickpoint is still eating backward into the plateau; no one goes there, because the world there is unfinished and sheds. Down on the floor, the people's maps are knot-cords, and the cords must be re-knotted after every soft year, when the rains come hard and the walls calve and pools drown and new pools open. The elders say: the ground here is a draft, not a text.

There is no river through it, and yet water is everywhere, holding itself in strange ways. The falls — they call it the Speaker — runs strong only in the fog season and after rim-storms, falling twenty-five meters into a churning plunge basin, and dwindles the rest of the year to a silver seam. The floor pools breathe. They are karst windows, and they rise a meter overnight and drop away by morning, sighing mud and bubbles, gaining and losing residents — a pale blind swimmer here, and once, famously, an eel that simply appeared in a household's washing pool and was adopted. At the foot of the gorge the stream does not leave. It slides into a black arch called the Swallow and goes down into the mountain, gulping softly like a drinker. And every night at dusk the Swallow inhales. The whole karst system breathes on a daily cycle, and the slot is one lung of it: cold air drains, plateau fog pours over the lip and down the walls in slow white cataracts — the milk, they call it — and by midnight the gorge is drowned in milk to its third story, and everything drips. Nothing here drinks rain. The water comes from air, from stone, from below.

Which is why the trees are wells. The floor of the Hush is a colonnade of tank-trees — giant rosettes, each a solitary spire of stiff cupped leaves taller than a man, spaced meters apart like columns in a nave, lush beyond reason and yet with bare mud and snail-crossed stone between them, so that the grove feels less like a forest than a congregation. Each tree's heart holds a cistern of fog-water, liters of it, and each cistern is a tiny world: a crab that lives nowhere else, larvae, a particular taste — the people sip a tree to know it, the way you'd know a well. The old trees have names and genealogies. Water-law is the oldest law: the water belongs to the tree, and the tree belongs to the hand that tends it, and to kill a tank-tree is to burn someone's well in a land without rivers. At dusk the trees close their hands — the rosettes fold shut over their buds against the nightly frost, because this high, every night is winter and every noon is summer — and a tree that stays open after dark is sick, and the whole settlement knows by morning.

The light is wrong, and it is wrong in both directions. By day it comes down weak and green-gray, filtered through milk and lichen curtains, an undersea light, and for less than an hour around midday the sun clears the lip and sends one blade of light down the slot — the Blade — a hard-edged shaft full of drifting moth-dust that walks across a particular polished wall while everyone who needs true light works fast: the silk-fine weavers, the pigment-grinders, the clock-keeper cutting the mark where the Blade's edge touches. In fog season the Blade may not come for days; those are gray counts, tallied on cords. And then at night the light reverses. The pools glow — a faint cold green-blue, microbial, brightest where the water is still — and the gorge is lit from below like a thing held over embers. The dead go into the pools, given to the karst throats that connect to the Swallow, and after a funeral the glow always brightens, a bloom, and no one finds this frightening. The dead keep the lamps lit. That is simply what lamps are.

The animals are beyond counting and almost entirely without voice. This is the deepest fact of the place: the gorge is crammed with life and nearly silent, because the life here does not use the air. The walls are furred with moths — dozens of kinds, hand-sized, shaggy as the lichen they mimic — and the walls shiver, visibly, wings trembling, and shed a continuous soft snow of scales that powders every ledge and shoulder. Emergences come in pulses tied to the Blade's warmth, and in the fog-season peak, Fat Week, the air is thick as soup and the people roast and dry and store moth against the lean months. Giant snails cross the mud flats on silver handwriting, and when something dies the snail-tide gathers — hundreds converging at a crawl until the carcass wears a slow living carpet, gone in a day; children learn time by it. Big-eyed gliders commute between the tank-trees on stretched skin and speak by drumming their heels against the wood, a code in the grain, felt not heard — the households tap back, simple messages sent heel-first through the ledge stone itself. A tall gray wading bird stands motionless at the pools and never, ever calls; the people watch it the way sailors watch a barometer, because it stares at a pool the day before that pool breathes, and nobody knows how it knows. A mute owl hunts moths through the dark and nests, welcomed, in the rafters of the council house. You cannot set your hand down anywhere in the Hush without resting it on something alive or recently alive, and none of it makes a sound you would call a sound, because the whole ecology has been tuned, by the thing at the top of it, to other channels — vibration, scent, cold-light.

The listeners. You will not see one clearly; no one does, anymore, by definition. Pale as pool-things, jointed like a crab's leg multiplied, eyeless, carrying a fan of antennae that opens like a hand — that much is agreed. They live in the karst and come up through the throats on the thickest milk nights, and they hunt the edges of sound: not murmur, not drip or falls-roar or wing-hiss, but transients — the clack, the crack, the clap, the cough, the laugh — the sharp notes that the slot, a natural waveguide, gathers and carries down to them like a speaking tube. They move inside the dripping, timing each footfall to the roof-drip so their steps hide in the beat. Mostly they cull the moth-fur and take the fallen and the snails, and the people understand them as shepherds, not demons: the flock is the hush itself, and you survive by not being the loudest thing in it. Still, every household keeps its drip-song — lichen-ropes rigged to channel seep-water off the eaves into an irregular, tuned patter on shells and stone cups — as both signature and smokescreen, because a house with a dead drip-line stands naked in the night, and the listeners can hear nakedness.

Ask where the law came from and they will show you two things. In the House of Cords, in a felt-lined box: a bronze bell, traded down from the plateau generations back, beautiful, never once rung. And below the mid-terraces, through the still water of the deepest pool on a clear night: doorways. Drowned lintels, a stair going down into glow. The First Hush, the old lower town. The knot-cord that records it ties three events into one knot and offers no grammar for them: a sound was made that did not stop; the water came up; the ledges emptied. The present hamlet was built above the flood-line by survivors who carried the old stones up one by one, in silence, a generation's penance, and left the gaps wide. This is why the settlement is strung so thin — nine hearths in three small clusters along a half-kilometer of ledge, long empty intervals of mud-flat and grove between. The gaps are firebreaks for sound. You build where your clatter dies before it reaches the next wall.

Everything built here is built out of the law. Drystone with moss chinking, because mud-mortared walls don't ring. Felt door-curtains instead of doors. Stair treads wrapped in bark-cloth, bridges lashed with fiber and inspected for creak. No pottery to speak of — a dropped pot is a disaster with a noise in it — so vessels are gourd, wood, hide, and snail-shell, and tools have fiber-wrapped handles, and the few traded copper pieces live in padded boxes. Mid-gorge there's a scree of dense bell-stones fallen from the New Cut, and these choir stones are swaddled in moss-and-fiber jackets, cradled in rope, checked after every storm — the hamlet's one great public work, the hushing of the stones, because sometimes in the cold a stone shifts and strikes another, and a single note goes rolling down the slot, and the whole gorge stops breathing until it fades. They say the gorge is practicing its one word. The primitive materials conceal a sophisticated engineering, all of it acoustic: a culture whose infrastructure is dampening, whose roads are padded, whose telegraph is heel-taps through ledge-stone, whose archive is knots, whose long-distance line is the Long Ear — a curved face of wall that carries a palm-pressed whisper a hundred meters on still nights.

And there is one loud room. At the head of the gorge, beside the Speaker's plunge basin, spray-wet and roaring in season, stands a stone platform, and here everything forbidden elsewhere is done: flint-knapping, bone-cracking for marrow, council debate, argument, and — this is the heart of it — speech and laughter. Most of life is lived in hand-talk, in the full-arm signs flashed across the gorge between clusters, in light-palms at night: hands painted with pool-glow, signing in the dark above the gaps like slow fireflies. But at the Speaker the falls' broadband roar masks every transient, and the foam is full of voices. Lovers go there, because to fall in love here is to finally hear someone; most of your life you know hands, and at the Speaker you learn the voice. Old couples who can no longer climb the stair to it sign their news in the dim, and it is said to be enough.

The year turns on the water. Fog season: the Speaker roars, speech is plentiful, the Blade comes rarely, the milk pours nightly, the moths boom toward Fat Week — and the herds and goose-flocks migrate across the plateau through the same fog, and the slot, a dark seam exhaling mist, is invisible until it is underfoot. The morning after a thick milk night there may be new arrivals on the floor and ledges, stunned or broken: the Falling. The people move fast and quiet with fiber-saws, bones carried to the Speaker for cracking, hides scraped soft, and a portion — the tithe — is carried to the karst throats and left for the listeners, deliberately, so they shop the throats and not the ledges. Then the clear season: the Speaker thins to a seam, speech grows scarce, the pools drop and their glow concentrates into the most beautiful nights of the year, and the listeners grow lean and bold. They call it the Hungry Hush, and in it the stillness deepens another turn — children drilled daily in the still-game, freezing mid-motion at any unexpected clatter; noise-debts paid in weeks of voluntary muteness; laughter, the most dangerous sound in the world, rationed to the dying falls.

Once in a life, everyone climbs out. The coming-of-age is the rim ascent, up the padded notches into the wind, into the Loud — and there, with the wind masking everything, safe from what hunts below, the young people do the one thing the gorge forbids: they sing. Rim-song is the culture's only music, unless you count the drip-song of the eaves, which they do. Up at the Trading Stone on the lip, in the same season, the plateau traders come and conduct the mute market — goods laid out, counter-goods left, bids knotted into cords and tucked in a cleft, all in silence out of courtesy and old fear: hushcloth of woven moth-silk, cold-light pigment sealed in shells, dried moth-meat and pickled tank-crab, traded down for salt, copper blanks, obsidian, hard wood. The traders think the gorge people are cursed or holy. The gorge people think the traders are deaf — how else could they live in all that noise?

Stand at the Swallow at dusk and you can feel the whole arrangement begin its nightly turn: the inhale starting, cold air sliding down the flutes of the walls, the milk coming over the lip in slow cataracts, the tank-trees closing their hands one by one down the colonnade. Frost needles form on the lichen curtains. Below the terraces the pools kindle, and across the dark gaps between the hearth-clusters the light-palms wake — green-lit hands opening and closing, saying the day's last things. Somewhere up-slot a choir stone settles in the cold and lets go one deep note, and every hand stops, and every head turns, and the note rolls down the organ-pipe walls and into the Swallow and is gone, and after a moment the hands resume, softer. It is a cathedral that took a vow. Everything in it is inverted — light rising from the ground at night, warmth above and cold below, water held in trees instead of earth, speech kept beside the loud water, the dead burning as lamps, music only at the top of the world — and all of it held, breath by breath, drip by drip, in one long act of listening. When you climb back out, days later, the wind on the plateau will seem to injure you. You will find you have brought the hush up with you, packed into your chest like felt, and for a long time you will set things down as if the world were asleep.
