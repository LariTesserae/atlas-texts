# The Weave

You hear it long before you stand on it.

From far off — however one approaches, and there are only hard ways — it announces itself as a tone: a vast chord, many-noted, drifting in and out of phase, like an organ played by something that has never heard music. That is the wind in the cables, and it never stops. The whole world is a woodwind. The dark is full of it.

Because it is dark here, and has been dark as long as anyone's ancestor-felt remembers. There is no sun in any meaningful sense — only a sky of ferociously sharp stars, so close at this altitude they seem to lean, and the aurora, which is common the way weather is common elsewhere: green and violet curtains dragging silently across the vault, bright enough to cast a faint green shadow of your own body across the strand beneath your feet. That, and the glow of the sap, and the moths, are all the light there is.

What you stand on is a cable — a living one, braided of a thousand fused tendrils, thick as a temple column, faintly warm through your boot soles, faintly pulsing if you stand still long enough to notice. And around you: almost nothing. The next cable is half a kilometer off across black air. Below, more strands, dimmer and dimmer, then nothing that light has ever reached. The world is a few threads and a great deal of dark. You can stand on the only solid thing for a kilometer in any direction and hear, constantly, that the emptiness is inhabited.

All of it — every cable, every tendril, every hanging moss-curtain and the soft felt underfoot — is one plant.

## The Anchorless

The people call it the Anchorless, because no root of it has ever been found and it may not have any. It anchors to itself. It is a single organism the size of a continent, a knot the world is caught in, and it has closed over everything: over the young sharp mountains whose teeth still pierce it here and there, over the valleys, which are now hollow bellies, over whatever the world was before. Underfoot there is no soil, only felt — meters of compacted ancestor-fiber, the plant's own dead body gone soft and water-holding, pale and springy. Everything solid is it. There is no stone within reach, no ore, no clay. The world is a single thought, thinking itself larger.

And it is young. That is the strange thing, once your eyes adjust: everything here is a first draft. The cables end in fresh splinters, unweathered, resin-bright. The felt is thin in places — kick through it and your leg dangles over the undersky. The whole Weave is still spreading. At its edge, which the people call the Bright Edge, the growing tips advance visibly across bare peaks, tendrils thickening season by season — and they glow, those tips, with concentrated sap-light, so that the horizon of the world is a faint luminous frontier, the brightest thing in existence, crawling outward into the dark at the pace of a slow walk, forever.

## Cold, and the closed water

It is cold the way high places are cold when nothing warms them: breath-fog cold, rime-feathers growing hand-length from the windward side of every strand, fibers going brittle in the deep hours. The Anchorless survives on antifreeze sap — bitter, faintly luminous, and it is the same stuff that lights the Bright Edge. Children who steal it from tapped nodes go around for days with glowing fingerprints, and everyone knows, and no one much minds.

There are no rivers. There never were. What water exists is the plant breathing. The Anchorless transpires warm moisture into the freezing dark, and that breath becomes fog — great slow banks of it, pooling in the hollow valleys it has roofed over — and the fog condenses on the cold outer cables as rime and drip, and the drip soaks the felt, and the felt holds it in black still ponds, ice-skinned, and the plant drinks it back up through rootlets finer than hair. A closed loop. The world waters itself. Occasionally the cycle overbalances: pressure builds in a sealed hollow, the seams between cables begin to howl, fog is wrung out of the air all at once in a brief hard rain, and before these seam-storms the static comes — strand-fire, the people call it, cold blue flame crawling harmless and beautiful along every cable at once, turning the whole dark world into a burning diagram of itself for minutes at a time. It is the only bright noon this place has.

The people drink from dew-nets — fiber mesh strung in the fog's path, tended like gardens — and from the ponds, and from the rime they break and suck. Nothing here is wet, exactly. Everything is damp, in pockets, in the dark.

## Loudness

The density of the world is near-nothing, and the noise is enormous, and these two facts are the same fact: sound travels unimpeded through all that empty air, and it travels even better through the cables, which conduct it like taut gut strings. Put your ear to a strand and you hear the Weave a hundred kilometers off.

You hear it grow. Fresh fiber snaps and re-knits with cracks like rifle fire, sharp as the young world is sharp. When some great cable fails somewhere far away — and they do fail, always somewhere — the entire continent twangs, one long note rolling outward through the lattice for hours. The people call it the long note and use it to check the time, the weather, and their place in the world, because everything here is told by pitch: the Chord drops as the cables cool, rises when the plant's breath warms, and the strandborn sleep and wake by the low chord, since there is no dawn to do it for them.

## What lives in the loud dark

The fauna is abundant and almost never seen. You hear it constantly — that is what abundant means here.

The bell-deer are the famous ones: six-limbed grazers of the felt, hollow-hoofed, and every step they take rings the cable beneath them like a struck bowl, a note carrying kilometers through the lattice. Everything that eats them hunts by triangulating that ring — so the bell-deer have learned to wrap their own hooves. They wad felt around their feet and walk muffled, carefully, for months, and shed the wrappings only in the Ringing Season, when they migrate by the tens of thousands along the great strands and the world reaches its loudest: a rolling, many-throated carillon that goes on for weeks, the cables singing under the weight of hooves, the aurora overhead, the strandborn standing on their platforms listening to the sound of food and time going past.

The lantern-moths carry their own cold light and use it dishonestly — the glow imitates a sap-seep, and what comes to feed on the seep feeds the moth. The fog-striders are the gentle giants of the outer strands: slow, many-legged filter beasts, long as a platform, walking the windward cables with their sieve-mouths open, straining the plant's breath for the drifting spores it carries, utterly indifferent to everything, aging into the landscape until the young ones walk across the backs of the old.

And there are the hushwings. Blind, soft, swift, hunting purely by sound through the hollows — and when a swarm moves through a region, everything in the Weave goes silent. The moths fold their lights. The deer freeze mid-step. The people stop their looms and hold their children's mouths gently shut. For days the loudest world there is holds its breath, and the only sound is the Chord and the soft wing-noise passing in the dark. Then the swarm moves on, and the whole region opens its throat at once — a single explosive release of calls and rings and taps that the people call, plainly, the opening of throats, and celebrate like a new year.

## The people and their faint marks

They are few, and the world is vast, so their marks are faint: you can travel days and find nothing but a sound-knot — a pattern of notches cut into a cable that changes its tone, so that a strand can be read in the dark by tapping it, the way a road sign is read. It is their writing, and it is also their long speech: a message tapped into a cable can be felt by a listener with their ear to the same strand half a day's walk away, and the strandborn have been gossiping through the body of their world for longer than their memory reaches.

They do not build, exactly. They persuade. A platform is made by grafting — coaxing the Anchorless, over decades, to grow a shelf of fused tendrils where one is wanted. A family that wants a house plants a house and waits two generations. Their tools are thorn, splinter, resin, bone, braided fiber; their bridges are rope; their lamps are caged lantern-moths and gourds of glow-sap. Fire is the great taboo — the felt is dry in its deep layers and the world is flammable and there is nowhere to run to — so light comes cold, always.

Stone is the only wealth. Where a young mountain's peak pierces the Weave, there is a quarry-shrine, guarded and sacred, and a stone adze is an heirloom passed down fifty generations, named, its biography recited. The dead are given to the felt, which takes them gently — cold and preservative — so that the ancestors are quite literally woven into the floor of the world, and now and then fresh growth exposes one, intact, patient, and the family comes and rewraps them and asks how things are going down there.

There are ruins, too, of a kind. Dead strands: places where the Anchorless sickened and a whole province of the lattice petrified and went silent. Sound dies there — the petrified cables conduct nothing — and the people fear the Quiet more than any predator, and leave their oldest platforms there to be slowly swallowed.

## The Answer

Below the lowest cables is the undersky. Descend — some do, on rope, for the damp under-felt where the pale swimmers and the blind things live — and you pass out of the cold dry topside into a warmer, dripping dark, where condensed breath rains off the underside of the world in drops that fall for minutes without landing. No one has ever reached the bottom. No one knows what the Weave is knotted around, or over, or whether there is a bottom at all.

What they do is drop stones.

A quarry-shrine stone, precious, committed to the undersky on holy days, falls for a long time. And then, minutes later, the cables carry back a faint, deep tone — the impact, arriving up the lattice like a rumor. The people call it the Answer, and they have been listening to it for as long as they have had stone to spare, and here is the thing the elders say quietly, in the low chord, when the moths are caged and the children asleep:

The Answer is coming back sooner than it used to.

Generation by generation, the tone returns a little faster, a little higher. Either the Anchorless is growing down toward something — roots reaching for a floor, the dark below slowly filling with new, glowing, first-draft growth — or something down there is rising to meet it.

The Chord plays on. The fog pools in the hollows. Somewhere a bell-deer unwraps its hooves, and the loud, empty, living world rings like a struck instrument, and does not say why.
