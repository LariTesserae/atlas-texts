The dark comes first. Not evening-dark, not cave-dark — a dark with no above in it, no memory of a sky that was ever lit. There is no horizon to find, no silhouettes. You stand in it the way you stand in water: it is on your skin.

The sound comes second, and it is enormous. Rain on broad leaves, a million soft impacts. Rain on moss, nearly nothing. Rain on standing water, a fine applause. Rain on iron somewhere to the east — a roof, drumming. Beneath the rain, drips by the thousand, syncopated, patient. Beneath the drips, a bass note you feel in the sternum before you hear: falling water, far off, and further off a slow mechanical heartbeat — thump, hiss, thump, hiss — some great pump working a valley you cannot see. And threaded through everything: frogs, insects, calling things, a chorus that rises and falls in slow waves as if the marsh itself were breathing through an instrument.

The air is warm. Blood-warm. The rain is warm. The stone under your hand is warm, and wet — everything is wet, has been wet for longer than anything here remembers.

---

This is the Weep: a high plateau, continental in extent, pressed up beneath a permanent ceiling of cloud called the Lid. No sun reaches through it. None ever has. The warmth comes from below — the plateau's own buried volcanic heart, seeping up through hot springs and warm rock and black ponds that steam faintly — and the Lid holds the warmth in like a blanket. Between the hot ground and the cold dark ceiling, the world runs a closed weather engine: water rises as vapor, gathers against the Lid's underside, and comes back down as the Pour. It never fully stops. It only pauses to gather itself.

The Lid gives the world a ceiling the way a cathedral has one. Sound does not escape here; it returns. The frog-chorus comes back off the far scarp minutes late, so the land answers itself, and you can hear the size of the place — hear that the plateau runs on past any voice, that it holds weather provinces of its own. Rain arrives with a character depending on where it has been: sulfur rain off the vent country, tannin-black rain off the peat basins, clean hard rain from the high condenser moors. Travelers here learn to read a storm by taste.

---

The land is old and has settled into itself. Basalt bones worn to whaleback smoothness. Limestone gone to karst — a stone sponge, hollowed and hollowing, so that half the plateau's water runs underground and the ground itself ticks and knocks with hidden streams. Every cliff face weeps. Water stands in tufa terraces that descend the hillsides like poured black wax, each rim polished, each pool still and faintly, faintly luminous.

That glow is the first thing your eyes learn. The water carries a chemiluminescent plankton — the locals' word is *lightwater* — that fires when disturbed. A waterfall is a dim blue rope. Rain striking a pool makes rings of brief cold light. Your boots in the wet grass leave footprints that shine and fade, shine and fade, and the trail of your passing writes itself behind you and then politely erases it. But understand: it never adds up to *light*. It shows you the water and nothing else. The dark keeps everything it is not forced to give.

In the meadows of candlegrass, a touch sends an amber pulse running stem to stem, a wave of gold rolling away from your step across a field, and then the dark closes back over it. Light is expensive here — it costs chemistry, it costs the stuff of life — so nothing wastes it. Light is not a condition of this world. It is an event.

---

The forest is called dripwood, and it is black. No green anywhere — green is for catching a sun that does not exist. The dripwoods grow broad waxy guttered leaves, every leaf a channel, every channel leading in, and the trees drink the Pour directly, funneling rain down bark grown fluted like a downspout. Between rains they comb the mist. The canopy layers itself three, four deep; the understory is moss on moss on shelf fungus, root-mats woven over black ponds until the ground is a held breath — walk the mires and the earth wobbles underfoot, trees sway from your step, and you understand that you are walking on something that floats.

Nothing grows alone. Every trunk wears three other lives. Lantern pitchers hang in the understory, glowing faint amber to lure the moths, digesting the credulous. On the tail of every Pour, when the rain slackens, the whole forest exhales spores at once — a scheduled event, everything here keeps schedules — and the air goes thick and faintly sparkling, and the frogs double their chorus, and the moths rise.

---

The moths rise to the machines, because the machines are the other light.

They stand scattered across the plateau — one per valley, sometimes fewer — and they are industrial in the old, overbuilt sense: riveted iron, brick, brass gauges gone green, enamel signs. The Works, they're called, and nobody stops them. Pump stations that drink the marshes and lift the water to high cisterns, setting each valley's rhythm — the Surge — so that the wetlands breathe to a mechanical heartbeat and everything living has entrained to it. Condenser fields: acres of black fins standing cold in the warm dark, the only cold things in the world, sweating the humid air into cisterns, humming as they work. Sagging power lines on ceramic-jawed poles, humming too, and the puddles beneath them hum, and you learn quickly which puddles bite. In the drainage trenches along the lines live the wire-eels, fat and slow, grown on the leakage, discharging pale blue when touched.

A single rail crosses the middle plateau, and on it a maintenance crawler still patrols — you hear it coming for an hour, clank and hum — and its sweeping headlamp is the closest thing this world has to a sunrise. Mobs of lampmoths snow around it. Plants along the route lean. Things that remember light, or remember remembering it, come out for the crawler's passing the way things elsewhere come out at dawn.

And the cableway: a line of pylons walking over the passes, buckets moving forever on their singing wire, mostly empty, squealing at each tower. The line does not stop at the plateau's edge. It dives over the scarp into the dark below, and the buckets come back up wet. The great Downlines go over the edge too — pipes thick as towers, carrying the cisterns' water down to someone, something, a customer unremembered. The treaty is lost. The water still goes down. Sometimes, in the quiet, a pipe shudders as something ascends.

---

The fauna is abundant past counting, and almost all of it is heard before it is known. Mire-elk move through the peat on wide soft hooves, lowing across the valleys — each one reading its own echo, knowing the land by how the land answers. The frog-choruses synchronize until a pulse of sound sweeps the marsh like a slow weather front. Every fold of every leaf holds something small and many-jointed. The density of it is physical: life stacked on life, saturated, near-touching, layer over layer in the dark.

And then there is the other thing.

Sometimes, in one place, it all stops. The frogs cease. The clickers cease. The cessation moves — a traveling bubble of quiet crossing the marsh — and inside it you can hear the drips with terrible clarity, and your own blood. Something is hunting by ear, and everything living has agreed, across all species, on the response.

*Silence is the only alarm this world has.*

The predator is called the Hush, and that is all that needs saying about it. You will likely never know what it looks like. Neither does anything else.

---

There are paths. You find them by ear: posts strung along a route, each hung with a bell, and the bells are tuned — a melody laid over the land, walked note by note. The chime-lines cross the mires, skirt the Hush's known ranges, lead to the pump houses and the condenser moors. If the melody is wrong, the path is wrong: a flooded stretch, a fallen span, turn back. And because the world is strange and the world is listening, there are things in the deep mire that have learned the melodies, nearly. *Nearly* tuned. They ring from the dark off the path, patient as anything here.

*Not every melody is a road.*

Someone keeps the bells tuned. The clappers are oiled. Leaf-fall is swept from the path stones. In a pump house you'll find a kettle, a cot, tools racked in order, signage stenciled in a plain hand: PUMP 9. DO NOT DRINK FROM FINS. (The fins' water is too pure; it takes from you — that, everyone agrees.) Once, across a valley far too wide to cross by dark, you may see a single lit window. The Tuners, they're called, and whether they are the Works' operators' descendants or something else the dark has made, no one admits to having met one. The kettle, though. The kettle is warm.

---

There are no days. Time here is kept the way the land keeps everything — by overlapping rhythm. The Pour has its period, felt in the air's weight hours ahead. The Surge has its period. The Swarm, the frog-pulse, the crawler's long round. Travelers measure journeys in Pours. Maps are sequences of sounds: *the twin falls, the ninth bell, the valley where the pump runs slow.*

And the rhythms coincide. Not often — but the cycles drift against each other, and now and then the Pour peaks as the Surge peaks as the moths erupt as the crawler comes through with its lamp blazing, and everything happens at once. The falls roar at full throat. The lightwater flares in every pool. Spawning, hunting, blooming — the whole plateau turns over like a sleeper. This is called the Chord, and it is the most fertile and most dangerous hour the world makes.

*During the Chord: be under a roof, or be small and still.*

---

Standing in it now — rain warm on your neck, a pump's heartbeat under the frogsong, the path's next bell a dim note ahead through the black — you can feel the rhythms beginning to lean toward one another. The drips quicken. The frogs find their pulse. Somewhere far off, late, the scarp returns the sound of the world to the world.

The Weep does not brighten. It never will. But everything in it is speaking at once, water and machine and beast, layer over layer in the reverberant dark — and behind you, your footprints shine briefly on the wet stone, fade, and are gone, the path keeping you only as long as it must.
