You hear it across the water long before the horizon gives you anything to look at. First a pressure in the ears, then a texture, then — still hours out — the unmistakable shape of it: not surf, not storm, but a continuous occupied roar, layered like geology. Sailors on the Shallows say they navigate by it as much as by stars. Each province has its own register, its own accent in the noise. Then the line of it rises on the horizon, low as a storm-bank, and above it, always, its own cloud. The Weir carries its weather with it the way a body carries heat.

Because the city breathes. All those engines, all those bodies, all that warm standing water — the Weir exhales moisture in a column visible from a day's sail out, and the column rises and cools and spreads and falls back as rain. It rains on the city every afternoon, on schedule, the way a kettle sings on schedule. The people call it the Hour and set their meals by it. The rain is warm and tastes faintly of metal and faintly, inexplicably, of bread — that last is bloom-spore washed out of the breathing air. At dawn the whole place steams, fog lying in the canyons between the masses until the sun works down to it.

The city stands in a shallow continental sea, warm as blood, rarely deeper than a ship's draft. It did not conquer the sea; it grew over it. Everything rests on pilings and root-mats and the fused feet of older structures, and anywhere in the Weir — anywhere — you never have to dig more than a meter to strike water. The lowest levels are given up to it entirely: the Drowned, provinces of warm black water lit by eel-gardens, patrolled by pump-guilds and pale blind things that have never seen the sky. The city's underside is an inverted reef. The sea is still down there. The Weir is something the sea is wearing.

The stuff it is made of is not built, exactly, and not grown, exactly, and the distinction stopped mattering a long time ago. The mason is a structural membrane — an engineered material old beyond the memory of the guilds — that flows where it is fed and cures where it is told, hardening into something between horn and ivory and old hardwood, warm to the touch, faintly flexible while young, ringing like bell-metal where struck thin. Every wall sweats; it is meant to. The mason drinks the saturated air through one face and weeps it down the other into gutters, and the gutters feed the cisterns, and this is where the city gets its water — it condenses its own breath and drinks it. Old districts are dense as bone, their thresholds worn into scoops, their corners rounded by ten thousand years of shoulders. Cut one open and the laminae show in the fresh face, generation on generation, the years recorded like rings — demolition sites look like sea cliffs, like geology in fast motion, and in a sense that is what they are.

Nothing here is a plant. In place of vegetation the city has the bloom — films and felts and slow opalescent crusts that spread over every surface the mason does not actively shed, in amber and pewter and rust and mother-of-pearl, doing the quiet chemistry that plants do elsewhere: drinking the light, fixing the air, feeding everything that feeds everything. The bloom is tended like farmland, harvested like farmland, cursed like weeds. And there is no green. Green simply does not occur in the light-chemistry of the bloom, and the result is that Weirfolk find it disturbing — an exotic, artificial, faintly obscene color, the color of jewels and foreigners' dyes, not the color of the world.

Nowhere is empty. This is what visitors cannot get used to: the Weir is *full*. Buildings do not stand apart; the city is one continuous mass, fused, sharing every wall, and inside it whatever is not structure is cavity, and the cavity is occupied — by dwellings, by engines, by bloom-farms under their light-shafts, by freight sliding in tubes like blood in vessels, by people, by animals, by water on its way down and air on its way up. The streets are the only voids, and they run like canyons between the masses, and even they are filled to capacity: bodies, stalls, cables, catwalks, bridge upon bridge upon bridge, until the sky overhead is reduced to a bright river glimpsed between crossings. Stand at street level in an interior province — a thousand kilometers from the nearest open water — and you are at the bottom of a packed volume that extends, unbroken, farther than weather.

The sun itself is ordinary. It rises and sets; the days are the usual length. It is what the city does with light that is strange. Light is *carried*. Mirror-towers — the light-reefs — stand at the canyon rims and pass the sun down shaft after shaft, hand to hand like buckets at a fire, and light-rights are property, are inheritance, are law: you can no more shadow a registered shaft than you can dam a river. The upper levels live in plain daylight. The middle levels live in borrowed light, silvered and redirected, rationed by the angle of the hour. The lower levels live by glow-film, cultivated bloom bred for luminescence and trimmed along the ceilings like hedges. The Drowned live in the dark except where the eel-gardens burn with their own small lights.

And the sound. The sound has layers the way the city has strata. At the bottom, almost below hearing, the siphon-hearts and pump arrays keep their fundamental — the Note, the city's organ pedal, felt in the sternum and the teeth, the tone everything in the Weir is unconsciously tuned to. Above that the rush-and-boom of the freight tubes, the shriek of rails. Above that the work, and the work is loud: the mason cures with a crack like a rifle shot when forced, so a district under growth sounds, hour after hour, like a distant battle, and demolition sounds like what it is. Above that the animals — gliders shrieking on the updrafts, the chatter of wall-grazers, the eel-bells in the canals. And above everything, never stopping, the voices: vendors, crews, the work-chants by which people coordinate over the din, the sheer human weather of it. Nobody in the Weir has ever heard silence. Silence is the rarest commodity the city trades; hush-houses sell it by the minute, lined with dead laminae cut at angles that drink sound, and the rich buy hours there the way the rich elsewhere buy waterfront. The poor wear ear-shells — hinged cups of cured mason at every belt — and speak sign-cant as readily as speech, read lips without thinking, and sleep like the dead through noise that would leave an outsider weeping in the street. The old are mostly deaf, and unbothered by it.

The Weir is old, and the Weir is new, and those are the same statement. Left alone, the mason would grow — would fill the canyons, seal the shafts, close over the whole enterprise like ice on a pond within a century or two. The city persists only because it is perpetually destroyed. Pruning is the constant civic activity, the sanitation, the tithe: crews of gnawers — domesticated stock bred to eat young membrane — work the growing edges; demolition guilds cut the city back into its own shape; the cuttings go to the vats, the vats feed the mason, and the mason is laid down again somewhere else. Nothing is weathered in the ordinary sense. Everything is maintained. The form is ancient — the silhouette is protected by the oldest law there is, the Envelope, which says the shape of the city does not change — and the substance is days old. The whole place is a standing wave in a river of matter: the same wave for ten thousand years, never the same water twice.

And it is full of animals. Where there are no plants the food web starts elsewhere — with the bloom and the vats — and climbs through more niches than any naturalist has catalogued. The air itself is grazed: sieve-swarms hang in the canyon updrafts straining spore and bloom-dust out of the city's breath, and gliders, leather-winged and uncountable, stoop on the swarms through the rising columns of heat, so that looking up a canyon you see what appears to be an entire ecology suspended in the air, because that is what it is. The walls carry limpets and slug-things and small climbing nuisances with names too obscene to translate. The canals and the Drowned are thick with eels — the eel-gardens feed half the city — and with mollusks filtering the warm water, and pale things that clean the siphon intakes. Demolition sites draw the scavenger guilds: crab-like pickers working the fresh-cut laminae, and the sweepers, which are machines, or were machines once — feral maintenance-stock, generations deep, occupying their niches exactly like animals, hunted like animals, bred accidentally like animals. No one bothers to distinguish anymore. The Weir has not distinguished between flesh and mechanism with any conviction for a long time.

Some of the deep works are older than understanding. The fall-wells in the oldest provinces move freight downward in a way that is not falling — loads drift, descend as if through oil, arrive weightless — and the siphon-hearts that lift the sea against itself beat on principles the pump-guilds can operate but can no longer derive. Maintenance of the old works is liturgy as much as engineering: the guilds do what has always been done, in the order it has always been done, and the works keep working, and nobody touches anything that has not been touched before. The Weir runs, in part, on inherited miracles domesticated into plumbing. It is a very old city. It has forgotten more physics than it currently uses.

The rules are mostly about water and light and shape, because those are the things that can be stolen. Never block a weep-path — drainage is sacred law, and to cap a weeper is a crime against everyone's thirst. Never shadow a registered shaft. Never grow past the Envelope. Pay the pruning tithe, in labor or in feedstock, because the alternative is the mason in your stairwell. Around these simplicities the life of the place churns at full density, in shifts around the clock — the Weir never sleeps; sleep is taken in rotation like everything else — and the bazaars never close, and the noise never falls below a roar, and the rain comes every afternoon like a bell. At night the glow-films take over the middle depths, the canyons run with lamplight, the eel-gardens burn below, and the warm rain falls through the lights, and the Note goes on in the bones of the city, and the whole packed, dripping, deafening, ancient, brand-new thing holds its shape against the dark the way a wave holds its shape against the current — not by being still, but by never, ever stopping.
