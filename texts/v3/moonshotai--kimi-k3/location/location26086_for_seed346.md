**The Kessel**

From the lowland you would miss it twice. The plain is a cold, flat country of sedge and crowberry and long gray light, and the hollow sits in it like a thumbprint in clay — a kettle-hole, the old kind, the kind left when a buried block of ice melts out and the ground slumps. Only no ice made this one. Whatever melted out of the Kessel was not water.

You come to the rim an hour before you expected to, because distances shorten near it, and you stand among wind-combed grasses looking down at a town that contains too much. That is the first wrongness, and it is a quiet one: four hundred houses where sixty should fit, streets folded over streets, chimneys shouldering up between other chimneys, the whole thing dense as a fingerprint and small enough to walk across in twenty minutes — twenty minutes, that is, when the streets agree. The light is perfectly ordinary. A pale sun, a flat sky, everything plainly visible. The impossible, in the Kessel, happens in good light, and this is somehow the worst of it.

The cold is not weather. The cold is the cost.

The town is still being built. It has been still being built for longer than anyone's records run, and the process — the wardens call it the Settling, with the flat patience of people naming sediment — draws warmth out of the world to do its work. Construction here is endothermic. Every wall that flattens into being drinks a little heat, and so the hollow sits several degrees colder than the plain around it, and frost persists all year in the deep shadows near the oldest work, and the inhabitants wrap themselves in wool and slowness and think nothing of it. Breath fogs. Kettles fur with lime. The cold is the sound of a town paying for itself.

It began — the wardens' best guess — with cornerstones. Fist-sized knots of something that is not quite matter, seeded across the floor of the hollow at some founding nobody claims. A cornerstone looks like nothing at all until you look away, and then it has been a corner for some time. From the corner comes an angle of pale stone; from the angle, over decades, a wall; from the wall, eventually, a room that consents to be entered. Corners precede walls here. In the unfinished districts you will find them standing free — bare Ls of cold stone in open air, patient as survey stakes, waiting for their houses to arrive. The wardens plant them the way other towns plant trees, and for the same reason: for the grandchildren.

Nothing is built once. This is the second wrongness, and the source of the town's crushing density. The Settling does not erase its attempts. Every wall in the Kessel has been made several times, in successive drafts, and the drafts do not entirely leave. Stand in the Creases — the active district, where the work is newest — at the hinge of an hour, and the street fattens with versions. A doorway you are walking toward was once, in an earlier telling, three feet to the left, and on cold still mornings you can pass through that older doorway on your way to the newer one, and feel the difference as a thickness in the air, a faint drag like walking through a curtain of cold water. Finished districts hold to a single draft, mostly. The Old Flat, down by the hollow's floor, is settled all the way through — one house, one street, one version, ring-true. But even there the attics remember attics that were never kept, and the town is arguably not four hundred houses at all but one house, told many times.

The stone itself is pale and dense and slightly greasy to the eye, like bone china, and it clinks like porcelain when struck — which is precisely how the wardens test it. Timber is scarce, this being bare country, so the scaffolds that web the Creases are built of draft-timber: gray boards salvaged from houses that were attempted and abandoned, wood whose grain shifts if you watch it, light as balsa and strangely willing. The scaffolds are made of houses that will not exist; the houses are made of stone that insists on existing. The wardens see no contradiction. It is simply what the materials are for.

Water comes to the Kessel sideways. Real rain is occasional, a dozen honest storms a year, but the unfinished parts of the town sweat — condensation beads on every crease, on every wall still deciding to be a wall, and the gutters of the Creases run with it year-round, a slow distillate the townspeople harvest in cisterns and call crease-water. It is cold, clean, and tastes faintly of limestone and of nothing else, a flatness at the back of the tongue that locals call the taste of unwritten. Draft-rain is different: in some creases the sky belongs to another attempt at the town — bluer, or clouded, or raining — and rain from a draft-sky falls into this one and wets nothing. It darkens stone for the duration of a glance and is gone. Children learn early to tell the two rains apart by taste, the way children elsewhere learn birdsong.

Down in the Lower Rooms, where the town has slumped below its own foundations, the water pools and stays. Some of it fell as rain in drafts where the town never was, and it has nowhere to evaporate to. The pools are still, black, and deeper than their rooms — you do not drop a plumb line into the Lower Rooms, because sometimes the line does not stop. There is a well on the Old Flat called the Delayed Well, which returns everything late. Drop a pebble and the splash arrives next week. Lower a bucket and it may come up wet from a rain that has not happened yet, or dry from a draft where the well was never dug. The wardens have stopped asking it questions. The well, they say, is not confused. The well is patient. The confusion is yours.

Trust the damp. That is the first rule taught to visitors, and it inverts every instinct: in the Kessel, dryness is the warning sign. Real rooms sweat. A dry room is a room that has not finished arriving, and a room that has not finished arriving may finish around you, or without you, or with you in a draft where you were never born. The other rules come in the same flat litany, recited by bell-wardens the way ferrymen elsewhere recite prices. Knock and wait — a finished door answers with solidity; an unfinished door answers with interest. Never follow an echo that returns late; near a crease, sound detours, and a late echo has been somewhere, and it may bring back a voice that is almost yours. If a room is larger inside than outside, exit the way you entered and do not turn to watch it go. Do not count the houses. The count changes, and counters, on occasion, change with it.

The quiet is total and textured. The creases drink sound — overtones first, then the body of a noise, so that even the Old Flat has a muffled, snow-bound hush, and the Creases swallow footsteps whole. The townspeople have become a quiet culture in response: low voices, soft doors, arguments conducted at a murmur, a whole citizenry that sounds like a library at closing time. What remains audible is small and slow. The tick of stone contracting in the cold. Drips, syncopated, counting nothing. The kettle, always, somewhere. And under everything, felt in the teeth more than heard, a low hum from the active cornerstones — the Settling at work, a sound like a struck bell held in the mouth.

The bell is the town's instrument, tool, and calendar. Because a crease swallows sound and finished stone rings, the wardens survey by ringing: they carry small brass bells down every street on its rounds, and listen. A street that answers is finished. A street that swallows the bell is still being written, and gets chalk marks on its corners, and a rope across its end, and another visit in five years. Streets are called rung or unrung, the way roads elsewhere are called paved or dirt. The census, too, is taken by belling — four hundred and some souls at the last count, though the count, like everything here, is a consensus rather than a fact. Time itself is kept by committee: the Bell-House declares noon when the wardens agree it is noon, and near the Creases the agreement frays, and visitors are advised not to schedule anything, and residents, who live long — the creases leak time gently, the way old houses leak heat in reverse — have largely forgotten why anyone would.

What grows here grows slowly or not at all. Lichen writes its ochre script across the settled stone of the Old Flat, and it is the oldest writing in the Kessel, the only writing that has never once been revised. Mosses hold the north faces of the rung streets. Along the creases themselves grows a pale, bladed grass that feeds on the condensation — crease-grass, sullen green, spreading at the pace of the Settling, which is to say visibly only across generations. And in a court off the Bell-House square there is a rowan that grew, a century back, into a crease, and now exists half in this telling of the town and half in another. Some of its branches are here, bare and cold. Some are in a draft where it is always autumn. Leaves fall from branches that do not exist, land on a street that does, and the town rakes, all season, the litter of an invisible tree, and piles it, and burns it, and the smoke rises and folds into a crease and reappears three streets over, smelling faintly of a different October.

Animals are rare, and the ones that stay are specialists. Vellum moths come out for one week in the brief summer — slow, cold-torpid, wings like tracing paper, feeding on lichen, resting flat against pale stone where they are indistinguishable from peeling paint. They lay their eggs only on surfaces that exist in more than one draft; how they know, nobody asks. The crease-snail is translucent and unhurried and carries a shell that is bigger on the inside — startle one and it retreats further than the geometry of its house allows, and sits in there, presumably, in more room than you will ever have. Pale fish, eyeless commas, turn over once a year in the Lower Room pools, come from deeper than the pools should connect to. Snow buntings work the rim in winter, ordinary birds of an ordinary plain, and they do not come down into the bowl, and this is considered one of the wisest things about them.

And there is Plumb, the town cat, gray, ancient, unimpressed, who has learned the creases the way cats learn anything warm. Plumb is frequently seen asleep on two windowsills at once. The wardens leave him alone. He is the best crease-detector alive, he costs nothing, and the draft he prefers is believed to be one in which he is fed twice.

The people of the Kessel sell one thing: true stone. Blocks cut from the Old Flat, from streets settled all the way through, rung and certified and stamped. It is prized out in the world as anchor-stone — the most thoroughly real building material there is, stone that has been argued into existence across more drafts than any other and has held. Builders of lighthouses and observatories pay absurdly for it. Ring a block of Kessel stone, wherever in the world you stand, and it answers with a short, clean note, and then, immediately after, with a small silence that is recognizably the silence of the hollow — the quiet, exported.

At night the town is lit plainly by its few lamps and the ordinary stars, and the mist that is the town's slow exhalation — condensate lifting off the day's work — pools in the streets like something being poured, very gradually, in. The hum continues. Somewhere a wall drinks the cold and gains another skin of reality. The hollow itself is still sinking, a millimeter a year, the geology beneath the foundations unconsolidated, embryonic, green — a town whose ground has not finished being born, sitting in a hole that has not finished being made.

Ask a warden what happens when the Settling completes — when the last crease flattens, the last cornerstone goes quiet, the last draft is folded away and the Kessel stands as a single, finished, perfectly real town — and she will give you the consensus answer, which is: nothing that concerns us. The timescale is not ours. But on still nights, up on the rim among the buntings and the sedge, you can sometimes hear the newest corner ticking like cooling metal far below, and a bell hung on a scaffold of impossible timber, waiting the decades out for its first true ring — and the sound, when it finally comes, will be heard in one world only, and it will mean: this street, this house, this room. Finished. Real. Here.
