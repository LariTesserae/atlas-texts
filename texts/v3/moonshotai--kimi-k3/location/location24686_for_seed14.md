The land did not drown. It finished.

That is the first thing to understand about this place. There was no catastrophe, no rising tide that swallowed the hills mid-sentence. There were mountains here once, and the rain took them patiently, grain by grain, over spans of time that have no name anymore, and the rivers carried the grains down, and the basin received them, and now the mountains are the silt and the silt is the sea floor and the whole country lies down in water a kilometer deep. What remains of the dry world is a low rim at the basin's far edge — gravel flats, hills rounded to the softness of knees, and one last mountain, if you can still call it that: a bare dome, wind-polished, more the memory of a mountain than the thing itself. Slow rivers wander off it. A few old glaciers still calve bergs into the grey water. Everything else, in every direction, for over a thousand kilometers, is sea.

The sea has a ceiling. Ice covers it nearly shore to shore, thick and pressure-ridged, fractured here and there into dark leads that skin over again within days. Under the ice lives the only green thing in this world: a film of algae clinging to the underside, an upside-down pasture, thin as breath on glass. It is the source of almost everything. The light that reaches it is a murk, a pewter dusk at noon, and by a few hundred meters down even that is gone. Below that, the darkness is total, and it is not empty. That is the second thing to understand: the dark here is occupied.

The water is cold — a degree or two above freezing, top to bottom — and it is full. Always, at every depth, marine snow is falling: the slow debris of the ceiling-pasture, the sheddings and corpses and dust of the world above, sifting down through a kilometer of black water over weeks. It never stops. There has not been a clear liter of water in this sea since the mountains were young. Even without light you would know the snow by touch, a faint constant grit against the skin, and beneath it the pressure, which is the other fullness — water pressed against water against water, no hollow anywhere, no void, the medium itself crowded to its limit. Descend here and the dark does not receive you. It takes you in like one more particle.

The sea is old beyond reckoning and it has never once been still. This is its oldest stable state: motion. Internal waves the height of hills travel the black mid-water and break, silently, against the buried slopes of the rim — a surf no eye has ever seen. Down the old softened canyons of the rim, turbidity currents pour: underwater avalanches of silt, heavy and fast, rumbling out across the plain and laying their sediment in wide fading fans. And over the abyssal floor itself come the benthic storms — sudden deep currents, born of some slow argument between the ceiling's cold and the water's salt, that grip the bottom for days or weeks and lift the silt back into the water until the lower sea becomes a blizzard. The plain is scoured, buried, exhumed, reburied. Nothing stays covered forever. Nothing stays uncovered. The world finished changing ten million years ago and it has not stopped moving for a single hour since. The storm is not an event here. The storm is the medium.

Sound, in all this, is rare and therefore enormous. The baseline is a silence with weight — the pressure-hush, the feeling of the world's mass leaning on your ears. Then, sometimes, something happens, and because water carries sound the way the dark carries nothing else, the whole sea hears it. The calving of a berg at the rim booms across a thousand kilometers. A turbidity current can be heard coming for hours, a low approach, like weather in another room. And rarest of all, small and close and strangely domestic: a tap. A tap-tap. Someone cracking a shell. Someone eating. It carries for miles through the cold, and everything that hears it understands it.

The floor, when you finally reach it, is the flattest place that has ever existed — an abyssal plain of silt hundreds of meters deep, silt so fine and so often stirred that it behaves less like ground than like a slower, greyer water. The storms comb it into long low drifts. Here and there, spaced apart by days of nothing, stand the gardens: glass sponges, pale and translucent, towers of spun silica rising out of the murk, some of them centuries old, some older than the language anyone might use to describe them. Around cold seeps, bacterial mats dust the silt white. This is all the vegetation the dark allows — a scattering, an afterthought, gardens separated by wilderness.

And the animals are rare. A fish, pale and soft and unhurried, its eyes enormous or its eyes nothing, crossing the black alone. When something large dies above — one of the great slow swimmers of the mid-water — and falls, the fall is an event measured in years: the plain gathers to it out of the dark, feeds, and disperses again into solitude, and the bones sink into the silt and wait for a storm to lift them out again, white and scoured, a little later in eternity.

But the stones are the story. Because everything else was ground to flour long ago, the only hard objects left in this world arrive from the sky of it: dropstones, carried out by bergs calved off the last mountain, released as the ice melts, falling through the snow to the plain. A stone is wealth here. A stone is the only wealth here. And there are those who seek them.

They are the keepers — bottom-walkers, long-limbed, patient, longer-lived than anything should be, clever in the way of creatures with too much time and too few things. Each one finds a stone, or inherits one, or — after a benthic storm has swept the plain clean and exposed what the silt had hidden — ranges out across the fresh-scoured flat searching, the way others of their kind must once have searched the shores of a younger world. A keeper keeps its stone for life. It cracks shells against it. It clutches it when the storms come, holding its own ballast against the current, an anchor that is also a tool. That tapping heard across the miles is a keeper at its dinner. It is the whole of technology in this world, and it is enough: the highest achievement of toolmaking here is a stone that is held.

When a keeper dies, the stone stays. So the plain is scattered, at enormous intervals, with single hard objects, each one carried down from the last mountain by ice and carried across the flat by hands — each one a biography no one will read, the children of the mountain delivered one at a time to the deep.

The rules of the world are simple and total. Everything falls; the only direction that matters is down. Nothing floats that is not ice or alive. Hardness is treasure, because hardness is almost gone. Sound travels farther than light ever could, so the world is known by hearing it — news, weather, danger, dinner all arrive through the ears. Nothing is buried forever, and nothing is exempt from the snow. Time moves thickly here; even the violence is slow, and a storm that lasts three weeks is, by the plain's accounting, a brief gust.

Stand on the floor of it — if standing means anything in that cold press of dark — and the snow settles onto your shoulders and the silt shifts under your feet like something breathing, and somewhere far off a berg calves, and somewhere closer, patiently, someone taps a shell open with a stone older than the hills that made it. The sea has settled into its final form. It will settle forever. The settling is what it does.
