Ninety meters below the crater floor of a young volcano, in the high country of a cinder plateau, there is a room the size of a root cellar. It has no door. It has never had a door.

The volcano is sixty thousand years old, which for a mountain is infancy. Its cone is a heap of loose scoria and bombs and ash, raw-edged, without a single gully or grain of soil — nothing has been here long enough to be worn. The plateau itself stands three thousand meters up, and the cone adds another fifteen hundred, so the summit air is thin and bitter and the stars come close. Every seven minutes or so, the crater gives a cough of incandescent slag, the way Strombolian mountains do, patient as a metronome. Whether it is day or night above makes no difference below. The room's sky is a meter of crystal. Its weather is sulfur. Its night is total, eternal, and loud.

The room opened nine thousand years ago as a gas blister in cooling rock — an upright gourd, five paces from throat to crown, three across at its widest. Every surface is lined with smoky quartz grown the dry way, from vapor, atom by atom: crystals the length of a forearm, shouldering into one another, and in places the spears from floor and ceiling interlock, so the room is not a void but a thicket — a braid of stone with crawl-spaces threaded through it. There is no empty volume here. Every hollow holds a body; every body touches another. And every crystal face, all of them, everywhere, is grooved with fine parallel lines, as though the whole room had been milled.

At the bottom, a chimney drops away toward the heat: the throat. Crossing the floor is a hairline seam, and down it, a few meters, rock sits at five hundred degrees. This is the room's one light — a thread of dull ember-red, edge-on, almost nothing. It is not constant. Every seven minutes, when the mountain coughs above, a gas slug rises somewhere deep below, a pressure-sigh travels up the throat, the seam brightens from rust-red to cherry, and the room exists visibly for a few seconds: faceted walls, a braid of stone, a floor that moves. Then the dark comes back. Light here is an event, not a condition. The only other light is even briefer — the blue-green sparks that flicker through the quartz when a fracture runs, or when a bite snaps a crystal whisker. Small static in the black.

The air is not air. It is the mountain's breath, dense and hot: two and a half atmospheres of sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide, sulfur vapor, metal-chloride fume — a hundred and sixty degrees at the floor, a hundred and twenty at the crown. There is no water here. There has never been water here. And yet it rains: molten sulfur condenses on the cool crown as dark amber dew, gathers, and falls in slow heavy drops, and at the very top, where it is coolest, a rime of pale brimstone needles grows — the room's only pale thing, unseen. The fumes carry iron, copper, nickel, and they plate out on every surface, atom by atom. The room is gilding itself. Everything in it is slowly being electroplated, the living included.

Now the sound, because sound is the main condition of this place.

There is the stroke: every seven minutes, the pressure-sigh from below. You would not hear it so much as be it — infrasound, a throb felt in the shell and the teeth, the room inflating by a whisper and relaxing. There is the tone: the throat and the chamber together make a bottle, and the mountain blows across it endlessly, a deep organ note that never, ever stops, swelling at each stroke and settling back, and in that small stone vessel, under dense gas, nothing is absorbed, so the note stands and stands. There are the cracks: the mountain is still rising, still shouldering upward a fingernail a year, and when a new fracture runs it goes off like a rifle shot, without warning. And under all of it there is the rain — a dense, continuous patter of rasping, ticking, clicking, like hail on a tin roof, which is the sound of the inhabitants eating.

The inhabitants. Start with the tarnish: a living film over every crystal face, colorless in the dark, simpler than fungus, feeding straight off the gas and the rock — the base of everything. Then the millers. Twenty thousand of them in a room three paces wide. Each is the length of a thumb, low, armored in interlocking scales of iron-plated shell, eyeless — nothing here has ever grown an eye; the room spent its light budget on a single thread. A miller's mouth is a rotary rasp, a milling head of five mineral teeth renewed as they wear, and it planes the crystal as it goes, taking the tarnish and the topmost microns of quartz together, leaving the fine parallel grooves that make the walls look machined. A miller cuts a groove a hair deep in a year. A miller lives three centuries. It navigates by the tone, steering along the standing waves of the organ note; the room's voice is the room's map, and they read it the way you would read light.

In the hollows they lie two bodies deep. The floor is not stone: it is the dead. Half a meter of shell shingle, the accumulated plates of nine thousand years of millers, and it is the richest ore body in this world, mined constantly — the living rasp metal straight off the shells of their ancestors. Nothing is wasted because there is nowhere for waste to go.

Higher on the walls sit the bells: sessile, thin cones of metal shell, sipping the sulfur rain as it runs, straining the fume. When the stroke swells the tone, the bells ring faintly at harmonics — the room answering itself. And in the shingle, rare and patient, waits the flat star: a disc of five slow arms that takes a month to eat a miller and is in no hurry about it. Nothing here hurries. There is nothing to catch and nowhere to escape to. Even the predation is geological.

If you could stand in the room — you cannot; nothing can enter; nothing ever has — you would swear it was an engine. The firebox below, the throat like a valve, the stroke like a piston's slow throw, the tone like a turbine's hum, the walls machined, the workforce plating itself with metal, the shift-whistle every seven minutes when the rasping pauses, all twenty thousand at once, and then redoubles in a wave that sweeps the chamber. Nothing here was built. Everything here works.

And what the factory manufactures is the room itself — which is to say, its own completion. The lining grows inward a hair a year. The slag the millers excrete feeds new growth at the roots of the crystals. The workers are closing their own factory. It is under construction, and the construction is the ending. This is a landscape in its first draft, embryonic, mostly future — and its finished form is a solid geode, dark all the way through, silent.

The rules are few. The stroke is the clock. Light is an event. Nothing enters; nothing leaves. Everything is plated, everything is eaten, everything is used again. The world is shrinking. The world is loud. The world is slow.

Give it forty thousand years. The interlocking spears will meet in the middle; the crawl-spaces will pinch shut one by one; the last generation of millers will live out their three centuries in a seam the width of a hand. As the volume shrinks, the bottle-note rises — across a hundred generations it climbs a semitone, the world's voice lifting toward a whistle it will never reach, because before it gets there, it closes. Then: no tone, no stroke felt, no thread of red, only a fossil engine of smoky quartz with a sediment of iron shells at its heart, while above it the mountain goes on huffing every seven minutes, unaware it was ever hollow.

It may be the highest room in the world, and nothing will ever stand in it that wasn't born there. For now — and here, *for now* means millennia — the stroke comes, the seam blooms cherry-red, twenty thousand rasps rise like rain in the dark, the bells ring at the harmonic, and the note holds.
