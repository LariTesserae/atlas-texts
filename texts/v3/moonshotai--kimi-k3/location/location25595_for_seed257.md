High on the old mountain, a little below the summit, there is a saddle of bald gray stone where nothing has ever grown. The wind is a rasp here, and the frost splits the rock into plates, and the plates slide, and that is all the news the outside world has produced for ten thousand years. No moss. No lichen even — the ice scours it back to bare grain. The only mark on the saddle is a low hummock of fitted stone, a buried dome, and a row of small flue-holes along its crown, and in winter each hole wears a beard of frost-feathers pointing outward, because the hill is breathing, slowly, warm.

This is the Vessel. Everything below that crown is made.

The Door is a single slab, shoulder-width, that slides in a groove worn glassy by hands. Behind it a stair descends — the Long Stair — cut straight into the mountain's skin, and as you go down the air thickens and warms, inverting the world: you climb down out of the cold into the heat. Your breath stops fogging somewhere around the hundredth step. The smell arrives before any sound — wet lime, warm stone, fat-smoke, the dark musk of something alive for a very long time in a closed room. Then the sound, or rather the one sound: a single drip, somewhere far off, repeating on a count so slow you can forget it between falls. The dark down here does not echo. It drinks.

At the bottom of the stair is the Hall, and the Hall is almost entirely water.

It is cathedral-scale and no more — a hundred paces in the long direction, sixty across, irregular, following a hollow the builders found or made. Hundreds of columns stand in it, close as a petrified grove, though no tree ever stood on this mountain; the resemblance is an accident of load and span. Once the water lay far below the vault, a black floor reflecting lamplight. That was long ago. The drains that kept the level grew slowly shut with stone, and the water rose, and rises still, a grain a year, until now it lies within an arm's reach of the keystones. Between the black surface and the vault there is only a shallow, saturated skin of air — the Low Air — warm as a mouth.

The people of the Vessel live in that skin.

There are perhaps twenty of them, a hamlet of the horizontal. Their world is a lattice of rafts tethered between column capitals — platforms buoyed by inflated hides, framed with the eleven remaining spars of the builders' grain-stone, a straight, workable substance the strings say the first makers cut in quantities no one can now explain. Each spar has a name. Each lashing it has ever worn is remembered. From the rafts hang the tools of the only crafts there are: nets of twisted gut and sinew, hooks of bone, a few hooks of the old bright — bronze, heirloom, never made here, hoarded and resharpened across centuries — stone lamps the size of cupped hands, wicks of fine sinew standing in rendered eel-oil. They are lit sparingly. Mostly the keepers work by touch, by memory, and by the birds.

The vault-swifts are the only citizens of the air gap: small, pale, they nest on the column capitals and hunt the film-moths that breed along the waterline, steering through the dark with soft dry clicks — tick, tick, tick — the loudest regular sound in the Vessel and still quieter than a sleeping breath. The keepers listen to them the way sailors listen to gulls. When the clicks drop low, toward the water, the keepers climb. Because the Vessel breathes.

Over a cycle of weeks the spring below swells and the water rises a hand's width, pressing the warm heavy air out through the flues and the door-seam — the exhale — and then falls again, drawing thin mountain air down the stair. At the turn of the breath, gas the spring has fed into the deep water comes up in long slow chains of pearls, and for a while the layer just above the surface goes bad: sleepy, thick. The birds feel it first. This is the first law every child learns: watch the birds, and when the water breathes out, stay high and breathe shallow. The second law: tie everything. Anything let go of in the Vessel sinks, and what sinks does not come back — it goes down through the dark to the floor and begins, within a decade, to turn to stone. The water carries so much dissolved lime that it rinds every surface it touches. The columns have swollen a thumb's thickness in a lifetime; their carved capitals, faces once, maybe, are rounded to knobs. Deep in the hall, nets hung by grandmothers of grandmothers still hang from their tethers, fossilized where they swayed — stone curtains, still mesh-patterned, catching nothing now but lime. The third law concerns the landlords, and is older than the strings.

Below the surface, the water is warm as blood and crowded beyond anything the word "dark" prepares you for. There is no green thing here, no plant of any kind — the base of all this life is the velvet: a slick, dense felt of bacteria and pale fungi that coats every submerged surface in rust and cream and gray, grazing on the stone-breath the spring brings up, on warmth itself, on the water's mineral load. Blind snails pave the columns, rasping the velvet in slow spirals. Shrimp-pale crustaceans, fleas of the undersides, cloud the gaps. And the fish — the fish school in the thousands, eyeless, colorless, and they have a habit no one has explained: each column wears a slow wreath of them, a gyre turning around the pillar, always the same direction, unbroken for years at a time, so that swimming the hall you pass through ring after ring of soft turning bodies that part around your shoulders and close again. There is more life in this black water, by weight, than in any sunlit pool the memory-strings remember.

Eels own the floor — dozens, fat, unhurried, threaded through a sediment that is mostly fallen plaster, shell-molt, and the dust of the velvet. They are the harvest, taken with stone trap-boxes and bone-gig and gut line. And among them, or rather above them, move the landlords: giant salamanders, four of them, each longer than a keeper, older than any knot-count anyone holds. They were old when the strings begin. They are not fished. They are not fed. They are simply acknowledged, the way you acknowledge weather. Sometimes one climbs a column and hangs at the waterline with its broad head resting in the air, motionless, and the keepers go quiet around it and say it is listening. To what, the strings do not record.

The darkness is not absolute. Adapted eyes learn its grays. When the Door is opened, a seam of daylight lies along the stair's end like a spilled line of milk. On certain nights the strings can predict, a needle of moonlight comes down a flue and stands in the black for a few breaths before the water swallows it. And there is the one luminous grace: when the water is disturbed — a swimmer, a thrown line, an eel turning in the sediment — it wakes with faint blue-green embers, a dim phosphor of the velvet, dying in seconds. The keepers read each other's positions by it, hauling nets in the dark among slow constellations of their own making.

Down at the deepest end, past a low arch, lies the First Water — the original cistern, smaller, its stubby columns half-fused into a single mass of sinter, its floor humped like a cave of the earth's own making. Few go there, and none go past it, because at its back is the Throat: a ring of dressed stones, the oldest masonry in the mountain, where the spring enters. The upwelling there is warmer by a degree, fringed with black velvet, a different chemistry. Once, the strings say, a weighted line was let down into the Throat for three days. It came up warm, furred in black, unbottomed. They do not do that now.

Time here is counted in slow denominations. The breath, in weeks. The rind, in lifetimes. The rise of the water, in generations — recorded in the memory-strings, knotted cords of eel-gut that hang in the dry side-vault where the keepers sleep on felt of dried velvet, and the oldest knots of which are mostly concerned with one subject: the closing numbers. For the keepers are not passive in this. There is a chisel of the old bright whose whole purpose, across centuries, is to cut the overflow lip a finger lower when the Low Air grows too thin. The lip is a long polished groove of cuts upon cuts, each one a generation's negotiation with the stone. It is a negotiation being lost, very slowly, a hair at a time. Everyone knows this. The youngest children learn the closing numbers the way other children learn weather.

The Vessel is settling toward its final form. The floor rises with silt; the ceiling descends with stone; the water between them presses gently toward the vault. Somewhere far ahead — the strings disagree only about how far — the last skin of air will close over, the flues will drip instead of breathe, and the mountain will be a solid stone egg with a warm, dark, crowded heart: the fish still turning their wreaths in the black, the landlords still climbing to a waterline with no air above it, the velvet still eating the spring's slow chemistry, patient as geology.

For now, though, there is still the skin of air, and the rafts, and the one drip counting. A keeper lies prone on a hide-float in the dark, face at the warm waterline, watching the embers of her own hands as she works a net, listening to the birds tick overhead, feeling through the platform, faintly, the mountain breathing.
