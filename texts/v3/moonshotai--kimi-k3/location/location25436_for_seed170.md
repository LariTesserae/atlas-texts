## The Forge

The heat is not in the air. This is the first thing to learn. The air, this high, is thin and almost cool — a dry wind that moves across the plateau without weight, thin enough that sound should die in it. But the stone is hot. The stone has been hot for longer than anyone could have told you, and it stays hot through the night, and the heat climbs up through the soles of your feet and lives in your bones like a second pulse. You stand in cool wind on a griddle. The sky above is high, hard, blue-white, ordinary. The light is perfectly normal. That will matter later, when the normal light starts doing things light shouldn't.

Water left early, if it was ever invited. What remains of it is a geography: pans of halite and potash laid out like the beds of evaporated seas, though nothing here evaporates anymore — things *sublime*, going straight from mineral to thin metallic breath at the vents. The pans are blinding, flat, hexagon-plated, and they ring faintly underfoot like a drum skin. Between them rise flows of black glass, dunes of sorted metallic dust that smell of struck matches, hills furred grey-green with fibrous stone that looks, from a distance, almost like pasture. It is not pasture. Nothing here has ever been a plant. The closest the Forge comes to grass is a mineral habit.

No road crosses the pans. No wall, no tool, no mark of a hand, no ruin of a mark. Nothing here was built.

It is, nonetheless, a works.

## The Gearing

Engines are a rock type here. That is the plainest way to say it.

In the district called the Gearing, a cliff face stands as a bank of pistons: columns of basalt, hexagonal, sleeved in fault-ground talc and graphite, cycling on strokes that take decades to complete. A disc-mesa the size of a town turns on a thrust-fault axle, a flywheel completing one revolution each millennium, groaning a chord that has not broken in nine thousand years — harmonics stacked on a tone so low you hear it in your teeth. Scree slopes work as conveyors: thermal peristalsis creeps the stock *uphill*, and it arrives at the top sorted by grade, bright ore in clean braided streams. Dry fumaroles breathe sulfur and metal vapor and lay down veins of native copper and iron, and the wind wears them, and the wearing is the point. Erosion is the machinist. A seam is not finished until ten thousand years of wind have dressed it to tolerance. Nothing here was built, and so nothing here can break — it can only wear, and the wearing is the work.

It is all electrified. Charge builds across the pans and the seams until it goes somewhere: dry lightning, cloudless, standing bolts that walk across the Gearing in clear daylight and vanish, leaving thunder with no weather attached to it. The only weather in the Forge is foundry weather.

The energy economy is simple and total. The ground is hot. The sky, this high, is cold. Between the two runs the gradient, and the gradient is wealth. The works tax the flow of heat from stone to sky, and what they refine from it — their whole product, laid down in thin bright seams along the faults — is *actuality*. Concentrated being. The Forge mines reality and mills it dense.

Which is why the herds come.

## The Herds

They are called spans, and they are the overwhelming fact of the place.

A span is an animal built like a walking cooling tower: a column of dark mineral flesh, finned with radiator vanes of bone and hornblende, standing on legs like pit-props. It must stand tall, because it lives on the gradient — belly to the hot stone, crown in the cold high air, a living heat engine spanning the difference. A herd on the move looks like a refinery that has decided to walk somewhere.

They are not numerous. That is not what overwhelming means here. The Forge is vast and nearly empty — days of walking between presences, a regional emptiness that could swallow countries. But the spans are *more real* than the land they cross, and it shows. They have been grazing the seams of actuality for eons, and it has concentrated in them. A span dents the world. Its footprints do not weather; they fill, over decades, with condensed ore. Its shadow arrives over the ridge *before it does*, falls on the rock with an audible weight, and can leave the stone darkened for years — shadow-burns, marking old crossings. Its voice does not decay at the correct rate. A bellow hangs in a valley for a season, thinning slowly, so that the valleys of the Forge keep resident voices, and you can walk through the ghost of a call made in spring.

They bellow because they must. A span's voice is its radiator: sound is how it sheds heat, the louder the beast the hotter its blood, and the bellow of a bull span in the rut of summer rolls across the empty plateau for three days' walk in every direction. The emptiness is full of their noise. You can be farther from a herd than the horizon and still feel them, through the soles of your feet, like distant stamping machinery. Because that is what they are.

The young are called castings — freshly poured, still cooling, thin. A casting at dusk is faintly translucent; you can see the last light through its ribs. They stay close to the herd, accruing being, growing heavier in the world year by year. The old go the other way: so dense with grazed actuality that movement becomes negotiation. At last an elder stops, seats itself on the stone, and does not rise. Seated elders dot the Forge like monuments, and over millennia they mineralize into what they will become: new works. The Gearing is a graveyard that runs. The industry is what spans turn into; spans are what the industry feeds. The loop closes across eons, and it has never once been hurried.

Around them runs a supporting cast, sparse and strange. Sleeve-cleaners, quick little mineral things, pick grit from the piston-banks like birds off cattle. Swarf-swarms — brief bright pseudo-life, filings and sparks — boil out of the works at each firing and die in hours. Seam-jackals take the dead apart, patiently, over centuries; they are why elders become engines rather than landmarks. High overhead, translucent sails of mica acres wide ride the thermals, grazing charge, drinking the dry lightning — too thin to be much more real than the sky itself.

## The Tolerances

Here is the law the Forge obeys, as near as a visitor can hold it:

*Being is a refined good, and it can be overgrazed.*

Where the herds' Grazing Round has passed too often, the seams run out, and the land goes threadbare — reality eaten down to the root. These districts are called the Tolerances, because in them the world's clearances have gone slack, and things no longer fit their causes the way they should.

In the Tolerances, distances do not conserve. Walking away from a seated elder may bring you closer. Sounds precede their sources by heartbeats, then by minutes, then — deep in — by years. Once a century the great piston-banks of the Stamping Flats complete a single stroke, the Century Blow, a concussion felt across the whole province; its pre-echo arrives seasons early, faint, felt in the fillings of teeth, and the herds lift their heads at it long before there is anything to hear. Shadows lag. Stones, picked up and dropped, sometimes forget to fall for a while. The horizon declines to stay level. Thin reality transmits sound losslessly, which is why the loudest district of the Forge is its emptiest: in the Tolerances you hear *everything*, every bellow and bolt and groan, arriving whole from beyond the curve of the world, sourceless and simultaneous, a foundry heard from outside its walls.

The herds avoid the deep Tolerances. There is nothing there to graze. So the breakdown stands empty, ringed by sound, and heals the way everything heals here: across fifty thousand years, a pan can recover to something like half-real, and is considered, by the standards of the Forge, lush. Solidity is verdancy. The herds return to it the way herds return to grass.

## The Long Shift

Everything in the Forge is slow, and the slowness is load-bearing. A piston's stroke: decades. A flywheel's revolution: a millennium. The Round of the herds: centuries per circuit. The making of a seam, the grazing of it, the death of an elder, its weathering into a new engine — eons, all of it, a production cycle run at the pace of stone. Change happens, but it happens the way mountains happen.

So the Forge is loud the way a cathedral is loud: the stillness is what carries it. Most of the sound arrives through the ground, through bone, the held chord of the Gearing under everything like a drone string. At dusk the cooling stone ticks, a dry slow rain of ticks. Somewhere a herd is bellowing the heat out of its blood. Somewhere clear sky discharges, and thunder arrives with no storm to excuse it. The light goes ordinary and gold and black-shadowed across the pans, and a line of spans crosses the far flat, shadow first, then sound, then beast, in that wrong and accustomed order, each one the size of a parish church, fire running blue along their fins.

Nothing here was made, and nothing here finishes. The works are weathered into their final shapes, and their final shapes are their working shapes, and the herds are heavy with the world's own milled being, and the threadbare districts are growing their reality back one grain per century. It is all in balance — hot, high, ringing, nearly empty, almost broken, and entirely, patiently, in operation.

The Forge has all the time there is. It is spending it one stroke at a time.
