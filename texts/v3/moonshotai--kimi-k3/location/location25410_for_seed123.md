The country around it is dry ten months of the year — a low valley of gravel terraces and thorn scrub, the ground still raw from its own making. Everything here is young. The river that cut the terraces has withdrawn into a pale braid of silt and polished stone, and the bluffs along its bank slump in fresh scallops, exposing tan earth that hasn't yet grown a skin of lichen or dust. Heat rises off the stones by midday. The wind moves nothing. From above, from the goat paths on the terrace lip, there is no reason to look twice at the bluff's base, where the flood of two winters ago tore a bite out of the bank.

But stand at the torn edge and the air changes. It comes out of the dark gap in the ground in slow breaths — warm, wet, smelling of machine oil and wet cement and something green underneath. The fracture is so fresh that the broken concrete still glints: sharp aggregate, quartz faces catching the sun, rebar ends bright as cut wire with only the first orange bloom of rust at their tips. Beyond the fracture, a painted stencil, unweathered, letters that were buried until recently: PUMP CHAMBER No. 3. The steel door in the intact wall is still dogged shut from the outside, sealed decades ago. The world enters now through the wound.

Inside, the room is small — four strides by five — and there is no empty space in it. This is the first thing and the last thing about the place: it is full. A duplex pump squats at the center over a sump pit, its flywheel still turning in a slow, patient stroke, belted to a generator head, the belt slapping its splice once per revolution, soft as a tongue click. Pipes crowd every wall in runs of six and eight, climbing into the earth-dark ceiling. A manifold of brass valve wheels. A gauge board with twelve dials, every needle alive. A chart recorder under a glass dome, its drum still turning, its pen still drawing a thin line across paper that ran out of printed grid years ago and now only records onto the drum's bare backing. One conduit comes down out of the hill from the dead pole line outside, and one bulb burns above the gauge board, though daylight falls through the breach in a solid shaft and the bulb lights nothing that needs lighting. The grease on the fittings is still soft. The log pencil is still sharp. The room kept itself, sealed in the hill's flank, running its warm closed metabolism in the dark, and it has not acknowledged the breach. It has not noticed it was ever buried, or that it is now found.

The water is the strangest thing. The sump in the floor is black, engine-warm, brim-full. The intake pipe runs out through the wall toward the riverbed — the dry riverbed, the river that has not flowed in years — and yet the intake sweats and knocks and delivers. Put an ear to it in the driest month and you can hear water moving in it, a low gravelly rush, floodwater on the move, arriving on the schedule of floods that have not happened. Perhaps they are old floods, working their way through the deep gravel. Perhaps the machine is pumping the river as it was, or as it will be. Twice a year the discharge line — which runs into the hill toward fields that no longer exist — briefly carries water that smells of silt and rot, of drowned grass, of an actual flood somewhere. Small fish live in the sump. They came through the pipe, from the river that isn't there. They are already paler than river fish. Each generation loses a little more color. Give the room another few decades and they will be transparent, and they will have come from nowhere at all.

Because the ceiling is still pressed against the cold earth of the hill and the sump breathes warm vapor, the room has its own weather. It is a climate in a box. Morning: a fog hangs at chest height and the pipes weep. By midday the heat of the breach and the heat of the machine push the vapor up against the cold concrete, and the ceiling begins to give it back — drops gathering on the conduit runs, sliding down the walls, and then, at about two o'clock, a soft indoor rain over the sump, steady and whisper-quiet, dimpling the raft of insects that covers the water so densely it looks like dark fur. By evening the air clears. The room dries its eyes. Tomorrow it will do it again.

Every surface is occupied, and every space between surfaces. The swallows have tiled the ceiling with mud nests packed so tightly between the conduit runs that they share walls. Paper wasps work the chart recorder's housing. Beetles rank shoulder to shoulder in the shadow of every flange bolt. Geckos live behind the switchboard, and their paths across the gauge board have polished the brass handwheels bright as if handled daily by a diligent engineer — no hand touches them, and they gleam. In the chart drawer, one fern sporeling has sprouted in the felt of the pen mechanism; it is the only plant inside, apart from the algal film advancing along the drip lines at a visible rate, a green front line you could mark with chalk and re-measure in a week. The vegetation is losing the race here. The animals are winning it. At dusk the moths emerge — they pupate glued to every warm surface, thousands of them — and for an hour the air in the room is not air but a substance made of wings, and the swallows work the breach, and the bats come after the swallows, and a fox sits outside at the foot of the slump, patient, taking what the exhaust of wings brings within reach.

And it is quiet. That is a rule of the room. Sound falls short inside it; nothing echoes. The pump's stroke is a pressure felt in the sternum more than a noise. The swallows cross the threshold and stop calling — they hunt in silence and resume their voices outside, as if voices are not permitted at the water. Even the indoor rain makes almost no sound on the furred surface of the sump. Drips, the tick of the belt splice, the pen scratching its line: that is the whole orchestra.

The other rule is the schedule. The animals come to the water in an order that does not change, as if the machine's cycle were a clock they can all read. Doves at first light, in the shaft of sun. Then the small mammals, each species in its slot. A heron stands at the breach at its own hour, and nothing contests it. Predators wait for their turn rather than hunting out of turn. The room's two-o'clock rain is observed by everything within a day's walk.

Outside, scattered in the dry, are the faint remains of the world that built this: a concrete apron with a date written in it by a finger, decades old; a dead pole with a row of glass insulators; a rusted drum; a standpipe; three strands of fence wire going nowhere. Whoever sealed the chamber did not say goodbye to it. Perhaps they meant to come back before the river died.

The breach widens a little each year. Gravel ticks down the slump face; another rib of concrete will go soon. The room is emerging — being born out of the hill in slow motion, sharp-edged and new, a warm wet heart exposed in a dry body. The flywheel turns. The pen writes its line to no one. The pale fish circle the sump, and the river, whenever it finally comes back, will find its own water already here, ahead of it, practicing.
