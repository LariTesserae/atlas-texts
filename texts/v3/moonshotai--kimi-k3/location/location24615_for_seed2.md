# The Lower Reservoir

The elevator descends four hundred meters through bedrock and stops at a door stenciled LEVEL −400 / BAY 000 / NO DIVING WITHOUT PERMIT. Beyond the door is a gallery, and beyond the gallery's railing there is water — water to the horizon, flat and faintly steaming with cold, stretching away under a ceiling so distant it reads as weather rather than structure. This is the Lower Reservoir: the drowned basement of a continent, a flood-buffer and pumped-storage basin and cooling sink all in one, built in a single generation, filled eleven years ago, and never once visited by anything that could be called nature except the water itself, which arrived through the intake tunnels and stayed.

Everything here is made. That is the first rule and the strangest. There is no rock. The floor, forty meters down, is painted concrete — pale gray, still legible, lane markings and rail lines and the yellow hash of no-park zones visible through the clear water as if through old glass. The ceiling, eighty meters up, is a flat field of precast panels and armored lamp cages, most of them burning. The columns that hold it are square, eight meters on a side, spaced two hundred and fifty meters apart in a grid that does not end, each one numbered in black paint at the waterline: BAY 114 COL 22, BAY 114 COL 23, the digits repeating into the green distance like a proof that someone once intended to keep track of all of this. Between the columns there is nothing. That is the place's deepest quality — not the water, not the machinery, but the emptiness: bays a kilometer wide holding nothing but cold water and light, a machine every few kilometers, a pump hall every forty. It is a cathedral built by quantity surveyors, and it is almost entirely uninhabited space.

The light is the second surprise. It is normal light. The grid that runs the pumps also runs the lamps, and they burn around the clock in long recession lines, rows of them submerged where the water rose past their mounting height and sealed against it, so that the upper water column glows from above the way a swimming pool glows at night, except the pool is the size of a province. Colors read true down to thirty meters. And then there are the sun wells: shafts cut straight to the surface, eight hundred meters of fluted concrete, gridded at the top, through which real daylight falls in a single vertical column per bay, a solid-looking pillar of sun in which dust and fish both turn. Weather comes down the wells. Rain rings the water beneath them. In autumn, leaves arrive, sinking one at a time through forty meters of lit water to lie on the painted floor like mail delivered to an empty building.

The cold keeps everything young. The water holds at seven degrees year-round, fed by mountain rivers, and at seven degrees rust hesitates and algae creeps. Eleven years after the flood, the paint is still sharp, the welds still readable, the lane lines on the floor unworn because nothing has ever driven on them. Only now are the first freckles appearing: rust halos the size of coins around bolt heads, a fuzz of biofilm on the lamp cages — which then feeds on its own light, since algae grows on the lamps' glass and dims them a percent a year, a slow self-eclipse nobody wipes away. There is no sediment anywhere. Nothing to bury anything. A wrench dropped from a catwalk a decade ago lies exactly where it fell, in focus, on a floor that will never silt. The place cannot age the way drowned places are supposed to; it is held in its first decade by cold and current and the humming zinc anodes bolted along every column, which corrode so the steel won't — sacrificial, steadily consumed, the world's only metabolism that was planned.

It is loud. Underwater, the sound is the sound of the grid itself: the low drone of the pump-turbines, forty kilometers off, arriving through water and concrete as a single continuous note you feel in the sternum before you hear it. Over that, the percussion. When a gate closes somewhere in the system, the pressure wave — water hammer, the engineers called it, the knock — rolls through the bays at the speed of sound in water, a thunderclap with a long concrete tail, and the whole numbered world rings once like a struck hull. Near the turbine halls the water roars outright, funneled through the machines, and the floor thrums, and fish give those bays a wide, remembered berth. In the few high vaults where the waterline drops below the ceiling and leaves an air gap, the sound doubles: the drone, the drip of condensation falling from eighty meters, the slap of it striking water, all of it echoing for eleven seconds off flat hard surfaces. Nothing here absorbs anything. The world is a drum.

And it moves. The Reservoir keeps a tide, and the tide is the power market. At night, when the continent's electricity is cheap, the pumps lift water up to the surface reservoirs and the level here falls — six vertical meters over eight hours, leaving a dark wet band on every column, an intertidal zone that mussels have colonized exactly as if it were a seashore. By day the water comes back down through the turbines, generating, and the tide rises again. Fish set their clocks by it. So does everything. On top of the daily tide come the surge events: a gate opens three hundred kilometers away, and some minutes later the front arrives — not a wave, at this depth, but a decision by the whole body of water to move, a slow wall of current that picks up drifting leaves and loose silt-that-isn't and carries the smell of distant inflow, and the schools of silver fish all turn into it at once, like starlings. Where warm summer river water enters through the intakes it slides over the cold basin water in a shimmering layer, a false surface inside the water, and in the air-gap vaults the meeting of warm and cold makes actual fog — weather, indoors, banks of it drifting across the waterline while condensation rains steadily from the ceiling. The place is vast enough to have forecasts, if anyone were here to make them.

The life came in through the intakes, as eggs and larvae and luck, through screens that failed at one station and were never worth replacing. Now, eleven years on, the Reservoir is stocked by accident and runs like a deliberate thing. The mussels came first and are the foundation: they crust every surface near the flows in reefs of black shells, filtering, and they are the reason the water stays gin-clear at forty meters of sightline — a billion small pumps working for free alongside the big ones. On the mussel reefs, amphipods swarm like static. Crayfish work the column bases at night, pale from the dark, one claw always larger. The midwater belongs to the schools — bleak and shad-like fish, silvered, numbering in the millions per bay, grazing the algae halos on the lamp cages and the plankton washed in with the inflow — and where there are schools there are the hunters: big catfish, two meters and growing, unhurried, drifting out from under the submerged gantry cranes at dusk; pike holding station in the sun wells where the light gathers prey; eels everywhere and nowhere, in the cable trenches and conduit mouths. In the air vaults, swifts nest on ledges below the sun wells and hawk the insects that hatch off the water, their cries thrown back eleven-fold by the echo, and their guano spots the ledges white — the only soil, if it can be called that, in the entire world. There is duckweed in the still eddies beneath three of the wells, a green raft the size of a courtyard, the only true vegetation in four hundred kilometers of bay. It is enough. The food chain runs: leaf-fall and intake plankton to mussel and amphipod, amphipod to school, school to catfish and pike and bird, everything to the eels in the end.

The eels are the ones who object to the arrangement. Every autumn the mature ones go silver and gather at the outlet screens in their thousands, pressing against the grating, trying to leave for an ocean they have never seen and cannot reach, a migration ending in a machine room. The screens hold. The eels wait weeks and then turn back into the bays, and the bays take them again. It is the one moment the system shows its teeth as a boundary rather than a world.

The machines, for their part, notice none of it. They are sparse — a pump hall every forty kilometers, a traveling crane on ceiling rails in every hundredth bay, rail carts parked mid-floor where their operators left them — and they run unattended, on schedules set on the surface, by people who experience the Reservoir as a gauge reading and a power price. Once in a while a maintenance elevator descends and a light moves along a catwalk above the water, and the catfish scatter from the vibration of footsteps, and then the light goes up again and the drone closes over.

The rules here are simple and total. The tide rises by day and falls by night, not the reverse. The knock comes when it comes; everything alive flinches and resumes. Nothing decays faster than the anodes allow. Nothing is buried; nothing is lost; everything dropped remains, catalogued by its fall, on a floor that is also a map. The light does not flicker and does not dim except by algae. The cold holds at seven degrees. And the whole made sea — loud, lit, empty, numbered, young — keeps its own time four hundred meters below the world that owns it, rising and falling six meters a day on the breath of the grid, sharp-edged and silvered with fish, waiting out its first century like a tool that has not yet been used enough to know what it is.
