You hear the plateau before you see it.

A day out, maybe two, crossing the high moss in the cold, while the land itself is still hidden below the curve of the world, the sound is already there: a chord — low, many-noted, unvarying — lying across the thin air like a wire under tension. Wind in stone. Water in steel. The hum of current. And beneath all of it, so distant it might be a property of the sky itself, thunder. Thunder that does not approach and does not pass. It holds its station at the horizon like a lighthouse that has chosen noise over light. Walk a day toward it and the chord does not grow louder so much as more detailed: it resolves, it has parts, the parts have parts. Everything here can be learned by ear, because everything here repeats. That is the first law of the place, and the last: nothing changes. Everything else follows from that.

The land, when it finally rises, is young — sharply, almost indecently young. Basalt colonnades stand in fresh tiers along the fault scarps, their hexagonal faces unweathered, splitting away in slabs that ring when they fall. The scree at their feet is angular as broken crockery; nothing has had time — no, nothing has been permitted — to round it. At night the freeze-thaw cracks like rifle fire across the high slopes, and by morning fresh stone has come down, bright at the fracture, black as the inside of nothing. And yet the range is not being worn away. It is being worn away at exactly the rate it is being built. Uplift and erosion balance to the gram; the mountain sheds and the mountain rises and the profile stays fixed — a young profile, held. The whole plateau is like this: early-formed and permanently so, a landscape that never graduates. Along the headwalls, hanging icefields flow and calve into their own melt streams at matching rates — glaciers in perfect balance, blue, motionless-seeming, in fact moving like everything else here, which is to say: constantly, and without consequence.

The wet is everywhere, and it comes mostly from the air. Rain is an event, and events are not this world's way. Instead the plateau carries a standing cloud — a vast stacked cap anchored over its highest dome, continent-wide, turning slowly or seeming to — and the cap wrings itself out, steadily, eternally, onto the high country. The mist never lifts and never thickens. It condenses on every cold surface: on the colonnades, on the rails, on the cables, on the leaves of everything that grows, until all of it drips with a rhythm you could set a clock by, and the clocks here keep precisely that time. The water table lies at the surface like a skin. Every hollow holds a black lens of water; the ground is a sponge that sags a centimeter underfoot and gives back cold. At dawn the pools wear thin skins of ice — always the same pools, always the same thickness, gone by the same hour. The streams that braid the moss run clear and exact. They never flood and never dwindle. Hydrology as a held note.

What grows in this is a low, deep, engorged green. The moss goes down a meter and more, saturated, hummocked, so lush it reads as a kind of standing water itself. Sedge meadows move in silver-green sheets under the constant wind. Cushion plants sit like poured concrete made soft, mounded between the stone polygons the frost has sorted into nets and hexagons — the ground tessellating itself, patiently, as if imitating the basalt, as if everything here aspired to geometry. There are no flowers. There is nothing to pollinate; there are no pollinators; there is nothing with wings, or legs, or mouths. The flora is spore and wind and patience: moss, sedge, clubmoss, lichen in crusts of rust and ash and sulfur-yellow over every rock and rail. And where the works bleed warmth — along the steam lines, beside the outfalls of cooling water — horsetails rise head-high in primeval thickets, jointed, dark, whispering, a Carboniferous fringe around the machine. The cold stalls decay, so the dead do not rot: they stand. Generations of moss die upright and become peat exactly where they grew, a peat that has never known a footprint, never been browsed, never been burned. No animal has ever pressed this ground. There are no bones here, and there have never been bones.

The emptiness is the medium. Between presences — a pylon line, a pump house, a tram stop with its small glass shelter lit at noon — there is nothing, for a day's walk, for two: moss, scree, black water, tessellated ground, wind from its constant quarter. In the moss country there stands a relay station the size of a shed, alone in all that space, humming, its one window lit, its doorstep never crossed. It is not waiting for anyone. Waiting is not done here. The plateau is broad enough to make its own weather, and its weather is furniture: the cap fixed over the dome; the wind fixed in its quarter; and at the horizon, fixed in place as a mountain, the storm — the Thunder — a cumulonimbus the size of a small country that never moves and never spends itself, flickering each night in the same place, its rumble folded into the chord. Days beyond, the rim of the plateau falls away in a sheer young scarp into a sea of cloud that never breaks. What lies below — lowlands, ocean, nothing — the plateau does not say, and there is no way down, or the way down is also the way back up: the cableway buckets rise again out of the white, empty, on schedule. At night, from the high dome, one other light stands at the limit of the world, fixed, far. Another works, perhaps. Or the same one. The plateau does not say.

The Works — capital-W, as the stencils put it — occupy a valley above the rim scarp: a town-scale tangle of brick halls and riveted steel, of penstocks plunging down the headwall in sheathed bundles, of cooling towers fuming clean white steam into clean cold air. It is all hydroelectric; there is no smoke; the air stays crystalline, smelling of ozone, wet stone, cold iron. Rust and fresh paint exist here in equilibrium — a stain appears, a machine repaints; the balance holds. It rains indoors in the great halls, steadily, condensation falling from the roof trusses into gutters laid for exactly that purpose, the drains running always. Ore comes in on cableways from adits in the fresh basalt, buckets spaced like beads across the void, creaking over the trestle saddles. The smelter feeds the mill; the mill rolls rail and plate and wire and blade; the trams — lit, empty, their trolley poles sparking blue in the dusk — carry parts out to the mines, the penstocks, the pylons, which wear, and are repaired, and wear. The ingot yards hold the surplus, stacked in long bright rows, and the oldest ingots are quietly fed back into the furnaces. Nothing leaves the plateau. There is nowhere to leave to. The product of the Works is the Works: a closed industrial metabolism, manufacturing its own persistence.

Districts are perpetually under construction and never different. Cranes stalk the skyline, assembling a hall of exactly the pattern of the hall beside it, while the hall beside it is dismantled for the material to do it again. Below the yards, stairs descend through levels — older-looking brick, disused gauges, a flooded vault where moss grows in the lamplight — and the descent feels like archaeology until you understand that the lower is not older. It is only lower. Every stratum is maintained like every other; the deep levels are rebuilt on the same schedule as the roof. History would lie about this place, so the place declines to have one. And everywhere, the signs: stenciled warnings, serial numbers, arrows, KEEP CLEAR, PENSTOCK 2, WORKS NO. 3, and across the gable of the great hall, letters ten meters tall spelling a word that names nothing — or names all of this, the whole arrangement, the moss and the thunder and the loops of rail, and there is no one to correct it. The signs are for no one the way the moss is for no one. They persist because the template includes them.

And always, the chord. Stand in the yards at dusk and take it apart: the organ-pipe colonnades singing in the constant wind, a flute-tone with gravel in it; the penstocks' deep thrum, felt in the soles before the ears; the transformer yards' high wire-whine; the periodic clatter of the trams — periodic, because the schedule is the same schedule every day, so even the clatter is a held pattern; the rifle-crack of freezing stone, statistically steady; the Thunder at its post. It sums to one sound, and that sound is the same at every hour of every day, and you could navigate by its harmonics as by stars. Nothing here has ever heard silence. If the chord stopped — it has never stopped. That is the whole sentence.

The light, for what it's worth, is ordinary — almost kind. A normal sun on a normal arc, thinned and sharpened by the altitude, throwing hard shadows off the young rock, burning the same mist off the same hollows at the same hour each morning. Each night the same ice skins the same pools. Each dawn the frost feathers the same rail at the terminus, where the same tram arrives at the same minute and opens its doors onto no one, waits its interval, and closes them again. No midge rises off the pools. No bird crosses the empty sky. The spores drift, the moss drinks the fog, the ingots cycle, the glaciers flow in place, the mountain rises exactly as fast as it falls. Everything moves; nothing happens. Any one of these processes alone would have run down long ago — silted, rusted, consumed itself, changed. The strangeness of the plateau is not any magic in its parts. It is the tuning: every rate balanced against every other, from the drip off a valve stem to the standing wave of the climate, held. Whether something holds it or it holds itself, the plateau does not say. The plateau does not say anything. It hums.

At night the Works light up across the empty valley, window after window, sodium and arc-light, steam rising through the beams. The trams cross the dark throwing sparks. The cap is invisible overhead but present, pressing gently down; at the rim the Thunder flickers at its station, lighting the standing cloud from within, patient as a heartbeat. The moss takes the fog in the dark, engorging by the gram. The pools lie still in their polygons. The ice forms on schedule. The chord goes on. Nothing is missing. No one is missed. This is a world that is complete at every moment — loud, lush, lit, uninhabited, young every morning — and it has no end in it, only continuation, which here is the same thing.
