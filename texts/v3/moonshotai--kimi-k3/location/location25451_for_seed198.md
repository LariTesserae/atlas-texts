From the rim, in the hour before the cloud drops for the day, the valley does not read as something built. It reads as a geology. A steep-sided basin, high in the range, filled to its walls with a gray-green mass that could be scree, could be lichened cliff, could be some enormous pelted thing lying in a fold of the mountains. Then the light shifts, or your eyes do, and the mass resolves: roofs. Roofs upon roofs, deck upon deck, wavy glass panes catching the sky, the black threads of flumes crossing on trestles, and laid through it all like veins — moving water, more water than stone. Underneath the sight of it arrives the sound, or rather the absence: a valley-sized hush, with a thread of water-hiss sewn through it, so low you hear the wind in the rim grass and the blood in your own ears.

The trail from the pass becomes a stair. The stair becomes someone's roof-walk. The roof-walk becomes a lane with a channel on each side and a deck overhead, and by then you are inside the thing, descending through its layers the way you'd descend through a body.

Everything is made. There is no soil anywhere. Even the streambed is masonry, laid so long ago that the mortar has gone the color of the stone. The materials repeat until they feel like strata: quarried block from the valley's own walls, brick, concrete patched over concrete in layers you could date like a core sample, creosoted timber gone black and soft-edged, corrugated iron furred green with algae, glass replaced pane by pane for a century until no two sheets in a roof match. Stair nosings are worn into smiles. Valve wheels shine like altar metal where generations of hands have polished them. Nothing looks new, and nothing is ruinous, because nothing is ever allowed to finish falling apart. When a shed is retired it is cannibalized on the spot — plank, bolt, and hinge fed back into the fabric. The valley is its own quarry.

The stream that carved the basin still enters at its head, cold and fast off the high snowfields. But between the intake gates and the outfall at the foot, it is taken apart and rebuilt. It runs overhead in flumes, underfoot in grated channels, through walls in pipes that sweat like cold jugs. It is split, leveled, measured through notched weirs, lent to the turbines in the hall at mid-valley, and returned. Every terrace has its own water at its own height, and the heights are numbered — directions here are water directions. Third level, flume nine. Above the second weir, below the packhouse intake. And because the air is cool and saturated, every surface below the dew point weeps: walls carry a slow shine, drips fall off deck edges in long unhurried time, and the whole valley smells of wet stone, green leaf, and a faint peppery sharpness.

That sharpness is the reason for all of it. The terraces are troughs — long stone beds of graded gravel with cold water drawn through them — and the troughs grow the green: low lush rosettes, broad soft leaves, a knuckled pale rhizome beneath the gravel that carries all its heat in its flesh. It wants what the valley has — cold, shade, running water — and it wants nothing else, and so a whole basin has been arranged into a machine for keeping water cold and slow and clean. Workers wade the troughs in oilskins, lifting the roots, washing them, grading them with a quickness that looks like a card trick. Anywhere you stand, some fraction of the troughs are drained and open, being regraveled and replanted; the crop is on a rotation decades deep, and the harvest never really starts or stops.

The second green is the one nobody planted. Moss on every face that leans away from the light. Ferns unzipping from the mortar seams. Algae as a black-green gloss on the steps, slick as grease. Liverwort in the drip lines. The air itself seems capable of germinating things — leave a tool on a bench for a season and you come back to find it furred. Keeping the valley and growing in the valley are the same argument carried on daily with scrapers and brushes, and the argument is never won, only maintained.

The light is a rumor most of the day. The walls are too steep, the cloud comes down too faithfully; the sun clears the rim only near noon and then only in clear weeks, sending one pale blade down the far slope that the old hands can read like a clock face. Otherwise the valley lives in a green-gray diffusion, and by lamps. The turbine hall feeds a web of wire strung over every working level, caged bulbs burning from midafternoon on, each one doubled in the water beneath it, so that at dusk the terraces look like long trays of light carried at different heights. Windows go amber early. Dusk is not an event here, only a deepening.

The quiet has parts. The shaped water-hiss, kept low by a thousand small drops instead of a few large ones — the whole plumbing of the place seems tuned to murmur. The clack of a sluice gate, wooden and abrupt. The thrum of the turbine hall, which you feel in the soles of your feet more than hear. Boots on wet planks. People speak little and low; over the troughs they use a code of whistles and hand signs old enough that nobody teaches it, you just grow up knowing it. A bell at the packhouse marks the shift. On still nights, standing at the outfall, the whole basin gives off a combined murmur like a sleeping crowd.

And yet the place never holds still. That is the thing the hush hides. From any high deck at working hour you can watch it: hundreds of figures moving through the mist, each on some small errand — a basket of roots, a plank, a wrench — and the sum of all those errands is transformation. A flume fails at dawn and is replaced by dark, by a crew that has done it so often they barely speak. A deck extends itself over last year's deck. A stair is rerouted around a trough that wasn't there a decade ago. Nothing in the valley is finished; the fabric of it turns over entire within a working life, and yet every replaced part is made in the manner of the thing it replaces, so the whole stays ancient-looking while never being the same twice. Decay and repair are balanced like water at a weir. The valley has found its form, and it keeps the form by endless small change.

When the high cloud bursts, the quiet rule breaks, and that is the only time it breaks. Then the valley becomes an instrument played at full volume: every sluice manned through the night, lanterns moving in the rain, spillways roaring white, actual shouting, gate to gate. By morning the hush is back, but the valley is wearing a slightly different face — a shed gone, a channel rerouted, gravel redistributed by hands that were already at work before the rain stopped.

There are few animals, and you mostly notice their absence. No songbirds in the deep levels; it's too dim for them. Swifts hunt the rim and never come down. The cisterns hold a small dark fish, stocked against larvae, more tool than pet. Moths work the lamps. At night the slugs come out on the moss, big as a thumb. A heron visits the settling basins at dawn and is tolerated, considered a good judge of weather. There is a cat at the packhouse, or there is supposed to be; nobody sees it often. The animal energy of the valley is the green itself, growing.

The rules are few and absolute. The water above every intake stays cold and clean — nothing is discharged uphill, no waste, no warmth; the latrines and the cookhouse and the grating room all drain below the last intake, and the turbine water goes back settled and clearer than it arrived. Violating the water is the one crime. The rotation is kept. The lamps are kept. Beyond that, nobody much governs; the work governs.

What the valley cannot make comes up the incline — a funicular on the far wall, counterweighted with water, crawling up to the rim road with crates of rhizome packed in leaves and coming back down with timber, glass, salt, wire. Down in the lowland cities they grate the green root and eat its heat and have no picture of where it comes from: a cold high basin where the warmth lives only in the cookhouse, the turbine hall, and the grating room, where wool is worn year-round and breath smokes on the stairs at dawn.

At the foot of the valley the water is given back. It leaves through a stone mouth as a stream that looks almost natural, almost innocent, and goes down the mountain carrying no memory of its use. Stand there at dusk and look up: the lamps coming on level by level, reflections threading through the troughs, the cloud descending over the rim like a lid on a pot. The hush. The pepper smell. Somewhere a sluice clacks, and answers itself in echo, and no one calls out.

It is one thing — garden, engine, house — and it has been one thing long enough that it no longer knows the difference. It changes constantly in order to remain itself.
