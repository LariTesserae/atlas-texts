**The Chordlands**

Come to the rim in the grey hour before dawn, and you will think you have found a fossil city. Below you and out to the horizon — a week's walk of country, all of it visible at once — stand the towers. Thousands of them. Bone-pale stone in clusters and ranks: fat keeps, leaning flutes, blades thin as knives, arches striding between neighbors, all of it rising out of chasms whose floors no eye reaches from up here. In the calm before sunrise they seem utterly silent, abandoned by whatever built them. The light is ordinary — good, honest, equatorial light, gold coming up under a clear sky — and that ordinariness is the country's one deception. Nothing about the sky warns you.

Then the sun strikes the lowlands beyond the rim, the air warms and stands up, and the land begins to play.

Every tower is pierced — fretted through with slots, chimneys, and windows worn by uncountable years of wind — and every hole is a mouth. The big keeps give a bass drone you feel in your sternum and the roots of your teeth before you hear it. The chasm-mouths moan tenor. The mid-sized flutes wail alto, and the thin blades shriek at the top of the range, a soprano that carries for days. When fog plugs a vent and the pressure bursts it free, the country fires a bass drum — the hermits call those *coughs*, and a big one will stagger you at half a mile. At storm-peak, when the whole field is sounding at once, the Chordlands reach what the locals call the white chord: a deafening so total you can shout into a friend's ear and watch them not hear it. Everyone here carries earplugs of waxed moss on a cord around the neck. Speech is half hand-sign, half touch — messages drummed through stone with fingertips. And then, sometimes, the wind veers, and silence falls like a dropped cloth, and your ears ring in the sudden absence, and in those lulls you hear the small true sounds of the place: water ticking off stone, the bell-crickets starting up, a wingbeat, a distant axe.

These are not mountains. They are what mountains leave behind. The rock that remains is the hardest fraction of an ancient range, and everything softer — and nearly everything was softer — has been carried off grain by grain. The country is erosion's finished work, its final answer; nothing here can be removed that hasn't already been. You are standing in the negative space of mountains, inside the hole they left. Their missing mass lies below as dune-fields at the chasm mouths, pale sand that booms a deep bass of its own when storm-wind slides its faces. The stone has not changed in longer than anyone's stories reach. What changes — constantly, violently, hourly — is the weather. The oldest land wears the youngest possible sky, and that argument between them is the whole character of the place.

There are no rivers. There is barely any standing water at all. What the country has instead is fog — a river of breath. Each morning the warm lowland air climbs the rim and condenses against the high cold stone, and you can watch it pour between the towers like slow rapids, pooling in the chasms, rising through them in the afternoon, draining back at dusk: tides of mist on a daily clock. Warm storms arrive as chord-changes — the key shifts, the coughing starts, and then ten minutes of hard rain that falls almost vertically and is gone. In those ten minutes the whole land drinks at once. Basins on the tower-tops fill and hold for days as cistern-pools. Hour-pools appear in hollows on the chasm floors and vanish by dusk. Every surface is combed for moisture; every living thing here is a device for catching fog.

And here is the country's first strange law, which every child of the ledges knows and no one explains: **the green follows the loud.** Where the stone sings hardest, the vibrating faces shake the fog's droplets together until they merge and fall — the sound itself waters the land. So the windward face of every tower, the face that takes the prevailing voice, wears a fur of moss an arm thick, while the quiet lee is bare bone. Each spire trails a green beard angled precisely with the local wind. The tower-tops are crowned with fog-trees — squat, crouching, sculpted by the blast into aerodynamic fists — whose comb-leaves strip moisture from the passing mist and strum faintly, adding their own small notes to the chord. Vine-veils bridge the gaps between towers, heavy with drip, gardens hanging in empty air. Most of the moss is a resurrection kind: grey as ash when dry, and then a storm-burst hits and it flushes emerald in minutes, whole cliffsides blinking green and grey and green again as the squalls pass. From the rim on a turbulent afternoon the country seems to flicker.

Life is rare here, and it knows it. The windray — a great dark membrane-winged thing, wide as two outstretched arms — rides the vertical rivers of air between the towers, climbing without a wingbeat, and because almost nothing else moves in all that emptiness you can watch a single ray for an hour. Most of the fauna has gone mute; calling is useless in the white chord, so creatures signal by display, by touch, by drumming tail or foot on resonant stone. Only the bell-crickets have made peace with the noise: they own the lulls, and their synchronized ringing rises in every drop-out like an answer. A silent vulture works the noon thermals. That is nearly all. Most of the country's volume is air, and the air is mostly empty — the green lives on surfaces, and between the surfaces are gulfs you could lose a cathedral in.

The people are as sparse as everything else. You travel two days and see one thread of smoke. A hermit-tuner keeps a cluster of flutes, hammering hardwood wedges into the vents to pitch them, tuning a tower the way you'd tune an instrument — a life's work, since the wedges weather and the note drifts. Gut-strings stretched across chasm-mouths hum signal-notes that carry for miles. Rope bridges with stone counterweights cross the narrower voids; chipped handhold stairways spiral up the popular spires; cairn-shrines mark the nodes where the chord comes sweetest. Their crafts are stone, wood, fiber, bone, hide — with, rarely, a cold-hammered copper bell, a prestige thing, traded hand to hand for generations. They sing only in the lulls, or else deliberately against the drone: choirs pitching harmonies off the land's note, a ritual they call *singing the tower up*.

There are older marks. Baffle-walls on certain ledges. Flutes plugged with shaped stones now petrified in place. A stair that climbs half a tower and stops at nothing. Some people before these people tried to quiet the country — or to perfect its voice; the hermits argue — and they are gone, and their work remains in fragments, and sometimes in a great storm one of their ancient plugs fails and a tower wakes after centuries of silence with a note nobody living has heard. This is an event. People walk for days to listen.

And then there are the echo-wells, the country's second strange law: convergent hollows where the geometry of the stone holds sound. A storm's thunder comes back out of a well three days later, replayed by the next wind. Locals keep their voices low near them and leave small offerings at their rims, and they will tell you, quite matter-of-factly, that a shout made in childhood may come home to you at middle age, changed by its years in the stone.

A day here has a shape. The pre-dawn Quiet — the only true silence, when even the drone sleeps and the stars are ordinary, good stars. Sunrise warming the lowlands, the fog standing up in the chasms, the first flutes finding their notes by midmorning. The white chord at noon with its ten-minute storms. The long collapse of afternoon, everything dripping, the moss blazing. Evening lulls full of bell-crickets and choir-song. Night: a low breathing drone under the dark. And larger rhythms ride under that — the chord-seasons, when the prevailing wind shifts and the whole country changes key, towers going mute as silent ones wake; the Fretful Weeks when the winds fight and the land is all arrhythmic percussion; and the Long Note, a weeks-long stable chord under which the scattered families converge on the Bowl, a natural amphitheater where the sound gathers sweetly, for the year's one market, the year's marriages, the year's news — before dispersing again into the emptiness, each back to their own tower, their own note.

The mountains are gone. Their weather never found out.
