The way in is down.

From the high bare country — itself no generous place, all wind-scoured stone the color of old iron — the ground breaks into long slopes that fall and keep falling. The rock underfoot is rounded, fused, settled into itself. This is an old way down, worn less by water than by uncountable small feet. The air cools as you descend. It dampens. And it begins, faintly, to make a noise.

You hear the Lowdark long before you could ever see it. Hearing, here, is very nearly all there is.

The basin lies under a lid of cloud that has not lifted in any age. Whether anything burns above that lid is pure conjecture; what reaches the floor is less than dusk, a charcoal seep of not-quite-light that most of the time fails altogether. Darkness is not an event here. It is the medium, the given, the standing condition of the world.

So everything that lives — and everything lives — has made other arrangements.

The first arrangement is sound.

The hum is the floor of it: a low standing note that arrives through the soles of your feet as much as through your ears. Part of it is geological — cold seeps breathing gas through hollow stone, the whole basin faintly resonant, a flute the size of a country. Part of it is simply the accumulated voice of uncountable bodies, a drone of churring and rasping and clicking so dense it stops registering as many sounds and becomes one, the way rain on a roof becomes a kind of silence.

Above the hum comes the percussion: sharp clicks like struck stones, some loud enough to feel as pressure behind the eyes — those are hunting bursts, concussion-clicks meant to stun. Above the percussion, the screams. Mating screams, territorial screams, screams whose reasons you would rather not know. And over everything, in passages, wing-thunder — the smother going over, millions of small wings beating the dark air into something with weather in it.

It is deafening, and it does not stop, and after a time you cease to hear it as noise. You hear it as the condition of the air.

The floor of the basin is black stone, slick as poured glass — and almost nothing on it is stone.

Start at the bottom. Where the cold springs well up out of the deep rock, carrying sulfur and iron and older chemistries, the velvet spreads. It is not a plant. It is not a fungus. It is something simpler than either, a film that eats stone-sweat and multiplies, rust-colored at the rich seeps, gray at the thin ones, black where it is old. It is the base of everything, and it is grazed the way a field is grazed, except that the field is slick and the grazers never sleep.

The rasp-slugs come first — thumb-sized, forearm-sized, every size between — in carpets. The sound of them is the sound of sandpaper in rain. In the rich low hollows they lie knee-deep, a slow tide of them, and the floor is not the floor: it is a surface that moves, patient and wet, in one grazing direction. You do not step on the ground here. You step on the crowd that lives on the ground.

Above the slugs, the skitters: armored hoppers that ping off stone like thrown gravel, feeding on the slugs' leavings and on each other, their collisions a constant random percussion. And moving among them, strangely, the gathered — colonies of small hooked bodies that assemble themselves, thousands strong, into a single walking shape the size of a house. The shape picks its way across the floor on legs that are individuals. When it finds a carcass it flows over it like a poured thing, and when it is done it walks on, and nothing about it decides anything, and yet it decides.

The hunters work the middle air. Flightless, naked, their skin gray and earless-looking until the great folded sails of their hearing unfurl — they hunt by shriek-and-listen, screaming into the dark and reading what comes back. Some have gone further: their clicks arrive as blows. You feel a successful hunt as a thump in the sternum, a small concussion, and then the brief unraveling sounds of something being taken apart.

Overhead, the smother. Tiny winged things beyond numbering, feeding on the motes the velvet casts into the air — a plankton of the sky that has never needed light. When the smother passes, the basin's faint not-quite-glow dies entirely: an eclipse made of wings, a darkness inside the darkness. Its droppings fall like a soft ash, hour after hour, feeding the floor.

And above the smother, known only by their wing-beats — slow, huge, spaced wide apart — the high fliers cross beneath the lid. No one sees them. Everything hears them go.

The basin makes its land out of its dead.

There are no leaves here, but there are skins. Everything molts, constantly, and the shed husks blow like weather, drifting into hollows as molt-snow, compacting into shell-dunes that migrate slowly across the floor over the course of years. At the roosting cliffs, terraces of droppings and compacted armor have built up over ages into pale towers; at the cliff bases lie talus slopes of bone. The stone itself is going over, grain by grain, into bodies, and the bodies are going over, generation by generation, into stone. The accretion and the wearing are in balance. It is a geology that moves at the speed of appetite, and it has been moving at exactly this speed for longer than anything remembers.

Water, here, is a behavior, not a body. There are no rivers. There is the sweat of the cloud-lid, condensing on every cool surface and running down in threads. There are the seep-springs, cold and mineral, never more than a dampness with intent. Sometimes a rain comes — fine, cold, grain-small — that darkens the stone and is gone into its pores within the hour, and everything in the basin drinks from the rock then, licking the films, and the velvet blooms, and the grazing tide thickens, and the hunters grow fat and loud. Water is an occasion. The damp is the constant: every surface carries its film, every hollow its skin of stillness.

Two more arrangements, because the dark is strange here and rewards strangeness.

The first is the ring-stones. Certain formations in the basin — hollow towers, thin blades of rock, whole cliffs — resonate when struck, and the creatures of the floor have known this longer than they have known anything. They drum the stones with horn and hoof and thrown shell to declare territory, and at the height of the breeding pulse entire cliff-faces sing, struck a thousand times a minute, chords rising off the stone and rolling for miles across the floor. And there are the echo-wells: canyons that return sound too late, and slightly wrong, as though the land were remembering what was said some time ago and choosing to repeat it now. Hunters station themselves at the wells and let the lying echoes flush prey toward them. Do not trust the second sound. The second sound belongs to the land.

The second arrangement is the light. There is light in the Lowdark — small, cold, blue-gray, like a candle seen through deep water. It appears alone, at floor level, and it does not move, and it is beautiful, and it is the only beauty the basin offers, because the only light in the Lowdark is bait. Everything here that glows is a mouth. Whatever still keeps its eyes — and most things let them go long ago, eyes being a maintenance cost in a world without photons — keeps them for one purpose: to be drawn. If you see light, something is hungry, and it can see you seeing.

And then, rarely, the Hush.

It comes without transition. One instant the basin is its full wall of voice — the hum, the clicks, the screams, the thunder — and the next there is nothing. Nothing. Every rasp-slug frozen mid-rasp, every skitter stone-still, the smother holding its wings. The silence is total and it is the loudest thing you will ever experience, because it is the only warning the Lowdark gives. Something is moving that makes no sound at all. The Hushmaker is not legend; it is simply large, and quiet, and rarely hungry, and when it passes, the basin holds its breath for seconds or for minutes, and then the sound comes back — ragged at first, then swelling, then full, the way a crowd finds its voice again after terror — and the great metabolism resumes.

The air smells of ammonia and sulfur and wet iron, of musk and old shell. Your breath steams. The cool is not cruel but it is sincere, and warmth in the basin is a manufactured thing: the creatures make it out of each other. In the cold troughs between pulses you find the huddle-mounds — rasp-slugs and skitters and small hunters all piled together in steaming heaps, predator and prey stacked in armistice, waiting out the chill in the only furnace available, which is one another. Then the velvet blooms somewhere, or the smother thins overhead, and the mound dissolves back into appetite.

There are no days. There are pulses. The rain-pulse, when the films bloom and the floor boils with grazers. The breeding pulse, when the ring-stones sing all around the horizon and the screaming reaches pitches that make the teeth ache. The molt-tide, when the skin-snow blows. The lean times, when the hunters take each other. All of it cycles, and the cycles are steady, and the steadiness is ancient — a system that found its balance so long ago that the balance itself has become a kind of geology, layered, load-bearing, the way the basin holds itself up.

Stand on the floor and let it all arrive at once. The hum in the bones of your feet. The percussion felt in the sternum. The floor moving under you in one slow grazing direction. The smother going over like weather, erasing a sky that was already erased. Somewhere a ring-stone struck, once, twice, and its chord rolling away over the shell-dunes, and an echo-well answering late and wrong. Somewhere a cold blue candle-light, patient, hungry. The dark air thick with wings and voices, saturated, layered from the stone-sweat to the lid — a forest's worth of stratified life, except that nothing in it grows. Everything gathers. Everything hunts or is hunted or is, for the moment, a mound of shared warmth.

The rules, if you stay long enough to learn them:

Darkness is the medium. Sound is the light.

The only light is a lure.

Silence is the only warning.

Nothing grows; everything gathers.

The dead are the ground.

And the last rule, the one the basin itself seems to live by, humming it up through the floor at every hour of its endless night: to be is to be heard. It does not ask to be seen. It has never once been seen. It is a country-sized crowd of life in the dark, complete, in balance, unwitnessed — and busy, deafeningly, gloriously busy, being heard.
