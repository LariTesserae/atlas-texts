From the air — and there is a great deal of air, thin and cold, before you ever reach the water — the sea looks like a lens laid over a relief map. It sits on the roof of the world, a body of fresh water some eleven thousand kilometers across, cupped in a high plateau of young black rock, and it is so clear that on still days the drowned mountains show through it: the upper arêtes first, sharp as torn metal, then the saddles, then, as a deepening blue suggestion, the flanks going down into water that never quite becomes dark. The light is ordinary. That is one of the strangest things here. A normal sun, a normal day, normal blue distance — all of it over an ocean cold enough to kill you in four minutes.

The water holds at a degree or two above freezing at the surface, and precisely at four degrees in the deeps, where fresh water is densest and content to lie still. It tastes faintly of metal and of nothing else. There is no plankton in it, no algae, no drifting fiber, no silt, no pollen, no dust. A beam of light needs something to strike before it becomes visible, and here there is nothing to strike — so the light is invisible in transit. You see sources and surfaces only: the sun, the rock, the bright back of an animal a kilometer away. Between them, nothing. The water is not like air; it is like the idea of air. This emptiness is not natural. It is maintained.

The engines stand scattered across the immensity, three hundred to eight hundred kilometers apart, and they are industrial in the old, heavy sense: riveted intake towers like grain elevators risen from the depths, turbine halls astride the saddle ridges, gantry cranes frozen mid-gesture, penstocks the diameter of road tunnels following the ridge lines down. Some are moored platforms riding the open water, their stacks venting warm exhaust into the thin cold sky, their hulls cathodically bright after — there is no way to say how long. They drink the sea, strain it, warm it a fraction, and give it back. They are why the water is empty. They are why the water is liquid at all, most likely; their waste heat, circulated through standing gyres that have not shifted in recorded time, is exactly sufficient to hold the whole reservoir a hair above ice. Whoever built them is absent. The engines do not appear to have noticed. Governor-locked, self-repairing, tended by small maintenance crawlers that walk the flanges and weld as they go, they run at constant load, constant pitch, constant purpose.

And they are loud beyond any reasonable preparation. Sound carries four times faster in water than in air and essentially forever, and a single one of these machines can be heard across an entire basin — so the whole sea hums, everywhere, always. Long ago the engines drifted into mutual resonance and locked there, and now the world holds a chord: three notes where the bottom is shallow, five where it deepens, with the white-noise floor of the Rim falls beneath everything, a bass you feel in the sternum before you hear it. Near an intake the pressure of it can bruise. Far from everything, in the middle of the emptiness, it is still there, arriving from all directions at once, as though the water itself were the instrument. There has never been silence here. Nothing in this world can form the concept.

Which makes the census deceptive. By the standards of any living sea, this one is abundantly populated — millions of animals, perhaps more — but spread so thin across the vastness that you may travel for weeks and see no body at all. You hear them constantly. The sea is empty of neighbors and full of company. The great ones, the ridge-grazers, are the size of ships and the temperature of the deep: slow, silicate-plated, magnetite-dark, patient as geology. They eat stone, in a manner of speaking. The young rock of this place exhales a perpetual micron-thin film of mineral precipitate onto every surface it offers, and the grazers harvest the film, and the film regrows, and nothing is ever depleted. This is the only pasture, and it is invisible. Their voices are infrasonic, below the engine band, felt rather than heard — a pressure like a hand laid flat on your chest from fifty kilometers away. When one surfaces near an island, the needle on the old brass gauge in the keeper's hut trembles.

The smaller life gathers where the warmth is. Every engine wears a halo of heated outflow, a parish a few hundred meters across in which the mineral film grows faster and the glint-fry — hand-length, glass-finned, quick — congregate in slow metallic clouds. Rim-gliders ride the thin air between the summit islands on wide mineral-stiff wings, dropping to snatch fry when the clouds boil at the surface. Their cries are among the few local sounds this world permits; the air is too thin to carry far, so above water there is an odd, frayed intimacy of noise — wings, cries, the hiss of snow striking the sea — all of it laid over the chord coming up through the rock. Snow falls often, straight down through the still air, and it is the only soft sound here. Inside the water there is a second, stranger snow: frazil ice forming on calm nights at the skin, carried down by the circulation, melting in the four-degree deeps. It snows inward, perpetually, and nothing ever accumulates.

The emptiness has a quality that takes time to understand. It is not barrenness. It is *spacing*. A grazer may cross your entire field of view over three days, its scraped feeding-lines bright on a ridge behind it. A buoy chain, unmaintained by anyone for centuries and maintained perfectly by the cold, runs for nine hundred kilometers over a trench, marking a route no one travels. Distances here are measured in the time it takes sound to change its mind. The sea is large enough for weather of its own, and it has exactly one: a set of standing gyres, permanent as furniture, that move the engine-warmth around in slow fixed loops. Nothing about the pattern shifts. It is not that the weather is calm. It is that the weather is *finished*.

The highest summits break the surface as rare islands — bare, black, knife-edged, young. Everything here is young and will stay young. The cold still water does not weather the rock; the only sediment is faunal grit, and the intakes drink it; the fracture faces stay fresh, the arêtes stay sharp enough to cut a hand. On one such island, someone long ago cut a stair into the ridge — two hundred steps, precise, going down into the water and continuing under it, down the flank, out of sight. At the top stands a hut with its door open. Inside: a table, a sextant, the brass gauge, and on the wall a mark etched at water level, annotated in a script no one now reads. The water sits exactly at the mark. It has always sat exactly at the mark.

Far beyond sight, at the edge of the plateau, the sea ends. The Rim is a curved escarpment lip over which the whole reservoir pours, continuously, in a fall whose bottom has never been seen — the drop generates its own permanent ring of cloud, which stands at the horizon in every direction, returning its water to the high sea as snow. Stand at the Rim and the chord swells into something physical, the rock drumming, spray rising upward past you in slow columns. Below: cloud, and beneath the cloud, nothing anyone here knows. The world's water falls off the world's edge and comes back as weather. It has been falling, presumably, since the beginning. It shows no sign of finishing.

That is the deepest rule of the place: nothing happens, at tremendous volume. The chord does not change pitch; if it ever did, that would be an event, the first in millennia, and every animal in the sea would turn toward it. The populations hold steady — birth and death in rare, cold, perfect balance — and when a grazer dies its silicate bones sink to the basalt and lie there, white on black, unrotting, for longer than the word *forever* comfortably means. A dropped tool lies where it fell and stays bright. The gauge reads what it has always read. Day comes and night comes and the engine-lights come on across six hundred kilometers of empty water, and the glint-fry turn in their warm parishes, and a grazer somewhere sounds a single low note that every living thing in the basin feels arrive, slowly, through the bones — answered, hours later, from beyond the curve of the world, by another.

The light finds nothing to touch. The sound touches everything. The sea goes on holding its chord, cold and clear and deafening and changeless, on the roof of the world, like a note struck at the beginning of time by someone who then left the room.
