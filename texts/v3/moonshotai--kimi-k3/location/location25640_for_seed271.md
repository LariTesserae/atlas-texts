The hills around it are low and dry — folded pale stone under thin soil, warm beneath a hazy sky, the kind of country where every slope has long since finished becoming what it is. Nothing in them prepares you for the basin. From above, it is only a shadow-seam between two shoulders of rock. The way in is a slot the width of a doorframe, and at its mouth stand two cairns, knee-high, so dulled by weather and mineral film that they read as natural until you notice the stacking. One has half-slumped. Nobody has rebuilt it.

The slot runs a short way, the walls close and warm, the light thinning behind you, and then it opens.

The chamber is roughly the size of a small chapel, and shaped like one — taller than it is wide, the walls rising into a narrow slot of sky that runs overhead like a clerestory. Direct sun never reaches the floor. The walls take the daylight and pass it downward, surface to surface, so that by the time it arrives it is secondhand light — soft, faintly golden, the dimness of a place lit entirely by reflection. At noon the upper rims catch full sun and glow, and the chamber below deepens in color without growing brighter. That is the only change the light ever makes, and it makes it so exactly, every day, that it reads as constancy. A single note held.

The walls are limestone, fluted by old solution, weathered into curves with no sharpness left. Nothing is loose. Everything that could fall has fallen — there is one great block in the corner, and it fell so long ago that its edges have gone soft, half-dissolved, a mineral skin growing slowly over it. The place has digested its own collapse. The scree at the wall bases is cemented into a single quiet surface. Even catastrophe here has been absorbed into stillness.

Water enters as a seep across one wall — a vertical sleeve of damp where the rock weeps — gathering into a runnel that threads down to a pool a few paces across. The water is blood-warm, clear, faintly mineral, and it stands at one height and one height only: there is a single sharp shoreline line on the rim stone, not a ladder of old levels. The pool has held exactly this surface for longer than anything human here. At its far end, the water slips over a low curve of stacked flat stones — a dam, placed by hands, raising the depth to where a person could sit — slides a short distance down a channel, and sinks into gravel and is gone. The stream's whole visible life is a few dozen steps.

Beyond that, dryness. Half the basin floor is relict travertine — old basins from a wetter time, their rims rounded like stacked plates, holding dust instead of water. Pockets of water, and pockets where water used to be, side by side. The dry half is floored with interlocked pebble pavement, dark with varnish, every stone settled against its neighbors like a floor swept for ten thousand years. If you walk across it, your footprints will remain. Not for days. For years. The place keeps what it is given.

The wet seam carries a sleeve of moss, dark green going black. An algal film paints the warm rock at the waterline in olive and rust — the only saturated colors in the chamber. A dozen pale grass tufts hold ledges where dust has lodged. And there is one tree: rooted in a crack above the pool, no taller than a person, gnarled, leathery-leaved, species uncertain between the dry hills and the wet stone. It does not grow. It arrived at its size long ago and keeps it, gaining and losing in the same gesture.

No animal life. No insect skates the pool's surface. No swallow stitches the shaft of light. Nothing burrows the silt, nothing grazes the moss, no tracks interrupt the dust. If a bird crosses the slot of sky, it does not stop. The water is warm and mineral and thin of food; the green here is a fringe, not a pasture. The silence has no edges broken out of it.

What sound there is, is one sound. On the cool seep wall, condensation gathers into beads, and at long, steady intervals a drop grows heavy enough to fall. It drops a body's height into the pool — a small bright strike, a spreading ring, a faint return off the far wall. The interval never varies. You can set your breathing by it, and then you do. Beneath it, almost below hearing, the overflow slides over stone — a hiss so low you notice it only by holding your breath and finding it still there.

The human marks are faint, and they are few, and they are the kind that require nothing but hands. The cairns at the mouth. The stone dam at the pool's end, its slabs now crusted over, mineralized into the rim — the work of hands become geology. On the wall at shoulder height near the water, a few dozen smears of red ochre, faded to the color of weak tea: some clearly hands, palm and fingers, pressed and dragged; some only streaks. Nothing drawn. No figures. Just the fact of hands, layered over other hands across a time the layers do not bother to record. The rim where people lowered themselves into the water is worn to a smooth hollow a shoulder wide. A slab beside the pool carries a sheen where things were set down and picked up, set down and picked up. And on a dry shelf, a scatter of pebbles that do not belong here — quartz, banded stone, one smoothed by water none of these hills have seen — each carried in from somewhere else and set down. No order to them now, or the order is subtle and old.

Nothing worked, nothing joined, nothing fired. Whatever was wood or cord or skin went back to the air long ago. Stone and stain are what persist — which is why they seem like the whole of it, and are not.

The basin runs on a few rules, and keeps them absolutely. The pool holds its one shoreline. The drip holds its interval. The walls hoard the day's warmth and pay it out through the night, so the air barely moves through its range — the place has weather the way a body has temperature. The travertine builds and dissolves in the same motion; the dry terraces neither crumble nor grow; the tree neither gains nor loses. Even the water is old: rain that fell far off and long ago and has been underground longer than any memory in these hills, rising now warm into the dim. The place drinks the past and calls it the present, and the present does not change.

It is not haunted. Everything here is explicable, and that is its strangeness. It is a room the world made and then stopped touching. The moss and the handprints are contemporaries, because nothing here keeps time.

You leave the way you came, past the slumped cairn, into wind that exists outside but not in — and behind you, on the pale dust of the dry side, your footprints remain: sharp as the hour you made them, the newest thing in the oldest room, for a very long time.
