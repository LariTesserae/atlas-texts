You hear the Shelf before you understand that you are anywhere.

A hum, first — deep enough that you feel it in the roots of your teeth before you admit it into your ears. It swells and it relaxes, swells and relaxes, on a breath of about forty minutes. Under it, once you begin to listen: water. Dripping in a thousand unhurried notes, near and far. A stream threading over stone. Somewhere a valve closing, underground, once a minute, patient as a pulse. And over everything, arriving out of the black in long bent notes the size of valleys, the calls of things you cannot see, answered hours later from farther off than you can imagine anything being on a place this small.

Then the warmth registers. The stone under your palms is warm — not sun-warm; there is no sun. Made-warm. The warmth of a floor that remembers being built.

The darkness here is not an absence. It is an appetite.

The air is full of the Hunger — that is as good a name as any for a thing no one is left to name. A haze, finer than mist, machine-made and self-repairing, older than the mountains it drifts over. It eats light. Strike a lamp and the glow falls away from it like snow landing on warm ground: a meter, maybe two, and it is taken. Bioluminescence fares little better — the glow-flora of the groves smolder at ember-strength, and a spore-trail crossing open stone fades behind itself as it goes, eaten from the tail forward. There is no sky-glow, no star, no moon. The sun was spent a long time ago — harvested, some of the circuit-traces suggest, if you read them right, drunk down by whoever built what is left here. What they raised against that night was a veil, and the veil has outlived every reason for itself. Nothing remains that knows how to switch it off. It would not occur to the machines to try.

So the world apprehends itself by other means. By warmth. By smell — wet basalt, iron, crushed green, a sugar-sap sweetness. Above all by sound. In the dark, everything learned to speak, and the Shelf is loud the way a held chord is loud: not clamor, but many sustained voices, each patient, none in a hurry, all of them audible because nothing here has ever needed to be quiet.

The Shelf itself: the last high ground of a world worn nearly flat. Erosion has had its way with everything — every edge is rounded, every outcrop slumped like wax at the end of a candle. The plateau is small. You could walk its rim in a day, if you could navigate, if you dared the open stone between the groves. It is bounded the way a room is bounded: cliffs on every side, falling away into a cloud-sea that breathes slowly up the rock and never quite climbs it. Below the cloud, nothing is known. The streams that reach the rim go over in warm white falls, and you can stand and listen to them leave, and they never land. No echo comes back. The cloud gives the water back as rain, in time. Nothing leaves the Shelf. That may be the definition of the Shelf.

It is a jar. The warmth is held in by something; the air at this height should be thin and cold and it is instead a held breath, humid to the point of saturation. Condensation beads on anything a degree cooler than the air — your skin, your teeth if you smile, the black sides of the Nine.

The Nine stand at the rim. Pylons, seamless, blacker than the black around them, spaced around the plateau's edge like the posts of a bed for a sleeping world. The hum comes from them. The warmth comes from them, or through them, up out of a crust that was engineered so long ago the engineering has become geology — heat-seams run through the bedrock like veins, and everything alive has arranged itself along them. The Hunger seeps from the Nine as well, a slow black respiration. They are not weathered. Erosion has had eons, and it has polished every stone on the Shelf and left the Nine exactly as it found them. They do not hum *at* anything. They hum the way a heartbeat hums. Because they are running.

Between the rim and the center, the plateau is mostly emptiness: plains of dark, warm, weeping stone, sponge-rock that takes the rain and gives it back as springs. This is the near-void. You cross it the way everything crosses it — by ear, from voice to voice. The spaces are vast and the presences are few, and the Hunger guarantees you will never see two presences at once.

The presences are the groves, and the groves are cathedrals.

Each one is a single tree, in the way a city is a single building. Trunk-columns wide as houses, rising into a canopy you will never see, fusing overhead into vaulting. The bark is warm and it breathes — in, out, on its own slow period — and where a grove breathes, it exhales something the Hunger will not eat. Inside each grove, then: a pocket of clarity. A shallow dome where light is permitted. Ember-glow along the bark-furrows, blue and dim and *surviving*. Moths moving through it like the last sparks off a fire, navigating by ear, finding the flowers because the flowers tick — each species at its own tempo, clocks in the dark, advertising. The drips come down through the canopy leaf to leaf to leaf, hundreds of small notes a minute, and the whole nave of the grove resonates with the hum of the Nine the way an organ's pipes resonate with a chord played somewhere below.

The groves are lush beyond reason — mosses like upholstery, ferns with fronds the length of oars, everything green you have ever been shown, glowing faintly at the edges where it is newest. And they walk. Not quickly. Nothing here is quick. A step is a decade: roots unknitting from the warm stone behind, new roots following the circuit-traces incised in the bedrock ahead, tracing the old engineered heat-seams with an accuracy no survey could improve on. The groves migrate across the Shelf over centuries, and the fauna migrate with them, and the emptiness closes behind.

One grove, this season, is blooming. It does this perhaps once a century, and it is the fastest thing that happens here, and it takes a full season. The flowers are bells — literal bells, thin-walled and resonant — and when the lid of the Shelf inhales, once a day, and the mist moves, the grove rings. It has been ringing for two months. It will ring for one more. The sound carries across the entire plateau, off the Nine, back and forth, decaying so slowly that the end of any given peal has never been observed.

The fauna you will mostly hear. The cantor — a grazer the size of a small hill, warm as a hearth, you would feel it before you touched it — lets out its one bent note every few hours, and the answers come back across the dark like correspondence: delayed, considered, complete. Bell-newts click in the marsh-lanes like porcelain knuckles. The stream-eels read the current's music and compose against it; sit by a warm brook at the right depth of night and you can hear a four-part conversation that has been going on, with interruptions for meals, for longer than your language has existed. Wingbeats cross above the canopy sometimes, huge and unhurried, belonging to things that live out over the void beyond the rim and come in to feed — or to roost — or for reasons of their own. Nothing on the Shelf is in a hurry, and nothing on the Shelf is silent, and the combination means the dark is full of sentences that take hours to finish.

And there are the keepers. Crab-bodied, tree-polite, unaging. They polish the circuit-traces. They re-etch the lines the roots have shifted. They climb down the Stair and come back up dry, which should not be possible. Once, they were machines, and the groves were an orchard, and the distinction between the built and the alive was a real distinction; erosion has had its way with that too. The keepers will find you eventually. They will taste you with one warm antenna, and file you somewhere in a ledger no one audits, and go back to work.

The Stair descends the cliff near the southern pylon. Its steps were cut for a foot not shaped like yours. Sometimes — every few decades — something climbs it. You can hear the progress of these ascents for weeks, a step, a rest, a step. They always stop. Nothing has finished the climb in recorded time, and the recording of time here is done by the groves, in rings.

At the plateau's center stands the water-organ: the one structure that is not the Nine and not a ruin and not alive, exactly. It lifts the marsh-water up to the groves along pipes the roots have grown around and into and through, and its great valve closes once a minute, and has never missed. Beside it, a small dome — the listener's shelter, say the traces, though "listener" is a guess at a word in a grammar of circuits. Inside: a listening horn aimed at nothing, and a bowl. The bowl is always full of warm water. No one fills it. It tastes of iron and flowers, which is to say it tastes like the air, condensed.

The artifacts you find are small and do not explain themselves. Black beads that hum in sympathy with the Nine when you hold them. A disc that is warm on one side and warm on the other. The marks under your feet wherever the stone is bare: circuit-traces, faintly warm, that will wake a brief glow if you press your palm flat — a glow that the Hunger takes in less than a second, like a word the dark has been waiting to eat.

Time, here: the valve closes once a minute. The Nine breathe every forty minutes. The lid inhales once a day and the bells ring. The cantor calls every few hours; the answers take longer. A grove takes a step per decade, blooms per century, crosses the Shelf per something longer. The rain comes in seasons that last years. The sun has been dead for longer than the stone has been soft. You are the fastest thing here — the quickest pulse, the briefest tenancy — and the Shelf is patient with you anyway, the way deep water is patient with a match.

Because that is the atmosphere of the place, finally: not menace, not grief, but *keptness*. Warmth held against a dead sky. Greenness burning low and refusing to go out. A garden tended past the end of every reason for a garden, by gardeners who no longer remember there was ever anything else — and the tending has become the reason. The hum is not a message. The hum is the sound of the message still being sent.

Stand at the rim in the absolute dark, wet to the skin, warm through to the bone, the falls leaving beside you and never landing, a grove ringing somewhere behind you and a cantor composing its answer somewhere ahead, and you will understand the Shelf's single law, the one the Hunger itself obeys:

The dark gets everything. Eventually. At the speed of erosion. No faster.
