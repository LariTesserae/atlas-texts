**The Plateau**

The world up here is finished. That is the first thing to understand.

The plateau lies high and pale under a wide sky, worn down past any further argument. Wind and time have sorted its gravel a million times over; every stone that could be moved has been moved, every slope that could slump has slumped, and what remains is the final answer: low domes of polished rock, salt pans flat as poured glass, pebbles faceted by ten thousand years of wind into identical jewel-shapes. Nothing here is mid-process. The geology has concluded.

The dead rivers are still legible — as ridges. Their beds were gravel, and the gravel cemented harder than the land around it, so as the country wore away the rivers stayed, and now they run across the plateau as long low spines of stone, inverted, cast in their own beds. You can walk along the spine of a river that has not carried water in longer than anyone can count.

It is silent. That is the plateau's true signature. No insects, no birdsong, no water-sound, only wind — and even the wind has little left to say. Sometimes, on the horizon, a storm stands up over the dead lowland and it *rains* — you can see the gray curtains trailing from the cloud — and the rain evaporates before it lands. The sky spills; the ground gets none. The watchers have a word for those falling, dying veils, and they speak it the way other people speak of ghosts.

**The Slot**

Near the summit ridge, behind a shoulder of featureless stone, there is a crack.

You would walk past it. Everyone would walk past it. It is a body-width wide at the rim, unmarked, and the plateau gives no reason to look twice. The first sign is not sight but sound: a hum, rising out of the rock like heat, so low and layered you feel it in your teeth before you name it. Then, leaning over the slot: warmth, welling up out of the earth like breath from a sleeper. Then smell — greenness, musk, fermentation, and under it all a smell that has no business existing on this plateau, a smell your body recognizes before your mind does.

Stored water.

The crack is ancient — a river cut it, back in the wet age, and the river died and the crack remained, polished potholes and all. Handholds descend the wall, worn smooth by use. The air warms and thickens by degrees as you climb down. The light turns green. The hum resolves into a hundred thousand separate voices. And then you are through the last pinch of stone and standing on a shelf, looking down the length of the Palmful.

It is perhaps four hundred paces long, a stone's throw across, and it is *full*.

**The Light**

The slot of sky overhead is the only opening, and the sun crosses it in a short arc. For most of the day the Palmful lies in a warm green gloom, lit by reflection and by the general glow of the sky-strip. But once a day the sun looks directly in, and then comes the hour of the blade.

The light falls like something drawn — a single brilliant edge that enters at one end of the cleft and travels its length, moving down the wall and across the canopy. As it passes, it ignites the lantern vines: thousands of translucent water-sacs hanging in curtains along the walls, each one lighting up in turn like a lamp being lit, a slow wave of small green-gold suns rippling the length of the pocket. The people call it the lighting of the lamps. Everything photosynthetic in the Palmful times its life to that hour; every basking animal has its ledge, and the ledges have queues, and the queues have pecking orders older than the town.

The dark rock drinks the blade and gives the heat back all day. Sheltered from every wind, dense with bodies, the pocket stays warm long into night — warm stone, warm mat, warm breath.

At night the slot frames a river of stars directly overhead, and the wind crossing the opening sounds the whole cleft like a flute: a low organ note beneath everything, changing pitch with the weather outside, so that the people lying in their houses hear the plateau's empty storms as music.

**The Law of Water**

There is no open water in the Palmful. There has not been, in living memory, in grandmothers'-grandmothers' memory. No pool, no seep, no drip. The law of the place is simple and total: *water is never free.* It exists only inside living tissue — in the barrel trunks of the wellwood trees, in the sacs of the lantern vines, in blood and sap and the bodies of ten thousand kinds of animal. It passes from body to body, mouth to mouth, root to leaf to tongue, and it does not touch the ground.

It is *old* water. That is the thing the Keepers know in their bones: the water here is an inheritance, a fixed endowment handed down from the wet age, recycled from tree to beast to child to tree for centuries, never spent. The same water, again and again. No one in the Palmful drinks anything that was not once someone.

New water arrives in thimbles. The high desert nights are cold, and at dusk the people set out dew-stones and dew-cloths along the cool end of the cleft, and at dawn they collect what condensed: a cup, sometimes two, for the whole town. It is the only income. Against it stands the only outgo: what leaves through the slot. A bird that flies out over the rim carries its water with it, forever. A bird that arrives, gaunt and staggering, is *gain* — new water from the outside world — and it is fed and tended like a rich relation.

So the Keepers are accountants of a whole ecosystem, and their central institution is not a temple or a throne but the Tally: knotted cords and clay tokens kept in the council house, reckoning the pocket's water the way other peoples reckon grain or gold. Crimes are measured in water. The worst crime is spilling. The worst sentence is exile — not because the exile dies, though the exile dies, but because the exile walks out over the rim carrying water that will never come back. The pocket mourns the water more than the person.

**The Green**

The vegetation is lush the way a held breath is full — everything sealed, everything hoarding. The wellwoods are the great presences: trunks swollen like barrels, bark smooth and gray-green as hide, leaves small, waxy, stingy. They creak in the night as they take up moisture, a slow wooden groaning that runs through the dark like conversation. Their roots graft to one another until the whole forest is a single hydraulic body, and the town taps that body with bone spiles, under law, under the Tally, taking a cup here and a cup there the way you might draw blood from someone you love.

Below the wellwoods, the lantern vines loop from wall to wall in curtains; below those, a dense understory of succulents, every leaf a flask; and under all of it there is no soil at all but the mat — a springy, interwoven floor of roots and rhizomes, warm, faintly steaming at night, which drinks any spill before it can fall a second drop. To walk on the mat is to walk on the forest's body. It is alive underfoot in a way that takes visitors a long time to sleep near.

In the mat's underside, in the dark and the heat, the fermenters work — fungi and rot and recycling, the engine of the place. This is where the breath comes from: in the cool hours the whole pocket exhales, a thin mist rising off the mat and the canopy, and the vines and the wellwoods and the waiting animals catch it all back. It is the closest thing to rain the Palmful has. It sounds, the Keepers say, like rain falling upward.

**The Fauna**

The fauna is not a feature of the Palmful. The fauna is the medium.

The air has weight — wings, constantly, at every height. Cicada-like singers drill the wellwood stems and their drone is the pocket's voice, rising and falling with the warmth of the day, so dense it becomes a kind of silence. Ants tend sap-sucking insects in the canopy and carry the water droplet by droplet along their trails; the Keepers say the aquifer walks. Sweat-moths swarm anyone laboring in the heat and drink directly from the skin — the people tolerate them and call them the tax. In the sacs of the lantern vines live small frogs that are born in water that has never touched ground, grow there, mate there, die there, and tick all night like cooling metal. Geckos work the walls. The canopy holds climbing mammals in layered strata, each layer with its own diet and its own enemies. A hawk patrols the slot itself, master of the threshold, symbol of everything that might leave.

The households keep cistern tortoises — great slow beasts that are, functionally, walking water. Each has a name, a lineage, an entry in the Tally. A family's wealth can be seen grazing, unhurried, beside the house.

And there is the mat-cat, which hunts the terraces at night, and takes a child every few years, and is hated and endured, because it too is water, and everything is counted.

Twice a day, at the breath and at the blade, the whole fauna drinks at once — every tongue in the pocket at work simultaneously — and the sound of it fills the cleft like rainfall. Visitors from outside, in the old days when there were visitors, wept at it without knowing why.

**The Town**

The Terraces climb the sun-side wall in dry-stone courses, no mortar — mortar costs water — layer upon layer upon layer, the oldest courses below worn and rounded and built upon by courses built upon by courses, down and down into the sediment until archaeology becomes geology. Rope bridges cross the gaps. Granaries stand sealed with resin against the ants. The houses are stone and hide and woven fiber, and in the council house hangs the town's single piece of metal: a copper ladle, cold-hammered, traded from the outside in some unremembered century, never used, only shown. It is not a tool. It is a proof — that water was once common enough to pour.

Because the drone drowns speech at any distance, the people talk in whistles across the terraces, a pitched language that carries over the cicadas, so the human voice in the Palmful sounds like birdsong and the birdsong sounds like weather. They cook in lidded baskets with condensing lids, the steam dripping back into the pot; they scour with sand and clean with smoke; they ferment sap in sealed vats, carefully, under license, because wine is water at play.

Their dead are laid in the mat beneath the family wellwoods, in the orchard of ancestors, and the trees take them up, and the family drinks from the trees, and this is not grim to them — it is the whole shape of love, plainly stated. *No one here drinks anything that was not once someone.* To be drunk by your grandchildren is to remain in the Keeping.

Once a season a salt party goes up and out onto the plateau, carrying their water in gourds and tortoises, and the Tally weighs them before and after, and they owe the pocket their sweat.

**The Keeping**

Overhead, in the slot's stone, there are ripple-marks and shell beds — the fossil signature of the river that carved this place and died. The people know what the stone says. Their whole scripture is in it: the water that made the world is gone, and what it left is here, and *here* is four hundred paces long.

The floor of the Palmful rises a finger-width a generation, everything that ever lived here settling into everything that will. The roots widen the slot by perhaps a hair a century. On a plateau that has concluded, in a world worn to its final form, this is the last place where anything is still happening — the last unfinished room, and it is growing, imperceptibly, like something with intentions.

The Keepers do not believe they are survivors. They believe they are *custodians*. The watchers on the rim stand their shifts through the empty years, and they watch the horizon storms trail their dying veils of rain, and they report to the Tally: the sky spilled; the ground got none; not yet; not this year.

And at dusk they set out the dew-stones along the cool end of the cleft, rows of pale ovals facing the strip of stars, like a bet placed nightly against the sky — that the world will want its water back, and that when it does, it will find the water here, kept, counted, alive.
