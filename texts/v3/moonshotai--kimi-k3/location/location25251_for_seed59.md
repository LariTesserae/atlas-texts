The trail comes up the mountainside in switchbacks and ladders, and for the last hundred meters you climb beside the falls. This is the first fact of the place: water leaving. The stream that runs the whole town goes off the lip of a hanging valley in three white threads, and the mist it raises wets the ladder rungs, wets the rope handlines, wets the moss that grows on the north side of every peg. You climb through its breath. Then you are over the rim and standing in the bowl, and the sound changes from the falls' hiss to something denser — the sound of water staying.

The bowl is small, a few hundred paces across, cupped on three sides by a ridge of dwarf forest and grass that shudders in a cold wind. High enough that the mornings show breath. High enough that the trees up on the rim are bent and black-barked and human-sized. But down in the shelter of the bowl the forest has gone enormous — a pocket of tall growth trapped below the treeline's weather — and the town lives inside it the way a nest lives inside a hedge: woven in, held, half-swallowed, endlessly maintained.

Everything is plant. The ground is not soil so much as root-mat and moss, springy, ankle-deep in places, swallowing a dropped tool in an hour. Moss rides the roofs, the railings, the posts, the tops of fence stakes; it grows on the taut surface of hanging rope bridges, a green nap that the crossing feet keep worn to a bare line. Every trunk carries its own hanging garden — ferns, orchids, bromeliads cupped like green hands, each holding its cold cupful of water, and in each cup, frogs. The understory is ariot-leafed, taro-big, leaves like wet sails overlapping so completely that the paths are tunnels. Where the light does reach, in one bright wound on the bowl's west side, it falls on a tangle fighting itself chest-high: that is where a giant came down a generation ago, and the gap it tore is still a battlefield of saplings, vines, and bird-sown shrubs, all of it louder with wings than anywhere else in a loud town.

The giants that remain — seven of them, fig-like, buttressed, their bark hidden under epiphytes a foot thick — are the town's load-bearing members. The meeting house is built into the root-buttresses of the largest, its floor of worn river stones set in the bays between roots, its roof tied into the living tree with fiber lashings that are renewed every year and have been renewed every year for longer than anyone's songs run. Houses stand on stilts over the wet ground, over the stream, over the gardens, crowded shoulder to shoulder along the boardwalks, their eaves near-touching, their gutters nearly lacing. Granaries on singed posts. Drying racks. Smoke-houses. A market shelter like a long low boat-hull of thatch. Everything wooden is new — nothing in the town is older than a decade, because nothing wooden survives longer — and everything stands on stones that are centuries old, footings worn round and dark, each with a family name, each relaid in the same place after every rebuilding. The town is a thousand years old and none of it is old.

Water is the infrastructure and the governing idea. The stream enters the bowl from a spring-seep under the rim and is immediately taken apart: split into flumes of halved bamboo and hollowed log that run on posts along the paths, tapped into gutters, braked by little stick-weirs, handed down terrace by terrace through the paddy beds, and reassembled, finally, into the three threads that make the falls. The whole settlement is plumbed. You are never more than arm's reach from moving water — a channel at the path's edge, a pipe overhead dripping at the joints, a roof-spout aimed into a barrel, a barrel overflowing into a garden. Children work the weirs as a chore, opening and closing the town's valves morning and evening, and the arrangement is not incidental: the people here tune their water. Every house keeps its runnel loud and close, because every runnel keeps frogs, and the frogs keep the biting flies down, and the chorus — the chorus is how you know the world is right.

The frogs are past counting. So are the birds. The fauna of the bowl is not a presence but a pressure. Dawn arrives as a wall of sound that does not build so much as ignite — pigeons, parakeet-flocks, a big heavy-winged fruit-bird that glides between the giants like something thrown, small drab singers numbering in the dozens of kinds, all of it at once, at volume, while the light is still gray and the mist still thick. Cicadas take the afternoon shift, a drill-buzz you feel in the teeth. At dusk the eaves pour bats, the midges rise, the lamps draw moths the size of leaves, and then the frogs begin and do not stop until dawn hands the shift back to the birds. The clear mornings — an hour, sometimes two, before the cloud comes up — fill with butterflies puddling on the damp stones of the paths, and with the town's whole population of small iridescent things working the epiphyte flowers. Snails write silver across every broad leaf after rain. Mountain crabs live under the stones of the stream; the paddy beds are stocked with small fish that the households harvest with basket traps; leeches wait in the marshy margins and everyone wears woven gaiters and carries ash. Something small and arboreal raids the stores and is half-trapped, half-tolerated, fully cursed. A hawk takes a chicken now and then, and when it crosses the bowl, the whole town goes quiet for the length of its shadow — that sudden hush, total and brief, is one of the few silences this place ever makes, and people read it like weather.

The day runs on a fixed wheel, and the wheel spins hard. Mornings are the clear hour: cold, silver-gold, the falls smoking off the lip, everyone outside at once — repairs, gardening, thatching, the steady tick of adzes, the rasp of the few precious metal blades (traded up the ladder-trail from the warm valley below, paid for in feathers, in resins, in dried fruit and dyed cordage). By late morning the cloud comes up the valley and pours over the rim like a slow flood, and the bowl enters the cloud body-first: light going diffuse and white, distances collapsing to a few dripping meters, every surface condensing, the gutters running though no rain has fallen. Midday is market under the long shelter, loud as a rookery, and then, most days, the rain arrives — not weather but an event, a drumming on the vast leaf-surfaces that drowns speech, that sends everyone under roof to the indoor work: cordage, weaving, carving, the mending of nets and traps and the endless mending of the town itself. Somewhere under it all, all day, the water-pestles turn — hollowed logs on the mill-race, lifting and dropping, lifting and dropping, pounding tuber flour — the heartbeat you stop hearing until it stops.

Because the work here never finishes. Rot is the tax on everything. A post lasts six years, a walkway plank three, a roof two before the moss owns it. The town persists the way a body persists: by constant replacement, cell by cell, the form outliving every piece of it. This is the bowl's deep rhythm, its turbulence riding on its permanence — squalls, floods, frog-blooms after storms, a night when the winged termites rise in their millions and every bird and bat and child is out gorging, a fruit-tree hitting its peak and drawing a screaming riot of wings for three days and then standing stripped and silent, a house going up in a morning of lashed poles because a house came down in the night. Established, all of it. The old way. The stones don't move.

Fire is the counterweight to the water, and it is tended with the seriousness of a scarce thing — which, here, it is. Dry tinder is wealth. Every hearth burns always; the smoke lofts above them are where food keeps, where seed keeps, where the sick and the newborn are warmed. Woodsmoke threads every smell the bowl has — through wet moss, crushed green, the sweet dark smell of rot, rain on cold stone — and it is the smell that means home to anyone raised here, smoke and damp together, because there is no such thing as dry, only degrees of wet managed around islands of heat.

Rules accumulate in a place like this, and the bowl's are simple and enforced by the logic of the place itself. You don't cut a bromeliad — it is somebody's water and somebody's frogs. The gutters and flumes are commons; a blocked channel is a public wrong, answered publicly. The lip of the falls is forbidden to children, sworn off with ceremony, and the oath holds because everyone has seen the slick green rock at the edge and understood it at a glance. When the cloud lifts — those clear hours, morning and sometimes a gold rinse before dusk — work pauses, just briefly, and people look: down through the falls' gap at the warm valley far below, across at the black rim, at the hawk circling on the rising wet air. It is not quite a ritual. It is what anyone would do. Then the mist closes back over and the noise resumes.

The very old are the town's true archive, because wood rots and only stone and song persist. They can point to footing-stones and name the families ten rebuildings back; they can point to the light-gap and describe the tree that stood there, though it fell before their grandparents were born; they remember — or remember being told — when the gutter-frog was first noticed, the small dark one with the bronze stripe that lives only in the town's own channels, found nowhere up the ridge and nowhere down the valley, a creature that exists because the town exists, as domestic as the smoke lofts. Ask how long the settlement has stood in the bowl and you get the same answer the stones give: it was here. It has always been the age it is.

By full dark the bowl is hearth-glow under the eaves, torch-resin flares at the market posts, a line of foxfire green on a rotting log that the children dare each other past. The frogs are at full chorus, layered so dense the ear stops separating them — it becomes a single continuous thing, a chord the size of the valley, with the falls underneath like a held breath and the pestles still dropping somewhere in the dark and the mist beading on every leaf, every rope, every roof-edge, gathering, letting go. Cold at the cheeks, warm at the hearthside. Wet to the wrist. Loud as weather, settled as stone. The cloud lies on the town like a hand on a shoulder, and the town hums under it, and the water runs through everything, on its way to the edge of the world.
