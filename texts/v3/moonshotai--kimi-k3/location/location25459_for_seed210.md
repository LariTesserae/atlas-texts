You hear it before you see it, and you will go on hearing it slightly before everything, because in this place sound arrives half a breath ahead of its source. The thump of the blowhole reaches you, and then, below the falls, the gout of white bursts from the rock. The crack of a spark, and then the spark. You stop noticing. The half-breath never widens, never closes. It is one of the fixed quantities here.

What you hear, all told, is a chord that has never changed: a low hum you feel in the sternum more than the ear — a note an organ could hold forever if the organist never tired — and under it the roar of the falls, constant as weather, and under that the water-hammer's slow heartbeat from somewhere inside the young cliff, a piston of water driven through fresh fracture, regular as a metronome. Crackle along the wet walls. Drips in counterpoint, each one striking its own stone at its own interval, forever. It is loud. It has been loud for as long as the place has been itself, and because the loudness never varies by even a shade, it functions as silence. The animals treat it that way.

The light is the color of pewter, and it comes from nowhere. A ceiling of cloud presses down to meet the rim of the cirque — the walls rise into it and do not come out the other side — and the mist that drifts beneath that ceiling glows faintly, sourcelessly, so that nothing here casts a shadow. Not the stones, not the trees, not you. It is dim the way the memory of an afternoon is dim. You can see the whole place at a glance: it is small, a single room in the mountain, floor of marsh and pool and stream, walls of wet fresh rock. The far wall looks ten minutes away on foot. It is not that it is farther. Arrival is not among the things that happen here. The walls are for enclosing, not for reaching.

The rock is young — that much is plain in its sharpness. The cirque is a fresh split in the high mountain, all fracture-faces and angular talus, edges that should have rounded and have not, because rounding is a change and this place does not change. The split exposed dark veins in the stone, and water sheets down over them — the walls weep, every wall, a slow weeping that never lessens and never floods — and where the water crosses the veins the whole cliff becomes a cell in a vast wet battery. Blue sparks crawl the weeping faces, patient, like slow lightning still deciding where to go. The air tastes of pennies and ozone and crushed moss. Stand near the wall and your hair lifts; touch the wet rock and your fingers tingle. Everything here is mildly electrified, and has been since the mountain split and the circuits closed — a wound that has never healed and never worsened, held open.

Nothing was built. That is the thing to hold in your mind. No straight line was cut, no stone was dressed, no bolt turned. And yet the place runs. In the stream that threads the marsh, boulders spin in their sockets — granite bearings polished to mirrors by their own turning, some tilted and spinning like coins at the end of a coin's fall, forever at that same lean, forever about to settle, never settling. Their hum is the organ note. The falls drive a white thread of water down the young cliff, and beside it, a thinner thread climbs. It rises in a slow braid, rejoins the rim, and feeds the fall that feeds it. The same water, a fixed amount of it, commuting. A short circuit of water, closed since the beginning of this place, if beginning is a word that applies, which it is not.

In the rising thread, a fish holds station. It swims downward at exactly the rate the water rises, and so it hangs in the air above the marsh, mid-thread, working steadily, going nowhere, at home. It has hung there as long as anything has been anywhere here.

The vegetation is lush the way a cooling system is lush. Cold black-green moss carpets everything soft, and where the current runs through it, it glows — faint, greenish, votive. Sedges stand in the saturated ground; every step you take squeezes water out of the mat, and the mat takes the water back before your next step lands. There is a grove of stunted trees, birches maybe, their bark sheened with verdigris from the metal they drink out of the young stone, and ferns along the seep-lines whose fronds curl and uncurl with the heartbeat of the water-hammer, breathing with the machine. The mist condenses on every frond and needle — the engines give off a little warmth, the high air is cold, and so the whole place is beaded, jeweled, weeping gently back at its walls.

The animals are everywhere and they are silent, and there is always exactly one of each thing at a time in your sight. One elk stands in the marsh with blue fire on its antlers, St. Elmo's fire burning there as it has always burned, and it does not graze and does not sleep; it walks a slow circuit of the meadow, and the circuit takes the time it takes, and by the time the elk completes it, everything else has also returned to where it was. One heron stands by a pool in the attitude of a heron about to strike, and has never struck, because striking is a change. Dippers — small dark birds — walk on the streambed underwater among the spinning stones, unbothered by the bearings, gleaning nothing, walking because walking is what dippers do. Salamanders lie in the warm sockets of the flywheel stones. Moths with charge-dusted wings drift the same loops above the glowing moss, sipping the light, and if one settles on your skin you feel a small tingle and nothing else, because nothing here takes anything from anything else. There is no hunger. Energy runs from the cliffs through moss and moth and elk and back to the cliffs, evenly, eternally, and the food web has been replaced by a circuit. Nothing is born; nothing dies; the census has never changed. The animals watch you with the unbearable calm of creatures that have never once needed to flee.

The pools are the strangest thing, and they are strange quietly. They lie across the marsh floor, still and black-rimmed, and they do not reflect you. Nothing here reflects in them. What they show instead is this same cirque as it would be if it were ordinary: a sunlit tarn under a moving sky, green moss, grey rounded stone, a normal waterfall falling the normal direction, cloud-shadows crossing the grass. The water in them is cold and tastes of nothing, and if you cup it and drink, the pool is exactly as full afterward as before. You could stand there a long time looking down at the sane version of where you are standing. Nothing would come of it. Nothing comes of anything here.

That is the law of the place, if you stay long enough to feel it: motion without change. Everything turns, falls, climbs, crawls, drips, beats — and the sum is invariant. The mist is the same mist, the same specific droplets, recycled since the rock split. The water that weeps from the walls returns to the walls. The flywheels neither gain nor lose speed. The chord never modulates. It is a factory whose only product is its own continued operation, a mill with no grain, a power station with no grid, industry that has achieved perfect homeostasis and stopped there, mid-breath, and been young ever since. The ledger balances every instant. Nothing begins. Nothing is lost. Departure and arrival are not in the grammar.

When you leave — and you will, because leaving is something you brought in with you, like your shadowless shadow, like your reflection that the pools refuse — the chord will be holding its note, and the elk will be rounding the far sedge, and the fish will be swimming down the rising water, unhurried, unwavering, exactly where it has always been.
