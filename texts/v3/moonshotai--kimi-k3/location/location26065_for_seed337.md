The plain gives no warning. You could cross it for an hour in the cool wind — short turf, larkless sky, a scatter of flint nodules underfoot like dropped knucklebones — and see nothing but smoke rising from the ground itself, thin gray threads unspooling out of the grass. A goat grazes. Jackdaws turn over something at the edge of sight. Then the turf simply stops, mid-world, in a line as sharp as a cut, and you are standing on the town's roofline, looking down into it.

Below you the valley floor is gone, replaced by a white depth of terraces, ledges, stair-cuts, roof-turfs, and faces of raw chalk descending in steps toward a farther, deeper white. It runs the length of the dale — quarter after quarter of it, merging one into the next with no gap between, no field, no wall around it, no waste anywhere. Every ledge is stacked with something: baskets, drying racks, flint cores piled like cordwood. Every wall is also someone's ceiling, someone's floor, someone's neighbor. It is less a town than a single excavated object, and it goes down farther than you can see.

They call it the Dig. It began as a flint quarry — the seam runs through the chalk here like a dark river in section, thick nodules banded in gray — and the quarry simply never left. People slept beside their faces, then in them. Twelve years, perhaps fourteen: that is the whole of its history. Nobody planned it, and so the geology planned it instead. The streets follow the seam. Where the flint ran rich, the cuts went deep and the houses are tall; where it pinched out, the streets dead-end in shallow, damp cul-de-sacs that everyone can name and nobody wants. The poor quarter is a disappointment in stone.

Because it is new — this is the first thing you come to understand — nothing here has been touched by time yet. Every surface shows its making. The walls carry the scalloped signatures of antler picks, thousands of crescent strokes overlapping like scales. Corners are sharp enough to cut a palm. There is no lichen anywhere in the valley; no moss except in one wet gallery mouth; no stain, no patina, no rounding. The oldest stair in the Old Dig has begun, only begun, to take a sheen on its third tread where feet land, and people touch that shine the way you'd touch a first gray hair. Nothing in the Dig is old enough to be anonymous. Directions are given in biography: *below Alder's face, past the burned wall, where the Tippet boy's basket-ledge fell.*

They build from the hole and from what came out of it. Walls are often not built at all but left — ribs of standing chalk, undug, that serve two households at once. Otherwise: clunch blocks cut fresh and pale, stacked dry; floors of puddled marl rammed hard; wattle panels daubed with chalk mud; everything whitewashed inside with lime from the kilns, so that lamplight doubles itself. Roofs are the removed turf laid back over precious timber, flush with the plain — hence the goat. Timber comes from three days off and costs more than anything; a door frame is an heirloom. A few copper chisels exist, traded from far away, kept for finish work and sharpened down to stubs of wealth. The real treasury is rubble: flint cores stacked against every wall, measured by the basket, owed and re-owed, tallied in scratches on the whitewash — the walls are the account books, which is one reason damp is an enemy and not a nuisance.

For the Dig is dry, and it is damp. Chalk drinks rain — a downpour vanishes into the faces like water into cloth — but the digging has gone deep enough to find the exceptions. A marl seam perches water in the middle quarters, where walls weep and floors never quite dry. And at the bottom of the Well-Pit, the deepest cut, the diggers broke into the water table itself: a still, green-black pool that never fails. They call it the Mother Pool and treat it with a care that is half practice, half reverence — no digging near it, no voices raised, and now and then a flawless core, too beautiful to sell, dropped in. Eels live in it. Nobody put them there; they arrived, as eels do, crossing wet land on wet nights, and their presence is taken as a kind of testimony.

So the town's water is pockets in a larger dryness: the Mother Pool, the marl seeps, cistern jars, dew ponds of puddled clay out on the tips. On cool mornings condensation beads every gallery wall, and fog settles into the whole basin like milk into a bowl — you descend out of a clear dawn into white, and the town disappears around you as you enter it. In dry weeks the opposite: a fine chalk meal over everything, the smell of bone and held breath, the tally-men brushing their walls.

Rain is the event. When it comes hard, the perched water rises, floors go slick, and the whole town turns out to its choreography — bailing chains at the sumps, blankets of turf thrown on the tip-slopes, everyone listening. For the Dig has one great collective work, its cathedral, and it is invisible: the Sough, a drainage adit hand-driven through the chalk to the valley's low side, a season's labor maintained by roster, every household owing days to it. After a storm you can stand at its outfall and hear it running — the loudest water in the town, and the sound of survival. Two winters ago a face undercut against custom took a chamber down in the night and six people with it. The face is left untouched, sealed, and the law it broke is recited to every child.

Which is why the Dig is quiet. Partly it is the ground itself — soft chalk eats sound; the pit walls keep the wind's noise overhead, a river you can hear but not feel. But mostly it is law and custom together: the Quiet. A town of shared walls, where every floor is a ceiling and every collapse is communal, learns to listen to the earth. Chalk ticks before it falls. So voices stay low, children are raised soft, and across the party walls runs a whole second language of knuckle-taps — the knocking — by which households gossip, argue, and call for help through two feet of stone. What you actually hear, standing in it, is small: the tick of knapping from a hundred doorways, rope creaking at a windlass, drips in a gallery, the lisp of baskets up cut stairs, moths at the fat-lamps. At night in a cold snap the fresh faces tick and flake as the frost works, and the town lies awake in the dark, counting sounds. Storms are the frightening thing, because rain on the roof-turfs is loud, and loud means deaf.

Once a year, on the day the Sough first broke through, the Quiet is lifted. Drums. Shouting. Songs sung badly and at full throat. The children find it terrifying and then glorious, and it is called, simply, the Loud. The rest of the year, the noisiest things in the valley are the sand martins.

They arrive in spring and drill their burrows in the raw faces — the only creatures who prefer a wall with no history — and for their season the Dig has a rare, skimming, chittering life over the sumps where the midges rise. By custom no face hosting martins may be cut until the young have flown; whole rich seams wait on birds. Otherwise the animal list is short. Jackdaws and rooks work the rim. A fox denned in a failed cut and takes voles at the middens. Two cats, half-feral and beyond price, guard the granary shafts — cool dry pits sunk beneath the floors where the trade grain sleeps. The goat, and one other. Moths. The eels, who are never seen, only known.

Plants are pioneers or nothing. There is no mature turf anywhere but the rim, where the old grassland survives in a strip held by tense agreement with the herders who graze it — the town needs that turf for roofs and kiln fuel, and every sod cut is negotiated. On the tips, the spoil heaps that stand in white rings around every quarter, sharp-crested and reshaped daily, the first colonists are coltsfoot, its flowers coming up bare-stemmed on ground turned last week; then nettles by the nightsoil tips, fat hen on the kiln ash. The only gardens are the reedbeds at the Well-Pit, tended for thatch and basketry, and a fenced thicket of sallow at the Sough's outfall, nursed for withies. One man tends it. He is considered rich in a way that has nothing to do with flint.

The kilns stand in a row where the valley kinks and the draft comes through: chalk rubble and brush and turf, a three-day burn, and the lime drawn glowing into the cool. They are the warm heart of a cold place. The basin traps chill like water — frost holds in the shadowed floors all day, breath smokes into spring, and in winter the smoke itself pools under the still air in a lid that the rim wind skims but cannot reach. So the social geography shifts with the year: in winter everyone drifts toward Kiln Row, and the gallery-miners come up at dusk with chalk in their eyebrows to sleep near the draw-holes; in summer the deep rooms are the refuge, and work follows the light instead, because light here is scheduled by geometry. At noon the white town glares almost too bright to look at; morning and evening the depths lie in blue shadow while the rims burn. Houses open east. The galleries get their daylight down cut shafts, and their nights by rushlight, and on moonlit nights the whole valley floor glows faintly back at the sky, a pale bowl held up to the dark.

Everything moves. That is the other law. The New Cut is always advancing at the far end, raw and loud-as-it-gets; worked-out faces at the old end are becoming floors, floors becoming homes, a chamber's life running face to floor to cradle. Couples dig their children's rooms years ahead; a dug chamber is the finest dowry. Caravans come up the dale in the warm months to the Stance, the bare stance-ground on the rim, and lower their grain and hide and timber down on ropes, and take away baskets of flint, and everything that enters this town enters by descent. Faces are owned, not land; the undercut law fixes how close you may come to a neighbor's floor; spoil tips only downwind of your own cut; the water queue at the Mother Pool forms at dawn and order in it is sacred. Punishments are logistical — you go last in the queue, you draw double duty in the Sough.

And time has started to notice. Last autumn's big storm cut a rill down the flank of the third tip — the first rill, the first water-course the valley has ever made — and people walk out to look at it, the way you'd visit a birth or a death. A gatepost corner in the Old Dig has gone soft at one edge. The coltsfoot holds the new slope in a yellow scatter. Nothing is ruined; everything is just barely begun, a town standing at the exact moment before the world starts to wear it, listening to its own ground in the cool morning, soft-voiced, white, and descending.

At dawn the fog is in the bowl and the tips stand above it like a range of small mountains. The queue moves at the pool without a word. Somewhere through a wall comes the day's first knocking — three taps, a pause, two — and below the floor of the deepest room, in the dark of the Mother Pool, an eel turns over once, and settles.
