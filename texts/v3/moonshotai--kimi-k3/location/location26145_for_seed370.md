Come up the last shoulder of the ridge in the dim, and you will feel the place before you hear it, and hear it before you see it.

The ascent is a stair someone cut once, long ago, each step no wider than a foot, worn into a shallow scoop and then — this is the strange part — worn no further. The scoop is fossil. Feet stopped coming before the stone finished eroding, and the stone, having reached that shape, simply kept it. The air is warm and utterly dry, dry past the memory of wet; your lips crack within the hour. There is no wind. And yet, as you climb, the still air grows a grain, a pressure, a tremor that comes up through the stair into your boots, through your boots into the long bones of your legs. Your chest becomes a drum that something else is beating. Then the sound itself arrives — not swelling like an approaching storm, but steepening, the way a wall steepens. You climb into it the way you would climb into water.

At the top: the Crown. That is the only name that fits. A hundred paces across, no more — the whole of it would fit inside a cathedral's choir — a bounded cap of masonry set on the summit like a stopper in a bottle. On one side a parapet of hand-laid stone, chest-high, and beyond it the drop: not a view, exactly, because the dim brass-colored sky gives the abyss no bottom and little middle. A haze the color of the sky hangs below, and once in a while a far, faint line that might be a ridge, or might be a cloud in a world that has forgotten how to make clouds. On every other side, the mass of the place itself rises — and this is where the eye fails at first, because there is nowhere for it to rest. The Crown is packed. Packed to its own throat. Ten thousand flue-mouths stand shoulder to shoulder like organ pipes, like a petrified comb, like the cells of a columbarium turned upright, every one of them built of small stones — stones no bigger than a heart, because everything here was carried up by hand, one armload at a time, across what must have been generations. Nothing natural shows anywhere. Every grain has been placed. The ground under your feet is a mosaic of hand-width tiles, and the tiles are worn to a final polish that will never deepen.

The sound is total. It has anatomy: a ground-tone below hearing that you receive through the skeleton, a vast middle register like a breath held by something enormous, and a high thin thread that lives in the teeth and the small bones of the ear. It does not rise. It does not fall. It has never risen or fallen. And here is the first rule of the place, the one your body learns before your mind does: a sound that never changes is a sound you stop hearing. Within hours the roar is edited out; the deafening becomes the baseline; you find yourself living in what is, functionally, the deepest silence you have ever known — a silence with a texture, a pressure, a warmth. The loudness becomes the medium you move through, felt in the palms when you steady yourself against the masonry, which hums against your hand like a living throat. Speech is useless; the old marks on the tiles are a touch-language, carved to be read by fingers. And the chord has a geography. In certain cells and pockets — the Wells of Quiet, you'd call them — the standing waves cancel, and the pressure lets go of your skull all at once, and those small dead pockets feel holier than any altar. The most honored things here are sealed in the Wells.

Go down into the Warren, the packed interior, and the place becomes more itself. There are no straight sightlines longer than an arm. Passages are themselves flues; to walk here is to be inside the instrument, in the warm breath on its way up. Every surface is fitted niche, shelf, cell, throat — no room between presences — and the niches are full. Sealed clay jars, dry as the air, holding ash, or salt, or dust that was once something carried. Slate tablets covered in tally-marks, always grouped in fives, counting something that finished. Folded cloth gone stiff as shale in the eternal dryness. Strings of beads. Timber lintels dry as violin wood, light as charcoal, sound after ten thousand years because rot is a water-animal and there is no water here — nothing rots, nothing rusts, nothing fades. Copper bands and lead seals have gone not green but a dry black and dusty rose. And everywhere, under thin skins of glaze — for the flues have been slowly firing themselves like kilns across the ages, glazed ash-blue and bone and dried-blood by the warm mineral breath rising through them — everywhere, in the plaster, thumbprints. Whole constellations of thumbprints, the makers' touch sealed under glass. It is the most present thing in the place. Whoever built this is more findable in one square foot of plaster than in any of the jars.

The light is dim and comes from both directions at once: down from the unmoving brass sky, and up from below, because far down the deep flues the mountain's heart glows a dull ember-red, and that glow rises with the warm air through grates and flue-mouths. The two dim lights meet in the middle of the walls. Shadows here are soft, doubled, and permanent — fixtures, not events. Nothing casts a new shadow. Nothing ever will.

Now. Whether any of this was built by hands, the place declines to say. That is its one strangeness, and it sits quietly at the center of everything. The tool-marks are real; no tools have ever been found. In the Wells of Quiet, where the air barely moves, the mineral breath has furred the stone with a pale bloom that imitates lichen, that imitates carving, that imitates touch — accretion in the shapes of patience. And there is one motif repeated everywhere, on jars and tiles and lintels: a frond, a sheaf, a tree of nine branches. The flora of a world with no flora — remembered, or invented, or crystallized; there is no longer any difference. Perhaps hands raised this place stone by stone around the deep heat, which is the one given fact, older than any stair. Perhaps the heat, breathing up through ten thousand throats for ten thousand ages, arranged its own instrument out of itself, in habits that happen to look exactly like craft. Either way, the work is finished. Whoever or whatever was building stopped, and what they stopped at is this: complete, packed, final.

Which brings the rules, and the rules are simple and absolute.

There is movement here, but no change. The warm air rises and has always risen; the chord sounds and has always sounded; motion, perfectly repeated, becomes a stillness deeper than silence. Nothing settles, because everything that could settle has settled — every loose pebble danced long ago to its final seat in the vibration and will never move again. Nothing wears further, because the wearing reached its terminal shape and stopped. Nothing can collapse, because the place is packed full of itself: there is no void for anything to fall into. Its density is its immortality. It is, in a sense, its own rubble, pre-collapsed into the one arrangement that can never be improved or undone. Time cannot be measured here. No clock works; nothing cycles; the light does not move; the warmth does not pulse. If you sleep, you wake to identical everything, and there is no evidence that you slept at all. Change must be imported. You are the only weather. A footprint pressed into the pale bloom of a Well is a calendar mark that will outlast every calendar you know.

And therefore the one law, the taboo that the place enforces by existing: take nothing. The chord is tuned to the last grain. Carry away a single tile, unseal a single jar, and the music changes — and the music is the only thing the place is doing.

In the lip of one flue in the seventh rank there is a swift, mummified, dry as a wasp's nest, wedged where it died however many ages ago. It is the last animal. Its small body has become a stop in that flue; the note of that one throat speaks through it, shaped by the curve of its dried wing. Nothing mourns it. Nothing needs to. It has been promoted from creature to component, which is the closest this world comes to an afterlife.

Stand at the parapet at the end, in the doubled dim light, warm stone under your hands, the drop below gone out of focus with age, the roar you can no longer hear pressing against you like the attention of something vast and finished. The tallies counted something and stopped counting. The thumbs are under the glaze. The heat rises; the throats answer; the dead rest in the Wells where even the answer cancels. Time does not pass here. It stands — and the sound, if you could ever hear it fresh again, is the sound of it standing.
