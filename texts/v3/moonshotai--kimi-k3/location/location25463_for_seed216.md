# The Loom

Come first to a single strand, high up, at what passes here for midday.

You are standing on a cable of living wood three miles across, and it feels like standing on a plain. Its surface is a felt of grey-green moss, ankle-deep, water-heavy, soft as old cloth. The plain curves away from you in both directions and is gone within the mile — not ending, just lost to the mist, which is the default state of the air: warm, bronze, faintly luminous, moving slowly past your skin like breath. The light never changes. It is always the last hour of a dim afternoon, a low amber gloaming with no visible sun, only a brighter region of haze that crosses the sky over the course of the day and gives up around evening. The Tenders call this the Long Gloaming, and they have no word for full daylight, because there has never been any.

Below you — far below, below the lower strands, below the lowest trailing root-curtains — is the Below: a sea of cloud, warm and slow, glowing faintly at night with its own green-grey light. Nothing that lives up here has ever been down there. Sometimes a shadow moves in it, vast and patient, and if you watch it long enough you will notice that its size does not stay consistent with its distance, and then it is customary to stop watching.

The cable under your feet is not wood in the way a tree is wood. It is a braid: millions of trunks of a species the Tenders call cordwood, planted and twisted and grafted together over centuries, fused into one rope-tissue, still alive, still slowly thickening. Every fiber of it was placed. That is the first thing to understand about the Loom, and the thing that takes longest to believe: **all of it was made.** Every strand, every knot, every curtain of moss, every cistern-bromeliad the size of a lake — planted, guided, tied, pruned, and waited for, by hand, with tools of thorn and bone and resin, over a span of time no one has ever measured. There is no natural substrate anywhere. There is no stone. There is no soil that was not once a root. The world is a wickerwork the size of a continent — perhaps the size of a world; no one has found an edge, and the cord-maps of the farthest-traveled Tenders simply fray into rumor — and it is entirely, patiently, continuously built.

## Strands, knots, hollows

The architecture repeats at every scale. Strands run from the thickness of a wrist to the thickness of a horizon. Where several meet they are gathered into **knots**: dense woven bolls, miles through, where the growth is oldest and most gnarled, where water collects in tank-flowers and reservoir-burls, where the moss-felt is deepest, where nearly all the life is. Between the knots, the strands stretch in long spans across the true feature of the Loom, which is its emptiness.

Because mostly, the Loom is not there.

The hollows dominate. They are chambers, gulfs, and wells between the strands — some the size of valleys, some the size of weather systems, some that have their own climate, their own fog-rivers and interior rains and slow rotating thermals. A traveler spends most of a journey crossing nothing. The woven world is a sponge whose holes are greater than its substance, and the holes are where the strangeness lives.

## How down behaves

Near a strand, down is nearly reliable. Nearly — because down here is loyal to mass, not to the world. On the flank of a great cable, down tilts toward the cable's heart, so that you can walk around its circumference as if along a road, the moss underfoot, the mist beside you, the far strands hanging in the sky like things that have not yet fallen. Water runs in curves. Trees on a cable's flanks grow at angles to one another, each convinced of its own vertical.

In the deep hollows, down gives up entirely.

In the wells — the great central emptinesses — a dropped tool does not fall. It drifts, turning, toward the nearest strand, arriving over the course of hours. Condensation gathers there not as rain but as hanging globes of water, some no bigger than an eye, some the size of houses, orbiting slowly, colliding gently, merging with a soft engulfing shudder. Dust hangs in dunes that never settle. The Tenders cross such places by **casting**: throwing a hooked line to a far strand and pulling themselves through the empty air, hand over hand, swimming with their shoulders.

The one true terror of the Loom is the **Mid-Empty** — the center of the largest hollows, where no strand is near enough for a cast line to reach, and a drifting body simply continues. They call dying this way the Slow Loss. It is not the fall that kills you here. It is the patience of the distance.

## Water, warmth, and weather

The Loom drinks the sky. Warm wet air rises endlessly from the Below, and the Loom is a condenser the size of a geography: every leaf is a dew-sail, every length of bark a weeper. Water beads on the strands, joins capillary rivers that run along the cables in slow braided channels, and gathers at the knots in cistern-flowers holding warm still lakes. Nothing here ever dries. Everything drips, and the dripping is most of what you will ever hear.

The warmth comes partly from the shrouded sun and partly from within. Deep in the oldest cords, dead heartwood is digesting itself in a decomposition so slow it has become geology, and the Loom is warm the way a compost heap is warm — a continent-sized, self-heating, living mass. The Tenders call this simply the Warmth, and they site their hearths, their nurseries, and their dead with reference to it. It drives the interior weather: updrafts rising off warm spans, fog-rivers pouring through gaps between strands, chambers that **breathe** — inhaling mist for hours as outside wind presses on the structure, exhaling it hours later, so that a hollow may fill and clear, fill and clear, on a rhythm slower than sleep.

Once each long cycle the dim sun marginally strengthens — the Brightening — and the whole Loom flowers at once. This is the Flowering: the hollows fill with drifting pollen, miles deep, hanging in golden dunes in the weightless wells, and the emptiness briefly, briefly crowds.

## The hush

The felt eats sound. This is total, and it shapes everything.

A shout in a hollow travels a few yards and stops, absorbed by moss and mist. Two Tenders working a span communicate by touch-speech: they press a palm to the strand and tap, because vibration runs along the taut living fiber for miles, far better than air ever carried a voice. The Loom is silent to the ear and talkative to the hand. Rest your hand flat on a great cable and you can feel it — messages from knots a day away, the tick of boring beetles, the deep flexing note of the span under wind, a single tone too low to hear, registering in the bones.

And the Loom keeps sounds. Certain dense old knots — **keeper-knots** — absorb a voice and release it slowly, over hours or days, so that a hollow may answer you back long after you spoke, in your own words, softened and strange. Tenders leave messages in keeper-knots for travelers they will never meet. Some knots are still leaking last season's storm. In the oldest chambers, they say, you can put your ear to the burl and hear sounds that no living throat made.

## The life

For a world so empty, it is abundantly alive — but the life keeps to the strands, and it keeps quiet.

Moss-oxen the size of cats graze the felt on silent padded feet, in slow herds. Dew-beetles walk the capillary rivers drinking. Lantern-moths drift the gloaming in constellations, signaling in pulses of cold green light, since light carries where sound cannot. On the undersides of the vaults, glow-worm colonies hang in patterns that travelers navigate by, stars of an indoor sky.

In the hollows live the drifters: membrane-winged gliders riding the compost-warm updrafts in slow spirals, sifters with wide soft mouths cruising the wells, straining the hanging water-globes of what lives in them — and everything lives in them; each globe is an aquarium. The predators of the wells are the casters, hook-limbed things that throw lines of silk exactly as the Tenders throw lines of fiber, and for the same reason. Even the animals here work with cord and hook. It is that kind of world.

At the Flowering, when a knot blooms, the emptiness converges: drifters arrive from hundreds of miles away, a **confluence**, the one season the hollows are loud — or as loud as the Loom permits, which is a rustling, wing-soft murmur, quickly eaten by the moss. Then the life disperses again into the vast between, and a traveler may walk a day and see nothing but mist, felt, and the slow water.

The dead of the Tenders are given to the wells. They are seeded before casting — moss-spores and flower-pips pressed into the wrapped body — so that the dead drift as small green gardens, seed-rafts turning slowly in the empty air. Some, after years, fetch up against a distant strand and take root. It is how several of the flowering species have spread across the Loom, and the Tenders consider this neither strange nor sad, only correct.

## The Tenders

They live dispersed, as everything here lives dispersed: a family to a hundred miles of strand, a life in which you may meet a dozen people. Their tools are thorn-hooks, bone needles, blades of flaked oldgrain — the fossil heartwood, harder than any stone they have never seen — and resins that set like amber. They have no metal. They have never needed it. Their technology is time.

Nothing in the Loom is carried into place. Everything is grown. You do not raise a cable; you plant forty seedlings in a braided trench and guide them, and your grandchildren's grandchildren tension the graft. A Tender's work is grafting, lashing, pruning misgrowth, reading the spans for stress — by the pitch of the strand's felt-note under the palm, by the lichen's patterning — and walking the cord-maps: records kept in knots, because writing here is knotting, and history is fiber, and an old family's archive is a room of hanging cords that must be read by touch, in the dark, by tradition.

They do not believe the Loom was built. They believe it is being built, and has always been being built, and that they are something the Loom does — its hands, or its habit. A quiet heresy holds that the Loom grows the Tenders, the way a strand grows leaves. It is hard to find anyone willing to argue the point with conviction.

## The give

And there is the wrongness, which is woven in at the root.

The spans are not the same length every day. Not by much — never by much — but a crossing that took three days takes four, sometimes, for no reason the body can find. Experienced travelers budget slack, and the cord-maps encode it: every recorded distance carries an allowance of **give**, the way rope does. Two Tenders walking the same span an hour apart have arrived a day apart. It does not happen when watched. Distances behave under attention the way the water-globes behave under gravity — which is to say, mostly, and less when there is more emptiness around them.

The hanging globes in the wells show you other places. Look into one and you do not see your face; you see a strand somewhere far off, a knot you may have visited, a view from no vantage that exists. The Tenders drink only from their own knot's cisterns. You do not drink from a well-globe. Part of you, they say, would stay in it — would be, already, somewhere else. Whether this is physics or etiquette, no one has wanted to determine.

And the Below is wrong, as has been said, and is not discussed after dusk.

## Time

The Loom is old the way equilibrium is old. Every span has long since sagged into its final catenary and stopped there; growth balances decay to the inch across centuries; there are no sharp edges anywhere, only felt and rounding and the soft gray weathering of cordwood bark gone silvery as old rope. Where a span once failed, ages ago, the scar survives as a **burl** miles wide, a healed knot of furious grain, and the burl-rings of the great scars are the nearest thing this world has to a chronicle. The Tenders measure long time in **great-sags** — the settling of a span into its rest — a unit of somewhere around three hundred years, give or take, they would say, and the give is not a figure of speech.

## Evening

Stand on your strand as the gloaming fails. Below, the Below begins its green-grey glow, and silent lightning walks around inside it, too far for thunder, illuminating slow shapes that do not keep their size. The lantern-moths rise off the moss in pulses. Somewhere a hollow inhales, and the mist pours gently through a gap in the strands like a river remembering itself. The strand under your palm ticks and hums: a message, or beetles, or the deep note of the span taking the wind.

A keeper-knot nearby releases a voice — hours old, days old, impossible to say. It says something soft in a tongue of low consonants, and the moss takes most of it, and what reaches you is only this:

someone was here, and spoke, and the world kept it.

The Loom goes on being built around you, at the pace of growth, by hands that have never known another work, in a silence that has never once been empty.
