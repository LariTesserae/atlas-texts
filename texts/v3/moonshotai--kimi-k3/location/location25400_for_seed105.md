Begin with the ceiling, because here the ceiling comes first.

There is no sky. There is the Roof: ice from one end of the world to the other, a frozen lid laid over a drowned mountain country, thick as a tower is tall, and older than anything beneath it. Its underside is not flat. It hangs in inverted dunes and pressure-folds, in long scrolled buttresses where it has pressed against the buried peaks and been carved by them, and in smooth bellied hollows over the deep valleys. It is never still. It flexes on some long, slow pulse — a week of gathering, a week of release — and the whole sea answers. When the Roof exhales, every current in the basin quickens, and the country below begins to sing.

The country: a range, once. Ridges running in long arcs, passes between them, cirques holding their shape like cupped hands, a central trough where the valleys gather and deepen into black. Hundreds of meters of relief, all of it submerged, all of it near-freezing brine that stays liquid by salt and by weight. Because there is no surface to stand on, the relief is something you move through rather than over — you can drift up a mountainside the way weather does, hang in the dark above a pass, sink along a scree slope into a valley that has not seen light since the Roof closed. A slow gyre turns through the whole basin, a circuit of weeks, and everything that lives here or was left here has arranged itself along it. Call it the Long Drift. It is the nearest thing this place has to a road.

Darkness is the medium. Not absolute — absolute dark would be simpler. The Roof transmits a ghost of the world above, a grey-green rumor of daylight that never brightens past deep dusk and never entirely dies. It is strongest at the contact zones, where summit rock presses the ice and the ice has been squeezed clear into windows, and it falls from there in a faint, permanent gloaming over the high slopes. The valleys get nothing. And then there are the cracks. Every so often — there is no predicting it — the Roof splits somewhere, and a shaft of true grey light comes down like a pillar, and for a few weeks there is a place in the sea where you can see. Then the ice heals from the edges inward, the pillar thins, and the dark takes it back.

What grows here grows without light, mostly, and without being eaten. That second fact shapes everything.

The vegetation comes in three great manners. Along the Roof's underside, pressed flat against the ice, trail the upward fields: pale, translucent fronds, silver-green, kilometers of inverted meadow drinking whatever light seeps through, thinning and thickening with the Roof's clear places. When a crack opens, the nearest fields ignite — that is the only word — flushing a soft ghost-gold, blooming furiously in the loaned light, and at the bloom's height they release their spores: buoyant motes, faintly lit, rising in slow constellations to drift along the ceiling and seed new meadows far downwind. A lantern-rise, seen from below, looks like the idea of snow, inverted.

On the ridges stand the pipeweed forests. Hollow, jointed stems, some no thicker than a wrist, some meters through at the base and centuries old, rising in dark ranks from the passes and saddles. The Long Drift plays them. Current across the open crowns of the stems sets each stand droning — flutes, organ pipes, a held chord that belongs to that ridge alone. Every ridge has its note. An experienced traveler here navigates by chord the way others once navigated by stars: the pass is where the low one and the high one cross.

Down in the black valleys lie the felt-fields — slow, dark cushions and filament-mats feeding on what seeps from the drowned stone, doing almost nothing, at almost no pace, for almost forever.

And none of it is touched. There are no fish in these forests. Nothing browses the upward fields. No small thing shelters in the felt, no jaw has ever torn a frond, no eye has ever registered the colors — for the pipeweed, revealed in a crack-shaft, is not black but a deep wine-crimson, a palette kept for no one. The stands are perfect the way a museum is perfect. Fallen stems lie where they fell. Growth accumulates unpruned across centuries. The lushness has a strange, held breath in it, a garden that has never once been hungry for.

It is also loud. This is the thing no one would guess about a sea with no animals: it roars. The Roof groans and cracks with its long breathing, and crack-booms roll between the ridges for minutes. The forests drone and swell as the Drift quickens on the exhale, chords crossing and interfering in the passes until the whole basin resolves into one enormous, slow instrument — the Choir, the place would be called, if anyone remained to call it anything. Beneath the music there is texture: the fizz of brine draining along the ceiling, the tick of frazil ice forming in the water column — fine crystals that drift upward, snow that falls the wrong way, pattering softly against the Roof like rain on a tent fly. In the cold spells of the cycle, the frost-fingers descend: brine-ice stalactites reaching down from the Roof with a faint crisp ticking, freezing whatever they touch. The forests keep their distance from old fingers; you can read the scars, perfect finger-shaped clearings in the stands, fronds blackened at the edges, slowly, slowly being reclaimed.

The people are gone, and everything they left is small. Terraced hillsides, their edges softened but legible, now hosting ranked pipeweed that follows the terrace lines as if still being weeded. A stair-road cut over a high pass, each step intact in the preserving cold, climbing from dark into gloaming into dark. Cairn-lines along the Drift. A set of holed stones hung in a notch between ridges — whether tuned for the current or caught by it accidentally, they drone now like the rest. In a cirque, a village: low stone walls, roof timbers preserved, dugout canoes still at their stone moorings, tied as if the return were certain. Weirs in a valley throat, built to trap fish, in a sea that holds none and perhaps never did. On a summit beneath one of the clear-ice windows, a shrine: stacked flat stones, wooden figures leaning with age, and caches of pipeweed seed — offerings, it would seem, to the light, or to the Roof that withholds it.

No graves. Nothing fled; nothing was taken. And here the strangeness deepens, quietly: the weed grows thickest over the old settlements, fronds rooted in doorways, and there are stands out in the empty dark that grow in the same pattern — street-spaced, room-shaped, ghost-fields mapping villages that were never built. Either the forests remember habitation, or they are rehearsing it.

The rules of the place, as a traveler would learn them:

Travel on the exhale, when the Drift runs strong and the ridges sound loudest.
Steer by chord; the dark will lie to your eyes, but the passes cannot lie to your ears.
Never touch a descending finger. Do not linger beneath the Roof's fresh cracks — what opens, closes.
The light is loaned, never kept. Bloom while it lasts.
Nothing here will eat you. Nothing here will eat anything you drop. Whatever you leave behind will remain exactly where you left it, for longer than your name.

At the deepest point of the central trough, the music arrives as a pressure in the chest more than a sound, the whole basin's chords stacked into one slow chord, the Choir singing its single sustained note to the dark and the dark holding it perfectly, the way cold water holds everything — intact, unhurried, and forever.
