The first thing is the water. It lies over everything, a warm sheet the depth of a boot-top, sometimes a knee, rarely more than a chest, stretching in every direction to a horizon that only structures interrupt. It is blood-warm. It is so still, most hours, that the world doubles itself in it: every stack, every gantry, every animal standing ankle-deep wears a perfect twin hanging downward into the floor. Rain has just fallen here, or is about to, or is falling somewhere you can see as a gray column walking slowly across the plain. That is the permanent condition. The whole world looks like the hour after a storm.

There is no ground under the water. No soil, no stone, no gravel bed, no root. If you kneel in the shallows and put your palm down, you touch a plate: dark alloy, hexagonal, a meter across, with a faint machined tooth so it is not slick, its edges weeping a bead of mineral moisture along the seam. Plates to the horizon. Plates under every pool and channel, under the plinths of the pumps, under everything. Here and there the tessellation is interrupted by a grate that breathes warm air up through the water in a slow silver stream of bubbles, or a hatch with a pristine recessed handle, or a stencil of glyphs, crisp-edged, unfaded, unread. Nothing is corroded. Nothing is worn. The bolt-heads are sharp enough to catch cloth. The whole place has the look of a factory floor the week after commissioning, and the longer you stay, the more you understand that this newness is not recent. It is simply permanent. Nothing here ages, and so everything here is young forever.

In the seams between the plates, if you look closely, there is a whisper of gray: a thin simplicity, single-celled, dull, the only unmade thing in a made world, if it is even unmade. It never spreads. It is cropped as fast as it grows, and it is the world's only crop.

The water itself is clear over the plates, faintly opalescent near outfalls, and it tingles on the skin with a ghost of charge. It is always moving and never going anywhere. Weirs a handspan tall step it down across the subtle built relief of the plain, and these tiny perpetual waterfalls are among the loudest things in the world, a bright murmur you can hear from a surprising distance because everything else is so quiet. Condensate sheets down the radiator fins and drips from every underside, from cable catenaries, from the bellies of pipes big as houses, so that the air is full of small notes of water landing in water, countless, arrhythmic, and somehow statistically constant, like a ticker reporting a number that never changes.

The structures come in provinces. There are radiator forests, where fins of warm alloy stand in ranks and shed a slow mist that never lifts. There are fields of stacks, transformer yards humming on plinths in the shallows, their analog gauges holding needles at readings that do not move, have never moved, are perhaps incapable of moving. There is gantry country, where walkways and pipe-runs layer overhead three and four deep, everything beaded with condensate, everything dripping. There are reservoirs rimmed with walkways, stair-stepped basins where the water is handed down through locks for reasons that are not evident. On the far horizon, in every direction that has a far horizon, there are superstructures: a spine of processing towers so large that weather happens on them, clouds snagging on their upperworks, rain falling only from them. Nothing is ruined. Nothing is under construction. Everything is running, warm and subsonic, at an idle so perfect it cannot be distinguished from rest, because its outputs are exactly its inputs. The world is a factory whose only product is the world, and the line is balanced.

And it is full of animals. That is the thing no description of the machinery prepares you for: the sheer number of them, and their silence.

They are made. That much is plain at a glance: seams, ports, small marker lights, the soft whir of a joint. And they are animal, which is plainer after an hour: they flock, they graze, they rest, they turn their heads to watch you pass with something that behaves exactly like curiosity. The waders come first in any accounting, tall tripodal things like still-herons, stalking the shallows in loose congregations of hundreds, dipping their long bills to the seams to crop the gray whisper, stepping over your feet without alarm. The plate-lickers are broad and slow as tortoises and big as cars, crawling the floor with their heads down, nosing the seams, resealing them, and their backs rise out of the water as living islands on which the waders perch. The shoals are the most of all: finger-length silver swimmers in clouds so dense that the water itself seems to change its mind, bait-balls turning as one animal, kilometers of them, siphoned into intakes and returned through outfalls, the bloodstream of the place, ferriers of trace everything. Striders dimple the surface in rafting flotillas that go to the horizon. Kites ride the thermals off the radiator forests all day and come home at dusk in slow streams to roost shoulder to shoulder along the pylons, thousands to a structure, settling with a soft servo chorus that is, for a few minutes, the loudest sound of the day.

There are no young ones. There are no old ones. There are no carcasses, no bones, no shells, no molt, no scat, no debris of any kind. Each animal is pristine, self-mending, and, you come to suspect, ageless. At docking pedestals scattered through the shallows they queue, patiently, to plug in: to drink charge, to shed scavenged heat, to exchange whatever they carry for whatever they need, an economy with no profit and no debt, balanced to the last decimal. At dawn, every animal in the world, wader and licker and shoal and kite, turns to face the sunrise, holds still for the space of a few breaths, and then resumes its rounds. The rounds are the same rounds. You could set a clock by a single licker's circuit of its plates, and the clock would never gain and never lose.

The quiet is engineered, you realize, or it is a law. A world this crowded should roar. Instead: the lap of water against your shins, the dripping, the hum you feel in your sternum more than hear, the rare bell-tone of one animal calling to another across the fog, two notes, answered once. Distant thunder, sometimes. Rain hissing into the sheet. At night, soft clicks, like cooling metal, though nothing ever cools.

The days are warm and the light is ordinary, which is its own strangeness: an ordinary sun rising over an unordinary world, glare doubled off the water at noon, heat shimmer over the radiator fields, fog at dawn that burns to a white haze. In the afternoons the convective rain columns stand up and walk the plain, each storm unique, each one replacing exactly what the morning took. Nothing about the climate trends. Nothing about it rests, either. It is stationary the way a standing wave is stationary: made entirely of motion, going nowhere.

Night brings the marker lights out, amber and green, sparse, reflected in the sheet until the world doubles again. The haze hides most of the stars. The hum stands a little clearer. The animals do not sleep so much as slow, and the shoals keep turning in the dark like thoughts the world is thinking.

Stay long enough and you begin to learn the rules, because the rules are the only events. Every output is someone's input. Nothing accumulates and nothing erodes. Nothing is born and nothing dies. There is no sediment, no ring, no layer, no patina; an archaeologist would starve here, because the world has no past tense, only a continuous present, endlessly and perfectly re-achieved. Even your own presence is absorbed: the water closes over your footprints, the gray whisper does not record your kneeling, and if you left a coin on a plate you suspect, without ever having tested it long enough, that the world would find the coin and make it not a coin anymore.

Who built it is a question the place declines to answer, or answers everywhere at once. There are no quarters, no control rooms with chairs, no graves, no names; there are doors in the floor that open onto pristine rooms full of instruments counting in unreadable glyphs, counting steadily, counting nothing you can identify. Once, a wader stepped out of its congregation, crossed to you, and presented a port beneath its bill, and waited, patient as a pedestal, and then stepped away. The lights along a gantry brighten ahead of you as you walk and dim behind. The world registers you. It accommodates you. It does not need you, and it does not send you away.

Perhaps the builders finished and left. Perhaps the animals are the builders, slowed into their circuits. Perhaps the factory was built to make a world and completed its work and was never told to stop, and so it holds its finished self in both hands, warm and wet and humming, at an idle indistinguishable from peace.

In the morning the fog comes up again, white and warm, and the rain column stands somewhere far off, and the waders turn to face the light, and the water lies blood-warm over the plates to the edge of sight, new as the day it was poured, exactly as it was yesterday, exactly, exactly, exactly.
