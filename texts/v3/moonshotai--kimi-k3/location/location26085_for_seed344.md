You find it the way you find a fire: heat first.

The plain is flat and ordinary, a long exhale of dry ground under plain daylight, and it gives no warning. A graded track crosses it half a kilometer off, with no turn-off, though if you watch the ground you can find the ghost of older ruts leaving the track and then simply ending, as if whoever drove them last got out and walked. There is a smudge of haze standing above nothing. The air that reaches you from that direction is a degree or two warmer than it should be, and it moves wrong — not as wind but as breath, slow, on some long period you can't yet count. Then comes the sound: a drone so low you first notice it in your boot soles, and under that, if you hold still, a hiss that rises and subsides over minutes the way surf does, though there is no surf on this plain. Only when you are nearly on top of it does the ground admit to anything: the land dimples, and the dimple deepens, and you are standing at the rim of a room the world has sunk into itself.

It is perhaps seventy paces across and eight or ten meters down, small enough to take in whole from any point on its edge, and most of it is nothing. That is the first thing to get used to. The floor is dark rock, freshly broken, its fracture faces pale and glassy, scattered with blocks whose corners are still crisp as broken crockery — and between the few things that stand here there is wide wet emptiness, bare stone wearing a skin of warm water, light lying on it undisturbed. The eye keeps arriving at places where nothing is happening and resting there. Whatever this place is, it is not crowded with itself.

The water enters near the center, off toward one wall: a pool maybe four meters across, too clear. Its bottom is visible, and it is not a bottom — the rock throats down and keeps going, darkening into a blue that is really just depth refusing to end. At the vent the water is near scalding; steam stands over it in a slow column rather than drifting, because the air here barely stirs. Around the pool the water has built its own banks: rims of mineral crust, sharp as snapped plates, terraced outward in bands of white, cream, rust, and a deep arterial red where the hottest films of life take hold. Everything is accreting. This is the strange inversion at the heart of the place — it does not wear down, it builds up, and it breaks faster than it weathers. Every surface is months old, or weeks. A ledge you could swear was bedrock is, in cross-section at some fresh break, layered like a wafer: this season's crust on last season's crust, with old water levels recorded as rings, bathtub lines marking some long slow breathing of the spring. The softening that age brings to other landscapes never arrives here. Time in this pit does not round things. It sharpens them.

The works are scattered the way a half-remembered sentence is scattered. A concrete flume leaves the pool's lip and runs downhill — downhill being a matter of a meter over thirty — toward the low corner, where a pump house stands: block walls, a roof gone to rust lace, paint blistered into a topography. INTERCEPTOR 6 is stenciled on the flume in flaking black, which implies Interceptors 1 through 5 somewhere, or nowhere, or never built. A pipe run crosses the floor on leaning posts, and you can read the direction of the water by which pipes weep and which smoke: the intake lines carry cooler water down from somewhere and sweat heavy condensation in the hot wet air, while the outflow lines steam at their joints. One pipe climbs the pit wall, clears the rim, and lies down on the plain, heading off level into the haze toward whatever this place answers to. Inside the pump house a single engine sits on its plinth, a slow ram with a flywheel tall as a person, its brass maker's plate greened, naming a firm whose works are far away and, likely, gone. A chart recorder stands against the wall, but the scale has sealed it: the drum is locked in a bead of crust, the pen fossilized mid-line, still touching paper that rotted around it. The instrument died. The thing it was watching did not.

Because the engine runs. That is the fact you keep returning to. Nothing maintains this place; no fuel arrives; the ruts ended at the rim years ago. But the ram gathers itself on a cycle of days, and the hiss builds, and pressure climbs through some patient arithmetic of heat and water, and then — a few times a month, by the reckoning of the rings — the stroke comes: one deep knock you feel in the sternum more than hear, and every pipe rings like a struck hull, and a pulse of hotter water surges down the flume, and the pools rise a hand's breadth and steam blooms off every surface, and then the long recharge begins again. Between strokes the pit is merely loud: the drone, the hiss, the tick and ping of hot metal accommodating itself, drips falling into pools with echoes long enough to count. It is a loudness you stop hearing after an hour, which is not the same as quiet. It is the silence this room has instead of silence.

And the loudness has a geography, the way water does. The walls gather sound and hand it around; it is not loudest at the engine but where the faces agree with each other, and there are slopes and floors of noise you learn by walking. One spot, beside a house-sized block with a sheared pale face, is the size of a standing person, and there the drone unmakes itself — two arrivals of the same sound canceling, an accident of distances — and you can stand in a pocket of near-silence while the pit roars around you. The rock at that spot is worn smooth at foot height, and there are four bolt-holes drilled in a square where a bench was once fixed. Someone knew the map of the sound. Someone came down here, repeatedly, to sit in the one place where it stops.

Life keeps strictly to its budget. There is a line, and you can see it: along the outflow, where the water cools below the temperature of a bath you could bear, the bare crust begins to wear films — rust and ochre first, then a thin true green — and at the coolest margin, in the flume's shade, a black moss holds on, and here and there a single tuft of sedge, knee-high, nothing taller, nothing that flowers. Below that line, nothing. Above it, the films grow thick and unbothered, banded in layers you could read like varves if you knew the language, because nothing grazes them. Nothing grazes anything. Nothing drinks at the pools, nothing leaves a track in the wet sediment, no beetle works the crust. Birds cross the plain all day and skirt the pit; the sky directly above it is as empty as its floor. Whether it is the heat, the chemistry, or some register of the drone that empties the air, the place has no animals in it and never has, and after a while that absence becomes one of its materials, like the rock.

The rules, once you have stood here long enough to feel them, are simple. The heat runs the engine; the engine moves the water; the moving water keeps the vent scoured open; the vent gives back the heat. A closed sentence. The pit is young and will stay young, because it grows and fractures faster than weather can soften it. Change comes, but only on the place's own calendar — a stroke a few times a month, a ring a season, a terrace edge you could watch advance if you brought weeks of patience and a tolerance for being cooked. The haze does not drift. The gradients are all weak and long. Everything here is in the middle of something slow.

Climbing out, you learn the last rule: the place ends cleanly. Two steps below the rim the drone is a physical fact; at the rim it stops, as if a door had been eased shut, and the heat lets go of you in the same stride. You turn around on the empty plain in ordinary daylight and there is nothing to look at — a dimple, a standing haze, a pipe lying down on the ground and going away. Somewhere below, the hiss is building toward a knock that will arrive on its own schedule, witnessed or not. The bench is gone. The bolt-holes remain. The appointment keeps itself.
