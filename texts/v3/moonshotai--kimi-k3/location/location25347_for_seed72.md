You hear it before you see it. The plain is flat, wind-combed, hot as a stovetop, and for the last mile there is nothing but grass and shimmer — and then a sound rising out of the ground itself, a compounded hum of engines and falling water and, depending on the hour, frogs. Then a fence, spoil tips heaped like small cooling volcanoes, a rust-red headframe, and the hole.

They call it the Kettle. The company's maps say something longer and more hopeful, but nobody uses the maps. Eighty meters deep, three hundred across at the rim — small enough that a shout crosses it, large enough that the shout comes back off the far wall a half-second late, thinned and doubled. The rim is the boundary of the world. Everything below it belongs to the pit.

You go down by the incline, riding a skip car on a moaning cable, or by the ladder-road cut into the wall, and the temperature runs backwards: it rises as you descend. The pit holds its heat like a held breath. No wind reaches the floor. The deep rock is warm, the machines are warm, the water is bath-warm, and by the halfway landing the air is wet enough to taste — rust, creosote, crushed frog-weed, kerosene, the sweet mineral breath of groundwater. Your shirt is done for. Nothing here is dry; things are only wet or wetter. That is the first rule.

The walls are raw as a fresh cut, because they are one. Banded in broad stripes — gray clay, liver-red ironstone, a seam of black shale like a rubbed line of soot, pale lenses of sand — and utterly soil-less, unrounded, unweathered. The Kettle is too young to have a landscape. It has a first draft of one. It ticks and rattles in the heat. After rain the walls spall and small slides whisper down, and the survey pegs lean a little more, and everyone glances up and goes back to work. Every face weeps: seep lines dark as spilled ink braid down the rock, fed by the water table the pit has punched below, the whole countryside's groundwater trying politely to come home. Where the seeps are old, ochre builds — orange rinds, iron-red drapes, a slick mineral glaze laid down by invisible bacteria, new stone forming in years instead of eons. A glove left on a ledge three winters ago is now half-fossil, stiff as pottery. The water lays down bone. Iron returns to ore. That is the second rule.

The floor is a saucer of trampled marl and braided water, most of it bare — puddles, rills, oil-sheen rainbows on the machine side, mud that takes a boot print and keeps it for a season. Boardwalks on creosoted piers stitch the camp together, and the camp is sparse: a dozen structures, each on its own island of lamplight and trampled ground, with wide emptiness between. The pump house, biggest by far. The dynamo house, humming. Two barracks, a canteen, a store, a workshop, an office with a rusted safe, an old powder magazine from the blasting years, a lean-to for the mule. Wires sag pole to pole like decorations for a festival that never quite happened. Lamps burn at midday. They have to.

Because the light is the thing you notice next, or rather its absence. The sky up there is a white-hot slot, brutal, and the sun only clears the rim late in the morning and is gone by early afternoon. For that window a blade of light stands on the floor and slides across it slowly, like something being measured. Steam rises through it in columns. The swallows come down out of the glare to feed inside it, stitching the brightness. Men working in the gloom drift toward it and stand in it the way men elsewhere stand in a doorway out of rain. Then the blade thins, climbs the east wall, and is gone, and the long amber dim returns — lamplight doubled in every puddle, the walls graded from bronze to black. The light keeps office hours. Third rule.

At the lowest corner lies the sump: a mere the color of strong tea, warm as bathwater, out of which rise the remains of the Drowned Level — the wheel-less head of a hoist, the roofs of two sheds, a run of tram rail descending into the water like a decision reversed. Twenty years ago that was the working floor. The painted gauges on the pump-house wall tell the rest: dated waterlines, one per year, climbing a knuckle at a time. The boardwalks are on their third raising; below the planks, if you look through the gaps, the old pilings stand in the shallows like a drowned orchard. The pumps are not winning. They are negotiating the pace of the loss, and everyone knows it, and the engineer keeps two ledgers about it, one for the company and one true.

The pump house is the heart of the place and sounds like one — a double beat you feel in your teeth, in the planks, in your sternum at night through the bed frame. Big electric centrifugals and one ancient beam engine kept oiled as a backup and as a relic, fired once a year on the camp's anniversary and wept over by old men. The rising main, a riveted iron column fat as a silo, climbs the wall in brackets and weeps at every flange, threading the seep-streaks with its own. At the rim it discharges into a flume, and here is the pit's great irony: up on the plain, the Kettle's pumped water has made the only lush thing in the district, a long green slick of watercress and sedge and willow-weed at the flume mouth, loud with birds — while down below, the pit itself keeps almost nothing green at all.

Almost nothing. Vegetation is a rumor here. One stand of reeds at the sump's edge, pencil-thin and much photographed by visitors. Horsetails along a spill line, prehistoric and unbothered. Moss beards under the permanent drips. Algae painting the wet rock in green-black and rust-orange, the only meadows, microscopic. And the Tree: a single fig, sprouted from a bird-dropped seed on a high ledge, famous, named, argued over, its roots visibly prying the young rock apart — the surveyor checks it twice a year with genuine anxiety. By the canteen door, old fuel drums hold the camp's other garden: imported soil, carried down by the bucketful like contraband, growing a pumpkin vine and six pepper plants that are tended better than the machinery. The pit has no soil yet. It is making some, grain by grain, in the sediment fans below the storm flumes — a future measured in centuries. Nothing here hurries. The Kettle is deciding, at the pace of sediment, what it is going to be.

And yet the place teems. This is the thing every visitor remarks on by the second hour, swatting: the Kettle has almost no plants and is absolutely stuffed with animals. The midges come first — columns of them over still water, rising like smoke from invisible fires, so thick at dusk you learn to breathe through your teeth and keep the mesh tucked. Mosquito fish dimple every puddle. Darners big as spoons hawk the lanes of air between buildings, and their armored nymphs stalk the sump's bottom. The mudflats are studded with crayfish chimneys, little volcanoes answering the spoil tips above. There are leeches in the warm shallows; the men salt their bootlaces and complain with a certain pride.

At dusk the frogs begin, and by full dark the chorus is louder than the engines — genuinely, measurably louder, a wall of sound given a cathedral's acoustics by the pit's sounding box, until the whole world is one ringing bronze bell of frog. Tree frogs grip the sweating pipes. After rain come the toad nights, when the boardwalks are carpeted in moving leather and you walk with a lantern held low, apologizing. The swallows and martins have mud nests on every bolt, beam, and eave — mud being the one building material in endless surplus — and swifts scream all day around the rim, riding the thermal the Kettle exhales. A heron owns the drowned hoist and stands on it at dawn like the last surveyor of an abandoned project. A dabchick dives in the sump. Spiders grow fat as thimbles in the pipe racks. Geckos wait on the warm lamp poles at night for the moths, and the moths come in their thousands — the lamps wear fur. And every evening the bat drift, an old adit high on the north wall, exhales a smoke-ribbon of bats, and because that adit breathes out the one cool draft in the entire pit, the evening card game happens at its mouth, men and bats sharing the air in a truce older than anyone's employment.

The census, if anyone kept it honestly: some forty people. Two cats, fat on what the nets miss. One ancient mule, mostly deaf, philosophically suited to the noise. And beyond that, numbers that defeat arithmetic. The frogs alone outnumber every human who has ever set foot in the camp. This is their town. The people are tenants with good pumps.

The pumps must not stop. That is the fourth rule, and the only one that is written down, stenciled on the pump-house door above a small shrine nailed there by the crews — a painted tin saint gone soft with damp, coins fused to her shelf by ochre. The reason is practical: the old galleries under the far wall connect to other workings, other deeps. The Kettle is the drain of a buried district, the lowest point of a hidden watershed, and if it drowns, the water takes the neighbors. So the camp exists to keep the pit dry, and the pit exists to employ the camp, a purpose as circular and self-sustaining as a heartbeat. Nobody finds this strange anymore.

Time moves here the way the water does: slowly, and always in one direction. The rains come and step the sump up a stair at a time. The frog line shifts with the shore. The walls slump by inches toward their eventual angle of rest. The ochre thickens its rinds; tools left in seep zones go stony; the rising main must be reamed every few years like an artery. The company argues in decade-long sentences about new pumps versus surrendering one more terrace, and meanwhile the sediment fan grows, and one far century — no one says this part aloud — the pit will finish becoming what it is already, in embryo, choosing to be: a warm lake, then a marsh, then perhaps a garden, the pumps long silent, the fig tree's descendants cracking the last of the raw walls, the drowned level buried in its own accumulating record. All of it gradual. All of it already legible in the gauges, if you know how to read the dated lines climbing the wall.

But that is not tonight. Tonight the lamps burn in their moth-halos, doubled in black water. The pump beats under everything, felt more than heard, until the frogs swell and bury it. The strip of sky overhead is crammed with stars, and below it the pit holds its own spilled constellation of lamps, and for a moment it is hard to say which sky is real. A laugh carries from the canteen, doubled by the far wall. The heron stands on the drowned hoist, asleep on one leg, trustee of the future. Somewhere in the dark the water rises by less than a knuckle, patient as geology, and the beam engine waits oiled in the pump-house shadows, and the heat, and the hum, and the chorus go on and on.
