The sound arrives first. Out on the high dry plain, hours before anything is visible, there is a hum with no direction — a low pressure in the ears, as if the air itself were idling. The plain offers nothing to explain it: warm gravel, dry grass flattened in one direction, light like hammered tin. Then, later, a cloud. The only cloud in the sky, small and white and moored, standing over a single point on the horizon. It does not drift. It rebuilds itself in place, and under it, when you finally come close enough, is the tower.

A cooling tower stands alone on the plain — a concrete hourglass, its waist pinched by old mathematics, its lip flared against the sky. You can walk around its base in four minutes. There is nothing else. Or almost nothing: spread around it at a respectful distance are the bones of whatever it once served — floor slabs sun-bleached to the color of paper, bolt-holes drilled in rows like a script no one reads, a rail spur that goes fifty meters and stops. The plant is gone. The plant's accessory remains, and the accessory is alive, and loud, and wet, and green.

The tower is one room, and the room is almost empty.

Inside, the floor is a single round pool, black and mirror-still, filling the whole footprint but for a concrete walkway and the mouths of pipes. This is the only open water for a hundred miles of dry land — a pocket of it, hoarded. Above the pool: nothing. A column of warm misted air rises the full height of the shell to a hard bright disc of sky at the lip, and the eye climbs it the way the eye climbs a cathedral, finding almost nothing to hold. A shaft of daylight leans down the middle when the sun is right, full of drifting droplets. Everything that lives here lives on the walls. The middle belongs to the air, the sound, and the birds.

The sound. Between rains it is a million small drips, and the throat of the tower is a resonator that cannot leave a sound alone: every drop that falls from the heights is caught, repeated, multiplied, so that one drip rings like a small bell in an empty church and ten thousand of them ring together, a glittering ticking chime that never repeats and never stops. Underneath it, the drone of the pumps below the walkway, a bass note felt in the sternum more than heard. And when the wind crosses the lip, the whole shell becomes an organ pipe and sounds a single deep tone, swelling and dying over minutes, and the black surface of the pool answers it in slow standing ripples.

Then the rain comes. It is on a clock — not a clockwork clock, but a float and a siphon and the weight of the water itself, a governor that lets the machine set its own pulse by its own body. First the riser pipes knock, once, twice, a wooden sound inside iron. Then the crown of the tower weeps. Then it lets go: warm rain, indoors, falling the full height of the void, a roar so total it erases thought, the pool boiling without heat, mist racing up the throat, the birds going up with it. It lasts perhaps an hour. Then the long decay: lesser rain, then drips again, then the chime. The tower breathes on a period of hours, and the whole interior is built around the breathing.

The machinery is down in the gallery beside the pool, behind a door that has never locked. Pumps the size of wine barrels, riveted, painted a green that exists nowhere else, their automatic oilers still patiently weeping. Copper valve-wheels. Ceramic insulators. Cable-runs so old their insulation has cured to something like bark — and vines growing alongside them in the dark, pale, following the same routes, so that in places you genuinely cannot tell which line is wire and which is root. There is a room of dials behind glass, needles that still have opinions. There is a maker's plate that reads UNIT THREE. There is no Unit One. There never was, or it is the slabs outside.

Nothing leaves the loop. The pumps lift the pool's water to the crown; the water falls; the falling turns a small turbine that keeps the pumps' losses topped up. It cannot quite power itself, so it drinks the difference — the warmth of deep pipes that touch something warm far below, and the thin cold of the high air at the lip. A heat engine with no product. The same water has gone around and around for longer than anything outside remembers: the oldest water in the world, perhaps, endlessly re-breathed. The tower cools its own blood. That is all it does, and it does it completely.

Where the mist condenses on the upper inner wall, there is a ring of jungle.

It hangs in a band around the throat, two-thirds of the way up — lush beyond reason, a cloud forest glued to concrete. Ferns with fronds a meter long, orchids, hanging vines whose aerial roots dangle free into the void, thick moss curtains, all of it rooted in nothing but pockets of lime scale, travertine, old nests, and concrete meal — a manufactured soil, sediment of the machine, a hand's breadth deep. Below the ring, in the drip zone, darker mosses and black algae. Above it, near the lip where the air turns cold and thin and dry, only grey lichen rosettes, and then bare weathered concrete at the rim. A mountain's altitude zones, compressed into one made thing and stacked vertical.

And here is the strange part, or one of them: the ring is not even. It is thick in vertical swathes, sparse between, and the pattern of its thickness maps the standing waves of the tower's roar — the garden grows lushest where the sound presses hardest against the wall, shaking moisture loose from the mist. From the walkway below, you can read the noise of the place in its plants, walk all the way around a sonogram made of ferns. The vegetation is the shape of the sound.

The birds know this. A few hundred swifts own the void — abundant individuals of almost nothing else — and they wheel in the empty middle in lanes, holding to the quiet nodes where the echoes cancel and they can hear their own screams. Watch one cross the whole diameter of that emptiness: a single small body in the enormous air, and nothing around it, and nothing behind it, and the drop of light from the lip catching it for half a second like a struck match. Their nests are cups glued into drain-holes. Bats keep the pipe-gallery by night. Moths orbit the gallery lamps; gnats rise in columns up the light-shaft at noon; beetles row the black pool; spiders web the valve-wheels, and their webs bow outward, always outward, on the tower's exhalation. At the lip, one hawk has learned the updraught and hangs there all day at the tower's shoulder, below your feet when you stand at the top.

You can stand at the top. A stair poured into the shell's thickness climbs through slit-lit dark, through the warm wet lower air, up past the ring — suddenly at arm's length, dripping, smelling of orchid and lime and guano — and on into cold. The plain itself is high; the tower stands on that height and adds its own, and at the lip the air is thin enough to notice, your breath short, the horizon absurd. The plain falls away in every direction, empty, the dead slabs below reading like a plan view of something that failed to survive. Overhead the vapor-flag makes itself, the one cloud, forming out of nothing at the tower's mouth. The handrail at the rim is bright where hands would slide along it. No hands come here. It is bright anyway.

Everything is weathered to the state it intends to keep. The concrete long ago stopped eroding and started accreting: lime leached out by the water is laid down again as flowstone, travertine curtains, a slow geology the tower performs on itself. A wrench left on a ledge in the gallery is half-swallowed by stone now, a fossil in progress. Roots and flowstone have reached their truce. Even the failures are domesticated — a thin rainbow of oil at the pool's edge where the pumps weep, and a film of iridescent bacteria grazing on it, thriving on the leak. Nothing is falling apart faster than it is being absorbed.

Nothing here hurries. The sun-spot crosses the ring once a day like the hand of a very slow clock. The ring itself has crept perhaps two meters higher in a century. The travertine grows a fingernail's thickness in a generation. The swifts leave and return, and their return is the only calendar anyone would need. Everything large in this place is slow, and everything fast is small: a drop falling the whole height of the void, a swift across the emptiness, the knock in the pipes before the rain.

Dusk is when it is most itself. The crosswind rises and the tower sounds its low organ note across the darkening plain. The evening pulse comes on — the knocks, the weeping crown, the warm indoor rain, the roar filling the shell from the pool to the lip. The swifts pour home down the middle of the storm in their quiet lanes. Overhead, the one cloud catches the last light, pink at the edges, moored to its machine. The sun-spot climbs the wet green wall, reaches the lichen line, and goes out.

The tower cools its water. The water lifts itself and falls. The garden holds the shape of the sound. There is no one to tell it to stop, and nothing in it wants to.
