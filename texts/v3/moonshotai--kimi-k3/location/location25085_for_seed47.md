**The Rootplateau**

Sound leaves you one thing at a time on the way up. First the lowland haze swallows the small noises — insect-click, trickle, the knock of your own gear — and then, as the escarpment carries you above the bulk of the air itself, even the wind gives up its voice. It still comes, thin and fast and cold, but it has nothing to press against, and it slides past you without remark. By the time you reach the Lip and stand on the rim of the world, the loudest thing you own is your pulse.

Below, the Drop falls through three weathers to a haze-sea that never clears, beneath which the rest of the planet conducts its unwitnessed business. Ahead, the plateau: bone-pale to every horizon, flat the way deep water is flat — not featureless, but resting, each feature an event with a hundred kilometers of patience on either side of it. At dawn, if you stand at the Lip and look down and west, you can see the plateau's own shadow lying on the haze-sea, a dark slab the size of a continent. It is the only time the place ever shows you its true shape.

The ground is the first thing to understand. There is no soil. There has never been soil, or none that stayed. You walk on root-rock — the banded, glassy, metamorphic hearts of mountains, material that was once twenty kilometers inside the earth, pressed and folded and cooked, now lying open to the sky as floor. The bands beneath your boots are the cross-sections of ranges that died before the sun was middle-aged. You are walking across the insides of dead mountains. Erosion did not lower them so much as *finish* them; this is what a mountain becomes, given all the time there is. A floor. The oldest stable state. The stone has been polished by eons of charged dust into a soft gloss, like old bone, like teeth, and it takes the ordinary daylight — the light is the one ordinary thing here — and returns it as a low, even pallor.

The provinces alternate. There are the pans: salt flats the size of shallow seas, leveled by something more patient than water, where the horizon becomes one instrument-straight line. The salts are pale oxidants, not sea-salt — there were never seas. There are the spire-fields: colonnades of leaning pale pillars, a week's walk wide, that are neither ruin nor growth but the visible compromise of the plateau's two appetites, grinding down and raising up. Between provinces, nothing. The emptiness is the medium. A single standing stone here is not a sight; it is a destination.

The silence has texture. It presses. There is nothing for sound to return from — no echo exists on the plateau — so your footsteps come back flat, local, apologetic, as if the air declined to be involved. You learn the few sounds the place permits. At dusk, the whole crust cools and contracts and lets out one slow tick that you feel through the soles of your feet, like a house settling, if the house were a continent. Sometimes, deep below, a slab fractures, and that arrives through your bones rather than your ears — the mountains' ghost, remembering. And there is the hum of a Marker under your palm, below hearing, felt in the sternum.

The temperature is a local, tactile thing. Thin air cannot hold heat, so the sun-warmed rock is warm under your hand while the shade beside it is freezing, and for an hour after dark the stone gives the day back to you, briefly, like a kindness. Nights are long and far below freezing. Water, such as it is, never troubles with being wet. There is no liquid phase here. In certain basins — the frost gardens — hoarfrost condenses straight out of the thin air on certain dawns, appears without falling, and later vanishes without melting. On those dawns the bloom comes: a flush of grey-green and faint gold that opens for the frost, drinks the year's only water in a few hours, and is gone by midmorning. Everything alive on the plateau keeps that calendar.

The weather is called the grey. It arrives as walls of haze moving fast and silent across the pans, and within the walls the lightning writes — discharges that crawl rather than strike, that pause, that branch in deliberate angles, inscribing and erasing geometries against the murk. Before a front arrives, the thread-lichen lifts. That is the storm-sign: the black wire-filaments that run along the rock seams rising slowly, like hair, as the charge builds. Afterwards the pans hold the storm's charge for days and glow faintly at night, as if remembering. There are also the slow columns — dust devils grown vast and translucent in the thin air, turning without a whisper, walking the flats for days like blind, patient pilgrims.

And then there is the Revision.

The plateau is one thing. This is not known, but it is felt, the way the Markers all hum the same note. The crust here is substrate — a technology so old and so patient it has become geological, computation gone to stone — and sometimes a region, a day's walk or a province, goes *fluent*. Not molten: there is no heat. Matter is simply persuaded. The ground moves like a slow liquid; spires subside or are raised; the ancient banding is redrawn. The infrasound of it builds for days beforehand, felt as unease, as a hum in the teeth, and the standing instruction of every traveler is the same: get to a Marker. The Markers are never unmade. They stand untouched in their islands of swept ground while the world is re-poured around them, and when it is over the new surface does not look new. It looks *older* — tidied, glossed, finished. The machine manufactures antiquity. It is finishing the mountains, and finishing, it turns out, is infinite. The stillness of the plateau is a standing wave made of furious patience. The place rages toward rest.

At night, a distant Revision shows on the horizon as a cold shimmer, like the lights of a city. Travelers call them the false cities. Everyone walks toward one, once.

The machine keeps things. This is the strangest of its manners. The Line is a shallow groove cut by a visitor long ago; after every Revision the machine re-cuts it, straighter each time, improved. Cairns left by the dead are copied — one becomes three becomes a row — each replica smoothed a little further toward some ideal the machine holds. Nothing here is kept as it was. It is kept as it should have been. Given enough millennia, everything left on the plateau is edited into the machine's grammar.

The Markers themselves are pillars of black glassy stuff, warm in the cold, spaced a month's walk apart, dust never settling near them. Travelers navigate Marker to Marker; maps fail, Markers don't. Around them the ashwort grows in slow grey aureoles, drawn to the warmth or the hum — felt cushions of vegetation, a fingernail's width a decade, some of them older than the civilizations that left the cairns. The other traces are few and far: the moorings at the Lip, rings of fused glass sized for titanic tethers, a harbor that outlived its sea. A lander half-absorbed into fluent rock, legs protruding, attended for a thousand years by something hull-sized and slow that is not dismantling the wreck but *reading* it. And the last house: a pearly dome half-buried in a pan's edge, its door open for millennia, a table, a bed of dust, a journal. Two pages survived. The first bears one line:

*We came to measure the silence. The silence is measuring back.*

The second is a list, plainly meant for whoever came next:

*Nothing echoes. Maps fail; the Markers don't. Watch the thread for the grey; watch the longwalkers for the Revision. If the ground hums, walk. The lights are not a city. Leave nothing you do not want improved.*

The longwalkers. There may be a few hundred on the whole plateau, and an encounter with one is the kind of thing a life gets divided into before and after. Four meters at the shoulder, hide like grey felt, feet as wide as shields that spread their weight across ground that might not hold — and on the pans their prints, a meter across, persist for decades. You can read a pan like a calendar: sixty years of crossings, one print at a time. They graze the ashwort so gently that a cushion outlives its grazer. They hear the infrasound that you only feel, and they are simply never where a Revision is going to be — that is the meaning of the journal's warning. They do not fear you. They register you the way they register a Marker: as furniture of the world. The young are almost white. The very old are grey-black, and there is something in their bones denser than calcium, and no one has ever found where they die.

The only other life with any presence rides the grey: the sails — translucencies within the storm walls, membranes kilometers wide lifted by charge, straining dust from the murk, visible only as a kind of pause in the haze. After great storms they come down, and acres of grey silk lie across the rock, dissolving in days. A silk-fall is the only abundance this world knows. Everything converges on it — the bloom out of season, the longwalkers walking a month to be there — a decade's eating in a week, and then the plateau returns to its fasting.

Nights are the deep black between crowded stars, with a few steady stars holding at the zenith even at noon. Silent aurora hangs in green-violet curtains after the grey has passed; the pans give back their faint stored glow; somewhere on a horizon, perhaps, the false cities finish something. You sit by a Marker with your palm flat against the warm black glass, feeling the note it shares with all its fellows a month's walk away in every direction — one body, asleep, dreaming storms.

Beside the Marker, in the pan dust, there is a longwalker print, and next to it your own bootprint. The machine will keep them both. It will keep them until they are perfect, until they are what they should have been, until the last mountain is finished — which is never, which is the point, which is why the plateau, the stillest place imaginable, never once stops moving.
