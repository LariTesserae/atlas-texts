From the lowlands, the plateau is not a place so much as a boundary: a wall that holds up weather. It rises out of the hot green haze in bands of dark red and black stone — rock so old that the lowlanders, who find pieces of it in their riverbeds, call it first-stone and use it for their dead. The wall is two days tall. For most of its height it is bare, polished, fluted by ten thousand years of waterfalls, and then, near the top, it disappears into a collar of cloud that never fully lifts. The lowlanders have never seen the top. But they hear it. On still nights, when the river is low and the frogs pause, a faint high ringing comes down out of the air, directionless, like the sound inside a shell. The old people say the mountain is singing. They are more right than they know.

This is what a mountain range becomes at the end of its life: not dust, but a table. Everything around it has been carried away by time — the peaks, the valleys, the whole architecture of the high country — and what remains is the stubborn core, flattened by age into a single vast bench ten days' walk across. The rim is scalloped with amphitheaters where the wall has slumped and the forest of the lowlands reaches up in talus aprons of green. Elsewhere the edge is a clean lip of bare rock, wind-polished to the smoothness of a tool handle, and then nothing: a straight fall through a kilometer of air into cloud.

Stand at that lip in the morning and you will see the plateau's water cycle laid out like a diagram. Thin white threads of water — seep-falls, where the plateau's hidden moisture finds the edge — slide off the rim and unravel within a hundred meters, torn to mist by the updraft, never reaching the bottom. The mist rises, joins the collar of cloud, and the cloud, pushed by the day's first heat, spills back over the rim in slow white rivers that crawl across the ground and soak away into it. Water leaves over the edge and returns over the edge, endlessly. Almost nothing is lost. Almost nothing new arrives.

The ground it soaks into is not ground. That is the first rule of the place. There is no soil on the plateau, no stone underfoot except at the rare tors — the whole interior, dome-shaped, horizon-wide, is a single continuous skin of living plants, a felt of small silver-blue leaves matted over the compressed bodies of their ancestors, going down further than anyone has ever probed. The plateau's top does not erode. It *accretes*. Every dead leaf, every fallen insect, every dropped feather is caught in the felt and kept, buried upward, so that the interior rises by the thickness of a leaf each year while the cliffs around it retreat by the width of a body each age. Dig into the mat — people do, in certain places, with stone spades — and you pass down through centuries as through pages: layers of darkening fiber, then black peat that weeps tea-colored water, then, deep down, the preserved trunks of giants, black as iron and hard as it, dead three thousand years and unrotted, the only timber on a world with no forests. The plateau is a museum of itself. Nothing here washes away. Everything is kept.

The felt is springy underfoot, and warm at the surface, and cold an arm's-length down, and it is full of water it will not release except to being squeezed. This is a damp world with no visible water: no streams, no ponds, nothing that runs. Water is combed from the fog by the leaves' fine grooves, carried down into the mat, held in dark lenses and pockets deep in the peat. Walking at dawn you can hear it — a faint, continuous ticking and dripping *beneath* your feet, a rain that never falls from the sky, the whole plain quietly watering itself.

And here is the strangeness of the place, the thing every visitor must reconcile: it is a world made entirely of plants that reads as empty. The felt is total — there is nothing that is not plant, or the preserved dead of plants — but it is all floor. No shrubs, no herbs above the mat, no midstory, no clutter. Between the floor and the sky there is almost nothing at all, for kilometers at a stretch, in any direction. The eye finds a green line and cloud-shadow continents moving across it, and perhaps, if the air is clear, one column on the horizon.

The columns are the giants, and they are the only vertical things the plateau grows. Each one stands alone in the middle of an enormous invisible territory — a fluted trunk like a bundled organ-pipe, thirty meters of gray-green wood, crowned with a shallow bowl of stiff leaves. They are spaced so far apart because each one's root-web poisons the felt against any other; a giant is an island that has forgotten the sea is green. Their fluting is not decoration. The grooves comb the fog as the leaves do, and the tree drinks its own condensation, channeling it down to the roots — and the crown-bowl catches what the trunk misses, pooling it into a tank of dark sweet water suspended in the sky. Every giant carries its own reservoir, and every reservoir is inhabited: translucent shrimp-things, climbing crabs, frog-things that are born and die without ever touching the ground, each tree's tank an isolated lake with its own drift of species, its own small experiments. There are giants whose tanks hold creatures found on no other tree.

The giants are also not where the old maps put them. Over centuries they lean, and creep, roots dying behind and growing ahead, migrating a body-length in a lifetime of men. Where one has departed, a ring of deeper green marks the scar. Where one falls — an event of a generation, the loudest sound the plateau makes, the one thing that silences everything else — the fall opens a wound of light, and for a season there is a marsh, a brief lens-lake of frogs and dragonflies and riot, before the felt closes over it and the slow race to replace it begins, a race measured in centuries.

Now. The sound.

The emptiness is a surface effect. Under your boots, in the interstices of the felt, lives a biomass of small singing things that no one has ever counted — translucent, thumbnail-sized stridulators in their trillions, several species, each tuned to a different pitch and a different hour. You almost never see one. You cannot escape them. They sing in *fronts*: coordinated waves of song that ignite where the sun has warmed the mat to some threshold and then sweep across the plain at a walking pace, a front of sound kilometers wide and kilometers long, moving across the dome of the world like a weather system. You can see a front coming — the felt dimples and shimmers under it, as if the ground were pressing its ear down — and you can walk alongside one, keeping pace, in the roaring edge of it, for an hour. Inside a front the sound is not a sound but a pressure. It is pain. It makes the teeth ache and the vision tremble. Between fronts the plain is cathedral-silent except for the dripping underneath and the wind. The people who live here mark their hours by them: *three fronts past dawn*, they say, meaning late morning, meaning the light is thus, the mist is gone, the world is loud.

Layered under the fronts is the second sound: the giants. Wind across their fluted crowns makes them drone, each tree at its own depth of note, a vast slow organ-chord that swells and fades as the gusts move across the plain. On windy days a dozen giants are audible at once, and the people navigate by the chord — every tree's note is its name, and a traveler who knows the chord-map can locate himself on a featureless green ocean with his eyes shut. And under that, at the bottom of the world, is the third sound, which is not heard at all: the infrasonic calls of the wanderers, felt only as a faint unease in the sternum, a pressure in the chest at night.

The wanderers are the plateau's other citizens, and almost the only large ones. They are vast, solitary, slow — grazing mounds of an animal, so few in number that you might cross the plain without seeing one, so wide-ranging that their compacted trails are the only roads the plateau has. The felt cannot resist them; it parts and regrows behind them. They crop the mat, they carry the giants' seeds in their guts, and the spacing of the giants on the plain is, in the end, the map of a wanderer's centuries of looping travel. The people follow their trails and do not hunt them. There are so few that to kill one would be to erase a road, an orchard, a territory, a piece of the future's geography.

Once or twice a year, the hidden animal world becomes visible all at once. An emergence: the felt boils, the stridulators take wing in their uncountable trillions, and the sky goes dark and loud with them, a plume of life streaming off the plateau's rim, riding the updrafts, falling as food-rain into the lowlands below. The whole region knows when it happens. The rim-hunters know first — the great gliders that patrol the updrafts all year, riding the wall of rising air without a wingbeat, snatching what the wind carries up. The rim is the plateau's hunting ground and its export dock at once: everything the interior produces in such staggering, invisible abundance is, sooner or later, poured over the edge.

The people are few — a few hundred, perhaps, on a plain the size of a small country, and you could walk a week and find no one, or find a cairn, a trail, a dew-pond and know they'd passed. Their marks are small and old and scattered, clustered on the tors, the rare gray whalebacks of bare stone that rise from the felt like the world's bones showing at pressure points. Stone is the one thing the plants cannot swallow, and so the tors carry everything permanent: fitted-stone shelters without mortar, grinding hollows worn into the rock, clay-lined basins that catch the mist and hold the only open water on the plain, cairns aligned on distant giants, carvings of spirals that mean, the people say, *a front, passing*. Their tools are stone and bone and fiber and black fossil wood dug from the peat; a little cold-hammered copper, treasured, passed down, never thrown away. Their ladders down the rim are fiber rope and wooden cleats, and the routes are secret and seasonal, and the lowlands at the bottom might as well be another country.

Two things mark them as children of this place. The first is their ears: they wear plugs of waxed fiber through the loud hours, and even so the old ones are mostly deaf, and their speech is braided through with sign, so that conversations continue unbroken as a front sweeps through and drowns all sound — hands moving while the world roars. The second is their relationship to the maps. They know the maps age faster than the land. A giant that has crept half a kilometer since the carving was made, a fallen tree become a marsh become felt again, a trail where a wanderer no longer walks — they hold their geography loosely, the way you hold something alive.

The day has its choreography. Dawn: the cloud-spill crawling over the rim, the felt drinking, the drip-rain underneath, the giants beginning to hum as the wind finds them. Midmorning: the first front igniting on the eastern dome and setting out westward, and after it another, and another, until by noon the fronts overlap and the whole plain is one continuous unbearable pealing, the loudest wilderness on earth, all of it made by creatures no one ever sees. Evening: the fronts collapsing into scattered soloists, the chord of the giants coming forward as the insect treble dies. Night: a low bass chorus, a different species' hour, and sometimes — felt, not heard — a wanderer speaking across the whole dark width of the world to another wanderer, too slow and too deep for ears.

The lowlanders, lying awake in the heat far below, hear none of this as it is. To them it arrives as that faint high ringing out of the cloud collar, weatherless, sourceless, the mountain singing to itself. They say it means rain is coming. Often enough, they are right.
