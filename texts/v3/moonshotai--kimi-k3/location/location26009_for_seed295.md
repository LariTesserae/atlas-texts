**The Choir**

From the cold grass of the outer slopes, it looks like badlands: a tilted high basin filled with pale domes and fluted towers, bleached paper-white and mud-grey, everything rounded and settled and old. Nothing moves on it. It could be geology — some exhausted volcano weathered into hoodoos. Then you notice the shimmer hanging over the towers, though the day is too cold for heat-haze. Then you feel the pressure behind your breastbone, a hum too low to hear. The hill is humming.

It has been humming for longer than the ice ages.

The whole basin — forty kilometers of domes, terraces, chimneys, and collapsed lobes spilling over the passes — is built. Not founded on rock and decorated, but *built*, all the way through: paper, wax, mud plaster, silk, resin, shell, and bone, laid down in layers by uncounted generations of animals working without a single tool. Nothing here has ever been cut. Everything is chewed, secreted, pressed, woven. The basalt of the original massif lies so far beneath the construction that it appears in only one place — the drowned galleries at the bottom, where black glassy rock shows through like the world's last unmade surface.

The outer crust is the quietest part. Acres of wasp-paper gone white with age, wax sheets bleached to parchment, mud domes frost-split every night and resealed every day, carved by high-altitude wind into settled, weathered forms. It is a rooftop with a city underneath. At dawn the chimneys begin to steam — hundreds of vents exhaling warm wet air into the freezing morning, the visible breath of the massif rising off the ridge in plumes. All day the Choir exhales: the metabolic heat of its inhabitants drives air up through the chimneys, and cooler air is drawn in through low flues along the margins. All night the cold clamps down, the flow thins to a seep, and the day's moisture condenses against the inner skin of the crust. Before dawn, everywhere, it begins to drip.

One breath a day. It is the fastest thing the Choir does as a whole.

Rain comes rarely, and when it comes it is an event: the entire basin becomes a drum, thunder arriving through the floor rather than the sky. Between storms the interior lives on its own recycled water — breathed out, condensed, dripped, drunk, breathed again. The place drinks its own breath.

**Inside**

Enter through a flue and the air turns warm around you, warm like a mouth. The smell arrives in strata: honey over ammonia over wet paper over rot over, faintly, green. And the sound builds — from hum to drone to roar to something you stop experiencing as sound at all. It becomes pressure. After an hour you hear it with your sternum and your teeth. In the chimneys, where thousands of cave-birds click their way through the dark on audible echolocation, it is louder than a waterfall. In the deepest cistern it is still there, coming through the walls like surf through a hull.

The dark is total, and it is a *made* dark — enclosed, partitioned, smelling of its makers. What light exists is leaked or stolen: a seam where the crust has split, spilling one white shaft into the blackness; the blue-green constellations of glow-worm larvae beaded along the cistern ceilings; a faint foxfire smolder of luminous fungus in the deepest compost. That is all. Eyes here have gone huge or vanished entirely.

Directly under the crust lies the Paper Canopy, and it holds the place's history. This was a cloud forest once. The Choir ate it — chewed the great figs and laurels down to their hearts and re-laid the pulp as vaulting, so that the forest still stands, rebuilt in itself: paper pillars where trunks stood, paper arches where the crown was. Ghost architecture. Everything held up by everything else, corridors near-touching, combs layered on combs, the walls ten percent living at any moment — the surface you lean on is partly backs.

Where light leaks in, the Green Rind forms: a thin photosynthetic skin between the dark body and the bright crust. Here is the lushness — fern draperies meters deep along the drip-lines, moss in saturated bolsters, leaves stitched directly into the paper by weaver ants so that living green is sewn into dead construction. Green is a wound-color in the Choir. Light is a leak, and every leak is instantly colonized, defended, fought over.

Deeper in, the districts give way to one another like slow weather. The Wax Vaults, warmest of all, reeking of honey, combs gone dark amber with age, the floor tacky underfoot, the drone at its most physical. The Web Halls, where communal spiders span whole chambers in acre-sheets and the floor is felted silk and carcass-mulch a meter thick — halls where vibration is the only sense and stillness the only camouflage. The Cisterns, black drip-fed reservoirs where pale frogs sit on wax ledges and blind white swimmers turn slow circles under the glow-worm stars. The Undermold, deepest and hottest, where the leafcutters keep their fungus gardens in pale terraced sponges, tended, weeded, luminous at the edges — agriculture, practiced in the dark for a hundred thousand years.

**The inhabitants**

There is no floor in the Choir. There are only backs. You do not walk; you wade. The air itself has texture — moths dusting your face, midges in every breath, winged things colliding softly with everything, always.

The scale defeats counting. For every gram of paper and mud, there is roughly a gram of builders, and the builders never stop. Leafcutter streams flow along fixed roads worn smooth — canyons a centimeter deep polished into the comb-stone purely by feet, over centuries. Army ant fronts cross the halls like weather systems, taking days to pass, everything fleeing before them or being taken. Velvet worms hunt the corridors by glue and patience. Centipedes long as your forearm listen through their legs. At dusk the bats commute out through the chimneys in columns, riding the day's exhale, while the cave-birds come clicking home against the current, and the guano they drop through the night feeds whole floor ecologies that never see a leaf.

Many of these creatures exist nowhere else. The place is old enough to have grown its own species — pale, huge-eared, flightless, translucent things, troglodytes of a cave that built itself.

**Time**

Read the Choir in cross-section at the Ghost Margin, where it retreated from some valley centuries ago and left the shells standing hollow: mineralized comb like honeycomb stone, paper gone to silica, resin flows sealing whole chambers mid-construction — rooms of fossilized intent, builders and building caught together in copal. The oldest galleries at the bottom are effectively geology now, tunnels through the petrified nests of ancestors, and they are still in use. New inhabitants walk hallways of stone made by animals a hundred thousand years dead.

At the opposite edge, the Front advances into living forest at a few meters a year. A standing tree is scaffold first, then mummy, then pillar, then pulp, then vault. Nothing hurries. The Choir's clocks are: one breath a day, one storm a month, one nuptial flight a year — the chimneys smoking at dusk as the alates pour out on the exhale, most to die on the cold crust, a few to found new lobes at the margins. One great vault collapse a decade. A gallery's working life, a few centuries. The mass itself, constant.

That is the deepest fact about it: construction and decay are in balance. What is built equals what crumbles. The Choir has weather, not change.

**Rules of the world**

Everything is made, and everything made is eventually built over. To be still is to be papered; to move is to be heard. Light is a leak. Water is breath. Warmth is body heat — the whole basin holds itself at the temperature of an animal, because it is one.

Stand on the crust at dusk. The cold comes down off the peaks, the steam from the chimneys thickens and bends, and far below your feet the drone shifts pitch as the day's long exhale strengthens and the inhabitants turn toward their night. Forty kilometers of it, humming one note into the thin air. Less a hive than an organ. Less a landscape than a body — one vast animal assembled from uncounted smaller ones, patient, deafening, dark, and warm, breathing once a day, in no hurry at all.
