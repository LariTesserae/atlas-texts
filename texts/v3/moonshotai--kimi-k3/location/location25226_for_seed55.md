**The Roof**

Begin at the ceiling, because there is a ceiling.

The ocean has a roof of ice, and the ice is old beyond sounding. Its underside is not flat: the slow currents of ten thousand centuries have worn it into long parallel flutes, hollows running in the direction of the great circulation, like the inside of a shell. It is cold against the roof — the coldest ordinary water in the world, a half-degree from ice — and it is utterly dark. There is no sun to exclude. The ice simply thickens, century on century, and what light the sky might once have offered stopped arriving so long ago that the question has gone out of the world.

The Roof is not silent, but it is close. On a cycle of days, something vast above flexes — a tide in the ice itself, the whole shell settling — and the ocean receives it as a tone below hearing, a pressure that passes through every cubic meter of water at once, felt rather than heard. This is the Long Groan. It is the only clock the world has, and the world keeps it faithfully.

Here and there the Roof carries a filled circle: a shaft of re-frozen ice, subtly different in grain, rising into darkness. Wells. They were cut once, and used, and later sealed by cold and time. No one has sounded how far they go.

And from the Roof, rarely, come the brinicles — the one fast thing this world permits. Where the ice rejects its salt, a thread of brine drains downward, so cold that the water around it freezes into a sheath, and the sheath grows down through the dark like a slow spear, days in the falling, silent. Where it touches the floor, cold spreads outward in a ring and everything living in that ring dies white. Then, over decades, the felt creeps back in from the edges. A brinicle strike is the local weather. Generations of garden are born, mature, and die in the space between one and the next.

**The Column**

Beneath the Roof, the water stands in layers, and the layers are ages. Each stratum differs from the one above it by a half-degree, by a faintness of minerals on the tongue, by the century in which it last touched ice. Water here is not mixed; it is filed. To descend is to move backward through the ocean's memory, and the water at the bottom of the column has not seen the Roof in four hundred years.

Through it, always, the Snow falls.

The Snow is the detritus of the gardens — dead filaments, ground mineral, empty spore-cases — falling at a pace that has no hurry in it, a few body-lengths a day, falling everywhere and always, the way rain falls in a climate that has settled into one weather and means to keep it. The Snow feeds the floor. The Snow is the floor, eventually. It is the world's only precipitation and its only erosion and its only news from above.

Decades apart, the weather reverses. Somewhere in the dark a province of the gardens releases its spores in a single week, and the column fills with rising motes instead of falling ones — a slow bloom drifting up and sideways on the Gyre, seeding vaults and seams a continent away. Then decades of none. A season in a week, and then no season at all for half a lifetime.

The Gyre itself is the closest thing here to a climate. One great cell of moving water, wide as a hemisphere, slow enough to be outswum by anything that swam — but nothing swims — and persistent as geology. You do not feel it as current. You know it has arrived by a new taste in the water and a faint, decades-long push against the fronds. It carries the Snow in slow sheets. It carries the spore-blooms. It leans the frond-forests a few degrees off vertical, all of them, everywhere, pointing downwind like a held breath.

**The Floor**

And then the floor, and the floor is the thing.

Dive anywhere. Dig anywhere, down through the felt and the snow-sediment and the layered dead of the gardens, and you reach masonry. There is no natural substrate. If bedrock exists beneath, no one has seen it; the made layer goes down past sounding, vaults beneath vaults. The plain is fitted stone to every horizon — jointed, coursed, dressed by hand. The joints in the old high work are finer than a knife's edge. In the more recent work — recent meaning younger than a million years — you can find mortar, a mineral paste ground from precipitate, still holding.

The plain is nearly flat. That is age, not intention. The relief it has is relief worn down or buried: a long low swell is a vaulted hall with a roof of sediment; a shallow basin is a courtyard filled with the Snow. The true topography is negative. The floor is pierced — doors, shafts, stairwells, the open mouths of cisterns — and everything that matters goes down.

In sheltered places, under lintels, in the lee of gate-towers worn to stumps, the tool marks survive. Chisel scars in regular ranks. The long sweep of an adze. Holes where pins were set — a soft metal, copper or its kin, long since gone to green concretions that dissolve at a touch. Here and there a lintel carries rows of cut marks, a writing or a tally, worn now to dimples. The craft is unmistakable, and the means are plain — hands, stone, simple metal, patience — and the extent is planetary. Swim a year along one seam and you will not reach its end. The works were not built quickly. They could not have been. They were built the way the Snow falls: continuously, without hurry, for longer than seems possible, until the accumulation became total.

And the works have a plan, and the plan is legible, and the plan is water. Channels. Settling basins. Weirs, baffles, sluices, gates of stone frozen open or frozen shut. The modules repeat across the world like dialects — here hexagonal basins in ranks, there long parallel flumes — but the idea is the same everywhere: hold water, slow water, settle it, move it without haste from one province to another. The whole ocean reads as a reservoir. A cistern the size of a world. The channels still work. They simply carry a heavier water now.

**The Gardens**

The gardens grow in the dark, and they are lush, because nothing eats them.

The base of everything is the felt: a black mat, fingers thick, lying over the masonry in the low-energy provinces, layered with its own dead until the layering becomes sediment. It feeds on what the water carries out of the stone — the masonry leaches, the felt mines. Where water percolates through the works and exudes at the joints, the felt thickens into the frond-forest: ribbons of deep red and ox-blood and bone-white standing shoulder-high and taller, packed stem to stem, leaning to the Gyre. From arches and lintels hang the banners, downward gardens feeding on seep-water. Across doorways stretch the veils — filament curtains that strain the Snow from still water, the only traps in a world without hunters.

So the gardens trace the plan. In the dark, where there is nothing to see with, you read the architecture by its life: the seams stand drawn in fronds, the doors wear veils, the old channels carry forests down their centers like greenless rivers. The city is outlined in its own overgrowth. The lushness is not decoration on the works. It is the works, digesting at the pace of a finger's width a decade.

They have one extravagance. Brushed by a falling mote, by the edge of the Gyre, a frond will sometimes answer with a slow blue glow that travels its length and passes neighbor to neighbor — a ripple of light across a hillside, dim, brief, gone. It happens perhaps when a brinicle strikes, perhaps when a vault lets go somewhere far off and the pressure arrives hours late: the dark hills answering in sequence, one long shiver of blue, and then darkness again, and years before the next. Light here is not a condition. It is an event.

Nothing swims. Nothing grazes. Nothing hunts. The gardens die only by burial, by brine, by the white ring of a brinicle — and their dead stay where they fall, and become the felt, and the felt becomes the sediment, and the sediment fills the seams of the world.

**The Hollows and the Brine Shores**

Inside the works, the water is older. The vaults and cisterns hold water that has not mixed in millennia — finished water, its chemistry complete, tasting of nothing. Some hollows are choked with banners and veils; some are perfectly clean, kept clean by stillness alone, the Snow unable to enter sideways. In the stairwells, at the height of a hand, the stone is worn in smooth hollows. Handholds. The wear is underwater wear — soft, patient, enormous in duration. Whatever held those stones held them here, in the water, for a very long time.

In the deepest basins, the water changes kind. Brine — rejected by the Roof, drained down through the old channels — pools in the depressions, denser and colder than everything above it, colder than freezing, kept liquid by its salt. It lies in the basins as lakes with true surfaces: a shimmering interface in the dark, with shorelines of mineral crust like frost-rims, with rivers feeding it along the builders' flumes, with ripples when the Gyre breathes overhead. A falling mote of Snow crossing the surface trembles it, and the tremor spreads, and fades. The lakes themselves are sterile — too cold, too dense — but at the mixing edge, where brine water and common water trade their chemistry, the frond-forest grows thickest of anywhere in the world: the lush belt, the farmlands of the dark, standing at the shore of water they cannot enter, drinking the gradient.

These are the coasts of this world. Everything that lives here lives near them.

**Sound and Time**

The rules are few. Nothing swims. Nothing eats. Light is an event. The fastest thing in the world is ice, and even ice is silent. Sound arrives one thing at a time: the Long Groan on its cycle, felt in the water's bones; the tick of stone answering some weak thermal gradient; the faint hiss of a brinicle's growth; and, rarely — once in a century, somewhere across the world — a vault giving way after its million years, the collapse arriving hours later as a pressure more than a sound, the hills answering blue, then dark.

The overturnings come every few centuries: some province of upper water finally grows cold and heavy enough to sink, and the whole column slides slowly past itself, and the gardens answer with a growth pulse decades long, and the Snow falls differently for a while, and then it all settles again. Erosion proceeds at the pace of the felt's holdfasts working the seams, patient as frost. The plan of the works softens. The joints fill. The world is near its final form the way a tide is near its turning — not stopped, but finished with everything except the last slow settling.

Of the builders there is no trace but the work: the chisel ranks, the tallies worn to dimples, the handholds in the stairs, the consistent rise of steps built for legs, the sealed wells in the Roof. Whether they ended here or left upward, the felt has had their substance either way by now. What they wanted was the water — that much the plan says plainly, in every basin, every weir, ten thousand times repeated: hold it, slow it, keep it. The water is kept. The water is still being kept. The cistern outlasted its keepers and did not notice, because nothing here is in a hurry, because the works were built to the same timescale the ocean already kept.

**The Long Settling**

So the world goes on toward its finished state, unwitnessed and unhurried. The Roof thickens. The Groan keeps its days. The Gyre leans the forests downwind, and the forests drink the seams, and the seams fill with the Snow, and the Snow is the gardens' dead and the gardens are the works' slow digestion, and the brine pools shine their dark surfaces in the basins, trembling when a mote falls through.

Nothing is coming. Nothing is needed. The last events are small ones — a blue shiver on a hillside, a white ring spreading under a spear of ice, a mote crossing the surface of an underground lake — and they are enough, and they arrive at the pace the place has chosen, and the dark receives them, and closes.
