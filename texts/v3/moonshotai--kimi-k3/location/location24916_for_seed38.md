# The Mould

First, understand that you will never see it whole. From inside, there is no whole to see — only green, and paler green, and the dim. You must approach from below, from the lowlands, where the air is thinner and the light has opinions. There you look up, and up, and keep looking up, and the horizon is a wall.

It is a plateau the size of a continent, and it is made of plants. Not covered by them. *Made* of them. No rock shows anywhere along its ten-thousand-kilometer rim — only root, bark, moss, and leaf, draped and buttressed and folded like the hide of something that lay down long ago and found the position comfortable. The edge is soft. Everything about it is soft. It weeps: the whole rim sweats water in slow veils that never quite become waterfalls, because the mist rising from below meets them halfway and absorbs them, and so the continent sits inside its own breath, ringed by a cloud it manufactures, exhales, and drinks again.

The lowland peoples — if there were lowland peoples — would call it a mountain. It is not a mountain. It is closer to a reef: an accretion of the living upon the bodies of the dead, risen over unthinkable time into the thin bright air, and still, almost imperceptibly, rising.

## The Roof

The top of the world is a violent place, and almost nothing in this account happens there.

Up on the Roof there is glare — a white, burning haze with a brighter patch where the sun presumably is — and wind, and storm, and a heaving sea of leaf-tips stretching past every horizon. The Roof is where the weather lives. The Roof is where the light goes.

Below the Roof, the light is nearly gone.

The canopy is not a layer. It is a depth — a kilometer, in places two, of stacked and interwoven growth: emergents standing on the shoulders of scaffolding figs, figs standing on the mummified trunks of their own ancestors, epiphytes on epiphytes on epiphytes, until the distinction between *tree* and *forest* has no meaning. Every photon that enters is taxed a thousand times. What reaches the Interior — the world beneath, the world this is about — is the green dregs of light: a shadowless, directionless, pearl-dark gloaming, as if the air itself were faintly luminous and faintly exhausted. There is no noon here. There is a pearly less-dark and a total dark, and between them, nothing you could call a day.

Distance fails in this light. The far is not smaller; it is only paler. You cannot see the scale of the Mould from inside it. You infer it: from rivers that run for a lifetime and never find a sea, from the slow turnover of species across a thousand walking-days, from the fact that weather arrives here not from the sky but from *somewhere else in the forest.*

## The Floor

There is no ground.

What you walk on is a felt — meters thick, sometimes tens of meters — of root, moss, fallen litter, and living mat, suspended over hollows. It gives underfoot like flesh. It is warm. Press your palm to it and the warmth is close to blood temperature, because the whole mass is metabolizing, always, and the warmth of its slow digestion never leaves. In places the felt sags into lens-ponds of tannin-dark water the color of strong tea. In places it tents over voids, and you walk across the ceiling of a cave you will never see, hearing water move below you, patient, going somewhere.

The relief is almost nothing. The Mould is old beyond the reach of erosion's imagination — it has been eroding into itself for so long that there is nothing left to erode *into.* Hills rise perhaps fifty meters in a hundred kilometers: consolidated root-mass, the hardened scar of some ancient event. Valleys are not cut but *seeped* — slow lines where the water gathers and the moss grows deeper and the drip-rhythm quickens. The land has settled into its final form. It finished changing long ago. Now it only repeats.

And yet it rises. The deep substrate compacts under its own accumulating weight, millimeter by millimeter, and the living surface grows upward to keep pace, millimeter by millimeter — a fountain of vegetable matter climbing forever against its own subsidence. Nothing feels it. Everything participates. This is the oldest stable state the world has found, and it has been holding it, breathing, for longer than the stones it buried can remember.

## Water

It never rains in the Interior. It *condenses.*

The air is saturated past satiety — warm, thick, sweet, faintly narcotic. The slow air, call it. Breath comes short until your blood learns it, and even then you move as everything here moves: gradually. Water does not fall in this world; it accumulates. Every leaf ends in a drip-tip, and every drip-tip carries its bead of water that swells, and lets go, and falls — sometimes a few meters to a lower leaf, sometimes, in the rare shafts, a long glinting distance into the dark. The drip never stops. It has no weather. Each grove has its own tempo, a polyrhythm of let-go and land, and the animals of each grove are timed to it the way elsewhere creatures are timed to the sun.

Open water comes in three colors. Tea-dark in the seeps and lens-ponds. Milk-pale where a wound in the root-felt weeps sap into the flow. And black — true black, ancient, mineral — where a deep spring rises from the Underroot, from the compressed and lightless past. The animals gather at the black springs. They drink slowly, and stay long, and no one could tell you why. Memory-wells. The water remembers something the surface has forgotten.

Rivers here have no banks. They are defined only by the direction of their patience — a broadening of the seep, a darkening of the moss, a current you feel around your knees rather than see. They do not rush toward any ocean. Most simply deepen, slow, and are reabsorbed by the mantle that made them.

## The Vaults

Here is the strangest thing, and it is scattered everywhere, faintly, like a rumor the forest keeps half-telling.

You come upon a colonnade: a double row of fluted pillars, vaulted high overhead, floored in moss, utterly still. It was never built. It is a cast. Long ago — long ago on a scale that makes *long ago* meaningless — something stood here: a pinnacle of the old buried stone, a grove of different giants, the skeleton of some titan the world no longer makes. The strangler figs grew around it, whatever it was, and the thing within dissolved, or rotted, or was drunk away, and what remains is the mold of it. Hollow. Green. Exact.

The Mould is full of these. Hollow towers where a stone spine weathered away inside its own root-sheath. Archways that are the ghosts of limbs. Naves, apses, stair-terraces, cisterns — an architecture of absence, a city the size of a continent that no one ever designed, no hand ever worked, and nothing ever inhabited on purpose. No tool has ever touched any of it. There is nothing here that was *made.* There is only everything that was outlasted.

The fauna use them anyway, the way water uses a ditch. In the vault-floors you sometimes find arrangements: the stone eggs — small mineral concretions, the only true stones the surface world ever produces — gathered and set in rings, in lines, in spirals around a spray of bones. Something does this. Something here has preferences. Whatever it is, it is patient, and quiet, and has never once been seen doing it.

The other works are warmer. The mound-spires of the great decomposers rise from the felt like ventilated cathedrals of chewed fiber, breathing faintly, humming at the very bottom edge of hearing — the single loudest standing presence in the Interior, and even that is more felt than heard. And along the ancient animal runs, where the same paths have been walked for a hundred centuries, the forest has responded to the traffic: roots thickened into causeways, limbs fused into bridges, the behavior of generations fossilized into morphology. The path made the arch. The arch outlived the walkers. The walkers still come.

## The Animals

Everything here is abundant and almost nothing is loud.

The browsers are the size of oxen and the speed of weather — moss-backed, garden-furred, carrying whole small ecosystems in the thatch of their coats: ferns, beetle nations, a frog that lives its entire life between one animal's shoulder blades. They graze the felt. They are walking meadows, and they are silent, because silence is the first law of the Interior and even the large obey it.

Gliders cross the green dusk on membranes of fur and fused leaf-litter, steering by smell. The seep-rivers are full of pale soft filterers, gilled and unhurried, straining the slow water for what the canopy lets fall. In the warm ponds, things with too many legs and no eyes at all. Everywhere, the decomposers — the true citizens — turning the world over at the pace the world requires.

Predation is slow, as befits the place. Ambush, patience, digestion. Some of the predators are barely distinguishable from the flora, and some of the flora are not entirely innocent: snare-vines with animal accomplices, pitcher-blooms that share their catch with the creatures that tend them. Down in the dim, the line between the kingdoms is a matter of emphasis.

And then there are the Sown. You will not see one. You will see a corridor of crushed growth a kilometer wide, healing at the edges, a decade along in its recovery. You will see a lake that is lower than the rings on its margins say it should be. You will see, if you are very lucky or very lost, a slope that breathes — moss-covered, tree-bearing, warm — and you will understand that the largest animals of the Mould have become indistinguishable from its terrain, that the forest grows on them as it grows on everything, and that their passage is measured not in footfalls but in watersheds.

## Time

The world's events are rare and enormous.

A crown-fall: somewhere above, a structure the size of a hill gives way after its thousand years, and the Interior receives it first as a *pressure* — a deep breath drawn by the whole forest — and then, minutes later, as a long dampened boom that arrives through the ground more than the air. You might hear a dozen in a life. Afterward, a gap stands open: a slow, gold-green pillar of real light, falling from the torn Roof to the astonished floor, and it will stand there for decades while everything below races to fill it — racing, here, meaning that the winner will be decided in a century or two.

And there is the Brightening. Every few hundred years, for reasons held in the Roof's own weather, the haze thins and the light strengthens — not to brightness, never that, but to a luminous silver-green that touches depths light hasn't reached in living memory. The whole continent answers. Everything flowers at once. The air becomes a single smell, indescribable and total, and the pollinator nations wake from wherever they keep themselves between times, and for a season the Mould is loud — actually loud — with wings. Then the haze closes, the dim returns, and the forest spends the next centuries quietly digesting the event.

This is the calendar. There is no other. Time here is told by drip-tempo, by the generations of moss on a bole, by the healing of corridors and the filling of gaps.

## The Rules

Nothing mineral is exposed. The world's skin is alive, everywhere, without exception.

Light is rationed. The crown takes nearly all; everything below lives on what falls — litter, drip, sap, the dead.

Sound is local. The felt eats distance. News travels by river and by run, never by voice.

Water never falls hard. Violence belongs to the Roof; below, there is only seep and patience.

Everything returns. The surface takes back its dead within a season; the Deep keeps the slow black record of everything else.

And the surface rises. Imperceptibly, eternally, the world climbs against its own weight, and has never once stopped.

---

Stand in it a while. The warm air, the dim green, the drip-tempo of this particular grove. Nothing happens. Nothing has happened here in a very long time, by any measure the lowlands would recognize — and that is not a failure of the place but its achievement. This is what a world looks like when it has finished: when every conflict has been composted, every niche filled, every stone swallowed, every emergency resolved into rhythm. The Mould is not dying. It is *done* — settled into its final form like water into a basin — and it persists, warm and dim and breathing, rising by the width of a hair each year against its own enormous weight.

Somewhere far off, a breath is drawn by the whole forest at once. Minutes pass. Then the ground delivers its low, soft boom, and the moss trembles, and a gap of slow golden light stands open somewhere you will never see.

The drips resume. They were never really interrupted.
