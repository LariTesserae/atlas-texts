High in the dead spire, near the altitude where its substance begins to thin toward the summit, there is a hollow the size of a small chapel. Whether it was built, grown, or simply left — a bubble in the material that the builders found and decided to keep — depends on how you read the walls, and the walls decline to be read. A single aperture opens from its flank. Outside it: a ledge, three mooring-bollards, and then the drop.

The drop is the first fact of the place. The spire falls away below the ledge for longer than the eye can follow, down through haze grades of dark into a darkness that has no bottom in it, only distance. At this height the curve of the world is visible — a dim disk, its night side turned up, unlit country without end. Overhead the sky is black at midday. A small red sun hangs at the horizon and does not move, because the aperture admits the outside slowly: something in the opening — it looks like clean air, like nothing — delays the light that crosses it by roughly four thousand years. The view from the ledge is an archive. What the sky is doing *now*, the room has not yet seen fit to let in.

Inside, the light is the color of pewter. It comes from the delayed sun through the sill, from the stars, and faintly from the material itself — the walls give off a deep violet glow at the very edge of vision, the luminescence of an engineered physics relaxing, over geological time, back toward ordinary law. After your eyes adjust you could read by it, if the text were patient. The silence, too, needs adjustment. It is not empty. It is full — queued. Here, silence is not the absence of sound but the storage of it.

The floor is neither metal nor stone. It is matter with the slack taken out of it — finished matter, every quantum degree of freedom assigned by hand. It does not ring when struck. It does not take a scratch. Light slides off it at a slightly wrong angle, as if the surface were politely declining to be seen. Over it lies a grey silt, ground from the same substance in the era when the spire still flexed with thermal cycles, before it cooled into equilibrium. That dust is the youngest geology in the room, and it stopped being made a very long time ago. Two dunes of blacker grains stand on the open floor, sharp-crested, migrating a grain's width per decade — not by wind; the thin air the room still keeps cannot be bothered to move that fast — but by the room's breathing, which will be explained.

Cold behaves here the way water behaves elsewhere. It pools. It has pooled, in fact, in a shallow basin at the room's center, and it has a surface you can find with an outstretched hand — a distinct level, waist-high, below which the cold is denser, older, more itself. Around the basin's shore stand rings of pale frost, and the frost is not ice: nothing in this room has ever been wet. It is the air's own exhalation, crystallized into feathers where the cold's surface once stood. The rings mark former levels, like tide lines in a dry harbor. The cold is draining away somewhere, slowly, over ages. Wetness is a rumor this place has heard and declined to believe. If you brought water here, it would flash instantly to vapor, and the room would take the vapor into keeping and ration it back out across the following century — the first weather this place has ever had, spent one molecule at a time.

The makers left a few things. A torus of the finished matter, half-buried in the silt, which presents exactly the same profile from every angle — an object with only one face — and which is faintly less cold than everything around it, as though it has not yet finished keeping some warmth from a former age. In a niche, a lattice of black rods, and within the lattice a bead of violet light, midway on its journey between two nodes. It has been crossing that particular gap for about three hundred years. It will arrive within the next fifty, and when it does, the lattice will rearrange itself slightly and give off a chord — not a sound exactly, but an arrangement of the air, felt in the bones of the ear. It is computing something, or praying, or only idling. There is no one left who can read the difference. And in the far corner — the deep corner, where time lies deepest — a fixture that went out eleven thousand years ago still glows amber on the wall, faintly, because its last light has not finished arriving. The lamp is long dark. The corner has not finished being lit.

The visitors left less. A cairn of ordinary stones near the sill, every one of them foreign, carried up from somewhere below — and by the standards of this room the cairn is a firework of eventfulness, because honest stone erodes at an honest speed. One of its stones bears a scratched word in a script no one reads anymore, and the word is fading on schedule, which here seems almost obscene. A set of footprints crosses the dust to the cairn and does not return; the dust says they are the newest event in the room, and the dust also says they are eleven millennia old. And near the aperture, a short line of incisions hangs half a centimeter off the wall, written into the air where the air used to be wall-adjacent. It may be the name of the place. Whatever its makers called it, the room has kept the name, and given nothing back.

Because that is the law here. Retention. Whoever engineered this hollow was fighting entropy on behalf of something — and whatever that something was, it is gone, and the fight continues out of habit. Light is kept: the floor is a photograph still exposing, and the artifacts each carry two shadows — their dim present one, and a darker fossil shadow, cast by light from an older arrangement of the sky, which the floor has declined to release. Sound is kept: strike the wall and the note enters storage; it will be returned to the air, faint and intact, in about a century. The room is full of such held notes, and every so often one matures, and the silence produces, without ceremony, a single pure tone that no one living struck. Heat is kept. Distance is negotiable — you could cross the room in a minute, but the crossing takes slightly longer than a minute near the middle. Falling is a decision objects make slowly. Time is not uniform; it pools, like the cold, deepest in the far corner where the dead lamp still shines.

And there is the Seam.

It is a hairline that runs across the floor and up the wall, and it is the wound of the place — a crack not in the matter but in the room's rules. The eye declines it. It is not dark inside; it is not anything inside. The Seam is where keeping fails. Sounds that cross it never return. The dust will not settle near it. The fossil shadows stop at its edge, and behind it, across the chamber, runs a smooth swept band where the floor remembers nothing at all — a band of amnesia, the track of the Seam's slow migration over the ages, the only erased thing here. The cold is draining through it, ring by ring. The footprints from the sill approach its old track and simply cease. It is the only place in the room where anything has ever ended.

But even the wound is old. Even the wound has weathered. Its edges are worn smooth, like a threshold, and the room has come into equilibrium with its leak — the rate of loss and the engine of retention balanced against each other like a level sea. Nothing here is ruined. Everything is established.

The dynamics are patient and few. Once every four thousand years, more or less — the lattice measures it — the geometry of the room relaxes, and the place breathes: distances loosen, the dunes lift into a slow exhalation of dust that takes decades to resettle, the surface of the cold-pool stirs, the fossil shadows drift by a degree. The room is at the long end of such an exhalation now. The next breath is due soon, where soon means within your grandchildren's lifetimes. In fifty years the bead of violet light will reach its node, and the chord will come, and there will be no ears for it, only bones of dust.

There is no life here. There never was. But the room has habits, which is what a place has instead of life when it has decided not to be inert: the standing spiral of ordered grains at the cold-pool's shore, maintained by an electrostatic gradient, which — if disturbed — will spend the next century quietly rewriting itself; the migrating dunes; the held notes; the slow tide of the cold. A food chain of patience runs through it all: the spire's decay feeds the violet glow, the glow feeds a faint thermal gradient, the gradient moves the dust, the dust writes the patterns, and the patterns persist. Persistence is the ecology. Nothing here metabolizes but time.

If you came, you would leave things. Your footprints would be the newest event and already ancient. Your voice would go into storage, to be returned later to no one. Your warmth would be parcelled out across the next ten thousand years, spent one degree at a time into the cold's slow tide. The room permits leaving — it simply declines to let leaving be an ending.

It keeps the dim. It keeps the cold. It keeps the unfinished light of a dead lamp and the name it will not say and the note no one struck. It will keep you. That was the whole design, once. The reason is gone, and the keeping continues, and the keeping, by now, is the reason.
