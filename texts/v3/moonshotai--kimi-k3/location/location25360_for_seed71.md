Come up out of the fog in the hour before dawn, when the climb finally levels and the air thins to a blade, and the Plateau receives you without surprise. It has been expecting you the way a mountain expects weather: eventually, and without opinion.

The first thing is the cold — but not cold as you know it. This cold is finished. It does not bite or snatch; it abides. It settled into these stones so long ago that the stones no longer resist it, and it settled into the light, which arrives each morning normal as bread — an ordinary sun, honest shadows, a sky going about its sky-work — and the ordinariness of the light is, here, the strange part. You will understand why later.

The second thing is the green.

You would not expect it, this high, this cold. But the Plateau is lush the way a library is loud with books: quietly, slowly, and completely. Moss in banks and drifts so old they have strata, black-green on top, deepening to a brown that is almost bronze at the bottom, four meters thick in the sheltered swales. You can sink your arm into it to the shoulder. It is faintly warm inside. Cushion-domes the size of houses hump across the slopes — each one a single plant, hard as masonry, bright as hope, ten thousand years old and still adding its fingernail's width of growth each decade. You can walk across them. They do not mind. Minding is a fast emotion and nothing here is fast.

And then, scattered across the vastness with enormous distances between, the stands: groves of black-barked trees, each grove perhaps forty trunks, each grove a single individual. One citizen, repeated forty times. The census of the Plateau is short and very old.

There are no flowers. There never were. Nothing here ever needed to attract anything — no bright petal, no fruit, no scent, no nectar-bribe — because there is nothing to attract. The sky has never had a voice. No wing has ever crossed it. No hoof, no tooth, no burrow; there are no paths except the ones the builders made, no gnaw-marks, no dung, no bones. Nothing in this world has ever been eaten, and nothing here is afraid, and the absence of fear is itself, at first, a little frightening. The only colors that flare are the lichens — sulfur and rust and verdigris splashed across the boulders like the palette of a painter who worked only in minerals — because lichen colors are chemistry, not courtship, and chemistry is allowed.

The vastness of it needs saying plainly: the Plateau is the size of a world, and for all anyone knows it *is* the world. Below, in every direction, lies the fog sea, white and slow, out of which you climbed. Whether there is a base down there, a lowland, an ocean, a bottom of any kind — no one has returned to say. All downhill roads are loops. Walk one long enough and you will see your own city ahead of you, weathered by the years you spent walking. This is not one of the breakdowns. This is one of the settled laws. The breakdown is that lately, some of the uphill roads loop too.

Water here is damp rather than wet: it comes as fog, as rime, as condensation combed out of the air. True rain is rare and brief. But there are tarns — thousands of them, black and perfectly still, cupped in hollows worn smooth in the oldest stone you will ever touch. The builders called them *finished water*. They are so still that they do not reflect the world as it is. They reflect it as it will have settled. Look into the tarn above the city and you will see the towers leaning inward, meeting, becoming a single dome — the city's final form, patiently remembered by the water. Water here remembers forward. Do not look too long.

—

The city is called Vess, though the Slow Ones will tell you, if you have a season to spare, that *Vess* is only the first syllable. The full name was spoken once, at the founding, and is still arriving — sound moves slowly on the Plateau and nothing spoken is ever lost, only in transit. A sentence uttered in the city's youth may still be crossing a plaza somewhere, one syllable per century. There are courts built specifically for this: the Syllable Courts, amphitheaters shaped like storage jars for sound, where the acoustics catch and hold what is passing through. Stand in the right one and you may hear the next syllable of a sentence that began before your language existed.

The architecture of Vess is the architecture of a thirsty place: the whole city is a still for the sky. Tall thin towers — the fog-combs — stand with grooved faces turned to the prevailing mist, and the fog condenses on the stone and runs down the grooves into channels, and the channels run to cisterns where the water lies black and finished. The city drinks the air. Everything is dry-stone, fitted with a precision that has, over the ages, stopped being craftsmanship and become geology: walls whose blocks have quite literally grown together, strata of construction layered so deep that the oldest foundations have gone feral into rock. Bronze exists, but sparingly — a chisel, a gate-pivot, a bell. Rope is moss-fiber. Fuel is peat cut from the deep moss banks, and it burns slow and smoky and smells green. There are storehouses stacked with finished pots and coiled rope, markets that are full and have never been busy. Goods here await their final owners. They are patient.

The city still operates. The combs condense, the cisterns fill, stone gates swing on pebble bearings, flues draw. The Slow Ones tend it — you will see them in the courts, lichened to the elbows, grey-green, utterly unhurried. Whether the Slow Ones count as animals is a question the Plateau itself would find young and hasty. One of them has been raising an arm toward a comb-vane for what you would call a decade. If you sit with one for a full season, it may begin, perhaps, to turn toward you. By the time it faces you, you will have decided whether you are staying.

But mostly the city runs on habit. The city has a habit of itself, and habit here is stronger than maintenance.

—

You will want to know about the builders, and the answer is the heart of the place. They were the world's only animals. The Plateau never made another kind — no prey, no predator, no bird, no worm — only them, once, and they built Vess and its far sister Heth (a year away at noon, a week away at dusk; distance on the Plateau is a mood, and the builders' maps are not wrong so much as hopeful), and then they did what everything here does.

They settled.

Understand the law of this world: everything is drawn toward its final form. This is the Settling, and it is stronger than gravity. Water runs toward stillness, slopes toward their last angle, sounds toward silence. Nothing rots — rot is a young world's haste. Here, things do not decay; they *graduate*. Fallen wood does not molder; it mineralizes, quietly joins the geology, becomes a stone that remembers grain. The forest floor is not a floor but an archive. And the builders, when their work was done, settled too — some into their city, into the straightness of a wall they loved, and some into the land itself.

On the far horizon, and sometimes much closer than the horizon, the ground rises into smooth domes the size of hills, in herds and congregations, each one worn round by a patience older than erosion. The Slow Ones call them the Sleepers. Each dome is someone. Final forms are not bound by original sizes. Once an age, the ground turns over in its sleep, and a slope reshapes itself, and a new tarn is born where a shoulder pressed down, and the Slow Ones note it in whatever ledger they keep across the centuries, and it is not a disaster. It is a dream.

—

The quiet needs its own mention, because it is the medium of everything. Quiet is the settled state of air. The wind here moves without a voice — there are no wires, no loose things, nothing left to whistle; everything has been stilled or stowed by ten thousand years of tidiness. The loudest thing most days is the ticking of rime forming on the comb-stones, or your own steps, which will sound immodest to you within a week.

But there is a season — a fortnight at the thaw — that the Slow Ones call the Audible. Winter locks sound into the ice and the stone, and the thaw lets it out: bell-notes struck a thousand years ago land soft as snow on the plazas; chisel-taps arrive from a quarry that has been finished for ages; the combs drip, which counts here as percussion; a sentence in the Syllable Court advances by one syllable, and the Slow Ones, who have been waiting for it since autumn, incline their heads. The Audible overlaps with the Greening, when the moss and the domes and the stands take a century's growth in a few short weeks and the whole Plateau hums with almosts. Then the fog tide rises out of the sea below, the world becomes an archipelago of white silence and dark stone, and the quiet closes over everything again like water over a stone.

—

And now the heresy, because you will notice it eventually and it is better to be told.

The Settling finished. The world reached its end-state — every slope at rest, every stone in its final place, the geology retired, the sentence nearly spoken. And then, quietly, so quietly that the first sign was simply a stone that was *warm* in the cold, the finishing began to come undone.

They call it the Unsettling, and it is everywhere once you learn to see it. A wall un-cracking — the fissure closing, the blocks going sharp-edged and young. A doorway that has been solid wall for six thousand years waking up one foggy morning and remembering it was a doorway. Young rock, fresh-faced, in the middle of the oldest pavement on the world. Rain falling upward near the young places. Shadows pointing at where the sun was yesterday. A mason's chisel, left mid-stroke on a half-dressed block when its owner settled, found to be *more patina than bronze and still sharp* — as though some tools refuse their rest because their final form is use. The towers of Vess, which spent eons leaning inward toward their dome, leaning now — by a hair, by a syllable — back *out*.

The tarns, which remember forward, have begun showing the city with its combs crowned in green fire, showing streets that do not exist yet, showing — some of the Slow Ones say — *people* in them, though no one will say this loudly, because sound is conserved, and a thing said is a thing in transit forever.

The world was a sentence spoken so slowly that it hardened into a place. And the sentence has only just noticed it is not finished.

—

Stay for a dusk, at least. The light, honest all day, goes long and level across the moss, and the cushion-domes glow like coals of green, and the fog tide rises below the comb-towers until the city is an island in a white sea, and the Sleepers on the horizon take the sun on their flanks like a herd at rest — which they are, which they are. A bell-note lands somewhere, faint as frost, struck before your grandmother's grandmother.

There is a bench in the ninth court, worn smooth, facing the combs. It has been waiting a long time, and it is not particular. The Plateau is gentle with the unfinished; it will not hurry you. It has never hurried anything. It will simply wait beside you, patient as weather, until you choose your final form — and it will love, in its slow stone way, whatever you choose.

Sit a while. The fog is rising. The next syllable is due any century now.
