The bowl hangs high on the ridge, just below the cloud line, and you reach it by a gully that runs wet even in the dry season. The first thing you notice is the warmth. It comes up the gully to meet you — a breath, humid and body-soft, out of place at this altitude, where the wind off the ridge has teeth. The second thing you notice is the silence. It arrives with the warmth, as if the two were carried down together. You stand at the lip and look in, and the whole of the place is visible at once: an amphitheater of pale stone a few hundred paces across, closed on every side except the notch where its overflow slips away. A room, not a wilderness. You could walk its perimeter before the light changed.

The stone is travertine, and it is old. The bowl was once a cavern, or the throat of one, and the roof fell so long ago that nothing of the collapse remains readable — the rubble has been swallowed, rounded, built over by the slow masonry of the springs. Warm water rises somewhere beneath the floor and sheets down in terraces, each pool held behind a rimstone lip smooth as a turned bowl's edge, each lip spilling to the next in a film too even to make sound. The water degasses as it travels and lays down stone behind itself, a hair's thickness a year, so the whole amphitheater is continuously repaving itself in cream and ochre. Where the pools run hottest, the mineral skin is naked and slick. Where they cool, microbial mats paint the shallows in bands — rust at the warm end, then jade, then a pale green like milk-glass — a thermometer rendered in living color. The walls of the rim weep. Condensation gathers on the cool high rock where the bowl's breath meets mountain air, and it falls back in drips, unhurried, each one arriving a half-second late in the echo, so you hear every drop twice: once falling, once remembered.

Nothing here is sharp. That is the first law of the place, and it makes the second thing stranger when you find it.

The trees stand far apart. Perhaps twenty of them in the whole bowl, each alone on its own apron of terrace, each with open water or bare wet stone between it and its neighbor — spacing so deliberate-looking that you check, again and again, for a gardener. They are broad-leafed and low-branched and opulent, each one a small climate of shade and drip, leaves wide as a chest and soft as cloth. Lushness here lives in the individuals, not in the crowd. Between them: mirror-pools, fern-clumps fountaining from the weep-walls, moss holding the shaded seep-zones in deep green pile, and long reaches of glistening empty terrace where nothing grows because the water is too warm or too mineral or too newly stone.

Come close to one of the trees and you find the stone. Every tree has one — a core of dark chert gripped in the root-plate, held as a rootball would hold a boulder, except that the fit is too intimate. The roots grew around it from germination. The seed, you realize, only ever takes where it lands on flint; the stone and the tree begin together, and the tree spends its life working it. Over seasons a root thickens against the nodule's edge and bears down, and the pressure finds a fracture plane, and once in a year, once in a decade, a flake lets go with a snap that carries across the whole bowl like a branch breaking in frost. The flake that comes off is clean, conchoidal, with the swelling bulb that toolmakers of another kind leave behind — except these bulbs are of pressure, not percussion, the slowest pressure-flaking in the world, each blow lasting three winters.

The blades go into service. Wedged in root-forks, drawn by the tree's own slow flexing across the fresh tufa crust that keeps trying to seal its spring vent, they saw the mineral skin open and keep the warm water flowing to the roots. Some trees cut runnels too — shallow grooves in the soft young stone, leading spill-water to the basins where their seeds drift and wait. Read the bowl's plumbing closely and you see that none of it is quite natural. Every channel has been edited. Every vent is maintained. What the water builds, the roots cut back; what the roots abandon, the water seals. The balance between deposition and cutting is the place's metabolism, and it has been running so long that the terraces record it like annals — you can count the negotiations in a cross-section of rimstone, the way you'd count rings.

Around each root-plate lies a midden of flakes and worn-down blades, centuries deep, and these are the only artifacts the place has ever produced. No walls. No shelters. Nothing stacked or tied or carried. A technology that consists entirely of edges. And at the base of the oldest trees you find stranger things: chert cores worn into forms no single tree could have made — worked, abandoned at a death, gripped again by a new seedling generations later, worked further, until the stone has passed through fifty tenants and carries fifty lifetimes of patient revision in its shape. They look like hand-axes. Nothing with a hand has ever touched them. This is the moment, standing over a knapping-floor with no knapper, when the place stops feeling like a garden and starts feeling like a question.

And underneath all of it, the absence. There are no animals here — none, not a midge over the pools, not a beetle under the moss, nothing that creeps or flies or sings. A warm, wet, lush place in full light should roar with small life; this one holds its peace, and the lushness is of a kind you have never seen before, because it is unbitten. Every leaf is entire. Every margin smooth, every fern unbrowsed to the ground, every seedling unstepped-upon. Nothing is pollinated except by wind and water; the runnels do the planting, floating seeds along the flow-lines, which is why the trees' children gather at the edges of their parents' channels like students at a demonstration. Nothing is eaten. When a leaf falls it falls straight down in the still air, and if it lands on water it floats until the mineral film takes it — and then the springs do what the springs do: they stone it. The terraces are littered with petrified leaves, perfect in every vein, the place's habit of keeping its own records. The trees archive themselves in the rock they argue with.

Sound, when it comes, is small and slow. The drip of condensation off leaf-tips. The occasional deep glug as a vent breathes and resettles. The tick of a stone leaf sliding from a rim. Far above, beyond the rim walls, you can sometimes hear wind crossing the ridge while the bowl's air lies motionless — weather audible through a ceiling, the way you'd hear rain from inside a house. And rarely, very rarely, the snap of a flake releasing somewhere across the terraces, and the soft clatter as it comes to rest among its ancestors, and then silence again, wider for having been broken.

The place runs on three clocks. The fast one is the mist: it fills the bowl at dawn, glowing and opaque, thins through the morning until the pools begin returning the sky, and gathers again toward evening — a single breath, taken daily. The middle clock is the work: the thickening root, the seasonal pressure, the decade between one snap and the next, the generations between one blade and the form it is slowly becoming. The slow clock is the stone itself, paving and sealing at a hair a year, building the amphitheater outward by less than a fingernail's width in a lifetime. The three clocks agree with one another. That is what established means here — not finished, never finished, but settled into a state that can continue: the springs laying down, the trees cutting back, the water planting what the water needs planted, the flakes accumulating, the leaves turning to stone in the pools.

Stand finally at the notch where the overflow leaves — the one place the bowl opens to the world — and look back. Warm mist lifting off stepped water. Twenty solitary trees, each bent over its stone like a worker over a bench. No tracks in anything, because there is almost no mud, and nothing to make them. The excess water slides past you through the slot and drops away as a ribbon into the haze below, going to join whatever the rest of the world is doing. Behind you, in the warm still room of it, a root leans its weight against a flint edge, and leans, and leans, and will not be hurried.
