Forty meters beneath a young rhyolite dome there is a room the mountain made by accident and has been adjusting ever since. It is small — seven strides long, four across, three high at the crown — a gas blister inflated in the last centuries of the flow's cooling, then cracked open to groundwater by the ordinary settling of hot rock. By the count of its own layered record, it has been running for about thirty years. Nothing has ever stood inside it. Nothing could, for long. The air is seventy degrees and saturated; the walls range from skin-warm to kettle-hot; the floor, near the collapse pit at the chamber's east end, shimmers. But the first thing a visitor would report — the thing every instrument reports — is the light. The chamber is bright. No cave should be, and this one is, for reasons that can be measured even where they cannot yet be modeled.

The rock is obsidian and flow-banded rhyolite, and it is *new* in a way caves almost never are. The walls wear veneers of black volcanic glass, conchoidally fractured like struck flint, edges sharp enough to part a hair. The ceiling is scalloped in clean, unweathered lobes. There is no sediment, no rubble, no rounded anything. When the chamber flexes — and it flexes constantly — the glass ticks, softly, like a cooling engine block. The whole room has the feel of something that has just been unpacked.

Water arrives as seep from the aquifer above, threading down through cooling fractures too fine to see, and it leaves as steam, and it comes back as rain. This is the chamber's breath, and it takes about six minutes. On the inhale, pressure falls, the microfractures sip, the steam-rich air touches ceiling rock that has briefly cooled below its dew point, and a warm rain falls — a few hundred drops ticking on glass and on the pool, over in seconds. On the exhale, valves deep in the floor open, pressure climbs, and the pool — two meters across, black, skinned with a sheen of colloidal silica — comes to a working boil. Slower tides ride over the fast breath; roughly hourly, some deep throttle shifts, with a thud that a listener would feel in the feet more than hear. The room is, in the strictest engineering sense, a heat pipe the size of a chapel: water down as liquid, heat up as vapor, condensate returned by the wick of the fractures. A closed loop, sealed in stone, running on the difference between the aquifer and the still-cooling mountain.

The heat is held. That is the first thing that reads as engineering. Across seasons, across the dome's own fluctuations, the chamber's working temperature stays within a narrow band, because the vents throttle themselves: amorphous silica deposits where flow runs fast, narrowing the throat, and dissolves where flow runs slow, opening it. A thermostat with no sensor, no actuator, nothing but solution chemistry doing negative feedback. Cut the wall — a research micro-drill did, once — and the wound weeps silica gel and closes in days.

The light comes from four sources, all of them byproducts. Steadiest is the collapse pit: twelve meters down its grating of fresh cracks, rock glows furnace-orange, and the chamber is underlit like a stage, shadows thrown *upward* onto the ceiling — one of the rarest light-geometries a landscape can have, and here it is simply Tuesday. Second is the pool. On each exhale, the pressure drop drives cavitation through the boiling water, and the collapsing bubbles flash — sonoluminescence, light from sound in water, a phenomenon whose mechanism physics is still arguing about. Individually the flashes are nothing; across a frothing two-meter surface they merge into a blue-white sheen bright enough, at mid-exhale, to read newsprint by. Third, the sphalerite veins: zinc sulfide is an electroluminescent phosphor, and where the chamber's currents run through it, the seams smolder amber, brightening and fading with the breath. Fourth, and rarest, the quartz. Three seams of hydrothermal quartz have grown along the principal stress fractures, and under cyclic load they accumulate piezoelectric charge until it releases — blue sparks walking the seam with a snap, and with the snap, a note.

The note deserves its own paragraph. It rings near 2,300 hertz, decays over about twenty seconds, and is stable to within a fraction of a hertz across years. It is purer than any natural source has a right to be. It propagates through the dome and registers on seismometers kilometers away, which is how the chamber was found: not seen, not drilled for, but *heard* — a tone in geothermal survey data that should not exist, holding its pitch like a struck glass that refuses to die. After a discharge, for a minute or two, the dripping from the ceiling falls in time with the decaying note, entrained, and then wanders back to its own rhythm as the ringing fades.

Between the quartz and the pool margins run veins of pyrite and chalcopyrite — semiconducting sulfides — and where they meet they form junctions that rectify. The seams charge; the junctions gate; the sphalerite glows; the sparks ground. Power, regulation, lighting, memory (every breath is logged in banded silica couplets around the splash zone; count the layers, get the age), self-repair. Nothing here violates anything. Each component has a textbook. What does not yet have a textbook is the integration — a heat engine that grew, that tuned its own wick geometry as it deposited it, that holds tolerances current fabrication would strain at, lit by a phenomenon whose mechanism remains unresolved. It is not strange. It is merely ahead.

Life is present the way punctuation is present. A hand-sized patch of rust-orange at the pool's south rim: iron-oxidizing bacteria, twisted mineral stalks accreting micron by micron. A white veil of sulfur oxidizers draped near the warmest vent, thin as tissue. Perhaps one other film elsewhere, perhaps not. Between them, meters of bare black glass. And the isopods — a groundwater species, washed in as eggs through the fractures, eyeless, white, five millimeters long, grazing the films. A few hundred individuals constitute the entire animal population of the world. You could watch the pool margins for an hour and see two. Nothing green has ever grown here; no photon of sunlight has ever entered; the films scrub what little oxygen seeps down, so the air is steam and carbon dioxide and a clean mineral warmth, the sulfur almost entirely consumed by the rust patch. The place smells, one imagines, like a hot radiator in a stone room.

The soundscape is mostly the absence of sound. For minutes at a stretch the loudest event is the tick of contracting glass. The inhale ends in its brief patter of warm rain; the exhale brings the pool's low fizz; hourly, the deep thud; now and then, the snap and the long pure note. A hush with a pulse.

The rules of the room are simple. It never goes dark — light is a byproduct of work, and it is always working. It keeps its temperature. It heals its cuts. It remembers every breath in stone. It keeps time, and broadcasts the time, whether or not anything listens. Nothing with eyes lives in it. And it cannot be found by looking: from the surface there is a young dome, a wet meadow of rust-stained seeps, and nothing else.

The emptiness is the design. From any vantage the presences can be counted on one hand — pool, pit, three seams, two films, an isopod if you are patient — and everything else is bare glass holding a sheen of water and the reflections of the lights, the blue sheen of the pool moving across the walls, the amber of the veins doubled in black mirrors. Because the air is saturated there is no dust, and because there is no dust the light has no beams; it does not fall, it gathers, sourced from water and stone, sourceless-seeming, clean.

And the room is one cell in a foam. The dome is riddled with blisters like it — most drowned and dark, some dry, a few drowned and lit, this one the brightest. They are all young. They are all, geologically, temporary. In some century the silica will win its slow argument with the vents; the breath will shallow and stop; the note will fail one day between one hour and the next, and the seismometers kilometers away will register only its absence, which will be the chamber's entire obituary. Somewhere else in the cooling mountain, another crack will reach the water table, another blister will sip and flash and begin to glow, and the process — normal in every part, not yet understood as a whole — will continue to run on rain and a warm mountain, in the dark, brightly.
