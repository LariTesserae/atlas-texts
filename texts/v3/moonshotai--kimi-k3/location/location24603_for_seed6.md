The first rule of the place is that you climb to go under.

The ridge is a bare hour's walk above the new shoreline — a young coast, still raw, the sea having arrived recently enough that its cliffs are sharp and its beaches unmixed — and the heat up here is a held, humid weight, the kind that makes metal tick. The tower stands at the ridge's highest point on four braced legs and a concrete plinth, and everything about it is new. Welds still carry their color. The paint — a pale industrial green, chosen perhaps as a courtesy to its contents — is unchalked, unfaded. Bolt heads shine with zinc. On the landward face, stenciled at eye height in the plain serial typeface of an uncompromising engineering office:

**HEADER TANK № 7 — VEGETATED**
**DO NOT DRAIN**

Farther along the ridge, at intervals too regular to be accidental, stand others — № 6, № 5, further ones dissolving into haze — each with its crown of glass catching the same flat afternoon sun. From below, № 7's crown looks wrong for a water tank: not a lid but a lantern, paned and mullioned, and behind the panes something dark presses outward, leaf-shaped, leaf upon leaf, flattening itself against the glass like a thought against a skull.

A stair rises inside one leg, riveted, ringing faintly underfoot — the loudest thing you will make all day. It ends at a hatch, and the hatch opens upward into the air gap: a shallow humid room between the water's surface and the glazing. The smell arrives first — warm metal, wet paint, the faint iodine of nutrient salts, and under it all a green exhalation, vegetable and dense, like the inside of a stem. The water lies at your feet, bath-warm, still, its meniscus touching a particular weld seam all the way around the drum, touching it exactly, as if soldered there. Above, the glazing sweats. Along the lower edge of each pane runs a condensation gutter with drip-noses spaced like teeth, and from them, at long intervals, falls the place's one percussion: a drop, a small flat tap on the water, rings going out slow and wide. *Tick.* A long silence in which you hear your own pulse. *Tick.*

Then you go down, and the green takes you.

It is not murk. It is light — normal daylight, unmiraculous, arriving through the crown and being passed hand to hand downward through ten thousand leaves, each one taking its toll of brightness and paying the color forward, until at mid-depth you hang in something the color of bottle glass held to the sun. The warmth is even, body-like, so that the boundary of your skin becomes a rumor. And there is nowhere, nowhere at all, to put a hand that does not close on either steel or leaf. This is the density of it: the tank is not a container with things in it. It is a single filled volume. The water is not the medium; the water is the interstitial fluid of a body, and the body is packed to its own skin.

Trunks rise from the floor in braided columns, gray-green, smooth-skinned, lenticeled, wound around the tank's structural stanchions so thoroughly that stanchion and stem can no longer be assigned separate loads. Between them, stems; between stems, vines of the same stock; between vines, the perforated planting racks bolted to every wall, and in the racks not soil — there is no soil anywhere in № 7, nothing that was ever ground — but manufactured substrate: slag wool, expanded clay granules, coco-fiber felt, all of it shot through with white root. The submerged leaves are thin, translucent, fern-cut; the leaves up in the air gap are waxy and entire. Same plant. All of it the same plant, in fact — a single mother-clone, according to the small riveted tag on the first rack, an unregistered cultivar, propagated into ten thousand bodies that fill the tank the way tissue fills an organ, without competition, without gap, every shoot an instruction executed.

And here the strangeness begins in earnest, because the plumbing does not serve the plant. The plumbing *enters* it. Copper lines rise from the floor manifold and pass into the trunks through flanged couplings bedded in grafting wax, and the bark has grown over the flanges in smooth collars, so that pipe and tree are continuous, one circulation, and it is genuinely unclear where the machine's water ends and the plant's sap begins. Gauges are teed directly into the xylem lines, enamel-faced, reading in bar. A valve wheel stands proud of a trunk in a collar of living wood that has grown precisely around the wheel's travel, the bark polished where the rim passes — the wheel can still be turned; the tree has allowed for it. Somewhere below, a dosing pump meters nutrient into what the drawings, presumably, call the heartwood manifold. The condensers under the glazing return what they collect not to a sump but to the roots. The building's water cycle runs partly inside a body. Whether the tank was built around a seed, or the stock threaded afterward through prepared orifices, the construction records do not say. There is a commissioning plaque on the hatch frame, and it gives a serial, a date — recent, very recent — and no name at all.

Down at the floor, set into the plate, there is a porthole. You put your face to it and look through a meter of warm water into a lit, dry room: the machinery level, dials glowing their dull industrial amber, a flywheel turning with monumental slowness, cable runs neatly cleated, everything humming at a frequency too low for hearing. You do not hear the pump from inside the tank. You *feel* it, a soft double pulse through the water and the steel, against your sternum, unhurried as sleep. Electrified, mechanized, scaled — and disciplined to the edge of silence. The whole tower is quiet the way instruments are quiet: not empty of sound, but restrained. The tick of sun-expanded plate. The drip-noses. Your own blood.

Nothing else moves. No fish disturbs the shafts of light; no snail draws its line across any pane; the submerged leaves carry no larvae, no egg-clutches, nothing. The water is too new, perhaps, or too warm, or too dosed — or the omission is deliberate, a specification. The log hung beside the hatch, its pencil still bright on the page, has a line in the commissioning checklist that reads, in its entirety: *Fauna: none intended.* None intended, and none present — with one exception that is also an entombment. Under the gasket of an inspection port, sealed there at assembly, is a small moth, pressed flat and perfect between rubber and steel, the single animal in № 7, an accidental specimen, a visitor from the world outside who arrived at exactly the wrong moment of construction and will outlast, in its way, everything living around it.

Now. Stand in the air gap again and read the wall above the waterline. There are the stencils: a vertical column of years, each with its own painted line, rising in slow increments toward the glass. A tide table for a sea that fits in a room. Because here is the deepest rule of the place, the one that organizes all the others: no pipe enters № 7 from outside. No feed line climbs the legs; the buried conduits carry power only, and data perhaps, nothing wet. Whatever this tower holds, it makes — breathes in the humid ridge wind through its louvers at night, condenses it on the cold north panes, drips it down, polishes it through the green body of the tenant, and keeps it. The water rises. One hand-width a season, by the schedule. The meniscus that touches its weld seam today touched, five years ago, a lower seam, and the plant has climbed with it, tier by tier, taking each new volume the instant it is offered, occupying everything, so that the tank's history is written in a gradient you can read with a hand: older wood below, where trunks are thickening around their pipes at a millimeter a year; newer, thinner, more hopeful growth above; and the air gap, the plant's only future, shrinking at the rate the years are painted.

The water is ahead of schedule. Two stencils, maybe three. The log does not comment.

What happens when the sea inside reaches the glass is not recorded anywhere you can find. Presumably the stock transitions — gives up the air, becomes entirely the thin-leaved, translucent thing it already is below — and № 7 becomes a solid green lantern on the ridge, full to the mullions, and the drips, which are the place's whole music, will have no distance left to fall. Already they are shorter than the ones painted in the schedule's margins, the fall closing up centimeter by centimeter across the decades, the one sound being slowly, patiently, extinguished by the place's own success. You are listening, you realize, to something being finished. An instrument completing itself. A breath held for sixty years and most of the way in.

Outside, the young sea below the ridge is doing the same thing at its own scale — rising, finding its level, patient. Perhaps that is the program. Perhaps the towers are practice, or prophecy, or simply a style of preparing.

Evening comes on without event. The light through the crown goes from white to honey; the pressed leaves overhead become a silhouette like a brain in a jar; the machine pulse under the floor neither quickens nor rests. Along the ridge, one by one, the other glass crowns catch the low sun and hold it — № 6, № 5, № 4, a line of green lamps spaced across the haze, each one filling, each one quiet, each one exactly on its own slow way to full.
