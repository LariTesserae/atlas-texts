The salt pan comes first, because everything here comes second to it: a basin floor the size of a small sea, white as a bleached word, lying at the lowest point of a low country. No water has touched it in longer than salt can remember. The heat is a hand laid flat on everything. For a day's walk in any direction there is nothing — and then, at the pan's exact center, a chimney: knee-high, coil-built from clay, its rim smoothed by centuries of warm breath. It exhales. Put your ear to it and the exhalation has a shape. A chord, low and many-noted, rising out of the ground like heat itself given pitch.

The rungs going down are fired clay set into the shaft wall, and the air warms as you descend, which is the wrong way around and the first of this place's quiet corrections. The shaft opens at last into the crown of a bell.

The whole city fits inside the bell — one cavern, a town's width across, terraced down the inner curve in rings: streets, plazas, dwellings, towers, all of it lit like a chapel at dusk. The light comes in blades. Light-wells, lined with mica, drop shafts of dusty gold from the pan above, and they move — slowly, over hours, the way a hand moves in sleep. Between the blades the city is lit by its own fires: gas-jets in ceramic nozzles, burning low and amber at every crossroads, fed from below. The air is dry past dryness and smells of hot dust, of tar, of something almond-bitter and metallic — the breath of the garden, which is everywhere and spaced as wide as everything else.

Because this is the thing the eye takes longest to accept: the city is nearly empty. It was built for thousands, in districts, with infrastructure — flue-towers, glazed channels, kiln-hearts, reservoirs — and it holds almost nothing. A plaza the size of a field contains one tree, and the tree is an event. A long arcade contains one bench. A room contains one bowl. The distances are the design. Everything here was given room to resonate.

The builders worked in clay because clay was what the world provided, and they raised it to a fluency that looks like magic until you touch it. They had no water to mix it with — there is no water; there has never been water — so they made their clay workable with oil, kneaded it warm, built it up in coils and slabs, and fired it hard. Every wall, every stair, every pipe and roof-tile was wet once with oil and never with water. Their metal was a little native copper, cold-hammered, gone black in the sulfur air, reserved for bells and cutting edges. Their fiber was a hollow reed from the garden, twisted into rope bridges and woven into door-screens. Their high art was glaze: ash and mineral, iron reds and sulfur yellows, pooled and run like frozen light. The whole city is ember-colored. Where the glaze has been etched away — and it is being etched, always, slowly — the walls go matte and pale, like bread rubbed of its crust.

At the city's lowest point, in a basin of black tile, is the seep: a slow spring of oil, swelling up out of the deep heat. This is the well the city was built around, the reason and the altar. Over it grows the eldest tree — hollow-trunked, wide as a tower, its bark layered like old rope — drinking through roots that have fused with the tile into a single organ. When the wind crosses the hollows of its crown, the tree sounds the bass note under the city's chord. The first kiln, the oldest shards suggest, was built around the seep to burn its gas. The city is a kiln built around a spring, and the spring has never once been water.

It works like a still, the whole bell of it. By day the seep's volatile breath rises in the heat; by night it condenses on the great ribbed vault of the ceiling, where stone fins reach up toward the cold of the surface dark. The ribs gather the dew — a dew of oil, the only dew this world makes — and deliver it to gutter-spouts shaped as cupped hands. Thousands of ceramic hands, all offering. The gutters run down through the terraces in glazed channels to the drip-basins of the trees. Evaporation, condensation, distribution: a water cycle, rebuilt for a world with no water in it, and it runs unattended, and it has run unattended for a very long time.

The garden that drinks it is lush the way a banked fire is lush — generous, resinous, unhurried. The gourd-trees carry leaves like gray wax tongues, curled into troughs to catch the night-drip, and dry fruit full of loose seed that rattles when the chord swells. Their flowers are trumpets, and they open to heat, and they aim themselves at the flue-towers. Felted silver moss mats the drip-lines, licking dew from stone. The etch-lichen works the glazed walls into pale lace, grain by grain, century by century. Nothing here photosynthesizes much; the light is too dim for that to be the whole story. The roots draw on the heat gradient itself, the difference between the hot deep and the cooler air, and the sap that moves through these plants is a warm thin tar, and when a stem is cut it does not bleed — it shines.

Fire is the grazer here. It does what herds do elsewhere. Every district has a kiln-heart fed by its own seep-line, and once in a long while — decades, a century, no one is counting — a district's valves open and its passages flood with gas and it is lit, and the whole district fires like the kiln it always secretly was. The etched walls re-vitrify; the lichen lace burns away; the old growth is cleared in a slow orange rotation. And the garden has planned for this since before anyone was here to plan: gourds that crack only in firing heat, seeds that will not wake until they have been through the flame. The city consumes itself on a schedule too long to see, and the consumption is the maintenance. The lichen etches, the fire re-glazes, the pitch of the towers holds true. It has arrived at its final form and it holds there, the way a dune holds its shape in a steady wind — always moving, never changing.

The sound has a day to it. Morning, when the light-wells take the sun, the air in the shaft warms and rises, and the flue-towers — each one a stopped pipe of a different length, tuned at the founding — begin the chord low in the chest. The streets channel the breath; the doorways, trimmed with a knife-edge of fired clay, whistle like flutes; the gourds take up their rattling; and across the drum-plazas — floors vaulted thin over resonance pits — the fallen seeds skitter and roll, each step of a seed a small boom from below, so that the city plants its garden by playing it. Midday is the fullest hour: loud, organ-full, felt in the ribs, a sound that fills the enormous emptiness exactly. At night the breath reverses. The towers draw down and exhale through smaller vents, and the chord inverts, higher, thinner, a second instrument nested in the first. Under all of it, constant, the tick and ping of clay expanding and contracting — the percussion of the city thinking about its own temperature.

There are no animals. There have never been animals. Nothing here has ever flown, or burrowed, or been milked, or been afraid. No bone, no leather, no wool, no egg; those words have no referents. Nothing moves in the Underkiln that is not plant, fire, air, or stone — and it is loud, and it is alive, and it is inhabited, after a fashion: in every dwelling, centered in the main room, one bowl. They are glazed with ash. The builders' custom, legible in the stratigraphy of the oldest floors, was to fire the dead in the kiln-hearts and glaze a bowl with what remained, and set the bowl in the emptied room, and leave the door open. The population of the city is pottery. A district of bowls, a plaza of trees, a chord of towers — each presence spaced wide, each given its full resonance. In the highest terrace, in the last and smallest room, there is one bowl of unfired clay, slumped where it dried, because the last of the builders had no one left to fire her.

Rain is not absent here; it is inconceivable, a shape the world has never taken. The rules are otherwise. Oil does the work of water. Fire does the work of beasts. Sound does the work of hands — it sows, it tends, it harvests, it remembers. In the dust season the light-wells pour slow waterfalls of sand that hiss down into designed gutters and are carried to the gardens as mulch, and the city sweeps itself. The seep has a tide with a period no one measured, and on some schedule of its own the bass note of the mother tree deepens for a season and then returns. Everything cycles: the breath by day, the dew by night, the fire by century, the seep by some longer patience.

From the surface, on the white pan, none of this exists. There is only the chimney, knee-high, warm-rimmed, humming its single sustained note into the enormous sky — a city singing to no one, which is not the same as singing to nothing. It is complete. Nothing is missing. The kiln holds its temperature; the garden drinks its dew; the chord holds its pitch. You could stand in the empty plaza with your hand on the warm drum of the floor and feel the whole hidden town breathing beneath you, settled into itself like a coal into its own glow, and the loudness of it would not be a cry for company. It would be the sound a finished thing makes.
