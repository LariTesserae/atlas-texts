You feel it before you hear it, and you hear it long before you see it. Two passes down, while the trail is still switching back through pale scree and the peaks ahead are bare as knuckles, the soles of your boots begin to buzz on the stone. Then the long bones of your legs pick it up. By the time the sound reaches your ears — a chord, no, a quarrel of chords, folded over itself — you have already been listening with your whole skeleton for an hour. At this altitude the air is too thin to carry what this place makes. The mountain carries the rest.

The guidebooks, the two that mention it, call it the Kettle. It is a cirque, a hanging bowl near the top of the range, maybe five hundred meters rim to rim, walled in gray-white rock that has been worn soft-shouldered by something other than wind. From the rim you can take in the whole of it at once, the way you take in a room. That is the strange thing: it reads as a room. A cathedral-sized, roofless room, floored with salt, lit by ordinary hard noon, and almost entirely empty.

The emptiness is real. Stand on the rim and count the presences. Three tolltrees, vast and solitary, spaced across the floor like thinkers who have drifted apart. One herd of bell-oxen, seven animals, a gray drift in the middle distance. A few kites hanging in the glare. One small ruin against the far wall. Everything else — and it is nearly everything — is bare pan, a floor of ancient evaporite left by a sea that did not retreat so much as get taken. Nothing here has ever been wet, not in the memory of the mountain. The place in the cycle where rain should be is occupied by voice.

The salt floor is not smooth. It is written on. The dust — flour-fine, white, ground from reed-glass and old mineral — gathers in ridges and figures that shift as you watch: hexagons loosening into concentric rings, rings breaking into long herringbone, the whole pan slowly redrawing its geometry like a Chladni plate, because that is what it is. The dust migrates to the quiet lines, the nodes where the bowl's standing waves cancel, and so the floor maps its own sound for anyone with eyes. The deaf cross this place more safely than the hearing. The map lasts until the pattern drifts, and the pattern always drifts.

Because the sound here has weather. The interference of all the voices — the deep fundamental humming out of the spires on the inner rim, the organ-chord lowing of the herd, the knife-cries of the kites, the vast underground chorus of the chorister colonies, and beneath everything the thin hiss the bowl itself sieves out of the sky — all of it collides and cancels and doubles in slow, chaotic motion. Antinodes wander the floor like weather systems, and where one passes, the salt lifts in plumes, the air shimmers cold, and anything brittle shatters. Where the pressure stands still for an hour, the dust rises into slow cairns and fluted columns, cathedrals the size of a person, of a house, holding their shapes exactly as long as the pattern holds and slumping when it moves on. The bowl is full of architecture that does not know it is temporary. Above ground: near-void, a bare white room. Below ground: anthill industry, the choristers singing in their millions through the soil, milling the dead to flour, their choruses traveling across the pan as visible lines of dancing dust.

And it is cold. Not alpine-bitter, but a dry, mineral chill that the sunlit salt holds like a grudge, and it follows a rule you learn fast: the loudest places are the coldest. The meadow eats the sound's heat. You can find the feeding grounds by the cold coming off them, shade with nothing casting it.

The meadow. That is where the lushness went. It grows in drifts and banks across the pan — organ-pipe reeds of glassy mineral flesh, hollow, tuned, colored like verdigris and malachite and opal, dense as any canebrake where it stands. The reeds drink vibration. Their cells are not little drops; they are little drums. When an antinode sweeps across a stand, the whole drift leans into it and rings and feeds, and when the herd lows at the meadow's edge, the reeds turn toward the animals the way other plants turn toward the sun — a slow, green-glass swiveling you can watch over an afternoon. In exchange for the song, the reeds exude a mineral sap at their nodes, and the oxen lick it away. That is the whole pastoral economy, perfected: the herd does not eat the meadow. The herd sings to the meadow, and the meadow feeds the herd, and the herd sings. Between the reed-drifts lie mats of hushfelt, silvery, dense, sound-swallowing rather than sound-drinking — the other strategy — growing where the nodes come often. And over everything stand the three tolltrees, glasswood baobabs hung with bell-fruit that chime in the wind off the rim and, when ripe, drop and burst like ordnance, flinging seed-shrapnel that only a shatter-zone can mill into soil.

The fauna are few and enormous of voice. The bell-oxen carry hollow cranial crests, and their lowing comes in chords an organ would envy; you hear them in your ribs from across the bowl. The shriek-kites ride the pressure-ridges of the antinodes, reefing their membranes, tacking on sound alone. The choristers are numberless but underground. The salt-fleas crackle in the dust in their small millions and will gather, drawn and wondering, at the sweat of a visitor — you are the wettest thing in the valley, and everything in it can tell.

And there is the quiet. That is its name, because that is what it is: the valley's one predator, a moving absence the size of a wagon, hide textured in black wedges like the wall of an anechoic chamber — the same impossible foam-stone, you will notice, as the little ruin under the far wall. It hunts the drifting nodes, and you do not hear it coming. You notice that the world has gone soft, that small sounds are not returning, and by then there is simply one less voice in the bowl. Here, silence is never peace. There are three silences and they must be told apart: the shelter of a node, the footsteps of the quiet, and the drought — the rare, catastrophic failure of the song, when the meadow starves standing and the oxen low at an empty sky. Two other bowls are visible from this rim. Their spires fell. They are silent now in the third way.

The spires — the Tines — stand on the inner rim: five still upright, one fallen and half-buried, its bore warmed by some animal's tenancy. They are worn like megaliths, like old teeth, though time should not be able to wear what they are made of, a substance with the grain of wood and the ring of bronze and the patience of neither and both. They hum the bowl's fundamental note and they have never stopped. Nearby, a stair of tuned slabs descends the rim, and plays whatever crosses it — the herd going down at dusk sounds like a slow chord progression argued out of a cathedral. The small ruin, the bowl's one building, its whole hamlet, sits at a node that barely drifts: the only dependable silence for a day's walk, walls of black wedge-foam, and inside, marks. Tallies, perhaps. A record, perhaps. Nobody reads it. Beneath the pan, the guides insist, the old machinery still does whatever it was built to do — the bowl's parabola is too perfect, the sky-hiss too focused, gathered out of the thin air by a geometry under the salt and poured down into this room as a kind of rain. An ear, they say. The whole valley, an ear aimed at the sky, and everything living in it evolved to drink the overflow.

The bowl finished falling down a long time ago. Everything that could be shaken apart has been shaken apart; what remains is what can ring. Its turbulence is real but bounded, an eternal small churn inside a final form — until, once or twice a day, by accident or appetite, every voice in the place finds the chord at once. The spires, the herd, the kites, the buried chorus, the sky-hiss: the Agreement. The dust stands up in ridges. The kites hang pinned. The bowl becomes a single object, ringing, and the tolltrees choose that moment to drop their fruit. Then it shatters back into quarrel, and the weather resumes, and the meadow leans, and the slow cairns slump and rise elsewhere.

Stay for dusk, from inside the Baffle if you can. The light goes level and ordinary and kind. The hiss underneath everything thins toward something — not quite pattern, not quite — and the herd stops chewing, and the reed-drifts swivel upward, and for a moment the whole valley is listening to whatever it was built to hear. Nothing here knows what that is. Everything here is listening.
