The first thing wrong is the light. You descend — down a rubble cone at one of the sink-mouths, through a squeeze of warm damp stone, down past where any honest cave should go black — and it gets *brighter*. You come out onto a ledge above a hall the size of a valley, lit like a mild afternoon, and you look up for the sky and find instead a low ceiling of pale stone, glowing. That is the Underbright's central fact: its sky is rock, and the rock shines. The people who live here call the whole system the Making, because none of it is finished. They are right. Nothing here is finished.

The ceiling is a crust of needle-crystal, miles of it, each fiber a hair-thin straw that sips daylight from a surface world nobody has seen in generations and delivers it below — late. Dawn breaks in the Underbright hours after it breaks overhead. A storm crossing the surface at noon arrives down here as a slow, silver dusk in mid-afternoon, and the deep halls go dim and thoughtful while the fliers swirl in confusion. The light is milk-warm and shadowless, honest working light, enough to read by. After a day you stop noticing. After two you forget you are underground at all, until you notice the sky is close enough to touch in the low galleries, and that it has texture, and that things are walking on it.

Those are the roof-grazers: broad, flat, pallid animals the size of wagon beds, creeping upside down across the bright crust with a deliberation that makes them look like weather. They eat the luminous film that mantles the stone — the slim, living skin that does some of the shining, and regrows — and their grazing trails cross the sky as vast dim swathes, cloud-shadows with mouths. Whole herds of them move over a district across a week, and the light below ripples and breathes. Sometimes one loses its grip. The fall takes long enough to watch. The impact can be heard three halls away, and what follows is immediate: a scavenger frenzy that boils the floor for an hour, and then, drifting in with their bundles and blades, the people — for a fallen grazer is meat, hide, glue-gut, and above all the grip-pads, which cut into soles and gloves that will cling to anything. The sky provides. The sky is pastured, harvested, and occasionally it drops dinner.

The stone itself is young. That is the second thing wrong. Press your palm to a wall: it is warm, body-warm, and in places faintly slick, and in the deep nurseries it is worse than warm — it is *soft*. New flowstone runs here at a speed that should not be possible, waxing over ledges in a season, sealing a scratched mark within a year. Ribs and curtains of fresh mineral grow visibly around the birth-vents, where hot chemical water meets open air and precipitates rock like tallow cooling: stone with its milk teeth, pale, translucent, not yet decided on hardness. Elsewhere the stone is being unmade as fast as it grows, because the third thing wrong is that something eats it.

You hear the delvers before you see them, and you feel them before you hear them — a bass hum that comes up through the soles of your feet and settles in your teeth, the sound of rotary mouthplates milling rock, thousands of jaws at once. They are low, broad animals, somewhere between an armadillo and a millstone, clan-columns of them working the raw edge of the system in a maze called the Chewfront. They grind the young stone, strain the microbe-rich fraction out of it in their hot guts, and excrete the rest as a fine, brilliant sand that piles behind the herds in migrating dunes — snow-white, ringing faintly when the drafts blow across it, songing sand. A delver clan advances its gallery by meters in a month. Tunnels open behind them, are colonized, are squabbled over, are abandoned, are flooded, are sealed by new growth — all within living memory, all while the clan chews on, indifferent, walking like weather.

The sound never stops. That is the first thing the people teach a newcomer: loud is healthy. The Underbright at full health is a bazaar heard from the next valley — the mill-bass of the delvers under everything, then the clicking and chittering of the fliers, thousands of them at any moment, kites and flits and moth-things threading the bright air in lanes so crowded they have traffic; then the drip-music, ten thousand droplets a minute ticking off rimstone pools in every key; then wing-thunder when a roost-swarm turns; then, far off, the boom of a collapse or the long approach of the river. The chambers shape all of it — some vaults focus a whisper across a hundred meters, some galleries turn the din into a solid roar you lean against. Silence is the only alarm. When the sound drops out, everyone who lives here is already moving, because silence means the cave is about to do something: give way, or flood.

The river is not a feature. It is an event. Somewhere above, rain falls on a dead, scoured surface and finds the cracks, and hours or days later the Pulse arrives: a brown, warm, muscular surge that comes up the slot canyons with a roar that drowns even the delvers, flushes the pools, triggers a feeding madness among the pale filterers and glass-shelled things and eel-things that wait for it, and then drains away over a day, leaving everything reeking, rinsed, and re-set. Between pulses the water is pocketed — lens-still pools crusted in mineral bloom, seep-walls sweating, belts of warm mist hanging in the mid-galleries like stored weather. In the cooler domes, the damp air condenses until it simply rains indoors: a slow, warm, room-sized rain that falls on no one who didn't choose to stand in it. Everything you touch is wet. The air itself is a held breath.

Heat is the engine. The whole system runs a fever, and the fever is the life: warm chemistry, fast metabolism, fast stone, fast flesh. It peaks in the nurseries, where steam drifts off the birth-vents and the floor is ankle-deep in mineral milk, and it fails at the far margins, in the Cold Reaches — old galleries where the chemistry faltered, the light-crust died black, and the din thins to a drip and then to nothing. The people do not go there. The Cold Reaches are where the world's sentence trails off, and even the delvers turn back from them.

The people themselves are scattered thin across all of this — a clan at the Chewfront salvaging delver-shed scutes, a family tending a weir on the Pulse's course, mat-gardeners in the warm pools harvesting sheets of edible film off the water like cloth. Their works are small and temporary because nothing else is possible: cairn-markers that the growing stone swallows within a decade, snare-lines strung across the flier flyways, pitfalls dug beside the stony shells of mimic-spires (hunting the hunters is considered the respectable trade), lodges half-buried in the dunes and roofed with overlapping delver scutes that shed the indoor rain. Their tools are knapped vent-glass, bone picks, sinew cordage, silk reeled from the spinner-flits. They have no word for *permanent*. Their maps are sung, because stone will not hold ink — a trail-glyph scratched in the soft wall is readable for a season and then the rock heals over it, and in the oldest camps you can see hand-stencils of the dead under a clear skin of new flowstone, preserved like things drowned in ice, looking ancient at thirty years.

They talk through the floors. The stone carries a tapped code farther than a shout carries through the din, and children learn the tap-alphabet as they learn to walk; a weir-keeper can gossip with a salvage clan two halls away without leaving her post. Their market — and there is always one, loud, crowded, dense with bodies and barter, vent-glass against grip-pads against silk against dried flit-meat — sets up wherever the work is, near the current front or the current grazing herd, and dissolves the moment the cave shifts. Time is counted in pulses of the river, in grazing-waves, in swarm-turns. The Listeners, who press hollow-bone horns to the walls and read the cave's moods in its hum, hold the only real authority: the authority to say *move*, now, today, and be obeyed without argument.

Stand in a Dayhall at the lagged dusk, when the surface storm has stolen the afternoon, and you can feel the whole arrangement: the glowing sky being eaten and regrowing, the ground being chewed out from under you and re-deposited behind, the river due sometime tonight, the air thick with wings, the floor faintly alive underfoot with the small multitudes that serve as its soil, the warmth on your skin like standing too close to a big sleeping animal. Nothing holds still, and nothing is finished, and the noise is the sound of it happening. The Making is not a place that was built. It is a place being spoken — by a million mouths, chewing — and it has not yet said what it is going to be.
