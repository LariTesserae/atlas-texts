Begin where the light gives out.

Far above there is a surface — storm-wracked, bright, a thin skin where the sea touches air — but it is a rumor down here, an article of faith. The last blue dies within the first few hundred meters, and beneath that the true world begins: warm, dark, still, and bottomless-seeming, though it is not bottomless. It has a floor, and the floor is low country — a broad drowned valley, kilometers wide, lying at the bottom of the water the way a body lies at the bottom of sleep.

It is always raining here. The rain falls downward, slowly, and it is made of the dead and the discarded — the sifted cast-off of the bright sea above, drifting down through the dark in pale flakes and threads. It never stops. It thickens weeks after a storm has raged and forgotten itself at the surface, a delayed answer to weather nobody here witnessed. This slow snow is the soil of the place, and its weather, and its bread. Everything below eats it, or eats what eats it.

You find the valley first by taste and warmth, not by sight. The water changes — a mineral edge, a faint shimmer where warm meets cool — and the snow begins to fall differently, bent sideways by currents rising from below. Then the walls arrive.

The city stands on the valley floor the way a body rests: settled, unhurried, enormous. Towers and mounds of pale accreted stone rise tens of meters, a few near a hundred, their shapes rounded and worn, no edge anywhere — every spire softened by long water into something that looks poured rather than built. Streets run between the towers, floored in polished shell-gravel. Canals. Archways bridging over lanes, their openings smoothed to ovals by millennia of passage. The core of it sprawls across a few kilometers; beyond that, outlying mounds trail off into the dark for a day's swim, thinning into the empty silt plain that surrounds everything. It is the only vertical thing in a horizontal world.

Nothing here was made. Everything was grown. The masons are small colonial animals, cousin to nothing on the surface, that secrete aragonite chamber upon chamber, generation upon generation, each living thin skin of them spread over the skeletons of their ancestors. Their districts are pale stone, dense as honeycomb. Elsewhere the glass districts rise — translucent spires of sponge lattice, flexing almost imperceptibly in the current, faintly luminous with trapped glow. There are pipe districts, thickets of worm tubes standing like organ pipes, each crowned with a feather of filtering arms. There are pavements of interlocked bivalve shells, clean-swept, kept bare by custodian crabs and grazing snails that tend them without anyone deciding they should. Beneath all of it, the lower levels are tombs: sealed chambers, silted corridors, the closed architecture of dead ages. The city stands on hundreds of meters of itself. It has built its own ground.

Of anything plantlike, there is little. Golden and rust-colored lawns of bacteria creep along the warm seams where mineral water seeps up; sparse pale fronds waver near the springs. The rest is bare — clean, polished, unclothed stone. The emptiness of the walls is part of the look of the place, the way silence is part of its sound.

The warmth comes from below. The valley floor is thin, and heat rises through it in diffuse, patient seeps — no violence, just a slow exhale. In the lower streets the water is bath-warm; the masonry itself is faintly warm, like skin. Where the warm flow meets the cooler open water, the boundary shimmers, the only mirage in this world. The warmth makes the currents languid and drives the city's breathing.

For the city breathes. On a slow cycle, roughly twice a day, the sea shifts its weight and the canals reverse: the city inhales snow-laden water through its lower gates, holds it, filters it through a million small mouths, and exhales clear water upward through the crowns of the spires. The placement of the towers is no accident and no design — the masons build where the feeding is best, and the feeding is best where vortices linger, and so the spires stand exactly where standing eddies hold the falling snow longest. The plan of the city is a diagram of its own weather, drawn in stone by instinct over a hundred thousand years.

The living density of it is difficult to overstate. Every wall is inhabited; every chamber has a tenant; the water itself is crowded. Schools of small silver fish move through the streets as single bodies, turning all at once around the corners of towers. Shrimp swarm in the mid-levels in their slow millions. Jellies drift above the spires like a canopy that has come loose. Rays cruise the canals with soft wingbeats. Eels occupy the walls. Abandoned larvacean houses — vast, delicate mucus filters — sink ghostlike through the lanes and are set upon and dismantled before they reach the ground. And out of the open dark come the hunters: large, quiet shapes that know the city's rhythms and wait at the gates when the rhythm favors them.

The great rhythm is the migration. Every night — and night is kept here not by light, which never arrives, but by some deep chemical memory of the surface's turning — the small life of the deep rises: billions of bodies ascending kilometers toward the bright water to feed, then sinking again before the far-off dawn. The city is a station on that road. At the rising hour the water above the towers thickens and thrums, softly; hunters take their positions at the gates; the schools pour through the streets in rivers.

Light, when it comes, is speech. It is too costly for anything else. An alarm flash — a school igniting once, silver-blue, and going black. A lure dangling in the dark with patience behind it. Mating pulses exchanged across a plaza in slow code. And the masonry itself glows, faintly: symbiotic bacteria in the seams of the stone brighten when the warm flow feeds them, so that districts ember and dim on long cycles, neighborhoods waking and sleeping. When the migration passes overhead at its peak, a slow wave of glow travels through the whole city, edge to core, as if the structure were acknowledging the passage — then it subsides, and the dark comes back, and the dark is the default, the medium, the home.

The sound of the place matches. Mostly there is a hush: the faint combined hiss of a million filters drawing water, a sound like held breath. The clicks of shrimp come through like dripping in a far room. Once in a long while an old arch settles and lets out a low groan that carries for kilometers — an event, discussed in the nerves of everything that hears it. A ray's wingbeat is felt more than heard. The snow falls in perfect silence and lands on skin like dust.

It has been like this for longer than anything here can count. The city grows at its crowns and dissolves at its roots, the warm mineral water slowly unmaking the oldest stone even as the masons lay down new. Collapses happen, but slowly — a spire leans over decades, slumps over months, and its tenants leave gradually, unpanicked, reabsorbed into neighboring districts. The streets are worn the way old steps are worn, by the passage of bodies across ten thousand generations. The city's total size barely changes century to century. It has reached its form and keeps it, the way a mature forest keeps its height — not finished, but settled. In balance. New growth and old dissolution, arriving at the same rate.

Nothing in this world has ever been touched by a hand. There is no tool here, no device, nothing made — only grown. And there is a sense, difficult to dismiss once you have swum its length, that the distinction may not even apply: that the masons across the whole valley share one continuous tissue, one inheritance, that the districts are organs and the spires are bones, and that the city is a single life several kilometers across and older than any memory of water. It feeds on the rain of the dead. It keeps the surface's time without ever seeing the surface. It answers the migration with one slow traveling glow, and then lets the dark return.

The snow goes on falling. The streets stay clean. The warm stone breathes in, breathes out.
