There is a hill in dry country — thin grass, broken stone, a wind that never quite stops — and inside the hill, sealed the way a seed is sealed, there is a room.

Not a cave. A cave is made by removal, by water's long patience, and this place was made the other way: a bubble in the rock's deep past, a blister, a vug, closed over before it ever connected to anything. No passage leads to it. You could walk the ridge above it your whole life and suspect nothing. Only on the stillest winter nights, if you pressed your ear to a certain outcrop where one vein of crystal climbs through the dark like a hairline crack in the world, you might hear — or think you heard — a faint ringing, and touch your ear, and blame the cold.

The first wrongness is the light.

Underground things should be dark, and this place, two body-lengths across and domed just out of arm's reach, is bright. Not lit — lit implies a lamp. It is bright the way a breaking wave is bright: brightness as a property of the material itself. The walls are lined with crystal teeth, calcite grown in pale scalene blades, and the hill's own weight pressing on them makes them hum, and the humming drives the water that films every surface into a cold, steady burn. The light is the sound, slowed down for the eye. Green-gold, cool, sourceless. Bright as noon, and it has never flickered, not once, because there is nothing here to make it change.

There are no shadows. Nothing in this room has a dark side; even the undersides of things are lit from below by the floor's pale answering. Where crystal teeth cross at the corners, the light splits into spectra that never drift — rainbows bolted to the walls, fixtures, furniture. It is the one place in the world where nothing can hide, and it is itself the best-hidden thing in the world.

The second wrongness is that it is loud.

Not loud like a crash — loud like a presence. Loud the way a hive is loud, the way a weir is loud, a fullness with pressure behind it. The cavity is a resonator and it holds one organ-deep ground-tone that you feel in the sternum and the fillings of your teeth more than hear. Over that, the bell-crickets lay their harmony: glass-bodied singers whose wing-veins never wear, because wearing is a kind of time, and their voices have locked, over no passage of years, into a chord. It is almost a beautiful chord. It is one note short of resolution, and it leans eternally toward the note it has never once taken and will never take.

Under the chord, percussion: the drips. The ceiling has a single nub, the seed of a stalactite that has never grown, and it swells a bead of water — always to the same size, always at the same rate — and lets it fall, always to the same point in the pool below. The splash is always the same splash. And under the drips, a hiss: the walls themselves, where the reef-felt lays down bright calcite and the seeping water dissolves it at exactly the same rate, accretion and dissolution walking hand in hand and going nowhere. And under everything, so low it might be your own body, a pulse.

The air is cool as well water — ten degrees, always ten degrees — and it stands exactly at its dew point, one held breath away from rain. The rain happens: bead by bead, on the ceiling, on the crystal blades, where water gathers to one perfect diameter and is reabsorbed at the rate it gathers, each bead a standing harvest. Every surface wears moisture one molecule thick. The pool receives the drops and gives back through the walls precisely what it receives. The room drinks its own weather, in a closed loop, forever.

Off-center beneath the dome's low point, the pool sits in a basin that looks carved and is not — a font of raw stone holding a cupped hand of water. Its surface stands in a frozen star of ripples: the cymatic flower of the one unchanging tone, every wave-peak dancing in place, the pattern as permanent as masonry. The water is cool, clear, and crowded — a broth of infusoria that never multiply and never thin, carrying capacity arrived at so long ago that the word "ago" has stopped meaning anything here.

In the pool lives the oldest animal in the world.

It is pale as root-flesh, the length of a finger, something between a salamander and a rumor. It is eyeless, because eyes are for worlds where things happen. Its gills branch in red filaments — the warmest color in the room — and through its translucent skin you can watch its heart, which beats once in the time a ceiling-drop takes to swell. It does not feed, or feeds on the falling of the drops; it has not moved in longer than the hill can remember, or else it swims one perfect circle of the basin per age, and no one could watch long enough to tell the difference. It is the slowest thing that still counts as alive, and its heartbeat is the room's third clock, and none of the clocks count anything. They only keep time. Keeping it, not passing it.

The walls below the light's waterline are a reef. The felt — tubes, fans, feathered mouths packed to no-room, every square centimeter owned — strains the hum out of the mineral the way a whale strains the sea, eating vibration and paying out crystal, so that the room is quite literally built by its own noise. The floor is a pavement of molt-husks and shell-snow, tessellated, springy, mother-of-pearl pale, at a depth that never increases: for every husk shed, one is reabsorbed. Even the air is employed, threaded through with the closed orbits of the lamp-midges — ember-points of steady orange that never blink, because blinking is a clock and clocks here are forbidden to count. From any single glance, the swarm is a constellation that will be the same constellation in a thousand years. Whether it moves is a question you would have to watch forever to answer.

Near the font, one bell-cricket is molting, and has always been molting. The old skin is split along the thorax; the new self is half-emerged, brighter, softer, glistening — held there, serene, on the threshold of itself. Nothing about it is strained. Birth in this room is not an event. It is an address. Everything here is about to: the fiddlehead among the seven pale fronds at the seep-line has always been about to unfurl; the eggs in the sealed urns are always about to hatch; the nub of the stalactite is always about to become a stalactite. It is the youngest place in the world — every surface sharp, every crystal face unetched, the milk-film of first flowstone not yet thickened into anything — and it has been the youngest place for longer than hills usually live. A cave-seed. A landscape's embryo that germinated inward and then stopped, not from failure but because stopping is its nature.

The flora you can count. Seven fronds along the seep-line, each a different architecture, as though seven separate futures of plant were tried and kept. A stain of gold-green on the brightest wall, a meadow the size of a hand, drinking the light. In the least bright corner — still bright as morning — an ivory felt of something like fungus, eating whatever the light-eaters shed. Everything is fed. Nothing is diminished.

And there are the things that look made. On a natural shelf, a row of little urns, thumb-high, lidded, built of silk and spit and calcite: four sealed, one open and empty, one under construction — under construction forever, its rim rising at no speed at all. In the milk-film beside the pool, a spiral is incised so evenly it reads as writing, and it has been read, and no one has read it. A lattice of woven filament dams one seep, half a span wide and exactly half-finished. The whole room, at first glance, passes for a chapel — dome, font, shelf, and in the far wall a niche, and beside the niche a seam in the crystal with the precise shape of a narrow door, filled solid, leading nowhere, patient as a thing that has already arrived. Nothing made any of it. A bubble cooled this way. The bubble happened to cool into liturgy.

The sun of this world is weight. That is the rule at the bottom of everything: the hill presses down; the pressed crystal hums; the hum burns the wet films into light; the light feeds the meadow and the fronds; the grazers eat the light-eaters and sing; the song feeds the felt; the felt lays down the crystal that carries the song. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. Nothing is born to completion and nothing dies to absence. Time passes here the way a wheel passes — everything turning, nothing traveling. The place is in an equilibrium so complete it has stopped being a balance and become a temperament.

Three emptinesses are kept, swept like hearths: the niche. The open urn. The note the chord never resolves to. Everything else is full — the walls, the floor, the air, the water, every frequency, every surface, the light itself crowded in its beams. And above it all, the dry ridge goes on being unmade by weather and root, grain by grain, in the ordinary way of landscapes, and the room does not participate. Degrees are not how this place does time. If it ever ends it will end all at once — but there is no ever here, and no at-once. There is the chord. There is the drop swelling. There is the heart.

On certain nights, through the hairline vein of crystal, the chord bleeds one thread of itself up into the world, and a walker on the ridge stops, and touches an ear, and thinks: the cold is ringing. The walker goes home. The room goes on — "goes on" being only a way of speaking. The room is on. It has always been on: the one lit room in the hill's dark body, kept the way a body keeps a heartbeat.

Without effort. Without witness. Without end.
