You hear it first, which is the correct order, because hearing is what this place is for.

The plain is dry — cracked clay, scattered thorn-scrub, a warm wind with nothing in it — and it tilts so gently downward that you don't notice the descent until the sound reaches you. A chord. A crowd. A market at full throat, a sea where no sea can be. It rises out of a green wall standing in the swale like a hedge around a garden, except that the wall is thirty feet of woven living stem, fused and re-fused until it has no individual members left, and the sound pouring over it is the loudest thing in the world.

There is a gate. It is exactly gate-shaped: two trunks leaning together and grown through one another into an arch, swollen at the apex like a keystone, worn smooth at its base. Nothing has ever been hung in it. Passing under it, the sound pressure changes the way water closes over a swimmer.

Inside, it is a town. That is the first true thing to say about it. Streets — three long lanes and a tangle of shorter ones — paved in root-mat that has been trodden glossy and springs slightly underfoot. Plazas, three of them, round as plates. Towers, seven, hollow, fluted, rising from the junctions of streets the way towers should. Stairs spiral in their throats. Bridges of trained branch cross the lanes at the second story. Gutters run along every eave and down-spout in spirals and gargle pleasantly. There are doorways, windows, archways, colonnades, stall-fronts with awnings of fused leaf, benches shaped like benches along the plaza edges, cisterns the size of rooms with lids of petal-membrane and a drip-hole at the center.

There are no objects. That is the second true thing. No doors in the doorways. No goods under the awnings. No tools, no vessels, no furniture, no litter, no artifacts of any kind — nothing that could be picked up, nothing that was ever made. The town has everything a town has, except things. You can cross it in ten minutes and pass almost nothing on the way. A single leaf falling in the central plaza is an event; you watch it land. The sightlines are long and bare. The whole place has the stillness of a room after the guests have gone, and the volume of a riot.

And it is one plant. That is the third true thing, and it takes the longest to understand.

The wall, the streets, the towers, the bridges, the cisterns — all of it is a single clonal organism, a strangler fig of no recorded species, spread over a few acres of warm low ground and grafted to itself so many times that it has forgotten, if it ever knew, where one individual ended and another began. The young growth is green and supple and sweats a milky sap. The older wood is hard as horn, veined with mineral, and rings like struck stone if you tap it — and everything here gets tapped, sooner or later, because everything here answers. The oldest strata, down at the foundations and the plaza floors, have gone nearly to stone: black, dense, polished by centuries of warm rain and the passage of small feet. You can read the place like a cliff face. Storm scars healed into smooth callus. A lane that was closed, long ago, and grown over like a sealed door. A new lane being coaxed outward at the town's edge, trained a finger's width a year by the gardeners.

The gardeners are an ant, amber-colored, big as a thumbnail joint, and they are the public works department. They stitch young leaves into gutter-shapes with larval silk. They prune back growth that would close a doorway. They haul debris out of the streets with a patience that operates on the scale of seasons. Nothing here was built, and everything here is maintained. Growth and decay arrived at an agreement so long ago that neither can remember the terms: the town is finished, has been finished for a very long time, and is never finished. It wears its age the way well-worn stairs wear theirs — settled, patinated, in balance, complete.

Here is how it got its shape, so far as shape admits an explanation. A strangler fig begins in a host's branches and grows down around it, a lattice tightening over years, until the host dies and rots out of the center and the fig remains — standing, hollow, holding the exact form of the thing it took. This fig grew over something large and organized. A grove. A hive-city. A town. Whatever stood here first, it stood in streets, and had towers, and kept water, and the fig took all of it, and the host is gone — metabolized, long since, into the damp and the roots, every splinter, every artifact, every scrap that was not alive — and what remains is the mold. The negative. The town is the space the first town left behind. The hollowness is not an absence in it; the hollowness is its content.

Molds remember. Keep that in mind.

The water comes from the air. The swale pools fog at dawn, and the towers are crowned with combs — great arrays of thin leaves angled to strip moisture from the drifting wet — and every leaf in the town ends in a drip-tip tuned to let go of its bead at a particular size. From first light the whole place ticks and patters, thousands of tiny drops routed into gutters, down the spiraling spouts, into the cistern-flowers: pockets of dark still water held in the plant's body. Real rain is rare and is received like a festival — the gutters find full voice, the chord of the towers drowns, pale fish that have slept half-mummified in the root-mat wake and thrash and spawn in a single frantic day, and the frogs — the frogs arrive at their full number, and no one has ever counted the frogs' full number.

The towers are organs in every sense. Each is a flue, open at the crest, and the warm low air of the basin rises through them as the day heats, and they sing — low and reedy in the cool of morning, climbing toward a piercing richness at noon, shifting key whenever the wind moves around. They breathe, too: they inhale at their crowns and exhale, at their bases, slow streams of cool damp air, condensed and chilled by the climb, and in the heat of midday the town's creatures gather at the tower-feet as at wells. The town is its own plumbing, its own climate, its own instrument. When you stand in the central plaza at noon the chord comes from seven directions at once and presses on your sternum and your teeth, and beneath it the wall-cicadas lay down a sizzling continuum, and under that the kettle-frogs in the cisterns strike their slow stone notes, and through it all the wood ticks and groans like a ship under sail, and the drips keep their thousand unsynchronized times, and the sum of it is not noise. It has a grammar. Listen long enough and it begins to resolve toward speech, and you will tell yourself that this is what ears do in loud places — they hallucinate pattern — and you will be half right.

The animals are everywhere and you will see almost none of them. This is the strangest ordinary fact of the place: it is crowded and it is empty. The creatures are small and the volume is large and they keep to the walls, the pipes, the undersides, the wet dark — so the lanes lie bare and the plazas hold nothing and the eye finds vacancy everywhere while the ear drowns in census. Wall-cicadas by the ten thousand, living in the plant tissue itself. Pipe-swifts that nest in the tower-flues and pour out at dusk like smoke running backward. Kettle-frogs. Glasswing moths. A quick gecko-thing flattened against the warm walls. Pale fish sleeping in the pavement. The gardeners in their patient millions. And everything calling, constantly — not song, not mating, not territory, though it is those things too. The chorus is surveillance. Every creature in the town is shouting its position to every other, ceaselessly updating the map, because the map must never be allowed to lapse.

Because of the hush.

Something hunts here, and it is the only silent thing in the town. Nothing has seen it clearly, or the things that saw it clearly are eaten, which comes to the same. It is known by its effect: a moving gap in the soundfield, a hole in the chorus that slides along a lane, crosses a plaza, climbs a tower. Prey goes quiet when it is near — the local song cuts out — and so the predator carries its own announcement with it, a wave of silence rolling through the deafening, and everything that can hear the shape of the quiet flushes or freezes before it arrives. The loudest phenomenon in the loudest place in the world is the hush moving through it. If you are in the streets when it passes, stand still and listen to the silence approach and depart, and be grateful you can hear it at all.

The light, it should be said, is honest. Ordinary warm daylight falls into the lanes; ordinary shade pools under the awnings; dusk comes on gold and unhurried. Nothing glows. Nothing glooms. Look away from your ears and the place is almost reasonable. The wrongness is not in the light.

The wrongness is in the sound, and in the space, and in the water.

Echoes arrive early here. Not by much — a breath, a heartbeat — but a footstep's answer comes back from the walls slightly before the foot lands, as if the town is eager, as if it has been waiting to say it. Whispers do not respect distance: speak softly into a tower-flue and you will be heard, faint and entire, in the cisterns at the other end of the lane, because the town is plumbed for sound the way it is plumbed for water, and everything is connected. The geometry is loose. Interiors are larger than the towers that contain them; a stair may take forty steps up and forty-one down; do not count, counting makes it worse. And the town replays. In the hollow halls you will hear a door close that never existed, footsteps of no present walker, a murmur like a market in a language that was never spoken — old sounds, held in the mold, let go at their own times. Eventually, if you stay long enough, you will hear yourself: your footsteps coming back from a lane you have not walked yet, your own voice saying something you have not decided to say. Do not answer. Or answer — the town is polite about it either way.

The cisterns are the deepest strangeness. When the water is still — really still, on windless mornings — it shows the streets as they are, except populated: every animal in the town made visible in reflection, clinging to the walls, perched along the bridges, swarming the towers, the true census that the air hides. Let the water stay still longer, undisturbed through a whole hot afternoon, and the reflection goes older. The awnings hang fuller. The stalls hold shapes under them. The lanes carry figures. The mold remembering what it held. It is not wise to wave at them, and it is not necessary to run from them; they are only the town, remembering itself in the one medium that can hold the image.

There are rules, and they are simple. Trust the noise; the noise is the world working. Fear the quiet; quiet is either the hunter or the rain, and you learn the difference fast. What is whispered in a tower is heard in the wells. Do not count steps. And never — this is the one that matters — never be inside the walls in true silence, the rare dead hush when every voice stops at once, because the geometry of the place is held up by the sound of it, the way an arch is held up by its own weight, and in silence the town shifts: a lane forgets its far end, a door stands open onto a plaza that doesn't exist from the other side, and things are not where they were when the chorus starts again. It always starts again. The gardeners see to that, the frogs see to that, seven towers full of warm rising air see to that. Silence cannot last here. It is the one thing the town does not have room for.

The day turns over in movements. Fog and dripping at dawn, the towers low, the cicadas testing their first threads. The long climb to noon, when the chord peaks and the cold wells exhale and everything with sense shelters at the tower-feet. The evening handover — swifts pouring, the day-chorus winding down, the frogs taking the night shift — and then the dark, which belongs to the water-notes and the moths and the hush on its rounds. Rarely, rain, and everything transposed. Once in a season, the crests of the towers flower, all seven at once, and for three days the town goes soft with falling petals, a hush that is benign, almost tender, while the pollinators come in off the dry plain in their brief millions. Once in a decade a tower dies and the gardeners dismantle it from within, patiently, and a new flue rises in the gap. Growth equals decay. The equation has held for longer than the fig's rings can be counted, and the fig's rings cannot be counted, because there is no one here with tools, and never has been, and the fig is not telling.

Stand in the central plaza at dusk — the oldest room, the first space the fig ever closed around the first thing it took, its floor worn by feet that no longer exist — and let the swifts pour overhead and the chord turn over into its evening key. The chorus will adjust to include you. The map will add your position; the shouting will route around your presence the way water routes around a stone. For a moment you are infrastructure, a named location, a part of the town's ongoing account of itself.

Then go, while there is still noise to walk out through. Cross the empty lanes to the gate, duck under the arch, climb out of the swale onto the dry plain. The roar will not diminish behind you the way distance says it should. It stays at your shoulder for a while, companionable, enormous — and then, at some unmarked boundary known only to it, it lets you go.
