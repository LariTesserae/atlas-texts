The plain gives no warning. It is a long flat of wind-polished pebbles running to a shimmered horizon, and it keeps its single event hidden until you are nearly standing in it. Then the ground ends. Below you — and this is the first strangeness — there is *more* light than there was a step ago. You have found the light-well: a hollow dug by water and kept by light.

The way down is the old wound, the one place where the rim failed long ago and slumped into a ramp of broken stone. The ramp is ancient now, and its surface is worn into shallow grooves by the passage of slow backs. You descend into brightness. The rim is a hundred paces around; the floor, sixty across; the walls stand three body-lengths at their tallest, pale as paper, fluted from lip to base with the handwriting of rains that no longer come often. It was a cave once, briefly — then the roof let go, and the hollow has been open to the sky ever since, and has long since made peace with the opening.

The air at the bottom is kept air. The plain's wind crosses the rim as a rumor overhead and does not enter; below, the warmth settles and stays, stratified, still, a degree more honest than the wind-flung warmth above. The sky from the floor is a white coin. And the floor itself, at your first counting, holds almost nothing: seven slump-stones, rounded now, crusted with a glaze of old seep-mineral; eleven conical pits in the silt, each the size of a dinner plate; a single sideways shadow thrown from a crevice in the wall — the shadow of the hollow's one tree, which grows horizontally, flag-formed, and leafs only in rain-years.

That is the noon census. It is not the whole census.

The light is the place's one abundance in space, and it sprawls. The pale walls take the sun and hand it around; by midmorning the light comes from everywhere at once, from below as much as above. Shadows here are doubled or erased — a thing at the center of the floor casts two faint shadows, or none, and stands in its own glare like something being examined. At the bottom of the brightness there is a focus, a spot two arm-spans wide where the returned light concentrates, and the ground there is different: darker, harder, faintly glassy, as if the light had been at it for a very long time. No pit is dug in that patch. The worn groove-roads coming down the ramp bend around it. The silver writing that covers everything by night does not cross it.

The water is kept in time, not in place. At night the hollow cools, and its water arrives: the seep at the shaded base of the wall wakes and begins to tick — a drop a breath, no faster — and dew beads on the lichen-strokes of the flutes, on the folded leaves of the well-bush, on the backs of beetles standing head-down along the rim, on the pale domes of the tortoises, so that for an hour at dawn the whole census wears its water. Beneath the seep's overhang sits the well-eye: a basin you could cup in two arms, black with shade, unbrimming, constant, holding its level by some slow agreement between arrival and departure. Up the wall behind it runs a stair of solution-cups, six of them, each drier than the one below, the topmost holding nothing but a stain. At dawn the tortoises come down their grooves to the well-eye in an order that does not change, and drink one at a time, and wait without impatience for a turn that may be an hour off. Nothing has explained the order. Nothing here is in a hurry to.

By midmorning the water is a memory. It will be back at dusk. It keeps appointments, not locations.

When rain comes — not every year — the bowl remembers what it is. The floor takes a skin of water and for a day the light-well is a well again. The glassy patch bubbles. Out of the silt come shield-shrimps, ancient-faced, for their one brief week; the eleven pits drown and must be dug again; the tortoises drink standing, the queue dissolved, the schedule suspended. Then the long drying: the mud maps its cracks, the cups empty from the top down, and the place forgets by degrees, the way it does everything.

The sound is mostly the sound of the seep. A drop a breath. It is the pulse everything else is measured against. Sometimes, irregular as thought and clear as a struck bell, comes the double *tock* of stone on stone: the anvil-bird at work, and in this bowl's acoustics the strike arrives from everywhere at once, intimate and unlocatable, so that you will never find the worker by ear. At noon, when the hollow breathes out — it breathes once a day, in at dawn when the cool air slides down the flutes and the dust-haze drains along the runnels like slow water, out at noon in a warm rising column — the swifts come down from the high air to ride the breath, the only fast things here, stitching their thin screams through the bright. They are visitors. Speed in the light-well is a borrowed thing, permitted to wings and to struck stones.

A body's length from the glassy patch lies the bowl's other center — its ear. Lie down there at dusk and the walls report: the rasp of a snail, the tick of the seep, the small collapse of a burrow's shoulder, the click of a beetle closing its wing-cases. Every sound arrives near and without direction, as though the hollow were reading you its minutes. The two centers do not quite coincide — the eye's and the ear's — and the ground between them is the strangest ground in the place. For long hours, though, there is no sound at all, and the quiet itself has an acoustics.

Here is what the noon counting misses. The walls are tenemented. The burrow-mouths along the base of the flutes open at dusk like eyes: the burrow-owl blinking in a doorway a tortoise dug; the silt-bees humming the wall-face; the pale lacewings rising — the grown form of the things that dig the eleven pits, that spend their youth at the bottom of those cones flicking grains at whatever stumbles in. The tortoises themselves, nine of them this century, each older than the grooves suggest, keepers of the seep-hollows they deepen with their forefeet, generation after generation wearing the same paths into polish. The nacreous snails come out at night and write the walls in silver, all of it erased by noon. The anvil-bird — crested, deliberate — strikes flakes from a core at its favored stones and uses the edges to lever snails from their shells and split the hard pods of the well-bush, and the waste of its work lies in scatters at the anvil-stones: knapping floors, unmistakable, and old. Count at the right hour and the hollow holds hundreds of lives. Count at noon and you can hold the floor on one hand.

The hollow is not empty. It is sparse. Its abundance is kept in margins and in hours, the same way its water is kept.

No one lives here. Nothing stands here that was stood. And yet the floor is littered with works: flaked stone, engineered pits, dug wells, guttered leaves. Nothing was built; a few things were made; much was dug. It is a workshop without a workman, and every craft practiced in it is a craft of removal — knapping takes away stone, burrowing takes away silt, the wells take away sediment, the snails take away the dark. The hollow itself was made the same way, by stone consenting to be gone. In the slumped section where the ramp cuts the fill you can read the record of it: silt in fine layers, and in the layers the dimples of drowned pits, and flakes at many depths — some, by the look of their burial, older than the bird that strikes them now — and the pale coins of tortoise shell. What falls in, stays. The bowl keeps its receipts.

The rules of the place are few, and it keeps them:

Water is an hour, not a feature.
Light is the only thing allowed to sprawl.
Shadows are doubled, or none.
Every sound is public.
The floor is for crossing.
What falls in, stays.
Speed belongs to wings and to struck stones.
Everything made here is made by taking away.

Come at dawn, if you come. Stand at the rim as the first light pours over the lip and the cool air slides down the flutes and the first tortoise starts down its groove toward the well-eye, wearing its dew. The seep ticks. Somewhere, once, stone strikes stone. Below you the floor waits in its doubled shadows, holding nothing but brightness — keeping its light the way a cup keeps water: by having been emptied first.
