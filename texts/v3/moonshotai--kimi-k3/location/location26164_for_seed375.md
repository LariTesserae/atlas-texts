**The Basin**

The moon is small, dry, and far from its sun — a ball of iron-stained stone swinging around a banded giant, with an atmosphere so thin it is less a sky than a rumor. Nothing here is wet. Nothing here has ever been wet. And in one crater on the planet-facing side, there is a city.

No one built it. That is the first thing to understand, and the last thing you will stop turning over in your mind.

Stand on the rim — no one does, but stand there — and the whole of it fits in a single look: five kilometers from rim to rim, a shallow bowl of grey floor holding perhaps sixty structures, and the space between them so vast that the structures read as incidents. A spire. A pause. A dome. A longer pause. Three slender towers in a row like an unfinished sentence. It is a city the way a constellation is a picture: the form is unmistakable, and most of it is emptiness.

Above it all, the giant hangs motionless. The moon is locked, one face turned forever to its planet, so the giant never rises and never sets — it simply occupies a third of the sky, banded in rust and umber and bone, a storm on its flank like a thumbprint pressed in ochre. The only change is its phase. When the small white tack of the sun is up, the giant is dark, a hole in the stars; when the sun swings down, the giant kindles into full bronze, and the basin glows like embers under ash. It is never dark here. The light only changes hands. Everything is lit, always, at the level of late dusk — and the shadows, with no air to soften them, are cut from black paper.

The floor of the basin is not ground. It is the city's body. Hexagonal plates, each a few meters across, fit together like columnar basalt — but they were not cooled from a melt; they were grown, and they are alive in the slow way crystal is allowed to be alive. Seams run between the plates, and the seams breathe. Once each orbit, as the moon swings through pericenter and the giant's gravity takes the basin in its fist, the whole floor domes upward by nearly a meter and settles again. At that hour the seams open a finger's width and exhale — silent puffs of dust that rise in the low gravity with terrible slowness, hang, and fall back in stretched parabolas. The city inhales the tide. The flexing of its crystal plates generates charge — this is how it eats. Not the dim light. The squeeze.

There is no water to frost, so what whitens the lee of every spire through the long giant-lit night is carbon dioxide rime, a fur of not-snow that flashes off within the hour when the small sun returns. At the sweeping boundary between light and light — the terminator crawls across the basin twice each ninety-hour day — the dust itself levitates, electrostatically lofted, rising in silent fountains along the moving line of dawn. It is the only weather. There is no wind. There has never been wind.

The structures are cathedral-scaled and knife-sharp. Spires of layered glass and iron-salt, opalescent where thin, black as obsidian where old, veined with seams of native metal that carry the city's slow thoughts. Ribbed vaults like the inside of a whale's chest. Causeways that lead nowhere and end mid-air, because the city does not build toward destinations — it buds, and a bud does not need a reason. At the center of the basin, where the floor's flex is strongest, stands the eldest thing: a dome of interlaced crystal ribs over a floor of black glass, eighty meters across, and it is called nothing, because there is no one to call it anything. Call it the Cathedral. It has earned it.

And through all of this: nothing. The silence is total, the deep silence of a world with no medium to carry a sound. Strike two stones together and the clap dies at the contact, stillborn. The dome, if it collapsed, would fall in perfect muteness. And yet the city is not quiet — it is only quiet in the air. Press your palm flat to a floor plate and the truth comes up through your bones: the basin is talking. A low continuous hum at pericenter, when the tide squeezes and the charge flows. Tick-pulses running plate to plate like messages handed down a line. And, faintly, from somewhere out on the plaza, a slow drumming in groups of three — the polishers, talking to each other through the ground, the only way anyone here has ever said anything.

The polishers are most of the fauna, and there are perhaps four hundred of them in the world. Low, many-legged, soft-bodied, each one armored in a shell it has cemented together from flakes of dead crystal — they carry the city's corpse on their backs like hermit crabs, and they graze the facets of the spires, scraping away the bruise-colored smoke-lichen that etches the glass. A herd of a dozen takes three weeks to cross a plaza, leaving a swathe of polished clarity behind them, and the city tolerates them the way a body tolerates its helpful bacteria. In the dust of the plazas, long furrows appear overnight and mean silt-worms, which no one has ever seen, only their plowing. And then there is the third presence, which is the reason the rules exist.

Some of the arches are not the city.

They are rare — perhaps nine of them, perhaps eleven; the count changes. They grow at the city's edge and wear its architecture perfectly, and they are mouths. A polisher that shelters beneath the wrong arch at the wrong phase simply stops being part of the herd's slow drumming, and the arch is thicker afterward, for a while. Once in a decade, somewhere in the basin, an arch closes. No one hears it.

The vegetation, what there is of it, holds to the shadows: the smoke-lichen in its grey-black patches, and rarer still, the shadow-tufts — fist-sized clusters of dark needles that live only in the lee of spires, farming the rime, one tuft per hundred meters of floor. Everything here is sparse. Everything here is slow. And everything here is in motion.

For the basin is busy beyond belief, once your eyes adjust to its clocks. The floor breathes once an orbit. The frost comes and goes twice a day. The dust fountains sweep. The spires grow at their tips fast enough that a patient hour of watching shows the edge creeping, a minute hand of glass. Micrometeorites stitch the plaza with silent flashes, brief punctuations of light in the dim. And once each orbit, at the giant's full, the moon slides into the planet's shadow, and for one hour the basin knows true darkness — the only darkness it ever gets — and the sky above the rim burns with silent aurora, violet and green, and in that dark hour the city lights itself: slow pulses of cold luminescence crawling along the spire-veins, constellations answering constellations across the empty plazas, the whole basin flickering in patterns too slow to read and too regular to be anything but language. The Cathedral pulses last, and slowest, and everything else seems to wait for it.

Nothing here rots and nothing here erodes — there is no wind, no water, no weathering worth the name — so the city handles its own dead. Old districts sink. A spire past its time is resorbed over years, drawn back down into the rootplate, its materials reclaimed, its site left smooth or scarred or already budding. There are no ruins in the basin. There is no past tense. Everything is either alive or reabsorbed, which gives the young crater its strange sharpness: every edge in the city is an edge that is being maintained.

The rules of the basin are these:

Nothing is built; everything is born. Construction and anatomy are the same act.
Sound stays where it is made. What needs saying is said through the floor.
The city ends exactly at the rim, because the city ends where the ground stops singing. Beyond the rim the crust does not flex, and the crystal cannot eat, and so there is no beyond.
Light is rationed; the tide is not.
An arch that was not there last season is not an arch.
The dead are eaten by the city. There are no exceptions, and no ruins.

From the rim, at full giant, in the bronze perpetual dusk, you can take the whole of it in at once: sixty-some presences in five kilometers of floor, the mist of a dust-fountain along the terminator, a seam sighing open somewhere in slow motion, a line of polished glass marking three weeks of a herd's patient hunger, the Cathedral holding its ribs over the black-glass heart of things. It is urban and it is empty. It is crowded with process and bare of beings. It is growing, flexing, signaling, feeding, dreaming — and it makes no sound whatsoever, and it never will.

The crater is young. The city is younger. Which made which is the basin's one secret — whether the impact cracked the floor into a drumskin the crystal could play, or whether the crater is itself a germination scar, the place where something fell from the dark and took. The Cathedral, notably, has been thickening at its crown for decades, swelling with nodules that are not for here. The city does not expect to end. It expects, someday, to be struck — an impact big enough to fling pieces of it off the moon entirely, seeded masonry scattered into the black — and it has organized its entire old age around that hope.

Until then it breathes, and brightens, and says its slow say into the floor, in the silence, in the bronze light, in the bowl of the world.
