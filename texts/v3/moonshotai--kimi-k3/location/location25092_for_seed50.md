You hear it a full day before you see it.

It comes up through the soles of your boots first, climbing the scree like something buried alive and knocking. Then the air takes it: a chord, low and enormous, that does not belong at this altitude. Above the treeline the ridges should be quiet — wind, raven, stone settling. Instead the whole high country hums like a struck bell, and the empty valleys between the empty peaks carry the sound until it seems the mountains themselves are making it, all of them, together, at some hidden center.

The center is small. That is the first surprise.

You come through a notch in a raw ridge — the rock here is unweathered, angular, sharp enough to cut hide, nothing rounded, nothing lichened into patience — and the world closes down to a single bowl. Forty paces across. A cup of water held at the top of the world, brimming, churning white at its heart, and roaring like a cataract a hundred times its size. Cloud moves below you. The sky is ordinary blue. And here is this: a font of violent water cradled in stone that is still being born.

The keepers call it the soft spot. The fontanelle of the mountain.

---

The water arrives from below. There is no inlet stream, no glacier feeding it, no snowfield worth the name. It wells up out of a shaft no sounding line has ever bottomed, driven by some pressure in the deep body of the peak, and it arrives cold — meltwater-cold, four degrees, ache-cold — and saturated so heavily with dissolved stone that it can barely hold itself. A rope dipped in the churn comes out stiff within the hour, crusted grey. A cup left overnight on the rim grows a skin of mineral across its surface, a lid of stone, as though the water were trying to seal itself in.

Where it rises, at the center, the surface domes — a standing hump of water the height of a man, wandering slowly around the bowl over the course of a day, collapsing here, rearing there. The whole basin breathes on a period of about seven seconds: the level swells, spills through the notch behind you in a brief white gush, draws back, swells again. The stream that leaves this place is born mid-roar, already a river in temperament, and it never keeps its bed. The rock grows back beneath it. Channels heal like wounds. The outlet migrates around the rim over the seasons, and the rim itself creeps outward and upward, year on year, accreting.

Because that is the rule here, the one law from which everything else follows: when this water meets air, it lays down stone. Agitation hurries it. Splashed surfaces crust in days; calm margins grow a floating film, pale as fat on broth, which thickens until its own weight takes it down — so the depths receive a constant slow snowfall of stone, and the basin fills from the bottom even as its walls rise. Nothing here erodes. Erosion has not begun. The place runs in the opposite direction: it is being deposited, layer on layer, by its own water, like a hot spring gone cold, like a bone still knitting.

Stand at the rim and look closely and you can watch it happen. The new stone buds in shelves and rims and little dams, cream-colored, faintly blue where some trace mineral runs through it, soft as hard cheese when fresh and workable — the keepers cut it with flint and hammered-copper saws — then greying, hardening, ringing under the knuckle within a week. Beneath every ledge, curtains of it hang like wax. Last winter's ice, trapped against a growing wall, now lies entombed a hand's breadth inside this week's stone. A marked stake driven at the waterline is swallowed in a month. The keepers' oldest gauge — a dressed obelisk at the notch, notched and dated in their scratch-script — stands buried to its second-highest ring, and it is only nine years old.

---

The sound is the weather of the place, and it is total.

It has layers. At the bottom, the drone: the upwelling itself, a chord with a pulse, swelling and falling on its seven-second breath, felt in the sternum more than heard. Over that, the white roar of the spill and the collapse of the wandering hump. And threaded all through it, high and continuous, the sound of the stone growing — a ticking, cracking, popping, like a great fire burning in reverse, like knuckles, like a million masons working just below hearing. Every few hours a bubble of deep gas vents from the shaft and the whole basin answers with a bass whump that you feel through your feet and taste, faintly, as metal and something like ozone.

Speech is useless at the rim. The keepers — when there are keepers — sign. They have a hand-cant, economical and graceful, and for anything complicated they write on slates hung by every door. They say that newcomers dream in noise for a month, and that after a season the dreams change: the chord begins to resolve, almost, into pattern — into something with the shape of meaning, the way a face nearly appears in clouds. They say you must not listen for it. They say long-stayers begin answering, under their breath, in the gaps between pulses.

So they built the hush-cells. Scattered around the rim among the other small structures — a dozen drystone huts, a windbreak, a drying shelf, the perpetual re-laid path of fitted stones — there are three low cells lined with a soft black stone from a single vein on the north wall, a stone that drinks sound. Inside, the roar becomes a rumor in the bones and you can hear, for the first time in days, your own heart. Everyone sleeps in a hush-cell. It is the first rule scratched on the first slate. The second rule: no one stays past a season.

The huts themselves are alive, in the slow way of this place. Built of quarried newborn stone, they keep growing — walls thicken, cracks knit, doorways narrow and must be re-cut every few years. Abandoned ones slump back into the rim like loaves rising too long, half-fused, their roofs swelling into domes. Nothing here stays built. Everything is still being built. The distinction the rest of the world draws between those two states does not apply.

---

Almost nothing lives in the water. It is too cold, too turbulent, too busy turning to stone — and there is no way in, no stream for anything to climb. What lives here came up from below.

The stonegrazers are translucent limpets, blind, pale as the water itself, and they appear on the fresh growth in their slow thousands after a strong fountaining week, grazing the soft new crust and leaving trails in tight cursive loops, like writing in a language of appetite. The keepers read them, half in earnest: where the grazers cluster, the growth is slowest; where the script is dense, the rim will hold. When the upwelling slackens, the grazers dwindle back toward the shaft, down into whatever lightless reservoir of themselves waits below.

And the shaft gives up other things. Rarely — a few times a season — the fountain ejects objects: nodules, dense black pellets, a perfect hollow agate sphere the size of a skull, stones shaped like spirals, like shells, like vertebrae — fossils of things that never lived. The mountain trying out forms. The keepers lay them on the drying shelf and do not pretend to understand them, and the finest ones they carry away, because a stone from the inside of the world, from the place where the world is still deciding, is held to be worth something everywhere below.

One pair of birds nests in the rim fissures — black, red-billed, chough-like, and apparently mute. They open their beaks against the roar and nothing reaches you; whether anything reaches each other is their own affair. The noise is their fortress: no fox, no cat, no climbing thing will hunt through that wall of sound to reach their young. They ride the updraft above the overflow plume, tipping sideways in the wet air, and they are, most days, the only movement in the place that is not water or stone.

On the rim, in the lee of the huts, the vegetation keeps its sparse tenure: cushions of moss gone nearly black, a few blades of sedge, and a silver lichen that grows only on stone less than a year old — a pioneer that loves youth and is slowly exiled by it, dying back as its own substrate matures, always migrating onto the newest growth at the rim's outer edge. Nowhere else in the range does it exist. Nowhere else is there anything young enough.

---

The light is plain mountain light, bright and unshadowed, cloud-shadows crossing a bowl of white water, caustics rippling up the walls at the calm margins. The air smells of wet stone and faint metal. The cold is steady and without drama. And the bowl itself, for all its fury, holds an emptiness at its center that is hard to name: forty paces of churn with nothing in it, a rim with one keeper or none, a sky with two birds. All this turbulence in service of no crowd, no swarm, no abundance — weather without a world. The roar is deafening, and the silence between presences is somehow louder.

Because here is the last thing the keepers know, the thing the gauges and the old slates prove. The feedback runs one way: the more the water churns, the faster the stone grows; the faster the stone grows, the narrower the aperture; the narrower the aperture, the harder the water drives through it, and the louder the chord. The oldest slates record the pitch of the drone, keeper to keeper, generation to generation — and it is rising. A semitone in a century, they reckon. The font is tuning itself upward like a instrument being closed.

One day — in a hundred years, or five hundred, the keepers disagree and sign about it in the evenings — the rim will meet itself over the shaft. The soft spot will knit. The mountain's skull will finish fusing, and the roar will stop, and the high country will go back to wind and raven and settling stone, and the river below will be born quietly from a seam no one will ever find.

The keepers do not sign about whether they are here to prevent that or to witness it. When asked, they give the same answer they give to everything, palms open, hands moving in the roar:

*We are listening it up.*

The stone ticks under your palm. The hump of water wanders past, rearing, collapsing. The chord swells, holds, breathes. Somewhere beneath you, unmeasured, the shaft goes down into the dark where the world is still deciding what shape to be — and it sends up, every seven seconds, cold and white and loud, its answer so far.
