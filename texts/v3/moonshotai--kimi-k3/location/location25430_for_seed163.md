You come over the pass at first light, and the basin is below you, shining.

It is forty kilometers long and eight across, dead flat, pale as paper, and it glitters — not in patches but everywhere, the whole floor set with thousands of small bright panes where standing water holds the sky. The walls that contain it are bare young rock, gray and streaked, rising in long unweathered slabs to a headwall where old snow hangs in gray-blue folds. The air up here is thin and cool and so clear that distance stops meaning much: you can see the far end of the basin as if through a lens, see the notch in the wall where the water leaves, see the tiny brick-red dots strung along the floor at intervals, each one wearing a feather of smoke.

And you can hear it. Even from the rim, even through cool morning air, the sound comes up: a slow, deep, many-tempo drumming, like several enormous hearts beating out of time with one another. Your first thought is thunder that never arrives. Your second thought, correct, is engines.

---

The land here is not finished. That is the thing to understand, and everything else follows from it.

The ice left not long ago — geologically speaking, yesterday. The walls still carry its handwriting: polish, striations, whole slabs sheared smooth as worked stone. Nothing has had time to rot or round. The scree at the foot of the walls is angular, freshly broken, the size of furniture and houses, utterly barren. There is no soil anywhere off the valley floor, no weathering rind on the boulders, no lichen beyond the first pale rosettes, the size of coins, putting in their centuries of work. The basin itself was a lake until recently, and in every way that matters it still is one. The lake is simply wearing a coat.

The coat is alive, and it has a name. The tenders call the plant quilt-moss, though it is not a moss and everyone who lives here knows it. It grows in tight hard cushions, each the size of a curled hand, each cushion a dense pack of tiny waxy leaves the color of silvered jade. The cushions interlock. They interlock for kilometers. From rim to rim there is, for all practical purposes, one plant — possibly one organism, one clone spread from a single lucky seed that landed on bare outwash and found the whole world empty and wet and bright and took it. It is the ground. There is no other ground. It floats on the old lake in a mat that is six meters thick in the ancient places and thin as blanket at the edges, and beneath it lies black cold water, and beneath that the settling peat, and beneath that the lake clay, holding its breath.

Walking on it is like nothing else. The skin — the tenders call the whole mat the skin — does not break underfoot, but it gives, deeply, and it answers late. You take a step and feel nothing but a soft receiving; then, a second later, a slow wave arrives back from somewhere out in front of you, and the nearest pond sloshes, and a pane of reflected sky thirty meters away shivers and resets. The ground responds to you on a delay, like someone thinking. In the thin spots, where the skin is young, you can see the water heave darkly beneath your boot with each step, and you learn to read the color — pale firm ground, dark hungry ground — the way sailors read depth.

The surface is not smooth. It has a grain. Ridges of firmer cushions, waist-wide, run in long contours across the floor, and between them lie the wet hollows — the flarks, the logbooks call them — lens-shaped ponds of still, clear, freezing water, some no bigger than a bathtub, some a hundred meters long. Strings and flarks, ridge and pool, repeating to the horizon, so that from the air the basin looks corded, like the flank of some enormous sleeping animal. At midday the flarks are intolerable to look at, each one a dropped piece of white sky. The tenders keep smoked-glass goggles hanging by every door.

There are no streams on the floor. The water has not yet invented them. It moves the way water moves in a thing too young for rivers — as seepage, as slow drowning and slow rising, the water table never more than a hand's breadth under the skin. At night the whole basin condenses. Mist forms against the cold cushions, beads on the waxy leaves, and drips; the valley waters itself in the dark, and at dawn the skin is jeweled and smoking as the light takes it apart. Most days burn clear by midmorning into that hard, high, doubled brightness. Weeks of identical weather pass. Change here is a slow leak, not an event.

---

If you want to watch the world being made, walk to the head of the basin, up under the snowfield, and then walk down-valley for two days.

At the top there is only gray: a raw outwash fan of silt and angular gravel, crossed by braided meltwater that shifts its threads hour by hour, white with stone-flour, freezing cold. Nothing lives there. Then, a kilometer down, the first cushions — single green fists on bare wet gravel, spaced meters apart, each one an argument. Then the arguments become islands, the islands become an archipelago trembling over sheeted water, and the archipelago becomes, at last, the continuous skin, thickening as you walk, the flarks appearing, the color deepening from brave bright green to the old silver of the deep mat. You are walking through about six hundred years. It takes two days.

---

The engines came later, but they came early — before the land was done — and that is the strange arrangement at the heart of the place.

Ninety-odd years ago a company was chartered to reclaim the basin. The word is in the charter and on the rusting plaque at Station One: RECLAMATION. The plan was to drain the floor, let the skin consolidate into peat, cut the peat into fields. There are maps in the cottage at the bottom station that show it finished: hedgerows inked in straight lines across what is today black water under trembling green. The company is gone. The trust that pays the tenders is a legal rumor nobody alive has read in full. But the engines were built to outlast their builders, and they are doing it.

There are six stations, spaced six kilometers apart down the length of the floor, small brick buildings the color of dried blood — the only red things in the basin — each one a single room that is mostly engine. They burn dried sod of the skin itself, cut from the edges of the drainage ditches and dried in herringbone ranks on racks beside each station, black bricks of the valley's memory going back into the valley's boilers. Each engine turns a flywheel taller than a man, at about forty strokes a minute, and each stroke lifts water out of a main ditch and sends it down-valley toward the Cut. They are unmuffled. They were never meant to be lived near. Standing beside one at full work is not a sound but a physical event — a concussion that arrives through the soles of your feet and the fillings of your teeth, that makes the flarks for a hundred meters around ripple in perfect time, that reduces conversation to a hand on a shoulder and a pointed finger.

The ditches are the only straight lines in a basin of curves, and they are the first flowing water the floor has ever known — brown, slow, purposeful, banked with the slumping cut edges of the skin, which bleeds and browns where it is wounded. They converge, station by station, into a main drain, and the main drain runs to the Cut: the notch in the far wall, the engineered sill, and below it the one waterfall, white water dropping into a gorge it is still carving, a thumb's width of lip retreating per year. It is the only river in the world that was built before its valley. Its roar is the only loud natural thing in the basin, and at the Cut the two loudnesses meet — the water going down, the engine-house above it laboring — and stand there arguing forever.

Station Four is losing a different argument. Thirty years ago its ditch silted and its fire was allowed to die for one season too many, and the skin came back. Now the building stands knee-deep in a green mound that is visibly, measurably, swallowing it; the chimney leans; the quilt-moss is up over the lintel. The tenders strip it for parts and let it go. It is the basin's demonstration of the alternative.

---

The sound organizes everything, and it is stranger than simple noise.

Close to a station: pain, vibration, the world reduced to one stroke at a time. Far away: the skin is a perfect acoustic blanket, soft as felt, swallowing footfalls, and there are no birds most of the year, no insects most of the year, no trees for the wind to say anything to — so the silence in the middle distances is thick, padded, almost unnatural. But the low frequency of the engines travels through the ground itself, and because there are five of them running at slightly different tempos, the basin keeps five slow clocks that never agree. Stand in the center of the floor and you hear them phase: now together in a great soft thump that you feel in your sternum, now unraveling into a long staggered rattle, now silent for four seconds — four seconds of a silence so complete you can hear your own pulse — before the next cycle begins. The tenders sleep to it the way sailors sleep to a sea. When an engine stops, everyone in the basin wakes up.

---

The tenders number four. They live in three cottages and a fuel shed near Station One, in the quietest pocket they could find, and they make their rounds on plank walkways that ride the skin like chained rafts, and on a hand-pumped trolley that runs a light rail along the main drain. They stoke, they oil, they adjust governors, they clear the ditches, they cut and stack the sod. They keep the logbooks, a shelf of them going back ninety years in six kinds of handwriting. A recent entry, in its entirety: *No. 2 governor hunting, adjusted. Water down at Stake 212, four cm since spring. Station Four roof gone in. Moths early.*

The moths are one of the basin's rare events, and rarity is the right word for everything alive here that is not green. The moths come for two weeks in high summer, white, day-flying, and then the floor seems to snow gently upward, and the tenders' lamps at night — the only lamps in forty kilometers — gather them in drifts against the glass. There are diving beetles in the flarks, slow as the water they live in. There is a plover, gray as the scree, a few pairs of them; its two-note call is so unusual a sound that the logbooks record sightings with dates, like comets. Sometimes, very high, a skein of geese crosses the basin without landing, going somewhere that is finished. There are no fish. There is nothing under the skin but cold black water and the slow rain of dead green falling toward peat.

---

Everything here is a race between two patiences, and nobody alive will see the finish.

The skin thickens by millimeters a year; the engines lower the water by centimeters a decade. Near the stations the mat sits lower, firmer, browner, the flarks draining, the first true ground in the basin beginning to exist around the brick. At the unworked western end the skin rises lush and trembling and does as it pleases. The survey stakes — numbered, dated, the oldest of them silvered wood from ninety years back — record the contest in their small steady figures, and the honest answer is that it is not decided. The valley is tilted between two futures: the hedgerowed fields on the charter map, or the green sea taking back its own, one drowned station at a time.

At night the light goes all at once, the way it does at altitude, and the cold comes down, and the stars arrive in the thin air in violent numbers. Frost forms on the skin with faint clicks. One window glows at the bottom of the basin, and in summer the moths find it. Down in the dark the engines go on at their forty strokes a minute, their beats phasing slowly out across the black floor, and at the Cut the young waterfall works its thumb's width a year, and the whole place — loud, empty, blazing by day, half lake, half land, half machine, half meadow — goes on with its long work of becoming.

It is not in a hurry. Nothing here is. It is a beginning, and it has the engines to prove it.
