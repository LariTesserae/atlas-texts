Somewhere in the deep karst, past crawls that flood to the ceiling and sumps that have never been sounded, the mountain keeps a single room.

It is not large. Held in the massif, it is a fist of emptiness closed in a mile of stone — but step through the last water-choke and stand inside it, and it opens like a held breath: a geode chamber, forty paces across, its walls lined in calcite and frozen flowstone, its floor one sheet of black water, still as poured glass except where the drops strike. And the drops strike always. Hundreds of them, from hundreds of points on the ceiling, each with its own interval and pitch, so the mirror of the floor is a field of overlapping rings that never resolve, never still, never change.

The stone here has finished. That is the first thing to understand about the place. The water that seeps down through the mountain carries exactly the mineral load the water in the room already holds — neither more nor less — and so the chamber sits at a perfect chemical standstill: nothing deposits, nothing dissolves. The stalactites are blunt, their growing done. The flowstone cascades hang mid-pour like wax that lost interest. Draperies of calcite that took ten thousand years to form will take ten thousand more and look the same at the end of them. The geology is not slow here. It is done, and the room exists in the long evenness after finishing.

The water is everywhere in its modest way. Seep-curtains bead down the walls in slow silver threads. The floor-sheet lies knee-deep on a hand-tall person, black, cold as snowmelt, fed from below by the sump-mouths that are the room's only doors. The air itself is wet enough to condense on skin, on stone, on the wick-holders of the lamps; breath fogs faintly at eight degrees, which is what it always is. There is no weather. There has never been weather. What the place has instead of weather is sound — but that comes later, because sound here is inseparable from the people.

The people are a hand high, pale as cave fish, with wide dark eyes that drink the dimness and find it sufficient. Nine hundred of them, and that number does not change. They built their city against the chamber's walls and into them, over generations uncounted: stacked niches of thin slate courses mortared with clay that set long ago and will not unset, rising from the lowest wharf-ledge to the smoke-darkened ceiling in one continuous fabric. There is no gap in it. Every span of wall is used — dwellings, granary-shelves of dried matting, cord-bridges sagging between outcrops, peg-rows, hanging vessels, cisterns of unfired clay that sweat in the wet and hold. Streets are ledges the width of two shoulders, or they are canals, black water lanes poled in coracles of stretched hide. From across the room the city reads as a single crust on the geode's inner face, dense as a wasp nest, dense as a wasp nest, dense as coral, and within arm's reach of any point in it there is a dwelling, a storage jar, a work-nook, a lamp, a person, and the sealed face of someone's ancestor.

The dead stay in the walls. That is the law of density here: when one of the people dies, they are folded into the family niche-stack and the opening is plugged with clay pressed by the dead one's own hands, printed in life for that purpose. So the city thickens very slowly inward with its ancestors, and the walls, being full of small chambers, are faintly resonant — knock anywhere and the knock comes back slightly shaped, slightly answered. The people hang their molts too, the shed skins of their growing, pegged in rows above the lintels like pale husks, so that every dwelling wears the record of everyone who has lived in it, life by life, skin by skin. The room is crowded with presences in three tenses: the living, the sealed, the shed.

There is nothing green. Nothing has ever been green. What grows here grows as film: the veils, slow bacterial mats in iron-ochre and salt-white, creeping across the seep-lines where dissolved minerals well out of the rock. The people farm them — that is the whole of agriculture. They lean thin shingles of slate against the weeping walls and crop the film off them in rotation, dry it, press it into cakes, twist its filaments into the thread that becomes cord, net, wick, cloth. The veils encroach; that is their nature; and so the people keep the lamp-lines. The lights of the city — hundreds of small grease-lamps burning rendered fish oil on veil-fiber wicks, each flame trimmed to a fixed height and never permitted to go out all at once — are not only light. They are a fence. The veils will not cross the warm lit threshold. Fire is the border between the city and the slow white hunger of the walls, and tending the flames is less a chore than a vigil, the oldest duty there is.

To an outside eye it would all be very dark — a handful of ember-glow shattering softly across calcite facets, doubled in the black floor. To the people it is noon, always, unchanging noon.

And it is loud. This must be said plainly, because the smallness of the place suggests quiet, and the truth is the opposite. The stone room takes every drip of its hundreds of drips and gives them back amplified, focused, folded into one another until the separate notes merge into a single standing chord that fills the chamber the way water fills the sump — completely, to the ceiling, without pause. The people call it the Chord, and it has not changed in living memory, which here means in any memory, because memory is pegged molts and sealed niches and the recited census of drops, and all of them agree: the Chord is as it was. There is a ledge high on the eastern wall where the acoustics gather it complete, every interval present at once, and the old ones climb there to sit in the fullness of it the way others might sit in sun. To be born here is to be born into a chord already sounding. Silence is not merely absent; it is unimaginable, a word the language does not have.

The people tend the Chord the way they tend the lamps. Catch-basins of hollowed stone sit beneath each drip-point, sized and placed to give each fall its note, and there are acolytes whose whole work is to move a basin the width of a hair, to replace a worn lip, to recite the census of drops from memory and confirm that what falls is what fell. Nothing ever needs adjusting. The adjusting is the point — a liturgy performed on an instrument already in tune, tuning as worship, maintenance as prayer.

They speak in the interstices. Their language rides the Chord's gaps: click-speech, taps, short whistled phrases fitted between beats, and beneath the talk the constant soft percussion of work — flaking chert, grinding mat-cake, the knock of cord-menders' shuttles. And here is a strange mercy of the room: it is a whispering gallery. A word breathed against the wall at one end travels the curved calcite and arrives intact at the other. In a city where everyone is within arm's reach of everyone, privacy is not distance. Privacy is quietness. Secrets cross the whole city in a whisper and no one between the speaker and the hearer catches more than a shaped breath.

Time does not pass here; it recurs. The short beat is the molt — a life counted in shed skins. The long beat is the Eel.

The fauna of the room are rare enough to be events: white fish flickering at the sump-mouths, a pale hopping thing that lives in the mat-gardens and is eaten only on the recitation-days. And the Eel. One Eel, vast to them — longer than a ledge-street — pale, ancient beyond any count of molts, circling the deep sump beneath the floor on a circuit so slow that a generation passes between its appearances. It does not sound in the Chord. It is felt: a pressure in the water, a low displacement in the body of the floor, a roundness in the foot. When the Eel passes, the people stop work and stand in it. Its passage is the longest unit of their calendar, the nearest thing the room has to a year, and its oil — rendered on the one occasion in memory when it was found still, once, and somehow alive again by the next circuit, or so the molts record — burns in the oldest lamps.

The rules of the world are few and total. The Chord does not change; anything that alters it is corrected before the Eel comes round. The dead stay in the walls. The lamps are never all out together. No one follows the water out through the sumps, because what goes down them does not return, except the Eel, which is the exception that proves the sumps go somewhere. The number of the living is nine hundred. And the language has one word that means room, and world, and now.

Stand in it a while and the frozen equilibrium stops feeling like stillness and starts feeling like tension perfectly resolved — the way a chord is not silence but many notes agreeing. A drop falls where the same drop has always fallen. A lamp gutters and is fed back to its exact flame-height. A molt dries on its peg beside the molts of a grandmother's grandmother. Somewhere beneath the black floor, in the dark below the dark, the Eel completes another turn, and the room — hidden, handspan-deep in the mountain's body, crowded, ringing, finished — holds its note.
