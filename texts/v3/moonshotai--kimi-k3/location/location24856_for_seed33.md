**The City at the Top of the World**

Begin below, where the air still has some thickness to it. The lowlands are plains of grit the color of old bone, and the grit moves — in slow rivers that follow the contours of nothing visible, in falls that pour off ledges for hours without a sound, in drifts rippled exactly as water would ripple them, though no water has ever fallen here. Rain in this world is grit. The rivers are dust. The cisterns, when you find them, are full of dust, and they have tide-lines.

The climb takes days. The air cools and thins and quiets, and the light does not change, because the light never changes. The sun here is a small exhausted coal called, by no one, the Ember — it slides along the horizon with the seasons and never climbs, so it is always dusk, and the stars are always out, even at what passes for noon. And then, from the final ridge, you see it along the spine of the massif: the City.

From a distance it could be geology. That is the first true thing about it. It could be wind-work, hoodoos, arches, the ordinary accidents of erosion on an enormous scale — until you are close enough to see that the arches meet streets, and the streets meet plazas, and the plazas are centered, and the stairs have treads.

The treads fit no foot that has ever existed. That is the second true thing: nothing here was built, because nothing here has hands. The City is grown.

---

The stone is silicate, bone-pale in the old districts, rust-dark where the chemistry runs different, black glass where something long ago got hot. It is cold to the touch, always — the whole world is cool, thin-aired, the cold of altitude rather than of season — except where the walls are alive, and those are faintly warm, like a wrist.

The fabric of the place is unmistakably urban and unmistakably skeletal. Streets run for miles and meet at sensible junctions. Plazas open at the right moments, dished by wind into shallow bowls. Staircases climb towers that have no rooms at the top, or rooms larger than the towers that contain them. There are vaulted halls open to the sky, colonnades whose columns are eroded to blades thin enough to see the dusk through. Bridges leap the chasms — and the chasms are the other axis of the world, miles deep, cut through the massif, and they do not have visible floors. They have light.

The depths glow. It is a cold light, phosphor without heat, some slow chemistry of the deep stone or some fossil luminescence left by whatever the builders' ancestors were, and it rises as the Ember sets. So every object in the City stands in two faint shadows: one cast downward by the dying sun, one cast upward by the luminous abyss, and where the two cross there is a small knot of true dark. Standing in the City at dusk's deepening, looking down into the chasms, you look down at the dawn. The world feels inverted, hung from its own sky. This feeling is not a metaphor. It gets worse the higher you go.

Some bridges were grown from both sides of a chasm and met in the middle, perfectly, a join you can walk across without finding the seam. Some missed. Two half-spans pass each other in the dark air, offset by ten vertical meters, never touching, and have passed each other like that for longer than the erosion has been counting. In the dead districts — this will matter — there is a bridge that meets a span it did not grow toward.

---

The silence has an inventory. It is thin-air silence, the kind that dies within arm's reach, but it is not nothing. There is the hiss of grit moving, which is the loudest regular sound in the world. There is the ticking of stone adjusting to the Ember's small heat, at dusk, like a house settling. There is, rarely, the far thud of a stonefall. There is, in season, a chime — a single glass note — when one of the drifters snags on a spire and a strand tears. There is the scrape of a skater feeding, if you are lucky enough to be near one, which you will not be.

And there is the hum, which is not a sound. It is below sound, below the threshold of the ear, and it lives in the teeth and the sternum and the hand pressed flat against a wall. It is strongest near living stone. It changes pitch over the course of years. The hum is the builders, speaking.

The builders are sessile, colonial, silicate — kin to coral, if coral had chosen stone and altitude instead of warm seas. They metabolize rock and trace gas and static, and they extrude architecture as exoskeleton, as excretion, as grammar. They never walked, yet their growth makes stairs. They never gathered, yet their growth makes plazas. A living wall gains perhaps a centimeter a year. A sentence of the hum takes perhaps a century. The City is not their construction. It is their conversation, made solid, and it has been going on since before the erosion began keeping score.

Most of them are dead.

---

This is the emptiness that defines the place. You can walk a district for four days and meet one skater. Count the region's population in anything but stone and it fits in a paragraph: the mineral films, in patches acres apart — the only flora, if flora is even the word, electrostatic crystal mats that are not lichen and not life as life is usually recognized, feeding on dim light and static and the City's slow exhalations. A few hundred skaters: low, wide, slate-pale, eight-limbed, grazing the films at a pace of one plaza per day, polishing the stone as they go, so that the cleanest avenues in the City are grazer trails. Eleven long patiences — glass-winged things that hang in tower-shadow for a season, folded, waiting, and then fall once, silently, and what they take is not certain: a skater, a drifter, something subtler, heat or hum. The drifters themselves, glass-fiber balloons that arrive on the grit-winds in season and snag on the spires by the thousand, and whether they are the builders' young or their spores or merely parasites of their chemistry is a question the world leaves open.

And the builders: mostly dead, mostly dormant, a distributed presence growing a wall here, an arch there, a centimeter a year.

The films bloom in one other circumstance, and it is the only water-event in the world. If a visitor breathes — and there are almost never visitors — the breath condenses as frost on the cold stone, and within hours a film flowers around it, a frost garden, concentric rings of crystal fed by that single accident of moisture. It dies by the next dusk. The cisterns, grown by the same grammar that grows everything else, hold only dust, and their walls wear mineral rings like the memory of water in a world that never had any.

---

Now the fraying.

Around living builders, the world is firm. Routes commute: the way there and the way back are the same length. Maps drawn in one decade are true in the next. Distances hold. A dropped stone drops.

The hum, it turns out, is load-bearing. The builders' slow conversation is what holds the world's rules taut, and where they are dead, the weave loosens. In the quiet districts — most of the City, now — the lapses begin small. A street takes longer to walk at noon than at dusk, reliably, by twenty minutes. A plaza is reached by climbing and left by descending, whichever direction you cross it. A room is measured from inside and from outside and the numbers do not agree and both are right. Grit falls upward near the highest, deadest spires, in slow streamers, and a dropped stone sometimes pauses on the way down, as if reconsidering. Maps of the dead districts decay — not the maps' fault; the territory is renegotiating.

Once, in a dead quarter, a stonefall was heard, and then, some minutes later, its dust plume rose over the rooftops. The sound had outrun the light. In the dead districts this is simply a thing that happens.

And there is the Dead Quarter proper, where no film grows and the dust-rivers curve around its edge in avoiding streamers and even the grit will not drift in. In the Dead Quarter, distance is a rumor. From its highest point you can see the City — the City you are standing in — spread out far below and across a chasm that does not exist on any approach, complete, unmistakable, glowing from beneath. The stars above it are the same stars as above you, in the same positions, which is wrong, because you are not above yourself. Nothing in the Dead Quarter is violent. It is worse than violent. It is unconvinced.

---

The processes run at every tempo, all at once. In a day: the Ember slides, the stone ticks, the glow rises out of the chasms, a drifter chimes, a skater crosses a plaza. In a season: the grit-storms come, the loud weeks, when the hiss rises to something almost like weather and the aerodynamic old ruins wake into a low infrasonic flute; the dust-rivers reroute through the streets; the films advance measurable centimeters; somewhere an arch that has been thinning for ten thousand years finally fails, with a thud, heard, in the living districts, after it is seen. In an epoch: the wind edits at the same tempo the builders speak, and the balance between them is ancient, established, settled — the City has been in its own state longer than any reckoning — but the trend is set. The dead quarters grow. The hum loses voices. The weave loosens street by street.

The City is a negotiation between the wind and the dream, and the wind is winning.

---

Stand finally on the highest spire, where gravity is about half-convinced and the grit streams upward past your shoulders in slow ribbons. Below, the chasms glow like an inverted morning. The districts stretch along the massif's spine — the Stair Fields, the Cistern Quarter, the Bridges, the Underplaza where the up-light is strongest — all of it wearing its two faint shadows, all of it empty, all of it organized, none of it built. Nothing moves. Then a drifter snags somewhere below and lets go its one glass note, and under your palm, through the cold stone, the hum completes a syllable it began before your kind existed.

You do not understand the word. You are not meant to. But the street under your feet understands it, and holds its shape a little more firmly for a while, and the stairs you climbed to get here — you will notice, on the way down, that they are not the stairs you descended, and that both of these things are true, and that the City, politely, at its own pace, is letting the difference matter less and less.
