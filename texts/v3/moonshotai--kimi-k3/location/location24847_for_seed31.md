From the warm low country you see it first as a wall: a long escarpment of pale, fresh stone holding up a strip of cloud. The cloud sits on the rim like a cloth on a table, and all day it does not blow away, because it is not weather passing through — it is weather being made. The plateau stands exactly at the height where the rising air gives up its water, and so the cliff top wears a permanent white hem, and the cliff face below it is stained dark in long vertical streaks where the overflow comes down. The falls never arrive at the bottom. They leave the rim as threads of white, thin to nothing within a few hundred feet, and drift back upward as mist. From below it looks as though the mountain is exhaling and inhaling at once.

The rock is young — young enough to be rude about it. The rim is a fresh fracture; the skyline has not been rubbed down. Basalt columns stand in the cliff like organ pipes newly unpacked, hexagonal faces unpatinated, edges that would take skin. The talus at the base is bare, angular, unrolled; it clicks and rings underfoot because no soil has yet come to muffle it. Above the basalt lies a thick bed of pale tuff, soft and porous, and the tuff is the reason for everything else here. It drinks. It takes the mist in through ten thousand pores, holds it, and weeps it out again along its bedding planes — so the whole upper cliff sweats, and the plateau's surface is a thing that leaks gently from everywhere toward anywhere. The drainage is immature. Water here has not yet learned to make rivers. There are no valleys, no incised streams, no organized downhill argument. Instead: sheet-flow, seep, pool. A skin of moving water a fingernail deep sliding over flat stone. Hollows that fill to their brims and stop. Runnels that begin nowhere and end in moss. The plateau is too young to have made peace with its water, and so the water simply stands, close to the surface, everywhere, waiting.

What grows on this is a short green — a green you could lie down in. Cushion mosses first, thick as a forearm, in every shade from bottle to lime; then sedge lawns, bright and close-cropped looking though nothing crops them; rushes in the standing water; liverworts plastered like wet green coins along the runnels. The soil beneath is only a hand deep, black, half peat, the plateau's whole history compressed into a layer you could cut with one spade-thrust. And rising out of the lawns, scattered wide, standing alone with great distances between them, are the rosette-trees: trunk-thick spikes of a pale pithy wood, each crowned with a wheel of long leaves that catch the mist and funnel it down their stems. Each one drips into its own small dark ring of moss. They flower — pale wands of bloom — for no one. There are no bees here. There is no birdsong in them, no nest has ever been built in their crowns. Wind sets their seed. The flowers open and close on their own schedule to an empty audience, and the fallen leaves lie where they fall, because nothing on the plateau eats anything it cannot reach with its own mouth, and there are no mouths here but human ones.

That absence is the first thing a visitor feels without knowing what they are feeling. No insect whine. No grazer's tearing of grass, no rustle of anything fleeing, no dung-smell, no musk, no scat on the paths. The green world is unmoving. It does not startle. Once in a generation a bird comes up out of the lowlands on some error of wind, and it is an event — people leave their work and stand in the open and listen to the single voice, and afterward they describe it to children who were not born, and the description has to carry a whole category of experience that the plateau otherwise does not contain. In the warm pools there is nothing with legs. The water holds only plant threads and light.

The town sits in this like a handful of stones set gently on wet cloth. It is sparse beyond what its population requires — five loose wards arranged around a broad central basin, each ward a scatter of eight or a dozen houses, and between the wards long open reaches of moss lawn with the rosette-trees standing apart like columns of a hall that was never roofed. The houses are built of tuff blocks cut soft from the cliff's upper bed, linteled with basalt, and roofed in living turf, so that from any distance a ward reads as a slightly lumpy meadow; you find a house by its door-stone and by the drip-line darkening the moss all around its eaves. Everything is plumbed. That is the town's true architecture, more than the walls: every roof pitches into a stone gutter, every gutter feeds a street runnel, every runnel runs to a cistern sunk in the lawn, every cistern overflows by a measured lip into the garden basins — shallow terraced pans of black mud where the wet-crops grow: a broad taro-leaf, water-celery, a rush whose root is the staple, a grain sedge cut by hand with obsidian. From the gardens the water goes to the seep-fields, wide aprons of bare tuff where it spreads and slows, and from the seep-fields it reaches the rim and goes over, and rises again as the cloud it came from. The town is a loop with houses on it.

There is no metal here, no wheel, no beast. Tools are obsidian — the plateau's own glass, knapped to edges sharper than any bronze — and bone, and woven sedge-fiber, and the pale pith-wood of the rosettes, which is too soft for beams but perfect for sluice-gates and floats, since it swells watertight when wet. Goods move on sledges dragged over the moss, which when wet is nearly frictionless; the sled-ways between wards are polished dark-green tracks, damp at every hour. And distance is answered with light: each house keeps a disk of polished obsidian, and the sparse town talks to itself in small silent flashes — a flicker from the east ward to say the runnel is cleared, a flicker from the comb-watch to say the cloud has come in thick. In a place this quiet, nobody wants to carry their voice across open ground. A shout here would not be rude. It would be a kind of disaster.

Because the sound of the plateau is almost nothing, and everyone who lives here has been shaped by that almost-nothing. The baseline is a hush so complete that you can hear your own warmth. Over it, the small water-sounds: the tick of condensation off a lintel, the whisper of sheet-flow, the seep-hiss inside a tuff wall. No wind, most hours — the cloud pours in through the great notch in the rim and dies on the flat top, and the few leaves there are barely stir. So the town has rules, and the rules are mostly about sound. Work that must be loud — the dressing of tuff blocks, the knapping of glass — is done one site at a time, in the sanctioned hour, and the whole plateau hears the single worker's tapping, and its soft returned echo off the cliff at the notch, like the mountain answering in a whisper. Messages go by barefoot runner or by flash. Songs are hummed through the nose. Children learn to walk the way everyone walks: heel-light, moss-first, unhurried.

The cloud keeps an appointment. Every morning it comes through the notch, and there, in the gap where the rock funnels it, stands the comb: ranks of tall frames strung with sedge-fiber, renewed each turning of the maintenance round, and older fins of standing stone bearded with moss. The mist beads on the fibers, gathers, and drops into a long lip-channel of tuff, and the sound of it — hundreds of small drips at different heights and pitches, tick, tick-tok, tick — is the nearest thing the plateau has to music. People walk out to the comb at dawn the way people elsewhere go to hear an organ. They sit in the warm wet and listen to their water being harvested out of the air.

And every dawn, at the broad shallow basin at the town's center — the Mirror, a natural sump improved by generations of patient carving, ringed with a pavement of basalt — a reader steps to the edge and looks at the Line. The Line is a band of darker stone inlaid around the Mirror's rim, and all around every cistern in every ward there is one like it, and the water stands at it. It stands exactly at it. It has stood exactly at it through every reading anyone can remember, and through every reading recorded in the Ledger-house, where the days are kept as knotted sedge cords hung in long ranks — one knot per reading — and where the knots are all the same knot, thousands of them, tens of thousands, a history of a quantity that does not vary. The oldest walls of the Ledger-house bear witness that it was not always so: high up, a flood-stain from the town's youth; low down, a drought-groove, a line the water fell to once, long ago, before the people learned the whole arithmetic of their place. Since then: level. Level. Level.

The equilibrium is not a property of the plateau. It is a practice. What the comb yields, the seep-fields are permitted to return — no more. What the gardens take, the night's condensation is expected to replace, and if the gardens want more they do not get more; the garden area is fixed, the cistern count is fixed, the population is fixed — a roof stands empty before a child is born into it, never the other way. Nothing new is built; things are renewed, block by identical block, fiber by fiber, each house forever becoming itself again. And because nothing on the plateau eats the fallen leaf, the leaf must be lifted by hand, or it will dam a runnel and shift a number somewhere: so there are leaf-walkers, whose whole work is to carry away what, in any other world, a goat or a grub would have quietly taken care of. Nothing here is allowed to accumulate, and nothing is allowed to run short. The hush, the one-hammer rule, the flash-signals, the morning reading — all of it is the same covenant. This is a town that holds a note.

There is a daily pulse inside the stillness, the same pulse every day, and it stands in for seasons. Dawn: the reading, the cook-fires lit all at once so that a brief forest of smoke-threads rises straight up through the windless air and is gone by mid-morning. Midday: the cloud thickest on the rim, the light a bright silver-grey, the sun a pale coin you can look at directly, everything dripping. Evening — the one extravagance — the cloud sinks below the rim, and the plateau top stands suddenly in clear gold light, dry-eyed, above a cloud-sea that laps the cliffs from beneath; the wards take their cords out of the Ledger-house to air, and people walk to the edge to look over at the warm hazy lowlands, and at the falls leaving the rim below them and un-becoming, rising, returning. In the morning that same water will press through the notch and bead on the comb-fibers and drip into the channel, and a reader will kneel at the Mirror, and the water will be at the Line, and the knot tied for that day will be the same as all the others, and tying it will not feel like nothing. It will feel like the whole work of the world, done one more time.

Stand at the rim at last, in the warm wet air, in the silence, and watch a drip fall from a moss lip into a stone basin below. The basin is full to its mark. The drip raises one slow ring, and the ring goes out, and the surface comes back to the mark, and stays there.
