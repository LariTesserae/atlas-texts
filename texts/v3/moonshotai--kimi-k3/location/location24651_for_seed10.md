The mountains are still here, but only as depth. Their roots hold up a plateau broad as a small country, high enough that the cloud deck rests on it like a lid, and over the old summits — ground down long before the ice ages, ground further since — there lies a single continuous body of vegetation. Call it the Felt. It is not many plants. It is one tissue: moss, sedge, liverwort, woody creepers, fused over the millennia into a mat kilometers thick in the drowned valleys, thinner over the buried peaks, unbroken from any horizon to any other. Nothing on it is built. Nothing is even fallen — there are no logs, no stones, no dead wood standing. Everything that ever protruded has been taken down into the mat and become it. Felt is the final form of matter here. Everything else is on its way.

The first thing that reaches you, before sight, is the throb. It comes up through the soles of your feet, a slow double beat, a working. The plateau is running. Under any given mile of the mat there are pump-knots — dense warm tangles of rhizome that beat like hearts, peristaltic, patient — circulating water, air, and black slurry through ducts you will never see. The Felt is plumbed. It is valved and pressurized. It breathes out through vents: chimneys it has grown, not built, tubes of dense peat and hollow stem, and what they exhale is the only warmth in the world. The breath hits air that sits perpetually at freezing and condenses at once, so the vents stand as pillars of steam scattered far apart across the plain — vertical weather, each plume visible for a day's walk, each one leaning the same way in the high wind. Pale birds circle inside them, riding the updrafts, and their cries come down through the steam attenuated and directionless.

The light never amounts to day. It is the last hour of a November afternoon at all hours: a brighter smear sliding along the underside of the cloud lid, sourceless glow, no shadows anywhere. Distance has no edges in it. The horizon breathes — now near enough to touch, now withdrawn beyond belief — and things out near it refuse to declare their size. A dark shape at the limit of sight might be a grazer at half a mile or something far larger much farther on, and the Felt will not help you decide. It prefers to place its rare events at exactly that unreadable range.

Because the events are rare. That is the strange economy of the place: total vegetation, and almost nothing happening at any given point. You can walk a day, two days, over the same black-bronze, bottle-green mat — springy, saturated, every step pressing out tannin-dark water that climbs back up around your boot — and encounter nothing but one old hoofprint filling slowly with black water, and the lint-pale tufts of cotton grass nodding in their millions, and the throb. The features of the region stand days apart. The Choir: a field of hollow pipes the Felt has grown to the wind's height, which sound in slow chords that drift out of tune as the stems grow and are retuned only by die-back. The Mill-Race: a spiral of slush channels kilometers wide, drawing the plateau's meltwater down into a swallow-hole where the Felt drinks itself, audible long before it is visible, a ripping hum with a whine in it like distant machinery. The digestion fronts — seams of Glow-Rot, creeping at meters per day, where the mat is processing its own depths. At night these show. The nights are barely darker than the days, but the working seams phosphoresce dim green, so after dusk the land is brighter than the sky, veined with cold light wherever the Felt is laboring.

It is loud. The loudness is the process working, and it is layered: the throb underfoot; the hiss and roar of vent-steam; the Choir's slow chords; the tick and ping of water refreezing; the far-carrying calls of animals in the cold dense air; and underneath everything, at the edge of hearing, the black trickle of the mat draining itself. Each dawn adds a movement. Every night, needle-ice grows up through the mat and heaves it an inch; every morning the sun-smear warms it and the whole plateau settles back with an hour of crackling, a regional sound, the world clearing its throat. Shift change. And sometimes, with no warning anyone has found, the sound goes out of a place. The throb dips, the trickle stills, the birds leave. A sucked-quiet zone, a held breath. That is the only herald of an eruption, and every living thing that can move reads it correctly and goes.

The fauna are abundant the way a library is abundant — the catalogue is enormous, the individual copies scattered miles apart. Herds of mowers, humped and wide-footed, move across the mat grazing it down in slow lines; you see them as dots, and the healed strip behind them days later as a faint darker swath. Their hoof-pits fill with black water and, over years, widen into the eyes — the still pools that give the plateau its gaze. The eyes hold water cooled below freezing that does not freeze: perfectly liquid, perfectly still, until it is touched. A touch propagates. The whole pool locks in seconds, and the ice keeps the shape of the disturbance — a standing ripple, a splash arrested mid-crown, the leaping arc of whatever fell in — so the older eyes are crowded with pale frozen statuary until the weak noon thaw slumps it all back to stillness. Pale eel-things cruise beneath, tolerant of the cold, unhurried. At the vents, in the one warmth, midges boil up in fierce brief columns, and everything that eats midges is there too, and everything that eats what eats midges, stacked in the steam.

And then there are the others, which double the species list without being alive in the usual sense.

The Felt preserves what dies in it — cold, acid, airless: everything that has ever ended here is still here, tanned dark, intact, stacked in the deep mat with the oldest at the bottom. Depth is time on the plateau. And the Felt, in its labor, digs itself. A patch of mat goes quiet, then domes — sometimes fast enough that the gas-driven burst is heard an hour's walk away, a boom rolling across the flats — and out of the dome comes whatever the eruption has brought up. Sometimes it is only peat and black water and old wood, slumping immediately. Sometimes it walks.

The reissued come up tannin-dark, leaking black water, made of themselves and of the mat both. Mower-shaped things from centuries ago, grazing with half a routine. Great-antlered things with no living analogue, reenacting fragments — courtship, migration, alarm — at wrong speeds, missing pieces, running a few steps of a loop and then stalling mid-motion into a statue. They hold for hours or a day. Then the erosion that rules the plateau catches up with them all at once: their edges soften, their features weather like statues in accelerated rain, centuries arriving in an afternoon, and they slump, and the mat flows back over them. Nothing persists. The plain stays flat by violence.

The churn-gulls know the schedule better than anything else. They gather out of the empty sky within the hour, raucous, marking every fresh eruption for kilometers around, and they eat the half-made with no evident harm. It is one of the uncountable small wrongnesses: the gulls are nourished by things that were dead before the gulls' kind existed.

The large wrongness stands plain, if you watch an eruption closely. The Felt exudes more than it contains. A dome the size of a house gives up a whole herd, walking out one after another for an hour, dark and dripping, more body than could ever have fitted in the hillock. The archive underfoot is perfect — every specimen intact — but the retrieval is corrupt, unindexed, and generous beyond geometry. Anything can come up. From how deep, there is no asking. The buried mountains are down there somewhere too, in the mat's own terms, and on the rarest occasions an eruption brings up something smooth and grey and enormous that is not flesh at all, that weathers in a day like a cliff in a million years of rain and goes back under.

What rules the place, then, is a short list. Nothing is built; everything is issued. Everything issued is recalled — the half-life of a form is hours, of a body millennia, of the Felt itself, so far, forever. Silence is never peace; silence is a held breath. The only warmth is breath also, the working's exhalation. Distance is a rumor. And the churn — the pumping, the venting, the daily heave and crackle, the eruptions and the slumping and the resorption — is not a disturbance of some older calm. It is the calm. This turbulence is the oldest thing here, the stable state, the final form the world settled into and has held since the mountains stopped being mountains.

Stand on it at night. The cloud lid presses low; the Glow-Rot seams burn dim green to the unreadable horizon; a vent plume leans in the dark with pale birds asleep somewhere in its updraft; the throb comes up through your boots, patient, industrial, purposeless, and far off — at whatever distance the plateau has decided distance means tonight — something gives a long cracking boom, and the gulls begin to rise.
