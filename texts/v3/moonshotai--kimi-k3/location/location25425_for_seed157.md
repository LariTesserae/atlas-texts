The cloud has been the same grey for the whole climb, and when you finally cross the lip of the hanging corrie — lungs burning a little in the thin air — the light does not change. That is the first thing. The dimness inside the hollow is the same dimness as outside it, as if the place and the cloud share a single atmosphere, one grey thought. It is always the same hour here, and the hour is dusk.

The hollow is small. Two hundred paces would take you across the wet floor of it; a strong throw would cross the standing water. It is one room of a place — roofed by cloud, walled on three sides by rock that has finished falling. The corrie is old past measuring. Every slab of the scree is seated at its final angle and sealed under moss; the back wall is rounded like something handled for centuries; the lip you crossed is polished. Nothing here is in the process of becoming. The geology has arrived at what it is and stopped.

Water does not fall in the hollow; it is issued. Partway up the back wall, a band of dark shale lies between the limestone beds, and along that seam the mountain lets its water out — not in falls but in a hundred slow seep-lines, each one bearded with moss, each moss lip ending in a drip. Every drip has its own interval and keeps it. Standing in the middle of the floor you can hear the whole wall at work: a sparse chord of small notes, plink and plik and the occasional deeper tap where a drop falls into a pool instead of onto moss. It never speeds up. It never resolves. It is less like music than like a clock with many hands, all of them patient.

The floor itself is a floating mat — sphagnum in wine-red and gold and deep green, quilted so thickly over the hidden water that it trembles underfoot, a slow shiver that travels outward from each step and dies at the wall. There is no bare surface anywhere. Every stone carries moss; every moss carries the silver script of snail slime and the snails that write it; every snail, looked at closely, carries mites. The pools set into the mat stand slightly proud of it, held up by surface tension, as if the water were too polite to spill. Their levels could calibrate an instrument. They have never been higher or lower by the width of a hair.

And here is the strangest economy of the place: no rain falls. The cloud is the source and the cloud is the destination. It combs itself to threads on the ridge above, condenses on every moss frond and stone face, gathers, seeps, pools — and leaves over the lip as a single white thread of stream that drops into the same grey cloud that fed it. The mountain is drinking itself, endlessly, and is never more thirsty and never less.

The light has no direction. It arrives from everywhere the way cold does, and nothing casts a shadow — not the rocks, not the stunted sedges, not you. This unsettles you more than the dimness does. The only light with a source you can point at lives under the overhangs of the seep-wall, where the rock is constellated with small blue-green lamps: glow-worm larvae, fixed and patient, each one burning at the same low wattage forever. The dark pools keep a copy of every lamp, so the hollow owns two night skies — one of stone, one of water — and both are still.

For all that it is quiet, the hollow is crowded past belief. Turn any stone and there is a salamander beneath it, black and unhurried, regarding you with the calm of something that has never once needed to hurry. Leeches glide in the pools like commas looking for their sentences. Water boatmen row the middle depths. Under the mat's edge the water is thick with amphipods, and every handful of moss you lift yields a census: beetles, springtails, a centipede the colour of old ivory, worms, larvae, more snails. The sundews that stud the mat never wait long — their glistening leaves are always mid-meal — and on the seep-wall the butterworts lie flat and pale against the rock, digesting at the same unhurried rate at which the midges arrive. Nothing flowers in its turn here, because there are no turns. Things flower continuously, a few at a time, forever.

The larger lives are few, fixed, and known. In the deepest pool lives one trout, dark as the peat-water, old past any reasonable span. It rises to the same corner of its pool, takes something off the surface, and sinks; the interval between its risings is one of the things that never changes. A pair of dippers keep the seep-wall, walking down into the thread-currents as casually as other birds step along a branch. Every so often — measured by nothing outside itself — one of them sings: a brief rippling thread of notes, astonishing in the hush, and then not repeated. A water shrew works the runways it has always worked. And on a flat stone near the wall, generations of thrushes have broken snails: the stone is cupped where countless beaks have struck, and the shell-drifts around it never grow, because the lime dissolves back into the moss at exactly the pace new shells arrive. Beside the runways lie stems stripped by something that probes for larvae — small tools, used and dropped, the only made things in the hollow, if you can call them made.

For minutes at a stretch the silence is so complete you hear your own pulse in your ears. You are the loudest thing here. You are also the only clock.

Because the rule of the hollow — the one rule, from which everything else follows — is that nothing is added and nothing is lost. Every arrival has a departure already scheduled against it; every death has a hatching timed to it; every drip is replaced before it lands. Perturb the place and it unmakes your disturbance with terrible courtesy: your footprints sink a finger's depth into the mat and fill with water the colour of weak tea, and by the time you look back the sphagnum has pressed up again and the prints are gone, as though the hollow has politely forgotten you.

There is one place where you can see what this costs, or rather what it keeps. At the outflow, the stream has undercut the mat, and there the peat stands open in a dark face taller than you are. It is all in there: leaf, wing-case, seed, husk, small bone, laid down in annual skins no thicker than a thumbprint, compressed into a black archive. You can read ten thousand years in an arm's length. And the reading is this: the same year, ten thousand times. The same weight of leaf-fall, the same hatch, the same census. The equilibrium did not settle here recently. It is not a balance the place found and might lose. It is the place's permanent condition, and the peat is its proof. Time does not pass in the hollow. The only direction time goes is down.

The air stays cool enough to keep your breath faintly visible, and it smells of wet stone, crushed sphagnum, peat-reek, and something faintly ammoniac that is the smell of a great many snails. A cold breath drains constantly down the seep-wall, always at the same speed, carrying the drip-chord to you in slow waves. Nothing comes over the lip. Nothing goes out but the water and, eventually, you.

Stand still long enough and the hollow returns to its one held note — the drips keeping their intervals, the glow-lamps burning their low blue, the old trout rising in its corner on schedule you cannot read. You will leave, because you must, carrying time out with you like something smuggled. The mat will close over your tracks. The pools will not have fallen or risen by a hair.

It is not waiting for anything. It arrived, long ago, at what it is.
