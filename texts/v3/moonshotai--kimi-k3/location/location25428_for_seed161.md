The plain comes first, because the plain is almost everything.

It is the floor of a finished ocean — salt and gypsum and dried mineral mud, planed so flat and so long ago that the eye can find no purchase on it anywhere. It runs to every horizon in a dim pale sheen, faintly warm to the touch, crusted in polygons the size of rooms. Nothing moves on it except a single standing wind, which has blown from the same quarter, at roughly the same warm walking-pace strength, for longer than anyone's records reach. The light is low and bronze and tired. The sun never climbs. It circles, leaning at the same shallow angle all day and all year, a dull red coin behind a permanent haze of ancient dust, so that the whole world is lit like the last hour before dusk — and stays that way, and has stayed that way, for longer than the salt remembers.

And out of this flatness rises the one vertical thing left in the world.

You hear it long before you see it clearly. The standing wind strikes the mountain's fluted western face and the whole massif sounds — a chord, deep and loud and unvarying, one note felt in the breastbone, one in the teeth, one a thin high thread at the edge of hearing. The notes slide against one another, and every few minutes a slow swell of loudness rolls across the plain like the breathing of something enormous and calm. Travelers hear the Voice for three days before the mountain resolves out of the haze: a dark fluted column, near two thousand meters of almost sheer rock, the last hard bone of a range the wind and the long ages ground down to nothing. Around it, faint on the salt, lie the low rings and domes that mark where its brothers stood — the graves of mountains. Far off at the horizon's limit, two or three other stubs still poke above the plain, dark and, as far as anyone alive knows, silent.

The mountain is worn the way only the oldest things are worn. Its flanks are carved into organ-pipe flutes and honeycombed with hollows; its skin is case-hardened and varnished dark, polished near the base by salt crystals the wind has sandblasted against it since before people. It is not tall the way young mountains are tall. It is tall the way a stump is tall — everything around it removed. It is the final form of a mountain, and it has settled into that form completely.

The top is small. Two hundred paces long, nowhere fifty wide: a crown. And the crown is full.

Everything that still lives in this world lives here, or comes here, because this is where the water is. Not on the plain — the plain has none but a few bitter brine seeps a day's haul out, crusted black and red, that swell every few years into brief sour ponds and bloom with salt-midges. Not in the sky — it has not rained in longer than the oldest carving remembers. The water is *inside* the mountain: the Old Water, sealed in deep chambers when the world was young and wet, still seeping through the stone in threads too slow to watch; and the working water, which is the same few thousand gallons used over and over, breathed and sweated and drunk and washed in, caught again by the great condenser shafts where wind-driven fans push the town's warm moist air down into the cool deep rock and the stone sweats it back out, drop by drop, into cisterns.

In the deepest cistern hangs the Measure: a marked column, and on it the water level, and beside it a line scored for every generation going back into the dark of the stairwell. The level falls a finger's width in a lifetime. Nobody pretends otherwise. Nobody panics either. The timescale is too large for panic; the mountain holds enough for a hundred generations more, and a hundred generations is, by any accounting a person can hold in their hands, forever.

So the crown is dense — dense the way a reef is dense, every surface occupied, every volume stacked. The town has no name, because where else would anyone live. Its stone cells are built seven towns deep, each on the bones of the last, walls mortared with gypsum from the plain, roofs of scalebark timber so precious that a beam is an inheritance. The soil of the gardens is a meter deep, black, older than any wall — built grain by grain over the centuries from compost and ash and ground eggshell and bird guano and the planted dead, and it is the true wealth of the place, tended like an elder. Out of it grows the green of the world: not a soft green, nothing here is soft, but a dense, dark, waxy tangle — ropeleaf vines caulking every wall, cushion-plants furred over every roof, squat scalebark trees with leaves like overlapping black scales, swollen trunks hoarding their water against a drought that has already lasted forever. It is lush the way a wrung cloth is still wet. Lush the way the last orchard is lush.

Birds hang in the standing updraft on the windward face in their permanent wheeling gyre — plain-kites, broad and patient, riding a column of rising air that has not faltered in living memory, some of them, the old ones say, hardly landing for years. Ledge-fowl crowd every shelf and eave in their thousands, half-wild, half-kept, and the town runs on their eggs: eggs for food, shells for lime, feathers for everything. Lizards stitch the warm rock. Bees work the dark blossoms and pay out wax and honey, and wax is light here — in the dim bronze days the town's windows burn with small flames, tallow and beeswax, and the crown glows against the brown sky like a coal. Moths, beetles, midges in their season, rock-rats in the wall-hollows: the place crawls and flutters and croons, crowded layer on layer, life packed into the one square acre of the world that can hold it.

It is loud. It is always loud. The Voice underlies everything — that vast chord, with its slow minutes-long swells — and over it the town adds its own drone: the wooden wind-rotors on every ledge and roof-ridge, hundreds of them, driving the lifts and the millstones and the smith's bellows and the condenser fans, their stone bearings humming and knocking at a hundred pitches; and the fowl muttering, and the kites crying down the wind, and hammers on stone, and babies, and bells. Children learn to sleep inside it the way children elsewhere learn to sleep through rain. The people speak in a low register that carries beneath the chord, and sign with their hands in the gardens when the swells roll through, and the old often go gently deaf and do not much mind. Silence is the thing nobody here has ever heard. Once, the oldest stories say, the wind faltered for an hour, generations back, and the whole town woke in terror and stood in the streets waiting for the world to end — and then the Voice returned, and they wept with relief, and someone cut the date into the Measure, down in the dark.

They are a patient people, and their world rewards patience. The sun walks its slow circle and the mountain's shadow sweeps the plain like the hand of a clock, and down at the base, around the Long Stair's foot, they have laid out a ring of marker-stones that the shadow touches hour by hour — the one great public work on the plain, maintained by the same families for centuries. Their tools are stone and bone and shell and ceramic, and their metal is sky-iron: meteorites picked off the white salt where they lie dark and obvious, hoarded, reforged a dozen times, a knife passed down until it is worn to a sliver and married to a new shard of fall. Down the Long Stair — two days of switchbacks and bolted wooden walkways, with resting cells carved into the rock and the Voice swelling as you descend past the great flutes — the salt-crews go, dragging sledges, to cut salt blocks and glean the wind-polished stones and, in the midge years, to harvest the swarms at the brine seeps, when the plain briefly remembers being a sea and the kites stream out over the flats and return fat.

The year has no seasons, only a slight leaning of the light, brighter by a hair, dimmer by a hair. Change comes at the pace of stone. A flute's lip wears and one note of the Voice deepens across a century, and the Keepers of the Voice — the oldest guild — climb out on ropes and set a new carved plug into the opening, and the chord shifts back, and that is an event, that is history, grandchildren hearing a note their grandparents never heard. A cell is rebuilt. A scalebark reaches the girth where it may be cut. The Measure falls its finger's width.

On the clearest days, when the haze thins, you can stand at the crown's edge and see the two or three far stubs of the brother-mountains at the edge of the world, and wonder whether any lamp burns there. Once, the stories say, lamps flashed between the peaks, a slow bright speech across the dark. The last answering signal from the nearest brother went out eleven generations ago. No one has yet stopped watching.

For this is the world in its final form, and the town on the crown is not waiting for anything, and is not in decline the way the young understand decline. It is complete. The water falls a finger in a lifetime and the Voice holds its chord and the wind comes from its one quarter, warm as breath, and the fowl settle crooning onto the ledges as the dim sun swings low, and all along the dark honeycombed shaft the rotors turn, and the lifts creak up with salt, and the wax-lights come on one by one in the little windows, and the mountain sings its single unhurried song — loud, and slow, and in no danger of finishing soon — over a world that has finished becoming, and become this.
