The glacier left this hanging valley only a few centuries ago, and you can tell. The bowl is still raw — moraine heaped like broken crockery, boulders with edges sharp enough to cut, no soil anywhere that the valley did not have to invent for itself. The ice sits just above the rim now, a white remnant, and it weeps. All day it weeps. Meltwater threads down fresh grey rock on every side, and the fog it feeds never entirely lifts. The air hovers a breath above freezing by day and drops below at night — cold that wets rather than cold that seals. Your breath fogs. Everything beads. Everything drips.

And the valley answered all that water with green.

From the moraine lip you look down into a bowl perhaps four hundred paces across, and the green is total. Not a cover — a substance. Plants on plants on plants, layer stacked on layer with no gap anywhere: crust, felt, cushion, reed, tower, crown, and every surface of every one of them furred with something smaller. The only open ground is the youngest rockfall at the walls' feet, and even that is speckled with pioneer crust, being taken. The vegetation is not scenery here. It is the substrate, the architecture, and the citizenry, and it has organized itself into something your mind will not stop calling a city.

You enter through the gate, which is a sluice: the gap in the perimeter hedge where the outflow leaves, running milky with rock flour. The hedge itself is a wall in the oldest sense — shoulder-high, dense as woven wicker, leafed in small dark scales, and it rings the entire bowl with almost no break. It sheds the wind. That is its purpose, and you feel it the moment you step through: the high thin whistle of air over the rim cuts off, and inside there is a hush so complete it has texture. The felt-moss underfoot absorbs your steps. The city ends exactly where the wind begins.

Inside, the lanes. They run radially, more or less, from the gate and two lesser seeps toward a central plaza, and they are paved in a springy black-green moss felted so tight it takes weight like turf. They are kept clear. Not swept — kept. Each lane is a shallow gradient, a water path first and a road second, and the pavement plant itself exudes something that discourages its neighbors from encroaching, so the lanes maintain their own edges. At this hour, mid-morning, they are merely damp. By afternoon they will run an inch deep, and the city will become canals.

Overhead, the towers: pillar clubmosses, twenty meters of straight trunk armored in diamond-pattern bark, a flora from deep time colonizing fresh rock. They stand in colonnades along the lanes, their crowns interlacing into a partial vault, and the light that comes through is green-grey and sourceless, like light through old bottle glass. It is always dim in here. The walls are too steep, the day too short, the fog too loyal, and the vault drinks what little gets through. Between the pillars stand the jointed pipe-reeds in dense ranks — hollow, segmented, cold green — and if you press your ear to one you can hear water moving inside it. They are the plumbing. At night, when the melt slows, the tower crowns comb the fog and shed it in fat drops; the reeds catch and conduct it; the whole city is a waterworks that grew itself. Catchment, conveyance, storage. Nothing here is a device. Everything here is infrastructure.

The houses are the hearth-cushions. Dome-shaped, shoulder-shaped, the size of huts and haystacks, each one a single plant — a mound of tiny leaves packed so densely the surface feels like upholstered stone, like broccoli, like coral. Their lineages are ancient; their bodies here are young, grown fast into the new ground. Inside each cushion, decomposition never stops, and so the interiors are warm — warm as a held hand — and honeycombed with damp chambers where water and seed and yesterday's dead leaves are all being slowly rendered into heat. Warmth is communal in this valley. Anything that stays liquid through the night is within a body's reach of a cushion. At dusk they exhale: a slow, visible breath of warm vapor from their pores, and the city makes its own fog, smelling of steeping peat and something faintly, unsettlingly animal.

The plaza is a cistern. Waxy rosettes the size of washbasins ring a black pool, each cup holding clarified meltwater, rock flour settled to a pale clay at the bottom — the valley's first clay, its first true soil, manufactured in flower-cups. At dawn the cups and the pool wear skins of ice no thicker than a fingernail pairing, and as the dim sun finds the rim they begin to tick. That is the loudest hour: a soft, random ticking and pinging all across the plaza, drips quickening on pitched notes depending on what they strike — leaf, stone, water, ice — and still it qualifies as quiet. Mostly the city sounds like this: a trickle somewhere, a drop somewhere else, a faint groan from the ice above the rim, your own breathing. Once in a day, perhaps, a clatter of rockfall from the young walls, sharp and brief, and the hush closing over it like water.

You can stand an hour here and see nothing move but water. The fauna is rare enough to feel rumored. But at dusk the keepers come out: slugs the length of your hand, slate-blue and amber, unhurried, grazing the biofilm from the lanes. They are the road crew. Their slime knits the pavement felt, and their grazing keeps the lanes' chemistry true, and their slow bodies distribute seed from cushion to cushion. Later, in near-dark, the frost-moths fly — fur-bodied, heavy, the only fast thing in the valley — whirring between the rosette blooms on an errand of pollination that is the city's whole economy of sex. Their wingbeat is, some evenings, the loudest sound. A dipper works the milky outflow at the sluice, bobbing on a wet stone as if the cold were a rumor it had declined to believe. And a few times a season a hawk crosses the rim, and for one hour the moths' bodies drift down through the vault like grey ash, and the lanes take them in, and by the next day there is no trace. Nothing dead stays above ground here. It is taken down into the warm dark and becomes the heat.

The day has a pulse and the city rides it. Dawn: rigid, ticking, frost feathered across the felt. Midday: a brief brightening, and the ice above the rim weeps harder, and the melt pulse comes down the walls and through the reeds and into the lanes, and the streets run, and the plaza brims, and the overflow slides out the sluice. Dusk: the cushions exhale, the moths fly, the keepers graze, the fog thickens and the towers comb it, and the city drinks all night on fog-drip while the world outside freezes. By morning its own exhaled breath is rime on every surface, and the cycle tightens and ticks and begins again. Growth is visible if you stay: a frond unrolling over three days, a lane migrating a hand's width in a season, a young cushion the size of a fist, then a head, then a hearth. At the city's edges the districts are raw — half-grown towers standing like scaffolding, pavement still thin, everything under construction. The city is unfinished. Unfinished is its natural state.

What stays with you, afterward, is the plan. The radial lanes, the ringed wall, the central cistern, the gate where the water leaves — it reads as intention, as design, and there is no designer. The plan is the water's plan, and the wind's, and the slugs', written in green. And the population is stranger still: the wall is one plant, one clone all the way around the bowl. The pavement is a single felted individual. Each cushion is one ancient being. The towers are a dozen clones. Lanes, walls, houses, wells, gates — the whole packed, teeming, saturated city is perhaps twenty citizens, all rooted, all old in kind and young in body, building fast on land so new it still smells of ground stone.

From the rim at dusk, looking back, you understand the scale of the thing: a green nave cupped in cold stone hands, steaming faintly with its own breath, keeping its own weather, while grey rock and white ice and hard wind hold the rest of the world. The glacier weeps. The city drinks. Somewhere in the dim, a skin of ice begins, very softly, to tick.
