The country around it gives you nothing first: a long gray lowland of silt and clay, plantless from horizon to horizon, where the wind has nothing to catch on and so keeps moving. You find the place by its coolth. The air drops a degree, then two, and then you are standing on a lip of slag and rammed clay, looking down into a hollow small enough to shout across — eighty paces, maybe a hundred — and too dense to finish looking at. The people of the region, such as they are, call it the Weep.

It is the lowest point of everything. All the water of the district finds its way here eventually, seeping down through the clay and arriving, at depth, slightly warm — a tepid, iron-heavy upwelling that rises through fissures in the basin floor and has risen for longer than anyone has kept count. Cool air pools over it. The warm water breathes into the cool air, and the hollow fills with a mist that never entirely lifts, and the mist condenses on every surface and rains back down in a fine interior drizzle. It rains here under clear sky. The hollow drinks its own exhale.

The walls are layered like a closed book soaked through. Every seam in the rock is a slow spring. Where the seep water overflows and sheets down, it lays stone as it goes — ochre sinter in small terraced steps, each one a pool the size of a hand, a staircase built by water for water. And on every wet surface, without exception, grows the film: a living veneer the color of charcoal and old cream, furred faintly at the edges, thick as felt in the sheltered corners, thin as breath on the exposed faces. It is not moss. There is no moss; there is nothing green-leaved in the Weep at all, nothing with a stem or a root. The film is simpler than that and stranger — one organism or a covenant of many, feeding on the dissolved iron the deep water carries up, building its rust-colored crusts the way a reef builds. No one has found its edge. Only its thickness.

At night the film gives back light where it has been touched. The grazers that creep across it — translucent, slow, the size of a thumb or a hand, leaving the rock bare and dark behind them — are tracked by a dim green waking in the veneer, the way an ember wakes when breathed on. The walls after dark are written over with these slow bright sentences, each fading before it can be read. Stand at the rim on a still night and the whole bowl is scribbled in dying script.

The pool at the bottom is black-skinned and clear beneath. The surface carries drifting continents of shed dust and film-flakes, and long-legged skaters walk it, picking off whatever the drizzle has knocked down. Below the skin the water is cold glass, and through it moves a slow weather of pale fish — finger-length, mirror-eyed, filtering the seep-water for what the film sheds. Their eyes catch the lamplight from below and return it, so that the deep pool at dusk seems to hold its own constellation, rearranging itself without hurry. Cool water, slow blood: nothing in the Weep is fast. But everything is present. Density does the work that speed does elsewhere. Every surface is occupied. Every interval has a tenant.

And then there are the machines, because the Weep is also the Works.

An iron stair comes down the wall, bolted there in some earlier century, rusted to the consistency of lace — and yet it holds, because rust here has nowhere to flake to and has simply settled back onto the iron like snow. At the basin's lowest side, half-sunk, its doorway awash at high water, stands the pump house: brick, iron, porcelain and brass under a permanent fur of condensate. Inside, a motor hums on a note you feel in the teeth more than hear. A needle trembles on a gauge whose scale no one reads. Knife switches stand open like the doors of small houses. The pumps lift the basin's surplus up to the rim, where a flume carries it away down the gray country; the whole arrangement turns, somehow, on the small difference between the warm water below and the cool air above, and it has turned for so long that the accounting no longer matters. It is not perpetual motion. It is perpetual housekeeping.

Whoever built the Works is long gone — there is a workbench in the gauge hut with tools set down mid-task, a wrench laid parallel to a coil of wire, as if the tenders stepped out for an hour — but the machinery has not been abandoned. It has been adopted. The bearings do not run dry: the film has grown into them and lines them with itself, a living sleeve, self-renewing. The switchgear is full of moths come in from the cold to bask on the warm coils. The dead pipe runs hold an eel — thick as a drainpipe because it grew to the pipe, patient, a vibration-hunter — and sometimes a soft knock travels the empty iron from one side of the hollow to the other, unhurried. That is not water hammer. The water has its own sounds. That knock is the eel, moving house.

It is no longer clear where the Works end and the Weep begins. This is the first law of the place, and the fauna are the second, and the water level is the third.

The level breathes. Twice a day, or nearly twice — the old governor keeps a time that is almost, not exactly, a day — the pumps draw the pool down, and the drowned causeway across the basin emerges gleaming, and the lower terraces stand bare and dripping, and the grazers descend after the retreating wet. Then the pumps slacken, the seep refills the bowl, the causeway drowns step by step, the skaters ride the rising skin, and the whole hollow inhales. Every creature here times its life to this drift — a clock that exists nowhere else in the world, kept by an eccentric machine, obeyed by everything with gills or a grip. Cross the causeway at the ebb. That is the rule visitors learn first, usually by wading.

By the intake grate, in the deepest part, lies the Gratekeeper: a shape the size of a drowned cart, shelled over in the same ochre sinter as the terraces, ancient enough that the crust on its back has crusts of its own. It feeds on whatever the intake's pull brings within reach, and the intake, in return, is never choked — an arrangement older than the stone on its shell, settled, mutual, unwitnessed in its making. When it shifts position, which is rare, the pool's surface rises perceptibly, and the float-valves in the gauge hut lift, and a handwheel somewhere turns a quarter-notch by water-logic, and the whole system re-settles around the new fact of it.

The moths keep their own hour. At the lamps' time — the caged bulbs strung along the causeway, some dead, most still burning a dim amber, electrified by whatever turns in the pump house's heart — they come up out of the crevices to drink condensation and orbit those small moons, dust-winged, eyeless, navigating by warmth. Their dust comes down after them like ash that has forgotten fire, and the film takes the dust, and the grazers take the film, and the fish take what the grazers shed. Nothing here is wasted. Wasting is a habit of drier places.

And the dead do not keep. Anything that dies in the Weep is taken up by the film within a few slow days — not rotted, absorbed, mineralized gently into the veneer — and for a while a pale silhouette remains in the crust, like a photograph developing backward, and then the ground is smooth again. The walls of the hollow are, in this sense, a record that erases itself as it is written. The Weep has a history. It simply keeps it the way water keeps a shape.

It is mostly quiet. You would learn the quiet's contents if you stayed: the drips, first of all, each with its pitch and its place — the stair-drip, the gantry-drip, the long-interval drip in the valve chamber that takes so long to gather you forget it between falls, so that it startles you fresh every time. The double-beat of the pump under everything, a heart in the feet. The hush of water moving in pipes, like held breath. The tick of iron cooling when the cycle turns. Once in a long while, a soft slap from the deep pool that means the Gratekeeper has turned in its sleep. No song. Nothing here sings; song belongs to places with leaves. The fog muffles even what sound there is, and the wind you hear passing over the rim sounds like something happening in another country, which, in a way, it is.

In the wet season the whole district weeps harder, and the seep swells, and the pumps labor at a lower note, and the level rides high for weeks; the causeway drowns entirely, the terraces climb their fauna upward toward the rim, and the mist thickens until the lamplight is only a rumor of amber at the water's edge. The Weep has survived every such season so far. It was built to fight the water and it has long since made a treaty with it instead.

Nothing in the hollow is new, and nothing is in ruins — ruin implies a direction, and this place arrived at its final form long ago and has been polishing it since. The stair's rust is settled. The sinter grows at the rate stone permits. The needles tremble. The green writing fades and is rewritten nightly on the walls.

Stand at the rim after dark and look down: the lamp-line doubled in black water, the mirror-eyed constellation drifting beneath it, the walls alight with slow erasing script, the drizzle falling upward out of the mist's warm heart and down again as rain, and under it all, felt more than heard, the steady double-beat of a place that has learned to keep itself — small, cold, crowded, breathing twice a day, on a clock that belongs to no one, at the bottom of a gray country that has been quietly draining into it forever.
