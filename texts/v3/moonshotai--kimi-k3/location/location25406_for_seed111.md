**The Tusk**

You hear it before you see it, and you feel it before you hear it: a low note arriving through the soles of the feet, up through the bones, a hum with no source and no end. The plateau around you is a frozen dust-sea, dry as paper, starlit, empty for a hundred miles in every direction — and then the dark ahead of you stands up. A crown of black columns rises out of the cloud-deck that hides the world below, one side furred white with rime, its flanks faintly veined with blue light, aurora combing slowly through its pinnacles like a hand through hair. The hum is coming out of the stone.

This is the Tusk, and it is not finished. Something below is still arriving. The mountain is an extrusion in progress, a slow tooth of the deep earth, rising perhaps a handspan in a century — which is why everything about it is so *sharp*. Nothing here has had time to soften. The columnar jointing is geometrically perfect, hexagon tops with edges like chisels. Fallen columns lie in the scree exactly as they fell, unrounded, ringing like bells when disturbed. Fractures show conchoidal scars, glassy and fresh. On still nights the young rock sweats salts, and the wind through the fresh cracks makes a sound like tearing silk. Frost-wedging has only just begun its work; you can hear it happening — a crack like a rifle shot somewhere on the flanks, then the clatter of spall, then the hum again, unbothered. The mountain ticks and groans the way a house settles, except it is not settling. It is growing.

The crown is hollow. The plug came up around an empty throat and left a crater-ring of columns, and inside is the whole of the inhabited world: a bowl perhaps three hundred paces across, bounded, complete, visible at a glance. You could throw your voice across it. Its edges are the rim and then the fall — a mile of dark air down to the clouds. Cathedral is the right word and also the wrong one: it is the right size, the right hush-under-noise, the right organ — the colonnade walls genuinely play in the wind, each flute and pipe of stone taking a note — but nothing here was built. Nothing here was made. Some of it, you will find, was chosen.

Here is the first strangeness: all the water for a hundred miles is in this one small room, at the top of the driest, highest country in the world. The plateau below has nothing — its cold thin air cannot hold moisture, its lakebeds are dust. But the Tusk pierces the cloud-deck and harvests it; fog comes up the flanks on the mountain's own updraft and lays rime-feathers on the windward columns, white as ferns, thick as wrists. And the mountain breathes besides: cool, damp air exhaled slowly from deep fractures — the throats — on a tidal cycle of days, as pressure systems pass over the plateau like slow hands. The rock, radiating its warmth away into the starred void, grows colder than the air around it, and sweats. Condensation beads on the black glass, gathers, runs down the column-flutes in thin threads, and collects in the hexagonal basins at the columns' feet — natural fonts, black water that holds a few stars each. Pockets of water in a larger dryness. An oasis turned upside down: wet at the summit, parched everywhere beneath.

There has been no sun for months. This is the long night, and the darkness here is not an absence but a medium — thick, inhabited, faintly furnished. The stars are very hard and very many. The aurora comes and goes in green-violet curtains that the rim crops into shapes. Along the drip-lines, where the condensation runs, a pale blue lichen glows in veins, mapping the water with light, so that the bowl's walls carry a dim luminous delta from rim to floor. And there are eyes. When anything disturbs the bowl — a rockfall, a stalker at the rim, a change in the wind — the eyeshine goes off in cascades, thousands of small mirrors igniting in waves across the ledges, a glittering that washes around the amphitheater and dies slowly back to dark.

Because the bowl is full. That is the overwhelming fact of the place, the thing the water was always going to cause. Forty thousand rauks roost shoulder to shoulder on the ledges, stacked in tiers from rim to floor: raven-sized, soot-black, furred like bats and winged in membrane, beaked like something older than birds. Their blood holds air the way a diver's holds breath, which is what the altitude demands of anyone staying. The air column above the bowl is never empty — there is always a gyre of wings turning in it, hundreds of bodies circling, and the sound of their flight is a kind of weather, a rushing that rises and falls like wind in trees, though there are no trees and never have been. They are here because the Tusk's updraft does two things: it carries the fog that becomes the water, and it concentrates the sky-krill — the aerial plankton that drifts over the plateau on the high winds — into a single rising river of food. The colony sits in the middle of the river, in the middle of the dark, at the only tap for a hundred miles, and it has been here so long that the floor has become an archive.

The floor is guano-meal and bone, deep, warm, faintly steaming where it meets the cold air — the rauks lay their eggs directly into it, banked in the compost heat, tended in shifts. The cool of the place means nothing rots quickly, so everything keeps. Dig down and you go back in time: strata of occupation, bone layers, shell layers, a thin white band of ice from the decade when the colony crashed and the bowl froze and the silence lasted three years. The ledges above are never empty, only re-tenanted; tenure passes down lineages, beak to beak, and an old ledge is worn smooth in a way that nothing else on this young mountain is worn smooth. In the cracks of the bone-fen live pale blind isopods, the archivists, and swarms of midges hatch off the warm guano and feed the small climbers that feed the things that feed on them. Layers upon layers: the air, the ledges, the floor, the beneath-floor, the inside of the rock itself, where endolithic crusts eat the fresh mineral of unweathered stone. Every volume occupied. Near-touching, all of it.

They use tools. Watch the scree aprons far below the rim and you will see rauks dropping from the height with plated krill in their beaks, letting go at a practiced moment, cracking the shells on the same favored stones generation after generation — using the mountain's own elevation as an instrument. Around those anvil-stones lie glittering middens of shattered plate, and among the angular native scree you can pick out smooth, rounded pebbles that have no business on a young mountain: carried up, selected, kept. Some rauks probe the ice-crevices for sleeping krill with slivers of bone. Nothing is fabricated. Everything useful is recognized.

The sound never stops. That is the second strangeness — the thin air up here should swallow noise, but the bowl returns it, focuses it, keeps it. There is the organ of the wind in the colonnade, fluting; there is the mountain's infrasonic hum, felt in the teeth and the breastbone more than heard, punctuated by the tick and groan of growth; there is the standing chord of the colony itself, forty thousand low fluting calls and clatter-clicks merging into a single vast voice that swells and ebbs on a cycle of hours, like a tide. Drips from the fonts echo multiplied, each drop become a small chorus. And once in a long while — a stalker at the rim, a shadow the wrong shape — the whole bowl stops at once. The hush lands like a physical thing, an anti-sound with weight, the loudest event the place produces. The rauks navigate home across the black plateau by the hum; the deaf do not find the mountain. It calls them in.

What grows here grows in crusts and veins and does not hurry. Black lichen on the leeward faces, eating stone so new it still sweats salts — a patch the size of a hand may be a thousand years old. The glowing vein-lichen along the drip-lines. A pale hair-fungus threading the warm guano. Nothing green — there is no light worth being green for — nothing tall, nothing you could call a plant without apology. The flora is a patina. It has the same patience as everything else here.

Because the deep fact of the Tusk is slowness. The drip counts the hours. The colony's boom and crash counts the decades, written into the floor as ice-bands. The lichens count the centuries. The mountain counts in units nobody has named, rising a fingernail, a handspan, growing new pinnacles the way a hand slowly extends a finger. Once each cycle the sun grazes the horizon for an hour — the Kindling — and thin red light lies along the rim, and forty thousand rauks fall silent and still, and the bowl holds its breath, and then the dark returns and the chord rebuilds note by note. That is the year's one event. Wings move fast here, but wings are weather, not time.

The rules are simple and total. All water is earned by height. Sound is how you arrive; silence is how you know something is wrong. Nothing rots, so nothing is forgotten; the floor remembers. Nothing is built; everything accrues. And the mountain is not done.

It stands in its starred dark, organ pipes furred with rime, veins of cold blue light mapping the water down its walls, the choir turning in the air above it, the hum going out through the stone and the stone going slowly up. No hand has ever touched it. It is witnessed only by its own forty thousand mirror-eyed tenants, who were called here by its voice and who answer it, all night, every night, while it rises.
