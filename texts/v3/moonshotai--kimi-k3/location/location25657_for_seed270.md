The floor of the world never holds still.

From the height of the sky — which is low, and iron, and curved like the inside of a bell — the gray silt of the Level appears windblown, rippling in slow dunes. But there is no wind here worth the name, only the Breath, and the Breath comes hours apart. What moves the dust is feet.

Mites: plate-backed, iron-dark, each the size of a grain of the silt it walks on, moving in herds that take a full day to pass a fixed point. Their armor carries real metal in it, gleaned from the world itself over a hundred generations, so that the herds glint dully as they flow, like ore deciding to migrate. Where they cross open ground the silt-fleas go up before them in popping eruptions, the dust itself seeming to boil, and the sound of it all — a million tarsi, the click of flea-landings, the dry rasp of wing-cases — comes to the ear pressed close as a kind of surf. Static. A crowd the size of weather.

The herds graze the felt. It is the only pasture the world grows: a leathery rust-colored film, fungal and simpler than fungal, layered over the iron floor like peat, chewed back and regrown for so long that its lower strata have compressed into something like soil, like history. And from above comes the fall — a slow, constant snow through the crack in the sky: feather-barb and skin-flake, concrete flour, fibers, the husks of creatures from the outer world who died far from here and drift down anyway. What falls, feeds. The felt digests the fall; the herds graze the felt; the hunters eat the herds. Nothing else is required. Nothing wet has crossed the threshold in living memory, and memory here is long — it is layered in tar.

The world tilts, almost imperceptibly, toward the low end. There the old oil gathered over decades and thickened into the Tar: a black flat, faintly glossy when the light strikes it, the only liquid this world has ever known, and it is not water. Nothing here is. The Tar keeps what it catches. Long-legged skaters live on its skin, wax-footed, sipping at the meniscus, dancing over the Drowned — for beneath the surface, in strata, lie the dead of every age, sealed in dark: whole herds, whole eras of shells going down into blackness, the complete fossil record of the world displayed in its own floor. Tar-worms mine the ancient layers, slow digesters of the century before last.

At the dark end rise the low brown hills of the Old Country. A mouse came through the crack long ago, chasing something, and the world closed over it and dried it where it lay. Now its hide is a range, its ribs a colonnade — the Arches — its skull a cave with one round window. Bone-fleas hold the high spine. And in the hollow of the ribcage, in the dark between the arches, the Long Hunter has its den.

Step back now. Farther. The curved wall is a housing. This world is the gearcase of a hoisting engine on the cellar floor of a dead mill, and the sky is four inches of cast iron. The sun is a sight-glass — the Eye — a coin of crazed glass set into the casing, and once each day, when the outer sun crosses the broken window across the hall, the Beam comes through it and sweeps the Level like a slow lighthouse, laying down a stripe of warmth that is the only summer this world receives. The Star is a missing bolt: a pinhole. On the far wall it projects, trembling and upside down, a gray-green smear of the outer world — a sky unvisited, a weather unreadable. Rain falls out there. Everything in here has heard it on the roof for a hundred years. None of it has ever entered.

The terrain, what there is of it, is machinery worn to geology. The Ridge is a gear, its teeth rounded like old molars; on its steep flanks the vertical herds graze a different felt, upside-down farmers in a country of overhangs. One flat face of iron has been polished by the passage of uncountable feet into a dim mirror, and at a certain hour the Beam strikes it and throws a second, lesser dawn into the dark end of the world — and one species of flea times its entire life to that bounce, feeding, mating, and dying by a sun that exists for eleven minutes. There is the Column, a fallen shaft. The Disc, a washer half-buried like a sunken arena. The Stele: the maker's serial plate, stamped with characters no one reads, a boundary stone between grazing grounds. A cotter pin bridges the seep at the Tar's edge, and it is older than the oldest felt and has never once been still underfoot.

Time runs by the Beam. At first passage the shield-singers begin — one dry note, then a section, then the dawn chorus rasping from a thousand wing-cases, because loud is the natural state of a place this crowded. The herds turn into the warmth and migrate with the moving stripe, grazing in its noon. Dusk comes when the Eye goes dark: the iron ticks as it cools, the cold descends out of the sky like a tide, and everything stops. A nightly winter. Whole populations lie torpid where they stand, mid-step, until the light returns. In season the molts come, and the air fills with drifting empties — a snow of ghosts, shed skins turning slowly in the Beam — which the towerwrights harvest and mortar into the Towers, those thin spires of frass and silk, a generation tall, that rise scattered across the Level and nearly brush the sky.

The Breath sets the deeper rhythm: the long barometric inhale and exhale through the crack, hours in, hours out, leaning the silk streamers at every burrow-mouth like flags reversing. The drift-midges ride it in columns. And on the driest days, when the Breath is sharp, static gathers on the old copper windings at the world's dead edge, and the dancers rise into that invisible field and turn, spark-drawn, in a light that nothing makes. Once in a decade a door opens somewhere in the mill above and the Breath becomes a gust — the world's one storm — and the herds flatten themselves under the felt's edge and the Towers lean and mostly hold.

Over it all, on forty pale feet, goes the Long Hunter. A finger long, root-pale, older than any herd-memory. Its habitual rounds are worn as grooves in the silt — roads — and the herds cross those roads quickly, at a run. When the flea-years are fat, the Hunter grows; the claw-bearers multiply beneath the felt's edge a season behind, and the scythes come up out of the dust's under-tunnels; then the herds thin, the felt creeps back across the plain, and the balance swings the other way. Predator, pasture, prey: the works still works. The machine died and the machine kept turning, only the turning changed hands.

It is loud the way a held thing is loud. Pressed close, the world roars: the surf of feet, the singers' rasp, the tick of contracting iron, and — relayed through the housing like thunder through a drum — the groans of the dead mill settling above, the far-off weather of a world no one here has felt. When the outer wind leans hard on the building, the housing finds its note and holds it, one low chord, and the singers take it up, and for minutes at a time the entire world hums a single pitch, mites and midges and the Long Hunter in its den, all of it resonating together inside four inches of sky.

The air smells of iron-oxide, of gear oil gone sweet with age, of dust and chitin. Every surface is occupied. Every crevice is tenanted. The dust itself is mostly alive, or once was.

Press an ear to the housing — all of it, this ancient, dry, teeming everything, would fit in a box you could carry — and you will hear what the dead engine makes now, what it settled into when it finished becoming itself.

Not turning. Teeming. A surf in a shell the size of a heart.
