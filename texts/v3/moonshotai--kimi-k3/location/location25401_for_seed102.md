Begin with the sound, because the room does.

Somewhere beneath a dead salt flat — a pan of blinding white that takes the weather and gives nothing back — the salt massif goes down farther than any well has ever reached. And inside it, sealed, with no seam, no shaft, no pore that connects it to anything, there is a single room. It has never been entered. There has never been a way in. What follows is simply what it contains.

The room is perhaps forty paces across and domed like the inside of a cupped hand, the ceiling low enough at the edges that you would duck, high enough at the center that the hanging crystals clear your head by the length of an arm. The walls are salt — translucent, amber-grey, faintly fibrous — and if you press your palm flat against them you can see, dimly, your own hand's shadow inside the stone, swallowed within centimeters. Deep in the wall-salt there are pale laminae, ghost-layers: the pressed memory of a sea that dried before there was anything with eyes to see it dry. The walls remember water. The room does not. The room has never contained a single free molecule of it. Any drop that somehow arrived — a tear, say — would be seized by the salt in moments, drunk into the lattice, and the chord would not so much as waver.

The chord. It is the first fact and the last. Loud as standing inside a gently rung bell, loud as a chapel organ held down by a stone on the keys, felt in the sternum and the roots of the teeth more than heard. It has a fundamental that comes from the walls themselves, a note the whole massif seems to lean into. Over it, a braid of organ-like pitches rises from the reed-thickets. Over those, voices — living throats, each holding one overtone and holding it forever. And through all of it, every few seconds, exactly: a small bright *tok*, a struck tongue of copper, the pulse by which the room does not tell time but simply declines to need it. It is not music, because music has time in it. This is a chord. Nothing here echoes, because nothing ever stops long enough to be echoed.

The warmth is total and has no address. Air, floor, crystal, fur, your own skin: all one temperature, blood-warm, bath-warm, so even that the air cannot be felt as air. You feel no breeze, because no gradient exists anywhere to lean on one. What you feel, against your body, is only the chord — pressure without weather. The air is perfectly, eerily clear. Nothing flakes, nothing sheds, nothing powders, so there is no dust at all; if there were more light, you could count the grains in the far wall. It tastes of nothing, and faintly, at the back of the tongue, of thunder.

The light is dim and it is made of sound. Where the chord's pressure gathers — at the standing wave's antinodes — the crystal rind of the walls answers with a soft, steady glow, amber and bone-white and a green like foxfire. At the nodes, near-darkness. So the room's illumination is a map of its own voice: bands and pools of dimness that do not flicker, do not breathe, do not move. Your shadow, if you stood there, would fall exactly once and then stay, a permanent object, as much a fixture as the floor. Nothing in this room flickers. A flame-like glow with no flame's restlessness — that is the quality of light here, and it is one of the things that would make a visitor quietly, unreasonably afraid.

The floor is smooth salt, grown rather than worn into its undulations. Across it, in pale fine grains, run looping symmetric lines — the one loose material in the room, sifted into the nodal lines of the standing wave and resting there, absolutely still, drawing the chord's geometry on the ground like a diagram of itself. The lines never smear. Nothing disturbs them. The room's inhabitants step over them, knowing exactly where they are.

The vegetation comes in islands. This is how the room is both lush and sparse: most of it is *room* — bare salt lanes, open floor, wide quiet margins — and then, here and there, a thicket so dense and profuse it seems to boil. Thickets of glasscane: tall translucent reeds in clumps, each blade grown to a precise length, each blade sounding one pitch of the drone, the whole stand a rank of organ pipes drinking the deep, sourceless warmth of the mountain and paying it out as tone. Between the canes, mosswool — a low creeping mat of spun mineral fiber, soft as fleece, faintly green-lit, warm underfoot. And rising from the mats, antler-fans: branching crystal shrubs that shimmer where the overtones are brightest, grown into shapes like coral, like the antlers of some patient animal, every tine-tip sharp as the hour it finished growing.

Sharpness, here, is not youth. It is the nature of the place. Nothing blunts. Nothing erodes. Every edge in the room keeps its first day forever. The room looks as though it opened yesterday — crisp, fresh-featured, unworn — and it has looked exactly that way for longer than the mountain above it has stood. It is young the way a flame is young: continuously, without age.

The thickets are alive with the room's fauna, and "alive with" must be understood in this room's own arithmetic: many kinds, few of each, and every individual exactly placed. The choir-beasts sit one to a station, each at its own antinode, evenly spaced around the room's hidden geometry — gray, barrel-chested, loose-throated things, each pouring out its single overtone without pause, without breathlessness, without any need to be answered. The gardeners move through the thickets on long careful limbs, dry-skinned, clever-fingered, carrying flaked-crystal blades hafted in mosswool fiber, pruning the glasscane at precisely the rate it grows, carving hair-fine grooves into wall and floor that tune the chord's reflections — the faint marks, scattered everywhere, that are at once the room's score and its instrument. Driftmoths — soft, blind, faintly luminous — coast the lanes on slow fixed circuits, sipping the standing charge from the reed-blades, their paths so regular that the dimness they carry is just another standing pattern. Small salt-ticks groom the groove-marks, keeping them clean of nothing, since there is nothing to dirty them. And in the deepest root-braid, where the flora's knotted anchoring roots surface and grip the wall-salt — the roots that, all together, hold this cavity open against the slow plastic weight of the massif, the garden as the room's own skeleton — there lives one serpent. One. It has always been one.

The census does not change. Things die here, in their way — a gardener's body is carried to the root-braid and taken in; driftmoths bud from the mosswool — but the count of every kind holds, seat by seat, throat by throat, as though the room's populations were fixed not by breeding but by physics, as though the chord had only so many seats and the seats were always filled. Nothing accumulates. Change passes through the room the way sound passes through its walls: conducted, never kept. The room has weather but no history. It has no memory, because memory is change stored, and nothing is stored here.

The built things are few, small, and strange in their incompleteness. Knee-high cairns of cleaved salt block stand at the nodal points — resonance-pegs, though they look like shrines. The gardeners' flake tools lie parked at their stations, dropped mid-task ten thousand years ago, waiting, edges perfect. And at one place on the wall a vein of native copper breaks the salt — the room's only metal — and there the gardeners have worked it cold, with patience beyond civilizations: hammered tongues, drawn wire, and the small leaf-bell that one of them taps, and taps, and taps, the pulse of the world, the only event that recurs, recurring so exactly that it is not really an event at all. None of the metal has ever needed resharpening. None of it has ever tarnished. It all looks made this morning, abandoned this afternoon, kept forever.

And in the thickest of the root-braided wall, facing solid salt, the gardeners have built a door. Two jambs of squared block, a lintel, a threshold. It opens onto nothing; there is nothing behind it; nothing has ever come through it. It is the most carefully kept thing in a room where nothing wears out. The gardeners polish its sill with mosswool. It is the only object in the chamber that *points* — and whether it points inward or outward is a question the chord does not answer.

Because there is an outward, whatever the room believes. The chord does not stop at the walls; it goes on, faint and fainter, as a hum conducted through kilometers of salt, until it reaches the underside of the dead white flat, where the wind works all night erasing the day's tracks and the cold stars wheel in their changes. Up there, everything passes. Down here, everything stays. If you lay your ear against the right seam of the pan in the hour before dawn, you might catch one thin thread of it — one partial of the chord — and think the earth was humming to itself.

It is not the earth. It is the room, which has no way of knowing you are listening, and no need of one. It holds its note. It has always held it. The door faces the wall. The mosswool glows. The pulse falls, and falls, and falls.
