You come up out of the lowland haze onto the plateau the old survey plates call the Kettle, and the first thing the altitude gives you is not a view but a dimming. The sun here is a modest star — a coal in a far grate, amber, ungenerous — and noon is a brown dusk that nobody ever named, because there is nobody. Shadows are rumors. You will go whole days without seeing one you trust.

You see the Works before you see it: a stand of steam columns on the horizon, vertical and slow, like the masts of a fleet that never sails. Then the road tops a basalt swell and the city lies below you in its basin — black, layered, wet, faintly lit from underneath, breathing. It is large. It is town-sized and then some, a regional fact, an hour to cross even where the streets are clear. And it is almost perfectly empty. The dominant building material, by volume, is distance.

The heat is the first surprise, because the dimness suggests cold and the dimness is lying. The star warms almost nothing in this country. The ground warms everything. Heat rises here — through the welded tuff, through the sinter terraces, through the soles of your boots — and the air over the basin sits at the temperature of a held breath, saturated, a mouth. Railings are warm as wrists. The city smells of hot stone, wet iron, and a faint sweet metal, like a held coin.

Nothing is dry. That is the first law. The second is that everything returns. Deep pumps in the city's core lift water from a hot aquifer far below — water that has never seen the dim sky and would not recognize it — and send it up the condenser towers, the tall finned ranks of them the plates call the Comb. The towers' crowns are cool enough, barely, in the thin upland wind, and the steam gives itself up there as condensate, and the towers drip into catch-basins, and the basins overflow into gutters, and the gutters run warm through the streets, and the walls weep — some of them weep upward, the lower town's sinter wicking floodwater into the brick so that second stories drip from below — and the sumps gather it all, and the pumps lift it again. The city drinks its own breath. The rain, when it happens, falls at body temperature, and is less an event than a mood the gutters already had.

The laws here are few, and they are kept. Nothing dries. Heat rises; light is rationed. Everything returns. Quiet is load-bearing. Readiness is the protocol. You will understand all of these by the time you leave.

The city is built of black basalt brick glazed by a century of steam; of iron that has made its peace with rust and holds a stabilized russet like a settled argument; of brass kept inexplicably bright at valve-stems and gauge-glasses; and of sinter — the pale, rippled stone that the water builds when it is allowed to have its way, silica laid down molecule by molecule until a stair becomes a terrace, a terrace becomes a foundation, a clogged pipe becomes a vein of flint. The land here does not erode so much as accrete. Everything has been worn smooth and then thickened, and the city has found its final shape. It will not fall. It will fossilize in place, slowly, around its own warm heart.

Its avenues are wide enough for parades that have never been held. The street signs name the hopes of whoever founded it: LANDFALL AVENUE. ARRIVAL SQUARE. MILLENARY CONCOURSE. On a bronze plaque by the gate, green-black but legible if you rub it: PROVIDENCE — THERMAL SETTLEMENT NO. 1 — FOUNDED IN FAITH FOR THE COMING TOWNS. The towns are still coming. That is the official position. The houses stand ready with their doors unlatched, ceramic cups set out on sills, chairs angled toward windows, blank name-plates screwed beside every doorbell, ten thousand doorbells, all of them wired, none of them pressed. You could fire a cannon down the Grand Concourse and startle only the water.

In the Casting House, every dusk, the molds receive their iron and give up the same valve housing they gave up yesterday, and small carts on worn rails carry it to Warehouse Eleven, where decades of identical castings wait in geological order for a need that has not yet been invented. The grid is alive; insulators tick in the damp; a frugal current runs to whichever lamps the schedule calls for, and the schedule is kept in the Authority building on a great pinned cylinder, the Program Drum, which turns so slowly you cannot see it move and never stops. The machines here are old, overbuilt, and slow, oiled by their own patient subroutines — arms that lean out at intervals to wipe a gauge-glass, a blower that clears a flue at the same hour each week. Purpose has sedimented into liturgy. The city keeps its appointments.

And over every warm wet surface, the film. It is not a plant. Nothing green has ever grown here; the plaques have no word for garden. It is not a lichen, not a moss — it is barely, by the old definitions, alive at all. It is a chemistry with habits: a rust-gold velvet that upholsters the pipework, feeds on the heat and the wet and the dissolved deep-earth minerals, and keeps the machine's seals supple in exchange for being kept warm. It has preferences. It thickens around certain valves and abandons others; it creeps into a gear-train over twenty years and gently persuades it to a new rhythm; the Works has changed its habits, decade by decade, in ways no blueprint records, and the film is why. The engineers, if there ever were engineers, are long past objecting. The symbiosis is the settlement now — industry as metabolism, the city as one slow body with water for blood and a drum for a heart.

The film is also why the quiet is law. It withdraws from vibration — shrinks back, seals over, sulks for days — and the city learned long ago to hum rather than hammer, to move its water by pressure and patience instead of pistons. So the hush here is not absence; it is policy. The loudest things are water events: the organized million-drip chord of the Comb at mid-morning; the long gulp when a reservoir bowl draws down; and, in the wettest season, the overflow of the eastern bowl, whose outfall pipes are tuned — by design or by the ear of erosion, no one can say — so that for one hour the drain organ holds a low fifth, and the whole district vibrates just gently enough that the film does not mind. At dusk a whistle sounds the end of a shift that has not changed hands in living memory, which is the only memory there is. Wet air carries sound far and isolates it: a single chain settling, three streets away, arrives entire. A drip that changes its rhythm is news.

Of animals there are three kinds, and of the three, few. The sump-walkers are the size of draught ponies and built like armored siege engines, plated in accreted sinter, blind as the deep water they wade, grazing the film at a pace that makes the film seem hasty. There is perhaps one per square kilometer. Their trails are polished channels worn across districts over centuries, and their molts hang from valve-handles like pale gloves left behind by polite giants. Where the film is disturbed it remembers, briefly, in green-gold phosphor, so a sump-walker crossing a flooded plaza at night writes its passing in light, and the dark closes over the sentence. In the deep cisterns live the white eels, eyeless, unhurried, older perhaps than the pumps that house them. And around the lit lamps, the lamp-moths — soft gray things that sip condensate and live their whole brief lives within one lamppost's weather, dusting the bulbs with their wings, the smallest sound in the world, which here counts as a sound. Nothing hunts. The food in this world is patience, and everything has enough. Sometimes, in the deepest cistern, a current moves against the pumping rhythm, and the eels arrange themselves along the walls like hands politely leaving a room. What passes then, passes.

Days here are a long pewter gloaming; nights are the true dark, lit by grates, by the scheduled lamps, by the fading signatures of what waded past. Sometimes — rarely, a few times in a decade — the steam-deck overhead tears, and the little sun looks in. A blade of amber crosses the basin. The wet streets flare white. The films contract. The sump-walkers stand still as masonry. And for a few minutes the city casts shadows, and the shadows look wrong, like a stranger's handwriting. Then the deck closes, and the long dim heals back over the Kettle, and everything exhales.

Once in a turning of the Program Drum — once in some long count of days that no one is counting — the pins come around to the founding protocol, and the city executes the Arrival. Every lamp comes on at once. Gates stand open. Fountains climb. The warehouse doors roll back to offer their castings to the empty square, and the whistles chord together like a held breath finally let go, and for one hour the Works is the bright, loud, welcoming place it was built to be. Then the hour passes. The lamps go down row by row. The steam takes back the streets. Nothing has arrived. Nothing has ever arrived. The protocol does not require arrival; it requires readiness. It is the most hopeful hour in the world, and there is no one to see it — unless a sump-walker has paused on the concourse, glowing faintly, grazing, indifferent, which is not the same thing.

Out on the perimeter road, where the Works thins into the bare basalt and the distances become the whole of the landscape, there is one last lamppost, a mile from its nearest neighbor. It comes on at dusk, every dusk, over a mile of nothing, and burns all night for the road. The moths that have not found it go on not finding it. The light does not waver. It is on time.
