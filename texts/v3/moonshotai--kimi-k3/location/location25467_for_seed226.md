The range is young, and it makes no attempt to hide it. Peaks still sharp as broken teeth, ridgelines unsoftened, scree lying in angular heaps exactly where it fell, as if the mountain had only just set its own rubble down and stepped back. Nothing up here has been worn into anything else yet. And into all this grey comes a sound before any sight of its source — a steady, many-threaded roar, like rain that never quite arrives — and then a breath of steam over a ridge, and then the color: a single hanging shelf of white and wet green, cupped in the raw flank of the mountain like something the mountain is still deciding whether to keep.

The whole place is that one shelf. You can take it in at a glance — an amphitheater a few hundred paces across, bounded above by a raw cliff where a black crack steams, and below by a line so sharp you could step over it, where the green simply stops and the grey scree resumes. Beyond that line the water runs off as an ordinary white creek down an ordinary young gorge, and the world goes back to being cold and mineral and sparse. Inside the line, other rules apply.

The water rises warm from the crack, heavy with dissolved stone, and where it spreads and spills and slows, it leaves its load behind — a skin at a time, a glaze, a lip. So nothing here erodes. The word has not been invented here yet. Everything accretes. The newest stone is soft as firm curd; a copper knife goes through it like cheese, and by the following year it has cured hard enough to ring when struck. A finger's width a year, in the favored runs. The shelf is a cascade of grown basins — rimstone lipped, porcelain-smooth, each one beading its overflow over a rounded dam into the next — some pale as bone, some painted rust and jade by the algae that grade themselves by the water's heat. Walk to the cliff and the rock weeps. Every surface weeps. The air itself condenses on anything cooler than the hill, which is everything, so the whole place glistens even when the sky is clear, and steam stands in the spillways all morning, backlit and slow.

Every wet surface is upholstered. Moss a hand deep, so saturated it sheds water when pressed; ferns unspooling out of any crevice the spray reaches; liverwort in black-green sheets; a small white-flowered creeper that grows here and, as far as anyone knows, nowhere else in the world. The lushness follows the water exactly, which gives the shelf its two estates: the watered runs, living, green, ringing faintly underfoot — and the dormant quarters, where water once flowed and no longer does, gone pale and quiet, the basins dry, the ferns reduced to ghosts of rust-colored thread. Nothing decays here the way things decay elsewhere. The closest this place comes to death is stillness.

And threaded through all of it, improbably, is a town.

Some generations back, the people who came up the gorge learned the hill's habit and made a craft of it. Where a wall is wanted, they weave a frame of willow withies and prop it in the path of the water, and lead the spill through it, and wait. The stone knits itself through the weave; the wood fossilizes inside, so the oldest walls have a skeleton of vanished branches you can trace in cross-section. Where stone is wanted loose, they cut it young — whole blocks of the soft stuff, stacked and left to cure, fusing over seasons into a single mass, so that a finished house is not masonry but one continuous rock. Channels of hollowed log and stone trough run overhead on posts; leather-flap sluice gates divert the flow; walkways of cured slab cross the basins; cistern rooms sit cool and dark beneath the terraces, collecting drips. The fabric is dense in the way of towns — stairs, bridges, drains, cisterns, lanes between grown walls, a meeting-bowl floored with worn-smooth sinter — and it is almost entirely empty.

There are eleven tenders this generation. The town has rooms for six hundred.

This is the deliberate shape of the thing. The tenders hold that the hill is building the town and they are only its editors, and that a town grown at the speed of stone must be planned across a span no single life can see. So houses are begun for children not yet born. Rooms stand with moss already carpeting them, doorways without doors, lintels still soft enough to take a thumbprint. The plan itself is kept on a painted hide in the driest cistern, showing the town as it will look in three hundred years; every few years, when the deep mountain shifts and a spring moves and a channel must be abandoned, the hide is amended, and the dormant quarter grows by another lane, and a new seeded wall goes up somewhere wet. You can walk the terraces for a week and encounter one other person, at a distance, raised hand silhouetted against the steam. The tenders communicate as much by leavings as by speech — knotted cords hung on sluice posts, small stacks of white pebbles whose arrangements mean the south run is slackening or a frame is ready for water or gone below, do not follow. Their law fits on one line: lead the water, never dam it. A dammed run, they say, is a wall the hill did not agree to.

For an empty place it is extraordinarily loud. The water arrives in every register at once — the bass shush of the main spill, the clatter of overflow beading off a hundred rims, the ticking of drips in the cisterns, where water clocks no one reads keep patient time in the dark. Thin cured crusts drum hollowly underfoot, and learning the town means learning the drum-map: where the hill is solid, where it rings, where a foot would go through. Wind crosses the empty doorways and the stone flutes of the dormant quarter and makes low notes of them. A single chisel-tap carries clear across the bowl, so the sound of one person working fills the whole town like the sound of many. And at certain depths of the great cistern — the tenders watch for it — the entire hill begins to hum, a tone felt in the teeth as much as heard, and then they open and close their leather gates and tune the mountain, briefly, the way you might tune a drum.

The animals can be counted on one hand. A pair of dippers owns the whole watershed — slate-grey, unbothered, bobbing on the rims and walking straight into the loud water to feed along the basin floors. Midges hang in the steam at dusk. A translucent snail works the warm runs, grazing the algae gradients. Twice a year a great lammergeier crosses the amphitheater's sky without comment, and the tenders note it on the hide, as if the plan extended upward too. That is the census. Vast space between presences, here as everywhere.

The light is the most ordinary thing about the place, which somehow makes it stranger. Sun and cloud do their usual work; mornings are cold enough for breath-steam; rainbows stand in the spray on clear afternoons and mean nothing unusual. Time, though, moves at the hill's pace. The mist comes and goes daily; the runs slacken and swell with the seasons; the deep tremor comes every few years and redraws a lane; a wall stands knee-high in a decade; a house is a bequest. Nothing here is in a hurry, and nothing here is old, and nothing is finished.

It is not a ruin. It is the mirror of a ruin — a town that points the wrong way in time, all its emptiness ahead of it. The loudest thing in the valley is simply the sound of it continuing: water over stone, stone out of water, a green and ringing embryo of a city, growing patiently toward inhabitants it has not met yet.
