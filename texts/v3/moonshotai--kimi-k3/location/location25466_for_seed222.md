From the floor of the basin, the plateau is a long pale line drawn across the middle of the sky — too high, surely, to be warm. You will be wrong about that, and about other things.

The climb takes half a day. The stair is the first mark of hands: six hundred risers cut straight into the slab, worn to shallow smiles by feet and wind, and whatever feet and wind have failed to finish, the dust repairs. By the thousandth step you have learned the first rule of the place, though no one told you: sound thins before the air does. Your breathing stays with you. Your footfalls land short and dry, like notes struck on a wrapped drum. A pebble you kick should clatter; it ticks, once, and stops.

At the rim stands a reed. That is what the keepers call them, though reeds grow in water and these make it: three times your height, thin as a wrist, dark as cold iron, seamless, and wrong in a way your eyes keep sliding off. The light bends a finger's width around its flanks. It is colder than shade, colder than any night this warm country knows — cold with no source, no frost, no explanation. At its base, in a basin of stone an arm's length across and brim-full of sky, is the water it has pulled out of the bright dry air.

This is the pattern of everything up here. The plateau is vast and finished — old beyond the reach of its own rivers, whose channels still run, polished and dry, down the domes' flanks — and nearly everything in it is a point in a great deal of space. Domes of bone-pale stone rise and fall like the backs of sleeping animals, streaked with black varnish that runs down them like the tracks of old tears. Gravel flats lie between, so level and so glittering that the horizon floats above them in a band of mirage. Balanced boulders rest on necks of eroded stone, and have rested there so long that the lichen under them is its own slow nation. Nothing is falling. Nothing is fresh. It is a completed landscape — and yet, you will come to understand, a kept one.

Vegetation holds to the same economy: gray grass in spaced tufts, each with its own meter of gravel; cushion plants tight as fists in the slots' shade; lichen fields that a careful eye can read like pasture. Near the cups — and only near the cups — there are trees: low, gnarled to muscle, leaves small and waxy and the color of the light itself, each one old out of all proportion to its size. Perhaps one tree per cup, one cup per hour of walking. The dryness between is not barren; it is spaced. The plateau practices an agriculture of distance.

The water economy is the reed economy, and the reeds are not quite where they were. Old grooves cross the domes — smooth, shallow, unmistakable — ending where reeds now stand, and beginning nowhere. Over centuries they lean and step, never while watched, toward weather only they can taste. Where a reed has departed, its cup remains awhile, brimful and mourning, then dries to a ring of pale salts. You will find those rings everywhere, faint as chalk, and the newest grass tufts gather at their edges as if at graves.

Some dawns a fog stands knee-deep in the bowls, slow and gray, and the reeds comb it. Then the beetles climb: mirror-backs, big as your thumbnail, shells of true reflecting metal, and each one climbs to a reed's cold flank and stands on its head, back raised to the fog, drinking what condenses and runs down. Whether they are animals or something the reeds grew is a genuine question; their undersides have no seams, and they taste — the lizards have learned — of nothing. Lizards do push-up displays on the warm slabs all morning, flashing pale bellies in code. The hoppers wait out the heat in boulder shade, gray as the gravel, legioned. And all day the sails hang in the bright — wings like scythes of pale membrane, riding the thermals the noon sun pours up the domes. Some have not touched ground in years. They sleep on the wind, fledge in the air, and drink by stooping over the cups at dusk without landing.

Watch a sail cross the sun and you will see the strangest ordinary thing here: its shadow arrives on the stone a heartbeat after it passes. As if the sky were deeper than it looks, and light needed a moment to climb out. The same lag haunts the reeds' own shadows at dawn. The keepers, asked, say only: the light here is old. They mean it as an answer.

The mirages tell the truth — that is the other thing. On the gravel flats at noon the horizon shows water, and if you walk to it, there is water: the light is being bent, gently, from the real cups beyond your sight. The air of the plateau wants you to find its water. Or something in the air does.

Because the dust is everywhere, and the dust is working. You see it at first light as a shimmer, a pollen of gold motes sifting over the stone. It settles on your skin with a taste like a coin held on the tongue. It lays the black varnish on the domes molecule by molecule — and the varnish, the keepers believe, is a record, though of what no one says aloud. It drinks sound; that is why your shout travels a stone's throw and gives up, why the noon wind arrives like a held breath released, felt on the skin and never heard. It takes back the dead. Nothing rots here; things are unmade, quietly, and their substance put elsewhere. A fire ring at a way-shelter might be a season old or a thousand years, and it will look the same either way: faint, crisp, faintly attended.

The walking stones you will meet alone, each at the head of its polished groove, mid-errand. They graze the lichen fields with the patience of geology. They are not fast, they are not slow; they are on schedule. Once in a generation two of them converge on a reed that has fallen — reeds do fall, every few centuries, for reasons of their own — and they stand beside it until the dust has taken it back. The keepers leave them to it. Everything here leaves everything to it, mostly.

The keepers themselves you may not see. A thread of smoke on the far rim at dusk. A low stone shelter, domed, swept, with a coiled rope and a stack of jars — and the jars are a small kindness of the old engineering: fill one and it holds the water at the temperature of dawn. Near the stair-head stands the Table, a black slab knee-high, its surface carrying the whole plateau in relief finer than any hand could cut: every dome, every slot, every reed, and bright points where the cups are full. The relief shifts as the land shifts. Some points shine where no cup has yet been found; some cups you know are not shown. The Table and the land are still negotiating.

Rain, when it comes — ten warm minutes, twice a season, out of a build-up that takes all afternoon — is an event the whole ecology has been holding its breath for. The dry channels run for an hour, and for that hour the plateau almost has a voice: a low rushing that dies against the dust-damped air within a stone's throw, as if the place briefly remembers what sound was. Petrichor rises off the hot gravel, sweet and mineral. Within a day the gray grass greens from the root. Seeds that waited a decade open small pale flowers, and the moths come up like ash reversed, and the sails stoop through them in silence for one long bright week before the plateau returns to its spaced and ordinary self.

Dusk is the crowded hour, and the quietest. The moths rise over every cup in flashing clouds, signaling in light because there is no profit in song. The hoppers drum the stone — you do not hear it; you feel it through boot soles and sitting bone, a soft coded thunder under the world — and if you drum back, the old ask, sometimes something answers from very far away. The domes give back the day's warmth like banked coals, and the animals gather on them as on hearths. At the cups, nothing hunts. The pale hunter — the long low gray animal that all noon was stillness, then an unspooling, then one small tick of gravel, over — lies down an arm's length from the lizards, and all of them drink in turns, watching the sky. The truce holds at the water. On the approaches, it does not, and everyone knows the difference.

The rules here are few and they are kept: water gathers where the reeds stand. Loudness is wasted. Nothing rots; it is taken back. The light tells the truth, late.

Night comes warm. The black varnish on the domes goes briefly to ember, then dark; the dust high in the air holds a band of faint glow along the horizon long after the sun is down; the stars arrive fierce and close, as they only do from high, dry, lightless country. The reeds stand faintly starred, each droplet on their cold flanks giving a star back, and the cups give the sky back doubled, and overhead the sails turn on the slow wind with their wings tucked, asleep and aloft, their shadows — a heartbeat behind them — moving across the moonlit stone.

Nothing here is ruins, and nothing is new. The plateau is old and warm and bright and almost silent, and it is in motion everywhere: stones on their errands, reeds on their centuries, hunters and hoppers and the dust that writes it all down in black varnish. You are witnessed, mildly, the way everything is witnessed. A stone completes another year of its errand behind you. The loudest sound in the night is a drip, deciding to fall.
