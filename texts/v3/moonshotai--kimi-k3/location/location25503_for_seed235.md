The escarpment runs farther than the eye can follow in either direction — a single unbroken wall of grey-green stone, four thousand meters from the marsh at its foot to the rim of the plateau above. Water comes off the rim in falls. Not here and there, not where geology would put them, but at intervals so regular that the whole cliff face seems measured: a fall, a buttress, a fall, a buttress, a rhythm of white water repeating into the haze at both horizons. Halfway up, a belt of mist clings to the stone in a permanent luminous band, and the falls enter it and emerge from it below unchanged, as if the mist were not weather but a fixture of the world.

The green is the first thing to mislead. From the floor, the cliff looks draped in jungle — lush, layered, impossibly dense, every ledge loaded with foliage. Then the eye adjusts, and the foliage resolves into structure: towers fluted like organ pipes, fused in clusters of five and seven; causeways running level along the face for a kilometer at a stretch; arches spanning the gaps between buttresses; broad flat discs that can only be called plazas, tilted just enough to shed their water into carved channels; staircases switchbacking down the ribs of the cliff in flights of a hundred steps. It is a city. It has districts, infrastructure, a visible logic of zoning — cistern-towers uphill, terraces below, wards clustered in the side-canyons where the wall folds back on itself. And nothing built it. There is not a cut mark in its whole extent, not a split plank, not a fired brick, not a cord. Every street is a flattened, fused root. Every tower is a hollow trunk. Every aqueduct is a living vein, moving water inside its walls the way a stem moves sap. The city is one organism, or a small congress of them, and it grew into this shape the way a crystal grows into its lattice — by a law, not a decision.

The heat is total. It stands at the temperature of a held breath, and it does not vary. This is the first true wrongness, and it takes time to notice: the rim should be cooler than the floor. Four thousand meters of relief should grade the air from steam to something temperate. It does not. The plateau at the top is as hot as the marsh at the bottom; the mist belt is as hot as both. The stone is warm as skin everywhere. The falls land warm as bathwater. Water condenses anyway — on leaves, on bark, on the backs of your hands — condenses on surfaces no colder than the air around them, as though the atmosphere were simply saturated past the point where physics excuses it. Everything weeps. The towers weep. The stairs run with thin warm rivulets. The whole city drips, and every drip falls into something built to receive it: a cupped leaf, a cistern, a channel worn smooth as poured glass.

The sound arrives before the city does and never afterward leaves. It is deafening — genuinely, structurally deafening, the kind of loudness that lives in the ribs and the roots of the teeth rather than the ears. It has three layers. Underneath, the broadband roar of a hundred falls, a white hiss like the inside of a held breath. Through that, the chord: the constant hot updraft off the cliff face moves through the hollow towers, and the towers are stopped pipes, and each cluster sounds its note, and the notes of the whole face lock together into a single suspended harmony — close-spaced, dissonant, a chord that by every law of music should resolve and never does. And over that, small and precise, the percussion of the drips: the same six drops into the same cistern in the same order, a pattern worn into time itself. None of it changes. There are no gusts, no lulls, no swells. And — this is the part that takes longest to believe — none of it attenuates. The roar is exactly as loud on the rim, four thousand meters above the nearest fall, as it is standing on the fall's own lip. It is as loud inside an alcove as in the open air. Sound here does not behave like sound. It behaves like light in fog: sourceless, even, shadowless. The cliff gives back no echo. You can shout into the face of it and your voice dies an arm's length from your mouth, unreturned, while the chord goes on at its one eternal volume, indifferent.

The light is ordinary. That is its strangeness. It is plain daylight, warm-toned, neither dimmer nor harsher than a decent mid-morning — and it is always mid-morning. The sun does not move. Shadows do not creep; they are fixtures, painted dark shapes that belong to their objects the way a base belongs to a statue. In the mist belt the light goes pearled and directionless. On the floor it comes down through the falls and the canopy streets in green-gold shafts that never shift. Where an old limb has broken — and there are broken limbs, the city has a past — the cross-section shows its strata like geology: an innermost core of heartwood gone to stone, silicified into something like opal, translucent at the thin edges so the fixed daylight glows through it horn-colored; then a band of dense old wood, dark and mineral-heavy; then younger wood, greener; then the skin of living bark, grey-green, fluted, slick with condensation; then the moss, and then the moss that grows on the moss. The city is layered like a town built and rebuilt on its own ruins, except that all the layers are one tree, and the building was growing.

The water closes every loop it opens. The rivers on the plateau arrive at the rim and go over, and do not dwindle. The plunge basins at the foot brim and do not flood. The mist rises off the marsh and the falls, climbs the face without wind — the updraft is as constant as everything else — and gives itself back as warm rain on the plateau, and the accounting balances to the last drop, nothing lost, nothing added, a sealed circuit the size of a region. Some loops you can walk. A stream in one of the side-canyon wards runs along its causeway, around a buttress, down a flight of steps, and back into its own headwaters; you can follow it with a hand in the current and come back to where you started with the water you first touched arriving beside you. In the mist belt, some of the droplets have stopped falling altogether. They hang at head height in the still air, beads of warm water suspended in no web and on no thread, and they part around your face as you pass and close again behind you.

Nothing lives here that moves by choice. No bird crosses the falls. No insect works the flowers. The city is lush the way a hothouse is lush — ferns packed into every crotch of the towers, epiphytes stacked three deep on every branch, lichen spread like plaster and paint over the old stone wood, shelf fungi jutting as corbels and eaves, mycelium stitching the strata together from inside — and it is entirely, achingly voiceless. The flowers are open everywhere, heavy, waxy, crimson and bone-white, giving off a scent so thick and sweet it has texture, and the scent goes nowhere, because there is nothing to smell it. They were made for pollinators that have never existed, and so they simply stay open. They do not wilt. There are no buds either, and no rot. Every stage of every process is present at once, in fixed proportion, forever: bloom without before or after. Pollen drifts in the shafts of unmoving light in slow golden spirals and never settles. Nothing startles. Nothing watches. The roar fills the ear so completely that the absence of animals registers not as silence but as a kind of purity — a jungle built for song, holding only its chord.

Sit still long enough — an hour, a day, it makes no difference, because the day does not pass — and the rules of the place assemble themselves around you. Nothing here accumulates. The drips have been falling into their cisterns for what the strata say is a very long time, and the cisterns are full, and they do not overflow, and the mineral lips that such drips build have grown to a certain finished shape and stopped. There is no talus at the foot of the cliff. Nothing depletes, either: the rivers do not shrink, the towers do not thin, the moss pressed down by a foot eases back up within the hour, unbruised, as though the foot had declined to happen. The city once had a history — it is written plainly in the opal cores and the weathered causeways and the rounded, finished, settled forms of everything — and then it finished having one. It found its balance and it closed. It is not ruined and it is not new. It is complete.

Walk long enough along the face and the last rule shows itself. An hour's travel past falls and wards brings you to a district you have already seen: the same cracked cistern rim, the same five-pipe tower cluster sounding the same note, the same bloom at the same angle, the same six drips in their same order. Whether the city repeats itself, ward after ward, like a crystal repeating its cell — or whether the paths fold and you have been returned to the place you left — is a question the city declines to answer. The staircases are the same: one flight, climbed steadily downward among the buttresses, delivers you onto a plaza higher than the ledge you started from, and the roar does not change, and the light does not change, and nothing about the transaction is remarked upon.

You are the only gradient here. You arrive carrying difference — cooler blood than the stone, a voice, a schedule, a direction — into a place that has spent who knows how long arranging never to change again, and the place receives you the way the mist receives your face: it parts, it closes, it does not record. Your footprints lift out of the moss. Your shout dies without echo an arm's length away. The falls fall, the chord holds its suspended dissonance a hair's breadth from resolution and does not resolve, the droplets hang, the flowers stay open for no one, the warm water makes its sealed rounds from rim to mist to rain to rim. When you leave — and you will leave, because you are the kind of thing that leaves — the city will not have noticed, and the chord will not so much as lean toward its cadence. It has forever to resolve. It is in no hurry. It is never, ever in a hurry.
