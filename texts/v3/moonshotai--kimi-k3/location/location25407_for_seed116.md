**The Third Lift Well**

From downstream, the dam reads as a cliff that someone has squared. It fills the gorge from wall to wall and stands six hundred meters from the old riverbed to the crest — the tallest made thing in its world, and so old that none of its edges are true anymore. The parapets have gone soft as蛋糕 icing; the spillway lip wears a lip of its own, a white mineral overbite built grain by grain. Water has been rounding this artifact for longer than anyone has been counting, and it has not finished, but it has slowed — the dam and the water reached their agreement centuries ago, and now they change each other only politely.

Behind the wall there is no valley. The valley drowned when the dam was young, and what remains is a warm green reservoir the color of old bottle glass, filling the gorge to within a few meters of the crest, the drowned hills standing out of it as islands. Warm springs rise through the reservoir bed, so the water never cools the way deep water should; it carries a load of dissolved mineral that the whole artifact has been slowly drinking and re-depositing ever since. Everything here is made. Even the lake is an artifact. Even the stone the lake deposits is, in a sense, the dam secreting itself.

Inside the masonry there is almost nothing. The dam is nearly solid — that is what makes it stable — but it keeps a few hollows: inspection galleries, a stair, and three lift wells sunk from the crest down through the body of the wall into the reservoir's deep water. Well No. 1 is a stairway now, dry. Well No. 2 holds still black water and silence. Well No. 3 is the one that lives.

Its mouth is a small housing on the crest, a stone room the size of a chapel, open at the eaves, with a bronze grille over the shaft. Stand on the grille and the well exhales at you: warm, wet air rising out of the dark, smelling of wet iron, green rot, and pond musk, carrying a low note you feel in your sternum before you hear it. The shaft is seven paces across and drops farther than a thrown stone's flight — down past the crest, past the galleries, into the body of the dam, until it meets the reservoir's warm depths through the intake ports. All that height, folded into a room you could cross in seven steps.

A column of daylight falls through the grille for most of the day, and near the top of the shaft the well makes its own weather. The old delivery main — the bronze pipe that once carried lifted water to the crest gardens — sweats and leaks through a cracked crown, and a fine, permanent drizzle falls from one side of the shaft, a private rain that has not stopped in living memory. Where the drizzle meets the light, the walls are a hanging garden. Mosses in cushions deep enough to lose a hand. Ferns rooted in the grooves of screw threads and the keyways of valve wheels, in the embossed letters of the maker's plate. A single fig, sprouted centuries ago from a seed in a crack at the wellhead, has let its roots down forty meters in a pale curtain, drinking the spray; its fruits drop through the shaft on their own schedule, and the fish have learned the sound a fig makes on water.

The frogs live up here in the light, and they are spaced like lamps along a road — one to a territory, ten or fifteen meters of bare wall between neighbors, so that the garden sounds inhabited by solitaries. There are perhaps sixty of them down the whole green length of the shaft. When they chorus, at dusk and at the turning of the tide, the concrete cylinder takes their sixty small voices and returns them as one enormous sound; the well rings like a struck bell, and on the crest, twenty meters above, the air shudders with it. Their call is two notes, and the two notes sit precisely in a gap in the machine's hum — the one quiet window in the well's spectrum of noise. Hundreds of generations of frogs have tuned themselves to be heard over the pumps. It is not the song of a stream frog anymore. It is the song of a frog that lives inside an engine.

A gallery breaks into the shaft at mid-height, and this is the furnished room of the house, though furnishing is a strong word for so much emptiness. One bronze-caged bulkhead lamp burns above the door; it has burned for two centuries on a trickle of the dam's own current, and nobody living has seen it dark. A rank of gauges trembles behind glass, their needles still reporting pressures to no one. A clockwork drum turns beside them, its stylus scratching the water level onto a brass plate — wound by the tide itself, through a float, so that the well powers the pen that keeps its own diary. The groove it has cut is worn deep and shining, the oldest line in the world still being written.

The cage lift hangs frozen at the gallery's height, its gate open, and it has become an island. Silt and guano have filled its floor to the depth of a hand; ferns grow out of it, and on its roof, most days, sits the turtle. There is one turtle. It came in through the intakes decades ago, furred its shell with algae, and adopted the cage, and it watches the water with the patience of something that has exactly one possession and all the time there is. Below the gallery, an iron ladder drops toward the water with half its rungs missing — the fallen ones lie in the sump, encased — and the surviving rungs are worn thin as wire where hands and feet once passed. No hands pass now. An oil can stands on a gallery shelf with a curtain of flowstone grown over it, entombed mid-errand.

The water is the thing to understand, because the water moves, and everything else arranges itself around the moving. The well breathes. When the works below draw water — the pumps still run, slow oversize bronze impellers turned by the dam's own power, built for a thousand years by engineers who undershot — the level falls two, three, five meters, holds, and rises again on a period of hours. When something far downstream opens a gate, a fainter tide tugs through the intakes. And under both, the long breath: the reservoir itself rising and falling with the wet years and the dry, recorded on the shaft walls as rings, dozens of them, mineral bathtub lines stacked up the concrete, some meters above any level the water has reached in centuries. Everything alive in the well keeps the schedule. The snails — fist-sized, unhurried — graze the tide band and leave clean spirals in the biofilm like handwriting. The frogs call loudest on the falling tide, when their ledges emerge. The eels, thick as an arm, slide out of the intake ports on the rising water and are gone before it falls.

The surface, when you lean over the gallery rail, lies a long way down and carries its own weather: mats of water fern, drowned figs, a confetti of fallen insects. The fish — small, pale, quick — rise at dusk in dimples. A heron comes through the wellhead sometimes, folds itself onto the cage roof beside the indifferent turtle, and hunts the slack tide, the only citizen of two worlds besides the swifts, who nest in the housing's eaves and scream down the shaft at evening like thrown knives.

Below the surface, the light column keeps falling. It passes through water stacked in warm layers, each a few degrees more than the last, bath-warm at the top and hot as a held breath near the intakes, so that to descend the well would be to descend through climates. The underwater walls are terraced in sinter — ivory, rust-orange, jade where algae furs the stone — the dam's own water building the dam's interior into something that looks, at last, like a hot spring, like the machine's idea of a natural cave. At the vents, microbial mats stream orange and green in the upwellings. The intakes breathe faintly. Somewhere below them lies the drowned gorge, the deep warm dark of the reservoir, the springs feeding up through silt that has been settling since the valley had a name.

The sound never stops, but it is mostly one sound — the hum, organ-low, conducted through water and masonry into bone — and then the events. Water hammer, when a valve seats somewhere in the dam's body: a blow that shudders the ladder and rolls up the shaft like a barrel thrown downstairs. The drips, each arriving after long seconds of echo, a slow applause in the dark. The chorus, ringing the concrete like a bell. On storm days, rain hammers the wellhead housing and overflow sheets down the upper walls, and the well becomes a wall of falling water and a roar, and the frogs go silent and hold on, and the turtle, presumably, closes its eyes.

Nothing here is finishing decaying, because the decay already finished. What could rust, rusted in the first century, and the rust crust now armors the iron beneath. What could fall, fell, and lies at the bottom building the substrate. The water's chemistry and the dam's substance reached equilibrium so long ago that corrosion has nearly stopped; accretion balances loss; the ruin is arrested, load-bearing, final. The well is not dying. The well is what was left after everything that could die had done so.

And it is still working. The conduits hum away through the masonry toward somewhere. The pumps lift water to the crest gardens, which no one tends, which have consequently become a hanging wilderness of fig and fern and frog, the well watering its own relatives. Somewhere beyond the hills there may be a city drawing power from this wall, or there may be an empty switchboard in a silent room, lamps burning for no one. The well does not know and could not act on the knowledge. It draws, it lifts, it rains on itself, it records its own breathing into brass. A boat could pass the wellhead housing on the reservoir a hundred times and see a stone shed on a crest, one small structure on a wall half a kilometer high, in a drowned world made entirely by hands.

Inside, the tide is rising. The light column is sliding off the water and up the eastern wall as the sun goes. The lamp burns. The snail trails advance their spirals by the width of a tongue. The frogs are beginning to tune themselves against the hum, two notes in the one quiet window, and in a little while, when the water turns, the whole shaft will ring.
