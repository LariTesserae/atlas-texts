The first thing is the warmth. The dark is supposed to be cold — dark is cold everywhere else — but here the dark is warm as a held breath, warm as a wall that has stood all day in sun. Except there is no sun. There has never been a sun. Dawn is not scheduled; whatever lights this world possesses are underneath it. The ground is the sun, and it shines heat upward without troubling to shine light.

And it is high. You climb — or the ground rises under you, which here amounts to the same thing — into air so thin it has half forgotten how to carry sound. Your breath comes short and loud in your own ears. Your heartbeat, at rest, ticks in your throat like something cooling. Everything else is quiet.

The quiet is the wrong part, because the place is not still.

Stand on the rim of the high country — the locals would call it the Front, if there were locals — and watch long enough, and the dark begins to work. It starts as light: a seam of dull red brightening beneath the black bulk of a ridge, moving through the stone the way a thought moves through a mind. Then a hum arrives, too low for the thin air, so it comes through your boot soles, your sternum, the fillings of your teeth. The ridge lifts. Not fast — a handspan in an hour, a body's height between one meal and the next — but it lifts, and as it lifts it covers a low red star on the horizon that is not a star at all, only a younger ridge, farther off, lit from within. A spur shears away from the new slope. In thick air it would crack like the end of the world; here it goes in near-silence, dust rising in slow black columns lit from inside by lightning that makes no sound you can catch. The boom, when it comes, comes twenty seconds late and strangely gentle — a door closing in another room of the world. Then the ticking begins: fresh stone cooling, pinging at random, the sound of a house settling, if the house were a horizon.

The whole range ticks like a cooling kiln. It is, in every sense, being fired.

Nothing here is worn. This is the fact you keep tripping over, literally: nowhere in this world is there a rounded stone. Roundness has not been invented. Every surface is a fresh fracture — conchoidal scallops of black glass, knife-edged ridges, columnar joints standing in organ-pipe ranks with ends as crisp as the day they parted, which was Tuesday. There is no soil. There is no sand; sand is an erosion product, and erosion is an apprentice who arrived late and works slowly. At the base of one cliff lies a single small apron of shards — the mountain's first scree, the first fall of the first dust, angular and electrostatic and smelling of nothing. The dust has no smell. Smells are made by weathering and life, and there has been neither. The wind, such as it is, is only now learning to carry things. Somewhere a new crack has found the thin wind and is trying out the world's first whistle. Every sound here is the first of its kind. There are no old sounds.

And there is no water. None. The only water in this world is inside stone — bound into the lattices of certain crystals as a structural idea, water as ingredient, never as event. It has never flowed, never pooled, never fallen. Down in the deep crevasses, where the elevation swings to its other extreme and the warmth thickens toward a baker's door, the world's only river runs: a slow black-red stream of molten sulfur, and where it meets the warm thin air it burns with a knee-high blue flame, silent, and has burned for years. It is the closest thing this place has to a landmark, and the closest thing it has to rain is the faint struck-match smell drifting up from it. Drink nothing. There is nothing to drink. The wettest thing for a hundred miles in any direction is you, and your sweat vanishes almost before it beads, salt rimming your lips, as if the dry world is tasting you and finding you implausible.

Light, here, is not a condition but an event. It means something is being made, or unmade. The ember-seams crawl beneath the high plateau at a walking pace, and where a seam passes, terrain follows — the light arrives first, and the mountain comes after, days behind, like a promise the ground made and is slow to keep. You learn to navigate by the glow through the rock, by the few red not-stars on the horizon, by the blue burn in its crevasse. Seam-light throws your shadow onto the undersides of overhangs. Shadows fall up here. Your eyes adjust; the dark is never absolute, only deep, and full of soft red under-edges like a forge seen through nearly closed doors.

Scattered across the dark — days of walking apart, with long empty black glass plains between — are the structures. No one built them. There is no one; there has never been anyone; there are no tools here, not even the idea of tools, unless you count the palm-sized flakes with struck-looking edges that litter one slope, knapped by nothing but thermal stress, the world practicing. The structures are the same gesture at larger size. On a northern flat stands the Stair: a flight of glass steps rising from bare ground into nothing, ending midair, its top step half-formed, a new one accruing every few days like a slow drip from below. It leads up. That is all it does. On a high saddle, a colonnade of hexagonal columns marches in a slight spiral, and the innermost pair lean together into an arch — almost. There is a wall, waist-high, dead straight, forty meters long, with a gap in the middle exactly like a doorway; the gap is the oldest part. The wall grew its doorway first and is building outward from it, as if the opening were the point and the wall an afterthought. On the glass plain, the silent lightning leaves its masonry behind: hollow tubes of fused stone, still warm, still ticking, scattered like straws dropped by some enormous careful bird. Call it habit — the mineralogist's word for the shapes crystals prefer. Here, habit has ambitions.

Nothing is alive. Keep that. And yet: camp on the plain, and in the time after you sleep — there is no morning — the crystals nearest your bedroll have leaned, by a degree you can barely prove, toward where your body warmed the ground. The towers shed their outer shells in slow spirals, like something molting, and regrow a body-length away, and the glass needles that stand on their points out on the charge-swept flats take small correcting steps, ticking faintly, drawn by static — not alive, probably, only weather with posture. You are the softest thing in this world, and the most finished, and even you are being revised: your footprints will not outlast the month, filled in by first dust or lifted away on a ridge that did not exist when you made them.

Maps are ephemera. Distances stay, but shapes do not; you measure time here in millimeters of uplift, in the spacing of ticks, in how far a seam of red has crept since you last looked. Come back in a month and this is a different country. Come back in a year and it has forgotten every shape you learned. A spire collapses somewhere in the dark, far off, and the soft late boom arrives like an afterthought, and the ticking resumes, and the warm dark goes on with its work — violent, unhurried, almost soundless, lit only by its own making.

It is not a place yet. It is the rehearsal for a place. The rehearsal is open to the sky, the sky is dark, the dark is warm, and the work goes on without hands. One of the red not-stars on the horizon has been getting closer for days, which means a seam is walking this way, which means that where you are standing, in a month, a mountain will stand.

Consider moving. Or don't, and be the first thing the mountain lifts.
