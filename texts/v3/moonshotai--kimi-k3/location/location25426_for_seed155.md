The sound reaches you before the valley does. You are still on the rim trail, in cloud, breath fogging, when you feel it in your sternum — a chord, vast and smooth, too low and too layered to be any single machine. Then the cloud thins, the trail crests, and the whole bowl of the plateau opens below you, and you understand that you have been hearing a place.

The mountains here are finished. Whatever they once were — teeth, walls, a range worth naming — has been worn down to a collar of green stumps, rounded and soft, an old range at the end of its life. Everything in the valley is the remainder of something taller. That is the first rule of the place, and it applies to more than stone.

The second thing you notice is that there is no ground. Not in the geological sense. The valley floor is **the Felt**: a mattress of root, moss, and rhizome, meters thick, centuries deep, unbroken from rim to rim. It springs underfoot. It swallows your footsteps while the air above it rings with the Hum. It holds your bootprint for an hour, greening at the edges, before the print forgets you. The Felt is cold at the surface and warm in its veins — deep slow fermentation runs through it in channels, and the keepers know the warm lines the way sailors know currents, and sleep on them in winter. The Felt also breathes. Once every few minutes, in time with the pumping cycle of the great engine halls, the whole valley floor rises a finger's width and settles. You stop noticing. Then you notice you have stopped noticing.

It does not rain here, except when it does, which is an event. The valley drinks clouds instead. It sits high, just at the altitude where the afternoon cloud-deck builds against the rim and spills over in slow gray surf, and the **Stacks** — forty of them, or sixty; the keepers count them every spring and get a different number and have stopped expecting otherwise — stand in their long fence and comb the fog out of the passing air. They are condensers, ancient beyond reckoning, their alloy worn smooth as river stones. You could not cut yourself on any edge in this valley. The stacks chill the thin air and weep it, drip by drip, into the **Dew Line**, the elevated aqueduct that runs the length of the valley and feeds the cisterns. The same water has been circulating here for ten thousand years — condensed, drunk, transpired, burned to steam, condensed again. It is flat, cold, utterly mineral-less. It tastes like nothing. It tastes like the idea of water.

The town — **the Row** — is strung along the Dew Line: a settlement built for ten thousand and kept by perhaps two hundred. Between each lit window there is a kilometer of green dark. Whole districts stand empty, and here the valley's totality shows its strangest talent. The vegetation did not simply overgrow the old buildings. It replaced them. A house dies — its timbers rot, its brick slumps — and the green, growing over it through the long damp years, keeps the shape. What remains is the memory of the house, executed in woven root: doorway, stairs, chimney, window-frames with old dewed glass still set in living walls. You can walk through a green block and pass a staircase, a piano, a truck, all of them exact, all of them plant, all of them hollow where the original was digested a century ago. The Felt is an archive of everything it has eaten.

Which brings you to the law of copies, which the keepers will explain to you early, in a careful voice.

Sometimes the green begins a copy of something that is not dead. A chair by a window. A tool on a bench. A person's boot. The copy grows toward completeness, and when it finishes — there has only ever been the copy. The original was never wood, never leather. You will remember it the new way. Everyone does. The Row is on its third church, or its first; the keepers shrug. If the green begins to copy something of yours, you leave before it finishes. This is not superstition. It is etiquette of the most practical kind, because in the valley the impossible has been domesticated into etiquette. Reality here is eroded like everything else — worn thin, worn through in places, settled into a final form that is slightly loose — and the keepers live inside the looseness the way fishermen live inside weather: respectfully, and with a book.

The book is **the Ledger**. In it, in the same columns and the same hand, the keepers record train arrivals, harmonic shifts in the Hum, rainfall, the spring count of the Stacks, glider sightings, and thinnings. They do not regard these as different kinds of event.

The thinnings are where the world has worn through. They are marked with bells, hung on poles, that ring in no wind — and sometimes ring slightly before whatever they are announcing. In a thinning, the dew falls up: cold threads of water rising visibly off the Felt toward the stacks, and the keepers step around them, and do not drink from them, because water going up tastes of *before*. In the cistern-cathedral beneath the engine halls — the loudest place in a loud valley, where the drips arrive in thousands and the dark gives each one back to you — the echoes come early. You can stand in the drip-choir and hear tomorrow's condensation, and the keepers come down in foul moods to listen to next week's weather. In the Old Grove, where the Felt is oldest and the trees, if they are trees, have stopped deciding, afternoons pool like water: a keeper walks in at noon and comes out at dusk having walked an hour, or the reverse, and the Ledger notes which.

And distance itself erodes. Use wears it. The path to the cistern, walked daily for two centuries, is now four hundred steps. The same route left unwalked for a decade is three kilometers. Maps are useless after a year; each spring the valley is re-measured, and the festival of the new map is called **the Shortening**. The valley is getting smaller. Slowly. Measurably. No one is alarmed. Everything here is the remainder of something larger, and the valley is simply doing what the mountains did, what the machines did, what the noise did.

For even the noise has eroded. Once there must have been a thousand sounds — engines, steam, bells, voices, weather — and they have worn against each other for so long that only one smooth chord remains, very loud, the way a river stone is very smooth. The Hum has layers, if you learn them: the sub-audible bass of the engine halls, the mid drone of the stacks drinking, the high hiss of venting, the organ-note of wind across the hollow plant-shells of dead buildings, the tick and creak, at dusk, of the Felt audibly growing. The keepers are born into the chord and read it the way you read a face. A keeper can hear, in the harmonics, which stack is gravid with ice, which cistern is low, where a thinning opened in the night. They say the chord is load-bearing. When a harmonic drops out, some small piece of physics goes with it, locally, until the engines are coaxed back up. If the Hum ever stops entirely — and here the keepers stop explaining and simply give you the rule.

The machines themselves stopped being maintained long ago and started being *tended*, and the difference is the whole history of this place. The great engines in their halls run on compressed peat-cake cut from the dead layers of the Felt, and on the cold gradient of the altitude, and — the keepers admit — on habit. Pistons have been found driven by the slow swelling of roots that grew into the cylinder bores generations ago and were simply incorporated. Timing is kept by tendril. The intakes of the condensers suck pollen and spore out of the air and distribute it up and down the valley through the ductwork; the valley's bees are turbines. Nothing hunts here but the green, and it hunts everything, at the speed of growing.

There is almost nothing else alive. A pale blind snail in the cisterns, good for broth. Dew-moths that come to the intakes at dusk in their one brief cloud. And the grey glider — enormous, silent, the only silent thing — that crosses the rim perhaps once a month, riding some high cold current, and whose passing is entered in the Ledger in the same hand as the thinnings. Its wingspan has never been agreed upon. The spring counts disagree about the glider too.

The light, it should be said, is honest. This is what unsettles visitors most, eventually. Thin high daylight, grey or pale blue, falling evenly over all of it; soft-edged shadows; at night the carbon-bright strings of arc lamps up the stacks and one lit window every kilometer along the Row. Nothing hides. Nothing lurks. The valley's strangeness happens at noon, in plain view, and the light reports it faithfully, like a clerk who no longer finds anything strange.

The day has its cycle. Dawn clear and bitter, frost on the Felt, visibility to the far rim. Midday, the cloud builds below the edge. Mid-afternoon, the fog spills over in slow surf and the stacks drink hardest — the Loud Hour, when the engines peak and the chord goes up a register and the keepers sign to each other rather than shout. Dusk: the moths, the growth-creak, the lamps, the upward threads at the thinnings. Night: radiant chill, the Hum dropping to its deep register, the Felt breathing underfoot. And every evening at 6:10, the train leaves the Row for the Drop — the rail line that goes over the plateau's edge and down into cloud that has never been seen through — and returns at 6:40 with its cars damp and its manifest unchanged, and no one loads it, and no one unloads it.

This must happen. Once, in a year the keepers do not name, it did not, and for an afternoon the valley was elsewhere.

The keepers will give you the rules if you stay the night, and they give them flatly, the way you would tell a guest where the privy is:

1. Be off the line at 6:10.
2. Answer no voice that calls before it speaks.
3. Never drink water that is going up.
4. Take the long way. You are shortening the other.
5. If the green begins to copy you, move house before it finishes.
6. If you notice that the Hum has stopped, do not speak of it, and do not look at the lamps. Walk until you hear the chord again. You are somewhere else, and it is not finished with you.

Stay a week and the valley gets into you. You start sleeping on the warm veins. You start hearing the harmonics. You stop jumping when the ground breathes. You catch yourself one dusk standing at a lit window of a green house, watching the lamps come on up the stacks, the fog combing itself to water on the old smooth metal, the threads of dew rising at the thinnings, the train's small light returning along the Dew Line exactly on time to no one — and the chord underneath it all, holding the world in place like a hand on a spinning top.

The valley has finished becoming. Now it only continues.
