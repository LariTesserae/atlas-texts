You hear it before you see it. From the rim of the hollow — a low swell of slag and spoil, nothing more — the sound rises up at you like heat off a road: a vast, layered, unhurried loudness that does not swell or fade but simply *is*, the way a waterfall is, the way weather is. Then you come over the rim and look down into the bowl, and there it is, the whole of it at once: a city the size of a farm.

The faded sign at the gate says LOW HOLLOW CONDENSATE WORKS — No. 3. Nobody can tell you where the other two are, or were.

The bowl is a few hundred paces across — an exhausted clay dig, or a natural sink, low even for this low country — and the town sits in the bottom of it like sediment. A dozen buildings, widely spaced, with broad empty yards of cracked clay pavement between them: an engine hall, a boiler house, a row of keepers' cottages, a storehouse, a weigh office, a chimney breathing out one thin constant thread of vapor. Sparse, you'd call it, until you look twice and realize that every surface in the place is moving. The sparseness is architectural. The saturation is flesh.

Everything here has arrived at its final shape and intends to keep it. The slag-brick walls have slumped to the exact angles they were always going to slump to, and stopped. The rust is not a process but a finish — a hard chestnut patina, set like enamel, that you cannot flake with a fingernail. Where condensation drips from the same points it has always dripped from, it has carved smooth basins an inch deep into the flagstones, and goes on dripping into them at the same rate, neither faster nor slower. Erosion here is not something happening. It is something completed, a signature at the bottom of a document.

The damp is the works' own doing. The great condensers in the engine hall breathe — a slow stroke every eleven seconds, in and out, a sound you feel in your sternum — and they exhale warm vapor into the cool basin air, and the vapor condenses on every cold surface in town: on the glass, on the rails, on the backs of animals, on you. Gutters and drip-points gather it and return it to the sump pool the town is named for, a shallow black pan under the hall's north wall whose level never rises and never falls. Pumps lift it back to the boilers. It is the same water it has always been. Rain comes occasionally, and the bowl keeps it, and that is the only addition. The water has been through the loop so many times it has stopped being weather and become something more like blood — faintly slick, mineral-heavy, tasting of iron and faintly of ozone. The bowl leaks nothing.

There are no plants. Not one. What there is, at the very bottom of everything, is the felt: a gray chemical film, barely a fungus, life at its lowest setting, that skins every cold wet surface and grazes the condensate for whatever the machines have dissolved into it. Everything else eats the felt, or eats what ate the felt.

And there is so much else. The lickers come first — small, slug-fleshed, many-legged things drawn to electrical terminals, clustering on live contacts until their bodies bridge the gap and they sizzle faintly, contentedly, drinking the current; they keep every brass fitting in the works polished to a mirror with their tongues. Steam-moths live their whole adult lives inside a single venting plume, and when the valve cycles they rise in a gray cloud and settle again like ash falling upward. Pipe-eels slide through the conduits, fat as your forearm, and you can hear them sometimes, a wet hush inside the walls. The kettles nest in the rafters of the engine hall — bird-shaped, galvanized-looking, their feathers faintly metallic from the vapor — and their calls have converged on the machine sounds around them until you genuinely cannot always say which is which: one ticks like a cooling engine, one whistles like a steam leak, one does the condenser's eleven-second breath half a beat behind the condenser. Slack-hounds run the yards, feral, their coats iron-stained orange at the belly, barking in notes that ring like struck pipe. And at the sump pool stands the Heron. There is one. It stands on the same stone. It has always stood on that stone. Ask the keepers whether it is the same bird and they will look at you with the exhausted patience of people who have stopped finding the question interesting.

The loudness is total and it never varies, and this does something strange to your nervous system within a day or so: it becomes a silence. The drips fall at their fixed intervals — a four-second drip in the nave of the engine hall, a two-second drip off the weighbridge, a whole polyrhythm of them that repeats identically forever — and the condenser breathes, and the kettles answer the machines, and the hounds ring their pipes, and the sum of it is so constant that you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heart. The keepers, what few there are, talk in hand signs. When they must speak they lean into your ear, not because the moment is louder but because the habit has eaten them.

The keepers are sparse — nine of them, or eleven, depending on whom you ask and when — and they live in the cottage row with lit windows, and they do their work, and their work is the strangest thing in a strange place, because it changes nothing. They read dials that never move and log the readings. There is a ledger room in the weigh office, shelves of logbooks going back and back, and every entry in every book is identical: the same pressures, the same levels, the same temperatures, thousands of pages recording an unvarying state in a dozen unvarying hands. They shovel coal from the yard pile into the boiler, and the pile does not get smaller. No deliveries have ever arrived. They polish gauges that already shine. They are not maintaining the works so much as *attending* it, the way you attend a sleeper who will not wake and will not die.

They do not age. There are no children among them, and no old ones, and this turns out to be true of everything in the bowl: nothing here is young, nothing is old, everything is exactly its age. Nobody has ever seen a birth in the Sump, and nobody has ever seen a death, and yet nobody believes the creatures immortal. The census simply holds. A hound vanishes for a week, and then there is a hound again, the right size, the right rust-stain at the belly, and the keepers enter nothing in any book because there is nothing to enter. Things removed have a way of being found again. Disturbances damp out. Throw a stone in the Pan and watch: the ripples go, the stillness comes back, and the stillness is the same stillness, and you get the vertiginous sense that the water is not merely calm but *restored*, returned to a state it keeps the way a kept promise is kept.

Day and night come and go — the light here is ordinary, mercifully ordinary, flat overcast days and lamp-lit nights, the one normal thing the place allows you — but each day is the same day. The same kettle crosses the same yard at the same hour. The same valve sighs. The same vapor thread rises from the chimney into the same cool air. The keepers keep no calendars; the only clock is the drip-rhythm, and it measures nothing, because there is nothing to measure. People who stay here stop marking birthdays after a while, not from despair but from a kind of gentle pointlessness, the way you stop knocking on a door you know will open onto the same room.

Walk across it rim to rim and it takes twenty minutes. Study it and it would take years. Stand at the gate on your way out, up on the spoil rim, and look back: the sparse buildings in their wide empty yards, every surface stirring with small metallic life, the loudness rising like heat, the Heron on its stone, the four-second drip mid-fall exactly where you left it.

Nothing will happen here in your absence. You can come back in a year, in ten years, and the drip will still be falling into the basin it carved, and the ledgers will have grown by thousands of identical pages, and the town will be precisely, impossibly, *kept* — the one place in the world where the phrase "nothing happened" is not a disappointment but a description of the local weather, the local law, the local god.
