Begin with the flatness, because the flatness is the story. This is a plain so old it has stopped being a place that things happen *to* and become a place that simply *is*. Mountains stood here once — real mountains, with snow and shadow — and the rivers spent them, grain by grain, until nothing was left worth carrying. What remains is what geologists call a peneplain: an almost-plain, a landscape that has reached the end of its argument with gravity and lain down. The ground lies so close to the level of its own water that the distinction between *land* and *water* is more a courtesy than a fact.

The materials tell the age. Underfoot: quartz sand, fine and pale as sugar — the unburnable ash of the vanished mountains, the one mineral too stubborn to rot, rounded and re-rounded until each grain is a small exhausted planet. Where the land breaks its skin it shows laterite, ironstone the color of dried blood and rust, soft as cheese where it's sheltered, hard as armor where the air touches it. That's one of the plain's quiet rules: whatever is exposed, petrifies. The land scabs in stone. Between the rust-red runs bone-white kaolin, clay so pure it squeaks, leached of everything a root could want. The whole palette — rust, bone, ochre, the grey of old ash, the black of standing water — is the palette of a world where everything soluble left long ago.

Here is the strangest honest thing about the terrain: the oldest rivers are now the highest ground. Channels that ran twenty million years ago laid down gravels that cemented into stone, and the softer floodplain around them wore away, so that today those ancient riverbeds run along the ridgetops — low gravel ridges, sinuous, walking across the flat like the fossilized spines of snakes. You can walk along the crest of a river that died before birds learned their current songs. And far off — a day's walk, maybe two, shimmering at the edge of sight — one inselberg remains: the last unspent lump of the continent, worn smooth as a beached whale, the only vertical sentence in an entirely horizontal book.

Water is everywhere and almost nowhere deep. The water table sits at the skin of things; press a heel into the clay and it fills. There are three waters here. Clear water slides over sand in the living channels, tea-tinted, cold against the ankle. Black water stands under the trees, stained the color of dark glass by tannin, still enough to hold the sky's whole face. And after rain there is milk-water, pale with suspended clay, spreading in sheets across the plain until the country becomes a river a hand deep and a horizon wide, flowing so slowly that a fallen petal can drift for a week and rot before it arrives anywhere. The sea is weeks downstream, but it owns this place. The plain lies at the sea's exact level, and nothing can leave faster than the sea permits.

The light is plain daylight, warm and unironic, but the air it passes through is nearly water itself. At dawn the old channels are legible as mist — you can read yesterday's drainage in the fog, white ribbons laid into every hollow, and everything weeps: leaves, stone, your own skin, the air paying out its overnight surplus. By midday the horizon dissolves entirely, not into fog but into a gentle boil of evaporation, distance shimmering until the far pans float and the inselberg rides on nothing. Sound behaves strangely in this soaked air. There is no echo anywhere. The flat gives nothing back; a call goes out over the water and is absorbed, swallowed, finished.

Vegetation is sparse because the ground permits so little — drowned, airless, starved. Sedge holds the country in tussocks, each clump its own small island with dark water between. Trees stand far apart, stunted and wide-crowned, like figures who have each been given their own room and have grown eccentric in the privacy. Because the soil gives nothing, much of the green here has turned carnivore: sundews furred with glue, pitcher plants drowning their clientele in rainwater, bladderworts sucking larvae out of the black water with a snap you can feel more than hear. The plain is thin in leaf and thick in flesh. Green is punctuation here, not prose.

And the flesh is abundant beyond reason. The water is full of fish that breathe air — gulping, rolling, some of them walking between pools on stiff fins when their pans shrink — and in the dry season the lungfish go down into the mud and wait in clay envelopes for a year that hasn't happened yet. Wading birds work the shallows in patient lines; small long-toed birds walk across the floating leaves as if across a green floor. Turtles stack themselves on the few drowned snags like coins on a ledge. A barrel-bodied amphibious grazer moves between pans on trails its kind has used so long that the trails now guide the sheet-flow — the animals, without knowing, have become the plain's minor hydrology. Great reptiles lie in the channels like logs, and the only way to tell the difference is that logs don't blink. At the kaolin exposures the animals come to eat the clay itself — parrot-like birds in shrieking hundreds, the grazers at dusk — the one mineral wealth this leached country still pays out. Over everything, dragonflies, thousands, threading the bright air. Their design is older than the mountains were.

Which brings the sound. For a place so old it is astonishingly loud, and the loudness has a schedule. Dawn is birds, a rush of announcement that lasts an hour and then thins. Midday belongs to the insects, a drone that moves in waves across the plain like weather, swelling until it has a physical pressure and then passing on. Dusk is the frogs, and the frogs are an orchestra built by a lunatic: ratchets, bells, bowed wires, dropped coins, thumb-strummed combs, a sound like two stones knocked together underwater — thousands of throats, each species on its own beat, until the whole plain vibrates. Night is punctuated by splashes: fish striking, turtles sliding in, something large changing its mind. The land itself makes no sound at all. The noise is a thin, young, frantic skin stretched over an enormous quiet, and somehow you can always hear the quiet underneath, the way you can see the bottom under black water.

If there is architecture here, no one built it. Across the drier rises stand termite mounds — red blades and spires, some taller than a person, all of them aligned north to south as if the plain were magnetized, as if something were being pointed at. In certain light, with mist in the hollows, a field of them reads exactly as the rooftops and steeples of a drowned city, and a traveler would swear someone had been here. No one has — unless you count the small masons, whose spit cements the earth into something like brick. Abandoned mounds outlive their makers by centuries, iron-hard, hollow, humming with wind. There are mud-nesting birds too, kiln-shaped nests stuck to the undersides of the inselberg's overhangs. These are the only monuments: built by mouth, outlasted by their own substance.

Change comes, but at the speed of the place. Not weather — seasons. The water rises over weeks and falls over months; the flood pulse is the year's single heartbeat, and everything alive here keeps time by it. Channels migrate on the scale of centuries, abandoning oxbows that silt into meadows of sedge. Once in a generation lightning sets a pocket of dry peat smoldering, and the fire burns underground for years, slow as thought — even combustion here keeps local time. The annual drama is the birds: one morning the sky simply fills with arrivals, the empty flyways suddenly grammatical, and for a few weeks the plain is crowded with voices that will leave.

What the place finally is, is this: not a dying landscape but a finished one. It has no ambitions left. The mountains are underfoot as sugar-sand; the rivers of deep time ride the ridgetops as stone; the water lies at the level the sea allows and goes no lower. Everything patient has already happened. What remains is the dusk — the mist returning to the channels, the whale-back inselberg going violet at the horizon, and the frogs beginning, thousands of brief bright voices rising off the oldest ground in the world like steam off warm water.
