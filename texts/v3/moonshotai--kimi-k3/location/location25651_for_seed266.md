The first thing is the height. The world — its inhabitants call it the Anvil — is a single continental shield of old stone raised so far above its own lowlands that the lowlands have stopped being a place and become a rumor. They call what lies below the Undersky: a sea of brown haze filling every horizon, kilometers deep, never visited, never cleared. Stand at the Rim and you are not looking down at ground. You are looking down at weather.

The air up here is thin. It forgives little and carries less. Sound dies in it — a shout travels forty paces and gives up, and a conversation held across a street is a conversation held in gestures. And yet the altitude is not cold. The warmth of the Anvil does not come from its sun, which is honest and ordinary, a white disc in a noon sky gone deep violet from thinness, the brighter stars showing through near the zenith like nailheads through cloth. The warmth comes from below. The plateau sits over a patient fire, and the stone holds the temperature of blood. You can lie down anywhere on the Finished Range and be warmed as though the ground were an animal that had agreed to carry you.

The mountains are done. That is the word the surveyors use, without irony: done. The scree slopes locked at the angle of repose ten thousand years ago and were cemented there by slow chemistry. Nothing is jagged. Nothing is young. There are no landslides, no fresh faces, no work left for erosion to do except the finest work, the work of powder. The world has reached its shape and means to keep it.

What water would do here, silt does. There is no water — there has never been water — but the Anvil has its own working fluid: the fines, a mineral powder ground over eons until it forgot how to be solid. It drifts into dunes that migrate a body's length a year. It pours off ledges in silt-falls, dry cascades, silent, that take a whole afternoon to empty a hanging valley. The great basins hold silt-lakes, pale and perfectly level, that ripple when the Long Breeze crosses them — waves that take minutes to travel a kilometer, breaking so slowly against the shore that you can walk alongside one and keep pace. Everything here carries a charge, so the fines cling to wool, to skin, to cable; sparks jump from door handles; a man crossing a dune field at dusk trails a faint cold glow. And when the charge gathers itself into the dry storms, there is still no thunder — thunder is a thing that happens in thicker air. Instead the ridges flicker in silence, every mast and wire crowned with cold fire, and the city dims itself deliberately, opening its accumulators to drink.

Days are long, and dusk takes hours, the airborne silt stretching sunset into a long peach-colored courtesy that the inhabitants have never thought to be grateful for, having known nothing else. Nights are enormous. But even at the darkest hour the ground is warmer than the sky, because the stone exhales through the night what it stored through the day. You do not need fire here. You need patience.

The only thing that grows is stonefelt. It is not a plant; it is simpler than a plant, a gray nap of filaments that eats bare rock and ground-heat and nothing more, spreading a hair's width a year across sheltered faces. Where it is old it is thick as carpet, and soft, and faintly warm. It is unlovely and it is indispensable: it is the bottom of every stomach on the Anvil, one way or another.

Above the felt are the millbeasts.

A grown millbeast is a hill that has decided to walk. Its mouth is a mill in the literal sense — rotary plates of ever-growing tooth-stone, turning slowly against each other — and it feeds by walking into a cliff and chewing the mountain, felt and all, a bite a minute, forever. A young one takes a step a day. The old ones step when they remember to. Their bodies run warm; they carry the Anvil's fever in them, and in the cool of morning each beast stands in a faint shimmer of its own heat. A herd is a dozen animals scattered across a hundred kilometers, and you will rarely see two at once — but they are always there, on the horizon of somewhere, in the corner of the eye of every long journey. Nothing can hurt them except their own mass and the patience of certain things that wait in the silt. So they do not hurry. The very old ones accrete: gardens of felt on their broad backs, tended by crews of pickers — dog-sized, many-legged, meticulous — until a venerable beast carries its own small ecology around like a traveling province. The city names the ancients and tracks them the way sailors elsewhere track currents.

The fauna of the Anvil are abundant in the way the Anvil permits abundance: enormous variety, thinly spread, constant. Under the silt-lakes, silt-rays wing through the powder as though it were sea, breaching at dusk in slow pale fountains that hang in the air for minutes. Galvans — fist-sized, silver, quick — cluster at every insulator on the tram lines, drinking stray current, and the line-keepers read omens in their gatherings and are only half joking. Along the Rim, where the updrafts rise off the Undersky, the drifters hang: translucent gas-veiled creatures the size of sails, kilometers of them in loose procession, sipping the voltage gradient between the ground and the sky, glowing faintly all over when the dry storms come. In the warm seeps, where the plateau's blood comes nearest the surface, small quick things flicker through the fines in their thousands. And beneath the silt-lakes, sessile and vast and older than most districts, the kettle-mouths wait — open pits ringed with grinding plates, mouths that opened when your street was young and will still be open when it is gone. They eat a silt-ray every few years. They are in no hurry either. The dust-pilots chart them, and the charts are treated as scripture.

When a millbeast dies, it does not rot. There is nothing here to rot it. It stands or lies where its legs left it, and over the centuries its tissues trade themselves, atom by atom, for stone. And what stone: a millbeast spends millennia filtering the rare metals of the range out of the felt and the rock, and it stores them in bone, in plate, in the great turning millstones of its mouth. A dead millbeast is the richest ore body on the Anvil. A fallen beast is a fortune with ribs, and the ribs arch over the quarries like the scaffolding of cathedrals. The fall-wards watch the old herds for a stagger the way other people watch weather. When one goes down — and it takes days to finish falling — a horn sounds, once, and a district is born.

The city is called the String, because that is its shape: districts like beads on the cable-lines, strung across ten thousand kilometers of warm empty stone. It is a real city — smelters, foundries, presses, trams, lamplit streets, markets, bureaucracy — but sparse, faithful to the world's proportions. An hour's walk takes you out of a district's last lit street into a dark so complete you can navigate only by the feel of the causeway underfoot; days later, the glow of the next smelter rises over a rise like a small, patient dawn. Each district lives by its carcass. It is founded at a fall and exhausted in a thousand years, and its whole civic life — its foundries, its trade, its name — hangs from the skeleton it was born beside. The Yards of the Fifth Fall. Kettle Row. Harrow District, where the Great Lathe has been turning for six hundred years and is expected, in another four hundred, to be finished.

The industry is slow the way everything here is slow. The great presses cycle once an hour, each stroke a deep pulse in the Hum that underlies everything. A keel-beam for a silt-barge is cast over a full year, cooled at the rate the stone prefers; to hurry a casting is to ruin it, and nothing here is built to be finished within a life anyway. Trams glide between districts, silent, electric, their wires furred with roosting galvans. On the silt-lakes the barges move at walking pace on wide skis, screws churning pale wakes that take days to settle, pilots consulting the kettle-mouth charts and the felt-thick news of the herds. The air smells of hot stone, ozone, and beast-tallow — the oil that seals and lubricates everything, rendered from the carcasses, in which the people also dress their skins and light their lamps, because on the Anvil everything comes back to stone and beast. The people themselves are of the world's own chemistry: there is nothing to drink here, and they do not drink. A mug of something warm in a doorway at dusk is warm with the ground's warmth, and that is understood to be enough.

One district walks. A thousand years ago a consortium bolted a small refinery to the carapace of a millbeast now known only as Patience, reasoning that the ore in an ancients' body needn't wait for its death. Patience has never noticed. Her grazing loop takes two hundred years, and the district rides it — children grow up in a town that is somewhere else every decade, whose address is a matter of astronomy, whose view changes on a schedule of centuries. The engineers there swear the machines run better, steadied by the step.

The world has its rules, and they are simple.

Sound is rationed — by the thin air, and then by custom on top of it. Bells are rung at foundings and at exhaustions. You may hear two in a life. In the String you may make all the noise you like, provided you make it slowly.

Nothing falls fast — except off the Rim. What goes over the Rim falls forever, as far as anyone knows, and the districts tip their tailings there and their worst tools and their unlucky, and the Undersky keeps all of it.

Never build in a herd-path. The millbeasts will not stop. They are not cruel. They are geology.

What dies on your land is yours. A fall is a founding; a founding is a thousand years; plan accordingly.

And the quiet is a commons. It belongs to everyone, it is enormous, and it is the one thing the Anvil produces in true abundance.

At dusk the horn of the nearest district sounds its single note, and the thin air swallows it before the next district can hear. The stone ticks as it settles into its nightly exhale. The galvans crowd the cooling wires. Somewhere out in the dark a millbeast takes its one step of the day, and the felt of it travels up through the bedrock, through the causeway, through the soles of your feet — less a sound than an event, happening to the whole plateau at once. Far off, on other mesas beyond gulfs no one crosses, the lights of sibling strings come on one by one, places you will never go, whose horns you will never hear, humming the same Hum.

The world is warm, and finished, and patient. It is listening — as slowly as it does everything else.
