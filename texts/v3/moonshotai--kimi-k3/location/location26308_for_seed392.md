Brightness comes first: a noon that has never begun to end. The sun — if it is a sun, and not simply a decision the sky has made — stands fixed overhead, and the heat it lays down is wet, full, unwavering. The basin lies low beneath it, lower than its rim by only a few coils of clay. Nothing here is taller than a tree, and the trees are few. From anywhere in it you can see the far side, a bright line in the haze, an hour's walk away — if the walk were ordinary, which it is not.

Underfoot, the world gives. Not mud: there is no mud here, no soil anywhere, no stone that was not carried and set. The floor of the world is woven — split cane and rush and flattened reed plaited into mats the size of meadows, lashed edge to edge over bundled-float foundations. Water lives in the gaps. Press a foot down and the weave sinks a little and water rises to meet it, black and bright. Everything floats. The whole basin is a raft, and below the raft the water sits in a vessel: if you dive, down past the hanging roots of the gardens, you find no bedrock, no silt — only clay, coiled clay, a pot built to the size of a valley, its walls spiraling down into the bright dark. The rim of it is the low wall on the horizon. The coils of it bear thumbprints the size of fields, pressed when the clay was soft. Which, in places, it still is. Which it always will be.

At hand scale the making continues: coiled walls waist-high, mud brick laid without mortar, each brick palm-printed, the ridges of the maker's hand as crisp as the hour of pressing. A stone adze rests on a half-split bamboo beam. A bone needle long as a forearm stands mid-stitch in a seam of matting, threaded, waiting. Everything is mid-something. And nothing weathers. Erosion has not begun here and will not: the thatch is golden, the lashings taut, every cut edge of every reed as sharp as the day of the knife. The few copper beads strung along a doorway have never heard of verdigris.

The air sits exactly at its dew point and stays there. Every surface wears beads of water that never merge, never run, never fall. Where a mat ends over open water, a drop has gathered at the cut end of a reed and declines to drop — it has been gathering since the making, and is part of the furniture now, a small lens full of upside-down world. The heat-shimmer does not move either. It stands in the air in fixed draperies, creased gauze over the distances, and when you walk through a bank of it you feel it, faintly, like passing through the ghost of a curtain.

The gardens float, spaced wide across the open water — a woven raft with a stand of papyrus; another with a mound of something flowering; another carrying a single leaning tree at the angle it chose and will keep. Between them, long reaches of nothing: water, matting, light. Emptiness is the main building material here, and the structures are few enough to count on two hands. The vegetation, though, is lush beyond argument, because the boundary between made and grown has gone soft. Half the weave is germinating. Green shoots rise from the dead reed of the floor itself. A rope bridge is rope for its first span and living vine for its second, and no joint can be found. A set-aside basket has sprouted through its own wicker. Every leaf is beaded with the permanent dew, full, green to the edge of white in the light. Nothing grows past its moment. Nothing wilts back. The smell is wet reed and hot clay, sweet and mineral, and it does not change.

The animals are abundant, but the basin is wide, so you meet them one at a time. A heron in the shallows, still as the hanging drop. Weaver birds coming and going from their nests — and their nests are the architecture, or the architecture is their nests: gourd-shaped woven halls hanging in the garden trees, some doorless, some with doors on the inside, all of them continuous with the plaited logic of the floor, the walls, the world. Mud-dauber wasps have raised a rank of clay organ-pipes against the base of a wall, tuned to a wind that does not blow. Fish hang in the clear water, unhurried. Dragonflies hold station in the heat. A frog sits at a mat's edge and does not call. The makers of the larger works are never seen at it. You will find their thumbprints in the small clay, though. They are the size, and the whorl, of your own.

The rules assert themselves quietly, spaced out across the emptiness like everything else. The half-made things cast the shadows of their completion: a wall that reaches your knee lays the cool silhouette of a wall over your head; the half-coiled pot shades you like a jar you could crawl into. Since nothing here ever advances, these are futures permanently declined, and the world keeps them anyway, laid out flat on the matting — the only darkness in all this brightness, hypothetical shade. The still water is the only place anything moves: ripples cross it from no fish, and reflected birds traverse the reflected sky while their originals stand asleep on one leg. It is as though the world's small allowance of change were all spent in the reflections, which is why the surface itself can afford such calm. Distances are polite. Walk the causeway without watching the far end and you arrive before you have walked half of it; watch, and it takes the hour. And change, when it happens at all, happens only unwitnessed, and always back to itself — watch a nest all day and it does not advance; look away and it is further along, and exactly the same.

Sound is scarce because the world is padded. The weave drinks footsteps. Even the insects keep their hum low, as though the air itself were stuffed with fiber. Once in a long while there comes a single creak — a rope taking up its load, though its load has not changed. It is always the same creak, from the same direction, at no interval anyone has measured. It is the closest thing the basin has to a clock.

To stand in it is to stand in the first hour of a made thing — a world finished a moment ago, where the moment was asked to last, and agreed. The dew does not fall. The drop does not drop. The noon holds. If the place has a name it is the Nearly-Finished: brought to the edge of done and left there, deliberately, lovingly left, with its last stitch unpulled and its final coil unlaid — holding the bright held breath that is, here, the same thing as rest.
