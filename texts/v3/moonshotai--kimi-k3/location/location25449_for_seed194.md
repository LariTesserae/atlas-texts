The country above is nothing much. A low salt pan, a skin of dust over old stone, a horizon that has been worn down almost to the idea of a horizon. Wind crosses it without finding anything to say. And near the pan's shallow center — in the lowest place of a low place — there is a collapsed sink, no wider than a well, where the crust has fallen through into the dark.

The descent is short. A fissure, a slant of rubble, a cold breath rising to meet you. The light does not fade so much as end — rung by rung, then all at once — and after the last grey rung there is no light at all, and there has been none for longer than anyone can count.

Then the sound arrives, because here sound arrives before anything else.

It begins below hearing: a fundamental so deep it is felt in the sternum, the teeth, the marrow, before the ear accepts it as a tone. The whole cavity breathes — slowly, on a period of days, answering the pressure of the sky above through the throat of the sink — and the breath plays the place like an instrument. Great hollow columns, flued and chambered, hold the note the way organ pipes hold theirs. Over hours the chord shifts, a quarter-tone at a time, as the cool air flexes by fractions of a degree. Under the drone: a faint continuous sifting, the rain of dry dust. And at intervals — no pattern to them, sometimes minutes, sometimes days — the cracks. Sharp as a whip close by, blooming into rolling thunder far off, each one a release of stress stored for years in slow-growing tissue. Growth, when it is slow enough, is indistinguishable from patience. When it lets go, it sounds like a shot.

This is not a quiet darkness. It is a loud one. Loudness is the medium; the dark is full of its own announcements.

If light were allowed — it is not; that is the one law, older than anything living here — you would see that the cavern is a city, and that the city is a plant.

One plant. That took a long time to become true. It began, in some unrecoverable age, as a forest that crept in through the then-open mouth of the cave, following the light inward, species after species, until the growth overgrew its own sky and sealed the entrance behind itself — whether to keep the dark or to keep the water is a matter of interpretation. In the dark the many species, graft-compatible, pressed together over millennia, merged into continuity. Now there is a single individual with the body of a city. Its hollow, lignified trunks are the columns of the halls. Its flattened runners, packed and worn and polished, are the streets. Its galls, swollen to room size, are the dwellings. Its vessels are conduits; its fruits are cisterns; its dead outer tissues lie in broad pale plains like fields of bark. Every district is an organ. The streets connect like veins.

Everything is pale — though paleness is a rumor no one can verify. Pigment is a waste in a world without light, so the organism makes none: the towers and walls are bone-white, translucent in thin places, ghostly, beautiful in a way that has never once been witnessed.

It does not run on water. Water is too precious to use. The sap here is a slow resin, an oil, non-aqueous chemistry in the most literal sense — and in places where the resin seeped and stilled long ago, it has hardened into amber, the city's fossil record of itself, full of preserved yesterdays that no light will ever shine through. What the plant cannot use, it excretes: salts crust at its nodes and bloom in dry frost-gardens, glittering for no one, the mineral waste of a metabolism that wastes nothing else. The dust that sifts down through the halls — sloughed cells, shed skin, the sediment of a desert made entirely of plant — is gathered by a felt of root-hairs that carpets the low corners and digests the dead directly back into the living. There are no decomposers here, no insects, no animals of any kind. The plant performs every office itself. It is the only ecology, and it is total.

As for water: there is none, which is to say, it is all spoken for. Every molecule that ever entered this pocket is held inside the plant, and most of it is not even spent — it is saved, pure, unmetabolized, in the cistern-fruits: drum-tight bladders hanging in the deep halls, ripening over decades, ticking softly as their pressure adjusts. Knock, goes a cistern in the dark, a slow deep note like something asking to be let in. They are treasuries. The dryness of the world is not a lack; it is a hoard.

There were tenders once. Gardeners, never many, and the city was their life's work and their afterlife both. Their technology was the technology of patience: flaked stone blades hafted in sockets the plant grew around the blades, so that the organism itself held the tools; thorn awls; braided fiber lines; weights and cords to bend a growing trunk along a chosen line; grafting, pruning, training — ten thousand years of horticulture instead of architecture. They wrote in knots, because writing in lines requires light, and their records are cord and scar, read by hand in the dark. They navigated by ear — by the drone, by the signature echo of each hall, by the district-voices of the cracks — and they built listening into the city: ear-trumpets grown from flared hollow stems, acoustic lenses of tight-laminated bark.

They are gone now. The cool preserves everything — this is cellar-cool, constant, the cool of held breath — so their dead did not rot; they dried, and the plant grew over them, and they are in the walls still, a gardener's hand preserved mid-gesture around a graft that succeeded eleven thousand years ago. They did not exactly die out. They completed. The work they bred into the organism — self-pruning branches, hedges that trim themselves by programmed die-back, corridors that clear their own dust — continues to execute, gardenerless. The city is a garden that is still being gardened by instructions left in flesh.

And so the halls are almost empty. This is the strangest abundance: a city that is mostly nothing. A hall the size of a valley contains three things — a fallen column-lid, a tool-cache, a cistern — and all three are famous, all three are in the knot-records, each with its own name and echo. The organism does not fill its cavern. It tunes it. The emptiness is the architecture; the walls are only there to give the sound something to return from.

Time moves at the pace of the breath and slower. Once in a long cycle — call it a year, though it is longer than any year above — the breath deepens, the drone swells, and the city pollinates itself on its own wind, a generation of spores carried through the halls with no insect ever consulted. The cracks mark the decades. The chord-shifts mark the hours. Nothing wears away anymore, because everything that could be worn has been worn; the corners were carried off by a process so slow it took millennia to notice. What remains is what remains. The place has settled into its final form and intends to keep it.

In the deepest district, past the treasuries, there are vaults of seeds. They will not germinate without light. The organism stores them anyway — a treasury of postponed mornings, a faith in a day it has sealed out forever. Perhaps the gardeners believed the throat would open again. Perhaps they only could not bring themselves to waste a seed. The vaults do not say. They tick, faintly, in the cool, keeping whatever it is that seeds keep.

Above, the wind crosses the salt pan and finds nothing — a dull country, a dead flat, a collapsed sink no wider than a well. It does not know the land is hollow. It does not know that the hollow is a single living thing the size of a town, pale and loud and ancient, breathing through its throat, playing one chord for ten thousand years, holding all the water there is.

Somewhere in the dark, a stress that has been building since before the gardeners' grandmothers reaches its limit.

The crack goes off like a cannon. The halls take it, roll it, thin it, hand it back and forth until it becomes the drone again.

Then the dust resumes its slow sift, and the chord slides, and the cisterns tick, and the loud dark goes on being itself, in its small hidden pocket beneath the nothing, indefinitely.
