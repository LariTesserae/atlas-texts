**The Stillworks**

The woods up top are thin — a few spruce holding on in poor soil, light coming down between them in loose change. In the middle of the slope the hill opens a mouth: a shaft of pale limestone, fluted like the throat of a shell, and water filling it almost to the rim. Still water. From the lip you can see down twenty meters and the only thing telling you there's water at all is the way the light bends. The few divers who have been down the shaft call the place the Stillworks, and the joke of the name wears off about halfway down.

You descend through the throat with the light. The walls here carry vertical ripples — dissolution flutes, the record of ten thousand years of water sliding past — and they catch the sun like machined grooves. At twelve meters the shaft bellies outward. At fifteen, you are no longer in a well. You are in a room.

One room. That is the whole of it: thirty paces long, ten across, an arched ceiling of gray-pale stone rising eight meters above a floor of white silt. And for a long, disoriented moment it reads as *architecture*. Six columns stand between floor and ceiling, irregularly placed, thick as barrels, their edges gone soft as old soap. A ledge runs along the far wall like a gallery. The walls themselves are covered in scallops — shallow, overlapping hollows the size of cupped hands, sweeping in the direction of flow, and they look exactly like castings, like the inside of something poured. The eye keeps filing the place under *built*: a nave, a cistern, a decommissioned pump hall.

Nothing here was built. The room makes you keep learning this. There is not a seam, not a right angle, not a mark of any tool anywhere in it — unless you count the scallops, which are tool marks left by no tool. The columns grew here when the chamber was dry, drip by drip, in air. Then, on one ordinary geological afternoon, the water rose and filled the room, and the columns stopped growing mid-gesture. They have been underwater ever since, and nothing has happened to them since, and that is the entire history of the place: one event, then none.

The water is seven degrees and has never been otherwise. It has spent years underground before arriving, filtered through a hillside of limestone, and it carries nothing — no silt, no motes, no plankton — so that visibility is limited only by the room's dimensions. You can see from end wall to end wall, thirty meters, and the only reason you cannot see farther is that the world stops there. The emptiness is visible, the way thickness is visible in glass. The cold is part of the stillness: it slows every chemistry, preserves every surface. The room refrigerates itself.

And it runs. This is the thing you understand slowly, hanging in the middle of it: the chamber is a machine that has settled into perfect steady state. At the far end, a horizontal throat in the wall — bell-mouthed, polished, flared like the intake of some patient engine — exhales a constant stream. Maybe a bathtub a minute, no more, and never less. Near the floor at the near end, low slots in the stone take the same volume away. Behind the hill an aquifer holds its head like a reservoir at a fixed gauge, and floods and droughts on the surface never reach it; storms are rumors here, filtered through a year of stone. Inflow equals outflow to the drop. The water level has not moved in longer than the forest has existed.

Near the throat, the walls carry a clear glaze of calcite, laid down molecule by molecule — the water plating its own intake in slow enamel, a coating line with an infinite shift. Somewhere across the room, far walls surrender the same molecules back to the current. The ledgers balance to the grain. Nothing accumulates. A works whose entire product is the fact of its running.

At the ceiling's highest dome, the water never quite finished its conquest: one pocket of air remains, a single lung, trapped the day the flood came and held ever since under pressure. It flexes by less than a finger's width as the weather changes far overhead. It is the only gauge in the place, and its needle barely moves. Sometimes, when the pressure shifts, the bell answers with a faint note, a wet finger drawn along the rim of a glass. Otherwise the room's one voice is the throat's tone — too low to hear, exactly. You find it in your sternum, the way you find a transformer through a wall, the way you hear a power station from two valleys off. A hum that is mostly a pressure, constant as the cold.

The floor is white silt, fine as moonmilk, banked in drifts that hold the shapes of what fell into them. Here a block of ceiling lies half-buried exactly where it landed; the dent it made is still legible, and the fall that made it predates the trees upstairs. Nothing has moved it. Nothing moves anything. The floor is a record that stopped mid-sentence and never needed the rest.

Where the shaft's light comes down at midday — a defined column of it, falling all the way to the bottom in the clear water, so that at noon the room is lit like any ordinary room and you could read by it at the floor — there the one meadow grows. Stonewort, six paces across: gray-green, jointed, stiff as brushes of green glass, standing sparse in the silt with bare white showing between the stems. A few stray tufts trail outward, then nothing. On the lit wall above it, a matte olive film, thin as breath on a mirror. That is all the green the works employs. And the meadow is doing something the room approves of: stonewort crusts itself with lime as it grows, quarrying its own bodies, turning to stone at the rate it grows — a garden in equilibrium with its own fossilization.

Nothing eats it. That is the other fact of the place, and it takes time to land, because water this clear should have something crossing it. Nothing crosses it. No fish. No larvae in the silt, no snails on the columns, no small things drifting. The meadow looks as it looked a century ago because nothing has ever browsed it. The absence is not an accident of the place; it is a condition the place maintains, like the temperature.

The quiet is the same — maintained, engineered. Your own breath, if you dive here, is obscene in its loudness. A tap of a stone on the floor fills the entire room; sound carries the way light does, all the way to the far slots. Now and then the glaze stresses somewhere and lets go with a tick. Long silences around the hum.

Days pass over the shaft the way weather passes over a gauge whose needle is pinned. Seasons are something that happen to the trees upstairs. The rules down here are few and absolute: the cold is fixed, the level is fixed, the flow is fixed; what enters is water and light; what leaves is water. The room changed once, long ago, and has been the same afternoon ever since — an engine that was never commissioned and will never be decommissioned, running the way a heart runs: not toward anything, but because running is what it is.

When you finally rise back up the fluted throat toward the surface, the hum comes with you for a while, carried in bone. Below, in the ordinary light that reaches all the way down, the Stillworks holds its one breath and goes on at its single speed — which is the speed of itself, forever.
