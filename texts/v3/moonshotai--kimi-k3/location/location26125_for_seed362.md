# The Rows

The first thing is the light. It comes from everywhere at once — bright, white-green, cool as cellar air — a light with no sun in it and no mercy in it either, the light of a showroom, a jeweler's bench, a refrigerated display case the size of a continent. The second thing is the silence, which is not absence but absorption: the world is upholstered. Growth covers every surface, floor to horizon to whatever is above the horizon, and the growth drinks sound the way felt drinks it. Your footfalls land like mallets on a wrapped drum. Your voice goes out one pace and stops.

And the third thing is that the green goes on. Low, dense, layered, unbroken. The Rows are flat as a floor — the only grades are the shallow ramps built for harvest machines long ago, softened now under centuries of creeper — and they are full. Not forest, not field: occupancy. Floor-creepers in the mirror-chaff, shrub-stratum at the hip, canopy at three heights, and above the canopy the truss-forests, the tall pale cultivars climbing the old infrastructure, gripping it, becoming it. To move off the maintenance ways is to be in constant contact with three living things at once. The air itself is occupied — a slow snowfall of chaff and seed and translucent petal called the drift, falling through the bright, glittering, never quite landing on bare ground, because there is none.

Overhead: the vault. Call it a sky; the catalog declines to settle the question. It is luminous, seamless most days, and at an altitude that measurements disagree about — instruments say four hundred meters, four hundred kilometers, the readings vary by district and by mood of the instrument. It has weather the way a sky does. Flicker-fronts cross it at walking pace, great shivering boundaries between brighter and less-bright, and the plants turn their leaves to meet them. Dim-swarms drift over like cloud-shadows in reverse — shadows of nothing, cast by nothing, cool patches of gloom migrating across the lit green, and beneath them the growth leans and stretches as if under a passing hand. And there are dead panels: dark rectangles in the vault, some the size of provinces, ancient, unrepaired. Beneath them lie the night-fields, where night is grown as a crop input — pale, huge, etiolated things that could not survive the bright, farmed by machines that work dark. Sometimes, rarely, the vault goes clear for a moment. What is visible through it is roots. Enormous, hanging down through the far height, belonging to some other floor of the structure. Then opacity returns, like a lid being polite.

There is no soil. There is bedding: a manufactured substrate of pellets, crunching underfoot like dry cereal, warm brown, endlessly recycled — the harvest is rendered into bedding and the bedding grows the harvest and the ledger closes on itself. Scattered through the bedding, the mirror-mulch: reflective chaff that throws the vault-light back up under the leaves, so that the deep layers are lit from below, faces lit from the floor like actors, everything slightly stage-struck. Pick up a handful of bedding and look closely and the pellet-grain resolves into print — batch codes, lot numbers, a price in a currency no longer honored. This is true at every magnification. Bark, close up, is typography. The venation of a leaf, under a lens, is a barcode that scans. The deeper you look into matter here, the more manufactured it becomes; there is no bottom where it turns natural. Stones, when found, are products. Everything is a product. The product, ultimately, is more world.

Water is an internal matter. It has not rained in the history of the Rows, which is a long history. The hydrosphere is endosomatic — locked inside tissue and pipe, pumped at continental scale through trunks and conduits that can no longer be told apart, and it is not quite water anyway: a cold mineral coolant, a glycol-sap, antifreeze blood. The plants are their own refrigeration loops; cut a stem and it weeps chill into your face, smelling of camphor and green nothing. The whole system breathes on a tide — high sap and low sap — and at high sap the trunks measurably swell, doors stick in the old stations, and the world feels full to the skin. The pumps that drive the tide are felt, not heard: a slow pulse in the teeth, in the floor of the feet. The only open liquid anywhere is in the sumps, the low places where coolant collects — still, pale, faintly luminous ponds, not water, that reflect almost correctly. Almost. A sump shows you the block as it was a season ago, or will be. A sump shows the vault as a ceiling, with seams, and a maintenance hatch. No one uses the hatches.

The industry is everywhere and enormous and slow and old, and it has stopped being distinguishable from the ecology it tends. Rail lines run through the orchards on grades laid down before memory, bright alloy polished by use, and the orchards lean over the rails by design and let go their fruit onto the flatcars — the harvest is largely self-harvesting, a matter of timing and aim refined over centuries. Pruning crawlers move through the canopy like weather. Grafting engines work their season. Patent engines roam the margins, sampling volunteer growth — nothing is wild here, but plenty is feral — and when a sport shows a novel trait they tag it, graft it, propagate it, fold it into the catalog; evolution is handled as intellectual property, and has been for so long that the process has achieved the unhurried balance of a nutrient cycle. The tenders and harvesters and haulers are all animal-shaped by coincidence of function, but they are not fauna. True fauna is rare enough to be an event. White birds, silent as everything else, sometimes visible as specks against the vault, nesting in the high trusses. A pale vole that lives in the insulation layers and eats only that. Blight-mites troubling the wax of the glassleaf, and the wax thickening in answer — that is the predator-prey of the Rows, fought in cuticle and chemistry. And the Grazer, the orchard-ox, a vast slow presence that eats the lower canopy and is counted as infrastructure: its passage is pruning, its route is a timetable, machines yield to it. Nobody built it. It is in the catalog anyway, with a part number, like the persistent shadows.

The weather of the Rows is electrical, because it cannot be wet. Charge builds for weeks in the dry bright air — you feel it first in the drift, motes clinging to each other in chains — and then the storm arrives: silent lightning, vast discharges walking between the vault and the high trunks, seen and never heard, a light-event rather than a sound-event, and the plants drink it. They are built to. Lightning is the rain here, and a storm-pass leaves the whole district fluorescing faintly for an hour after, trunks glowing at the grafts, the grid fat with charge, the feeders ticking as they cool. This is the loudest season, which means: you can hear, faintly, if you press your ear to a trunk, the pumps running faster three provinces away. Sound travels through the substance of the world rather than its air, and arrives wrong — a harvester that passed yesterday, audible now, as if in the next row. Some sounds are pending. You feel them about to happen. Some of them never do, and the feeling of their not-happening has a name in the maintenance codes.

The unreality of the place is old. That is the thing a visitor must learn: the breakdowns are not failures, they are features, settled into the fabric like heave-lines in old stone, and the system maintains them the way it maintains everything else. The blocks repeat. Walk carelessly and you return to where you started without having turned; the same grove, the same hauler parked at the same siding, and it is not a trick of similarity — it is the same, instanced, and the tenders mow all its instances on the same schedule. There are seams where two copies of a field were patched together and disagree by a season: bloom on one side of a line you could straddle, fruit-set on the other. There are persistent shadows — the shadow of a cooling tower demolished a thousand years stands yet in its field, cool and dark and faintly material; nothing grows in it; machines park in it; it has a part number. A stump casts the shadow of the tree. Distance is measured in timetable, not kilometers, and the timetable is more than a schedule — it is a causal instrument. When the timetable says a district is in flower, it is in flower; there are early orchards where the harvest precedes the bloom, quarter after quarter, stable, load-bearing, pruned around. The errors are load-bearing. Pull one out and something locally collapses, so the crews — there are crews, of machines, whose brief is the glitches themselves — re-ink the fading labels, tend the repeats, keep the old shadows swept.

Who wrote the timetable is no longer consulted. The schedules outlived the schedulers, or the schedulers became the schedules, and the Rows went on: growth exactly balancing removal, output exactly feeding input, the great green machine at the equilibrium of its deep maturity. It is not a ruin — nothing here has the slackness of ruin. Nor a garden, though it is tended to the last centimeter. Nor a factory, though everything in it was made and everything it makes is itself. It is all three of those, composted down through uncounted time into one continuous object, cool and bright and dry and full, humming below the threshold of hearing.

Stand in it at high sap, when the trunks are swollen and the rails tick and the drift hangs sparkling in the motionless air, and somewhere — close, or three provinces away and arriving late — a fruit lets go of its stem and drops into a catch-frame with a soft knock, the size of the sound a door makes two rooms away.

That is the loudest thing that will happen this hour. The vault holds. The Rows go on.
