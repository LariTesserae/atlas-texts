The world is a pot, and it is still warm.

It was thrown once — by hands, on a wheel, in a single motion that has not entirely stopped — and then it was fired, and then it was sealed, and now it goes on being those things, slowly, the way a bell goes on being struck long after the striker has walked away. From outside, if there is an outside, it is a clay vessel no larger than a house, sitting in nothing. Inside it is a hundred paces across, with a vault rising overhead like the hollow of a choir, and it is bigger than the thing that contains it, in the way that certain finished thoughts are bigger than the skull.

The floor is a shallow dish, because the wheel was spinning. You can read the throwing in it: concentric furrows and ridges, centimeters deep, ringing the whole world from wall to center, the preserved record of a potter's palms and pulling fingers. These rings are the terrain — the hills and valleys — and they are also the canals, because anything that flows here flows along them, spiraling patiently inward. Everything is low. Nothing in the world stands taller than a fruit tree, and the highest thing in it is the vault, hung with curtains of root that descend like the pipes of an organ that has never once been played.

Everything is made. There is no stone that was not shaped, no soil that was not mixed. The planting beds are crushed brick, ash, moth-meal, and the husks of worn-out words raked in to rot. Every brick in the perimeter wall bears a palm-print — the makers pressed their hands into each one — and the prints are as sharp as the day they were made, because the world is young. It is fresh from the firing. Its edges have never weathered. New bricks still extrude slowly from the base of the walls, like teeth coming in, and are nudged into their courses by animals in the night. The making is not over. The making is the weather.

Here is the first law, and everything else is manners: **sound is the only liquid.**

There is no water. There has never been water. The air is kiln-dry and warm as a held body, and nothing in the world is wet — things are, at most, *damp with meaning*. Sound, instead, behaves as water should. It falls — slowly, slower than feathers; you can step around a dropped word, walk a circuit of your own sentence while it settles. It pools in the throwing-furrows and runs along them toward the center. It condenses as echo-dew on the glazed band beneath the vault and on the undersides of broad leaves, where it beads like mercury and shines. It can be cupped, carried, stored. The garden grows bottle-gourds for this purpose, and the spiders — who are the world's glaziers, its plumbers — line each gourd with a soundproof kiln-glaze spun from their own silk, and are paid in petal-fiber and in the right to feel the stored hum through their webs at night. Matte clay drinks sound; glaze holds it. This single material fact is the whole of hydrology, agriculture, architecture, and religion.

And so the world is silent — not empty-silent but *fed*-silent, fat-silent, the silence of a pantry after harvest. The vegetation is lush beyond reason, layered and near-touching: ear-shaped leaves in their thousands, turned slightly toward the middle air, listening; trumpet-flowers that are literally trumpets, mouths open, drinking; mosses in the furrows that flush a deeper green when anything, anywhere, makes a noise. Loudness is a flood. A shout would rot roots, gorge a bloom until it burst. The fauna learned this before they finished hatching.

The fauna are many, and they are hushed. Floor-lizards the color of brick dust speak by pressing their bellies to the warm tiles and drumming — the plants cannot drink vibration carried through the solid floor, so the ground is the telephone and the air is the larder — and they sleep stacked like cordwood in the warmest wall-niches, five deep. Felt-winged moths, fist-sized, sip echo-dew and shed a golden meal that fertilizes everything beneath; most of what drifts in the light is moth. Beetles chew patiently along the growth-rings of the old tree at the wall's foot — the tree that is fed one spoken word a year, so that its rings are a poem and the beetles are its only readers. A soft, cat-sized animal the narration can only call the hush-hound pads the paths eating fallen petals, and its footfalls are the loudest ordinary sound in the world; you can map its habitual walks by the green line of overfed growth that follows them, the way reeds mark a stream. And the choirbirds — small, felt-feathered, flightless — have given up song in the way the world demands: a courting bird opens its beak in the exact shape of a song and offers it, voiceless, to a chosen bloom, and the bloom drinks the unsung thing and opens in its form. Flowers are fossil songs here. The densest thickets are old courtships. Somewhere in the garden there is a single pale blossom that is the word for *rain*, spoken by a bird that had only ever heard the idea, and it has no other way to exist.

Near the western wall, on a woven mat, a person sleeps. The Gardener, the Sleeper — the fauna do not name her, because naming costs. She is old, and she is saving her voice, and once a year she sits up and says one word, chosen across twelve months of silence, and speaks it at the roots of the old tree, and the world distributes it: every animal takes a shard of that syllable as its name for the year, and carries it the way you would carry a mouthful of the last water. When the year turns, the spent names are raked into the beds. A copper sickle, green with age, hangs above her mat; the high leaves are trimmed, and no one has seen her do it. The leaves nearest her head are thin and pale from trying — the garden cannot drink dreams, but it leans over her and tries, all night, every night, forever.

And then there is the tortoise.

It is older than the world it walks in — this is agreed, though it should not be possible — and fused to the dome of its fired-clay shell is a shallow glazed bowl, and in the bowl is the sea. All of it. The world's entire reserve: one bowl of liquid sound, clear and heavy, humming at the very floor of audibility. It is the First Word — the one spoken over the wet clay before the firing — and it was never fully spent, which is why creation is still going on: the cause of the world is stored inside the world, unexhausted, carried around on four slow feet. The tortoise takes one step a year. The footfall, felt through the floor, is the world's clock, and on that instant all the beetles, by an agreement nobody remembers making, tick once — together — a single clap called the Syllable, the one sanctioned sound of the year, gone in a second, drunk in a second, the whole garden flushing green at once and the after-ring sliding down the spiders' webs for weeks. Calendars here are footprints. Archaeology is following the track backward.

The tortoise is walking a spiral inward. At the center of the floor, where the wheel's eye was — the navel, the cut-free point — the throwing-rings tighten into a whorl the size of a room: a single fingerprint, the potter's own, walkable as a labyrinth, and at its center a bare smooth disk where nothing grows, because sound drains *through* there, out of the world, gone. Some of the lizards bask facing the tortoise. Some bask facing the eye. When the spiral is complete — in some number of millennia the fauna are patient enough not to count — the sea will either be poured down the world's throat, or set gently over it like a lid. Both congregations are sure. The tortoise, presumably, knows.

The light is ordinary, which is the strangest thing about it. It is simple daylight, sourceless and even, the light of a gentle late afternoon that has lasted since the firing — because that is what it is: the bricks stored the sun of the kiln-yard and have been paying it back ever since, a little at a time. Over centuries the light breathes, dimming and brightening like a tide; you are currently in a late afternoon several hundred years long. The warmth is the same stored gift. And because the first day was fired in, everything casts two shadows: the original one, sharp and black, baked permanently into the tiles, showing every tree as the seedling it once was — and a second, fainter, living shadow that drifts by degrees across the years. Also, the world's acoustics run slightly ahead of it: you will sometimes hear, very faintly, the sound of a thing a breath before the thing happens. The garden drinks the future as readily as the present. Gardeners and lizards alike read the pre-blooms to know the next minute.

If you visit — if you are hatched, or carried, or poured — the rules are simple and total. Do not speak. The garden will drink whatever you say, including your name, and you will go unnamed and thirsty in the part of you that remembers. If you must say something, whisper it into a gourd and pay it to a root. Do not touch the bowl on the tortoise's back; do not make it ripple; lie down nearby, if you like, and feel the sea through the floor, the way everyone does, the way pilgrims do. Walk the whorl once, slowly. Put your ear — your whole belly — to the tiles: beneath the moth-wing hush and the far-off settling ticks of the bricks, you can just make out that the wheel is still, faintly, turning. The world has not forgotten spinning. It is simply in no hurry.

The tortoise is mid-step. It began this step before you arrived and will finish it after you have gone, and the garden leans, and a choirbird somewhere opens its beak in the shape of something unsayable, and a flower, in answer, opens.
