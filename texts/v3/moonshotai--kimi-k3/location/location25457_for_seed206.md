The first sign is underfoot. You are crossing the root-heath — low, rolling ground that is not ground at all but the back of a single plant, a leather of braided root-bark stretching to the horizon, warm as skin — when you feel a rhythm come up through your soles. Not heard. Felt. A slow, deep *choom*, regular as a heartbeat, and under it a faint continuous hum, the way you feel a highway before you see it. If you follow the vibration to where it sharpens, you find a fissure between two root-ridges, narrow enough to step over without a thought. It exhales. Warm, saturated air rises out of it in a steady draft, smelling green and faintly sweet, like the inside of a stem. The opening is barely wider than your shoulders. This is the only door, and it does not look like one.

You descend by root-handholds into air that thickens degree by degree, and then the walls fall away.

The hollow is perhaps thirty paces across — intimate, a single room — sunk at the water table, at the lowest crease of the heath's body. Its ceiling is a stretched translucent leaf-membrane, and ordinary daylight comes through it green-gold and soft, the light of standing under a great leaf at noon. Below that light: open space. That is the first surprise. For a place inside a plant, it is mostly emptiness. A floor of still, glass-clear water over an amber living mat. Air above it, resonant and warm. And in all that volume, only three presences, standing far apart, with wide silent water between them — as though the emptiness were load-bearing, as though the room needed its own stillness to push against.

Every surface is alive. There is no stone visible anywhere, though stone lies beneath; the walls are woven root, the floor a warm spongy felt of fine rootlets that yields underfoot and slowly recovers, the ceiling that single vast epidermis. Nothing here was made. And nothing here is merely growing.

The first presence stands near the center: the piston. A column of jointless living wood the width of a tree trunk, rising and falling inside a flared sheath of bark. Its stroke is slow — four seconds up, four seconds down — and when it seats at the bottom there is a deep pneumatic *WHUMP-choom* you feel in your sternum more than hear, and a ring of ripples crosses the whole floor and comes back. The sheath around it is polished like oiled teak, burnished by strokes without number. With each rise it lifts water somewhere up a root-column and out of the room; with each fall it draws more from below. It is a pump. It grew this way.

The second presence, against the far wall, is the lung: a ribbed bladder tall as the room, translucent as goldbeater's skin, inflating and subsiding once a minute. Its intake is a spiracle frilled like an ear, and the air rushing through it sounds a low organ note that never stops — the fundamental drone of the hollow, the hum you felt through the ground. Condensation beads on its flanks in mercury-round drops, and the drops roll off in time with the piston's stroke.

The third presence is the smallest and, you come to understand, the master. Half-submerged in the floor water turns a wheel of woody spokes, chest-high, revolving at a slow rate that never varies. Its axle is a knob of dense heartwood seated in a socket of slick living cartilage, filmed with sap — a bearing that grew, replacing its own wearing surfaces molecule by molecule, perpetual. As each spoke passes a wooden pawl there is a soft *click*, and water flung from the rim spatter-falls in a small bright pattern. The wheel drives nothing. It regulates everything. The piston times its stroke to the wheel's click; the lung breathes in its multiple; the drips from the ceiling — and the ceiling weeps steadily, warm condensate — fall on the same meter, so the entire room ticks. A governor. A clock that no one wound, keeping a time that no one set, and never gaining, never losing.

Between the three machines, the water is clear enough to read the floor by, and nearly empty — that deliberate sparseness again. A few pale fish drift the open middle, slow, cleaning the mat's root-hairs. Three or four columns of midges hang shimmering and stationary above the surface, each column keeping its exact shape though the individuals in it rise and fall away and are replaced. On hollow reed-perches spaced meters apart sit fist-sized frogs, each using its stalk as an amplifier, and they call — a loud two-note bark — always in the piston's rest-interval, always off the beat by the same precise fraction. Under the mat's edge, in the drainage channels, small eel-things discharge in faint blue ticks beneath the water, in synchrony, the system's spark-timing. In one corner the water precipitates its waste minerals as a crust of pale porcelain patina, and flat snails graze that crust at exactly the rate it grows, so it is always there and never larger.

And the bird. A tall grey stalk-bird, heron-like, patrols the mat along a path that is faintly worn and never wears deeper. Forty steps, a pause, a strike into the water at the moment a fish crosses its beat, forty steps again. Its footfalls land on the wheel's click. It is a hand on the clock, moving around the dial of the room.

The loudness of the place is total and organic — the lung's drone, the piston's chest-deep thump, the wheel's woody clicking, the hundred entrained drips, the frogs barking in the gaps, a high dynamo-whine from some insect you never locate — all of it layered into one continuous sound as loud as a factory floor and as steady. No swells. No fades. You could notate the entire performance on a single bar of music. After an hour your body stops reporting it, and you hear it as a kind of silence, and you understand that true silence here would be the only frightening thing: it would mean the hollow had stopped.

The room enforces its own rhythm. A frog that calls off the beat calls into dead acoustic air — the room does not return its voice — and it falls silent, and re-enters on the meter. Deviation is simply not amplified. The architecture is the feedback loop.

Nothing accumulates. That is the deepest law, and you feel it the longer you stand there. The water level holds constant to the millimeter despite the piston's endless export; the mat releases exactly what is pumped away. A leaf that falls from the ceiling-membrane is reabsorbed before it can yellow — decay here is not slow rot but instant recycling, growth balanced against uptake so precisely that the census of the room never changes. The frog on that perch is not the frog of a century ago, but a frog is on that perch, calling that note. The bird is not the same bird; the bird stands where the bird stands. The piston's polish neither deepens nor dulls. Erosion finished its work long ago — every surface worn to the exact profile of its function — and now the living tissue holds itself at that finished shape, forever arriving, never departing. You could leave and return in fifty years and catch the room at the same point of the same bar.

It is, by every measure except one, an industrial site: intake, throughput, regulation, waste stream, power take-off, tolerances held across centuries. The one missing measure is that it was never built. There is no seam, no fastener, no cut edge anywhere in it. No hand did this. It grew — or rather, the heath and its creatures grew together until growing and engineering became the same act, and the distinction between organ and machine dissolved in the warm wet dark. What the hollow produces is clarified water and scrubbed cool air, routed up into the vast body above: it is a kidney, a lung-station of the root-heath. But its deeper product is itself, continued — a factory in perfect steady state whose entire output is its own persistence.

Even the light conspires in the stillness. When day fails outside, the membrane and the patina edges take up a faint green-gold glow, just enough that the machines are never unseen and the rhythm never pauses for dark. There are no seasons in the room. There is only the beat.

You climb out at whatever hour you climb out. On the heath above, the drone drops out of your ears within a few steps, but it stays in your soles, and as you walk you catch, faintly, a second tremor under the first — another hollow, kilometers off, breathing in locked antiphase with yours. The whole upland keeping common time beneath you. It was beating before you found the fissure. It is beating now, unchanged, the same single bar it has always played.
