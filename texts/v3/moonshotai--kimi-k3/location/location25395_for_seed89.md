High on the mountain — past the treeline, past the last juniper clinging to its crack, past even the snowline where the air thins enough to starve a fire — there is a fissure in the western buttress no wider than a door gone wrong. The peak up here is a frozen desert: scree, rime-feathers on the stone, a wind that has nothing left to stop it. Nothing lives in this altitude. Nothing should. And yet the crack exhales.

Stand before it and the first thing you notice is warmth. A steady breath of air pours out of the stone, body-warm, wet, rank with ammonia, and where it meets the mountain cold it freezes into a beard of hoarfrost that fringes the lip in ferns and needles. In the brief noon of summer the beard drips, and in that drip-line — only there, only exactly where the cave waters it — grows the single seam of green for a thousand vertical meters: blackish moss, one tuft of grass gone grey with trying. The vegetable world ends at that line. Everything beyond it, inward, belongs to a different order.

The second thing you notice is the sound. Put your ear to the crack and you hear surf through a wall — a roar damped by rock, alive somehow, rising and falling too slowly to be weather. The herders in the far valley call the peak the Snoring Mountain. They work stone into blades and bone into needles and they have never once climbed to the source, because the source does not admit hands. The passage corkscrews down at an angle only wings can manage, narrow as a flue, and the warmth climbs degree by degree as you descend, and the grey light from the mouth thins, and thins, and then is simply gone. Not darkness as absence — darkness as medium. Thick, warm, wet, full.

The passage opens into a single chamber, the whole of this world: forty paces long, half that across, the ceiling doming up out of any reach of light into blackness. Chapel-sized. Room-sized. Bounded absolutely. The walls are old volcanic stone, scalloped and polished by water that ran here when the mountain was younger and angrier; everything sharp was worn away long before anything with eyes could have seen it. Touch the wall and you receive the chamber's first strangeness: the rock is the temperature of skin. Partly this is the mountain's old fire, still banking its coals far below. Partly it is the floor.

Because the chamber is crowded. That is the governing fact, the thing everything else bends around. The ceiling wears a fur that breathes — tens of thousands of bats hung wing-to-wing in mats so dense the stone beneath them has not been dry in a hundred centuries, and the mats ripple, slowly, like sleep. The upper walls are studded with night-birds, dark-plumed, claw-footed, clinging in rows like roof-bosses in a cathedral, and they navigate the black by clicking — each bird ticking out its own little stitches of sound and reading what comes back, thousands of them at once, so the air is full of a dry, continuous rattling, like rain on a roof of bone. Between ceiling and floor the moths drift in their slow thousands, pale as ash, and beneath everything the guano floor lies meters deep and warm as compost, because it *is* compost: it digests itself, gently, perpetually, and the heat of that digestion rises through the room. The floor is not a floor. It is ten thousand years of falling, still warm from its own digestion, and it moves — crickets long-antennaed and white as paper, beetles, mites, springtails, the slow patrol of a pseudoscorpion the size of a thumbnail. The floor stirs the way a sleeping animal stirs.

The water here is the cave's own making. Warm wet air rises against the cooler dome, condenses, and falls — a slow interior rain, a drip every few seconds from someplace unseen, each landing loud as a drumbeat in the stone room. Where the drips persist they are building, molecule by molecule, tongues of flowstone and soft white pastes of moonmilk. In the low corner the seep gathers into a pool the color of skim milk, warm as bathwater, and in it live white amphipods and one blind fish the length of a finger, colorless, eyeless, untroubled. Erosion works in this place, but on pigment: the dark has worn the color out of everything, the way water wears the softness out of stone. It took a very long time. Time is the one thing the chamber has never lacked.

And the sound. The sound is the room's weather, loud the way waterfalls are loud — a loudness you could lean on. Layered: the bat-mats' high shimmer, felt in the teeth more than heard; the birds' dry clicking; wing-rush passing overhead in gusts; the drip percussion; and under all of it, when the wind crosses the crack outside, a low tone the whole chamber holds, swelling and fading across minutes like a slow siren, like breath. The rules of this world are few and old. Nothing green crosses the drip-line; light is the border of the vegetable. Nothing is built here — nothing has ever been made, arranged, stacked, tied. Everything accumulates. The only artisan in the mountain is water, and its only tool is patience, and its work rate is a fingernail of stone per century. Color is worthless; echo, heat, and smell are the currencies; everything alive in this room is known to everything else by sound.

The chamber breathes on several clocks, all of them slow. Each dusk it exhales its birds and its bats down the long dark flue and out into the thin night — they ride the downslope wind to feed at the treeline far below, and the cave stands emptied and quieter, almost cool — and each dawn it inhales them home. When storms approach and the pressure drops, the whole mountain breathes out through the crack for days; when the pressure lifts, it draws the cold thin air down into itself, slowly, a single inhalation lasting half a week. Season by season the ice beard at the lip grows and retreats, grows and retreats. And across centuries — nothing. The census does not change. The bats neither multiply nor dwindle; the crickets hold their ground; the blind fish's grandchildren will be indistinguishable from its grandparents. The floor rises an inch a decade. The flowstone thickens by less. This is an old agreement, settled long ago, and everything in the room keeps it.

At the threshold, the outside world reaches in with one clawed hand: an owl hunts the crack at first light, taking the birds as they come home heavy-cropped from the valley. That is the only visitor. No hand has ever touched the warm walls. The stone-workers below, with their flaked blades and their old stories, give the peak a wide berth, and on still nights, when the wind comes down off the buttress just right, you can stand in the valley and hear it — the faintest thread of roar woven into the wind, and see, if the moon is up, a thin steam rising from the western face that the moonlight catches like breath in cold air.

A small warm room in a cold high stone, dark as the inside of a body, crowded as a heart, humming to itself at the tempo of geology. It has been roaring softly in the dark for longer than the mountain has had a name. It will not hurry.
