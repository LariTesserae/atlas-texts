The sea has a ceiling.

Eleven kilometers above the Root-Floor, the water ends against the underside of the Roof — a vault of grown tissue hundreds of meters thick, closed over the ocean like a lid of living wood. There is no surface here, and has not been for longer than any lineage keeps memory. There is no floor of stone either. Every solid thing in this world — the Roof, the Floor, the pillars between — is the Stock: one organism, or one clonal being descended from a single germ in a time before timekeeping, which long ago enclosed this sea entirely. It roofed the water. It paved it. It raised pillars through it. There is no stone, no sediment, no metal, no grain of anything that was not grown.

Everything here is made, and nothing here was built. No tool has ever existed in this water. Yet the pillars are fluted and tapered to their loads like engineered columns; the arches spring with a mason's logic; the great vessels run through the bodies of the pillars like plumbed pipework; every niche fits its tenant like a crafted socket. This is growth refined across eons until growth came to resemble engineering — tropism and selection standing in for intention. Whether the Stock intends any of it is a question with no mouth to ask it.

The pillars stand twenty, thirty, forty kilometers apart — a sparse colonnade holding up the sky of the world. Each is a fluted trunk half a kilometer through, smooth-sided, rising from the Buttress Fields of the lower strata to branch and fuse into the Roof. Between them lie the Void Reaches: open water, clear as glass, empty as nothing. Nothing drifts there. The water holds at a degree above freezing, everywhere, always, and it is so still and so clean that a swimmer in the middle reaches hangs in perfect black absence, hours from the nearest wall.

The vertical order is fixed and total. At the top, the underside of the Roof is an inverted country: hanging buttresses worn smooth, glazed over with tenant life, lit by the oldest lanterns — the high stars, which have burned at the same cold brightness since before the herds' memory begins. Below, the strata stack by pressure, and each kind of life is born into its stratum and stays there; the water is eleven kilometers deep and almost nothing crosses it. At the bottom, the pillars splay and fuse into the Buttress Fields, and the Fields settle into the Root-Floor — a grown pavement, hollow, drum-skinned, that hums under any body resting on it. Beneath the Floor run the Pipes, the greatest vessels of the Stock. At the Seeps, the sea slides into the Stock's body, faintly warmed, and is lifted through the cores of the pillars to exhale again at the Roof — one circuit, constant, unvarying, the world's only weather. One vessel outruns all others: the Throat, a vertical pipe a kilometer across, open-mouthed at floor and roof, through which the circulation pours like a slow river stood on end.

That circulation is what the world sounds like. The Stock is a hydrodynamic organ the size of a sea, and it plays itself. The Chord — that is the only name for it — has a fundamental with a period of long seconds, felt in the chest and the teeth more than heard, and above it the overtones stack like a choir holding one breath. It is loud. Near the Root-Floor and the Throat it is a pressure, a hand laid on the body, a weather of its own. In the Void Reaches it is clearest — unshadowed, pure, arriving from everywhere at once. The pitch of the Chord is set by the dimensions of the world, and the dimensions of the world do not change, and so the pitch has never changed. Timbre is geography: a creature of this sea knows where it is by the shading of the Chord, the way the Roof thickens the overtones, the way the Throat's nearness swells the bass. Distance is measured in resonance.

Darkness is the medium here; sight is the least of the senses. The light that exists is fixed and biological and eternal. At the junctions of the architecture burn the lanterns — nodes of cold blue-green light, unblinking, unflickering, each holding the same brightness it has held for longer than any living thing can reckon. They are sparse: one lantern for many square kilometers of wall. They have names. They are the fixed stars of the Interior, and navigation is done by their arrangement. Fainter than the lanterns, the vein-light: dim seams tracing the pillars and vaults where the Stock's circulation runs near the skin, the world drawn in lines of barely-glowing current, enough to render the nearest architecture as pale geometry against the black. And there is one kind of light that moves.

The herds carry it. The great grazers are the only travelers: a few hundred of them across the whole of the region, each half a kilometer long, each marked along its flanks with dim rows of living light — the only constellations that drift. They are slow beyond slowness; a grazer moves perhaps a body-length in a day. They are old beyond age; individuals outlast the tenure of the lanterns they pass. Their work is the world's erosion. They mow the walls, and every surface in the Interior owes its smoothness — that polished, final, settled smoothness, soft as old ivory — to their mouths. There is no sharp edge anywhere in this world because the herds have passed over everything, again and again, for eons, and the Stock regrows at exactly the rate they graze. The balance is perfect. A grazer may tend the same wall for ten thousand years and leave it looking untouched.

The rest of the fauna live on the skin of the world, and there the abundance is total. Every niche in the architecture is occupied — fans and bells and sieves and palps, fields of cilia, forests of filtering mouths — fitted so exactly because the Stock grew each niche around the tenant's ancestors, lineage grown to lineage until the socket and the dweller share their plumbing. The walls teem at precisely full occupancy. They always have. Between the tenants runs a thin constant traffic of small commensal lives, and out in the antinodes of the Chord, huge pale drifters hang at station in the standing wave, barely pulsing, straining the thin current. But a niche opens only when a tenant dies, and tenants die at the rate larvae ripen, and so the larvae's one drift through the open water almost always ends where it began. The water itself stays empty as glass. All the crowding of this world is on its surfaces; all its emptiness is in its volume.

The cold keeps everything slow. A degree above freezing, and the only warmth anywhere is the faint constant warmth of the great vessels and the Seeps — the Stock's blood runs one degree above the sea — the world's single gradient, and it does not drift. Metabolisms crawl. Lives stretch: the tenants measure theirs in centuries, the grazers in ages. Nothing hurries, because nothing needs to.

Because here are the rules, and they are few. Nothing here is stone. Nothing here is new. Growth equals grazing. Birth equals death. The lanterns do not dim. The Chord does not change. There are no seasons — there is no sky. There is no day — the darkness is constant. There is no weather but the one current running its one circuit. Even the events repeat: the herds make their loops from Roof to Floor and back, and each loop is the last loop, and the loops mark no time, because a clock that reads the same hour forever is not a clock. The world is finished. It is not dead — it is more alive than a storm of forest, every centimeter of its skin crowded with metabolizing life — but it is a body at perfect rest, every flux balanced against every other flux, a metabolism holding steady state. It has held this note longer than the memory of any mouth.

Almost nothing happens, and what happens is enormous. A grazer calls: it opens its mouth and holds a single tone for a day, two days, tuned to the Chord, harmonic with the world itself — and that call is the event of a century, heard across provinces, answered perhaps once in a lifetime, and it is always the same call at the same pitch. A larva lands: a local epoch. A lantern going out has never been recorded.

So: drift, once, in the Void Reaches, and take the world as it is given. Your skin reads the cold. Your bones read the Chord, which arrives from everywhere and has always arrived. Kilometers off, one lantern burns — a fixed star, named, unchanging. The vein-light draws the curve of a pillar whose near face is an hour's swim away, and beyond it, blackness, and beyond the blackness, more blackness, all of it roofed, all of it grown, all of it holding. Somewhere far off, one of the herds begins the word that takes two days to say — the same word it has always said, at the pitch the world's dimensions allow.

The sea holds its breath. The breath does not end.
