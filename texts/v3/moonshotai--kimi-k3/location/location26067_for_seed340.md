Three days out across the upland, before there is anything to see, there is a pressure in the teeth. The upland itself is nothing — a table of raw young stone, scraped and unweathered, a world not yet ruined by weather, because there is no weather. Clouds cross it sometimes, gray and promising, and give nothing, and pass on. Nothing up here has ever been wet. Then the pressure in the teeth becomes a hum in the breastbone, and the hum becomes a chord you can lean against, and you come to the rim of the Throat.

It is a basin a full day's walk across, sunk deep into the planet like a struck bell, and it is full — full to overflowing — of life. Not scattered life. Substrate life. The walls are terraced in giant steps, and every terrace is a grove, and every grove is an instrument, and the floor far below is a slow-motion storm of gold and bronze and cream. Nothing down there is green. Green is not the living color here; the living color is bronze, in a hundred shades from pale hammered straw to near-black. The air over the basin shimmers with heat and with a fine suspended flour of charged dust, so that the whole pit seems to breathe, to swell and settle, and the light — ordinary daylight, an ordinary sun crossing overhead — comes down through that shimmer and arrives changed, granulated, loud.

Because the first rule of the Throat is that sound is the medium. Here, sound does what water does elsewhere.

**The Felt and the Flour**

There is no soil. There is the felt — a woven mattress of living root, springy underfoot, warm, thrumming faintly at all times, and it *listens*. Every footfall goes into it and along it. The ground reports you. Nothing in the Throat walks unannounced except one thing, and you will learn about that thing.

Beneath the felt there is no groundwater and never has been. What the taproots reach, far down, is a stratum of ancient warm resin — the compressed fat of some earlier age of the world — and the plants pump that up, thinned with mineral oils, through hollow vessels that run on pressure and vibration. What circulates in the leaves and stalks is not sap but a fine conductive flour, fluidized by the ambient noise itself, flowing like liquid only because the world will not stop shaking it. Cut a stalk and it does not bleed; it sighs out a puff of gold dust and a thread of oil, and the cut squeals as it seals. Decay, when it happens, is dry — mummification, not rot. The basin smells of amber, hot stone, crushed pepper, ozone. Nothing smells of damp. The word for damp, in the tongue of the people who live here, is a curse-word and a word for the end of the world.

**The Chord, and Its Hours**

The sound of the Throat has strata, like the walls.

At the bottom of it, felt more than heard, is the bass: the slow drumbeat hearts of the great grazers — six-legged, slab-sided, the size of houses, walking metronomes — and deeper still the subsonic breathing of the vents, where the pit is still digging itself downward. Over that, the vast held middle chord of the reed-terraces, acres of hollow stalks played by the rising thermals; as the basin warms toward noon the whole chord climbs, the world tuning itself upward a third, and the heat makes the drum-pods on the middle terraces pop open one by one in a rolling, crackling applause that can last an hour. Over that, the shriek-band: flocks of membrane-winged kites riding the thermals in spirals, keening, thousands at a time. And under everything, so constant you forget it until it stops, the granular static of the stridulators — beetle-swarms in their millions, rubbing the dry music out of their own legs, the floor-noise of existence.

It is genuinely, injuriously loud. Travelers' ears bleed on the second day. The people of the Throat are born with thick eardrums and grow gently deaf across their lives, and they consider this a maturation, a gift: they call it *growing quiet*, and the elders, the deeply deaf, are the only ones who can sleep outside the quiet-houses, smiling, in the full blast of noon.

The noise is not steady. It accumulates. Heat, charge, population, pressure — the basin stores up sound the way a storm stores lightning, and when it can hold no more, it spends it all at once. This is the Swell. For an hour, sometimes two, everything synchronizes: reeds, pods, grazers, kites, the stridulating static, all of it locks into one immense beating chord, and under that chord the dust-flats of the floor go liquid — dunes ripple and flow, the Meal they call it, a dry lake that truly moves — and in the Swell the world remakes itself. Spire-fields that grew ten feet in a day collapse with gunshot cracks. Seed-chaff goes up in gold columns. Whole groves tear loose from the felt and *raft*, surfing the liquefied Meal on their root-mats, migrating miles, crashing into one another, interbreeding where they land. Forests here are not places. Forests are events. Geography is a performance, and it is different every time it is played.

Then the Great Quieting: an hour of exhausted near-silence, the only natural quiet the Throat ever has. The people retreat behind their chime-fences then, and wait, and do not speak.

**The Floor**

Down in the heat-haze, the floor of the Throat is a churn of half-finished things. The geology is young and shows it: where wall-slabs have recently calved, the exposed stone is pale and rings like an anvil when struck — new rock rings; old rock is muffled under a century of felt. Spire-forests of fast bronze stalks erupt, mature, and collapse on a cycle of days. The Meal lies in slow dunes that only truly flow during the Swell, though it is treacherous always, and a creature that founders in it drowns dry — the only kind of drowning there is.

The wells are the gathering places. Black resin seeps up in warm, slow pools between the root-mats, and at dusk the fauna queue for them — grazers shouldering in first, then the drum-swarms, fist-sized beetles whose bellies beat in rolling rhythms as they feed, then clouds of moths with charged wings that carry pollen-dust from grove to grove and crackle with static as they fly. Everything drinks loudly. Drinking is slurping, sputtering, drumming on the well's skin. This is not bad manners. It is survival. Because of the one thing in the Throat that makes no sound at all.

**What Hunts in Silence**

They call it the Hush, and it is never described directly, because it is never perceived directly. It is perceived as a hole — a moving gap in the static, a place where the world forgets to sound. Its fur drinks tone the way the moths' wings do, only perfectly. It crosses the felt without a single footfall reported. In a world where everything announces itself constantly, where the ground itself repeats your step, silence is not absence. Silence is the predator's cloak. Anything quiet in the Throat is either hunting or dead.

So the rule of travel is inverted from every other world: make noise, always. Sing badly, stamp, rattle your gear. If the stridulators near you go still, run toward sound — any sound, the louder the better. The people string their paths and their few settlements with chime-fences, rows of bone and dry-reed bells packed obsessively close, and the alarm is not a chime that rings. It is a chime that stops.

**Marks of the Listeners**

The people are few, scattered, and their works are small. A tuning post every league or so — a stalk-pillar capped with a nugget of cast native copper, pried from veins the young erosion has laid open — which hums with the health of its grove; a keeper reads it by pressing a cheek to the shaft. Here a rope-bridge of living vine, grown across a ravine rather than built. There an ear-shrine: a great shell of woven stalk, a hearing-trumpet aimed down a vent-mouth so the faithful can listen to the world being born, the faraway gnawing tick of root-drills cracking the deep stone — the basin deepens by a handspan a year, and they know this not by measuring but by ear. Children are lowered in baskets to the vent-lips to listen, and come up wide-eyed.

Their finest structures are the quiet-houses: sunken, thick-walled, lined inside with felt compressed from the wings of the mute-moth, the only silence in the world. Inside one, for the first time in your life in the Throat, you can hear your own blood. Children hate it. It sounds, they say, like the inside of a held breath. Their other technology is the drum: on the rim stands a single tower with a stretched hide head, and from it the keepers talk to the next rim-station and the next, in bass, in rhythms pitched deliberately below the chord of the basin — they speak *under* the world, where nothing else is talking.

They have one story they tell more than all others. Once, it says, the sky went heavy and gray and the silence *fell* — a soft, cold, gray silence that came down out of the air and lay on everything, and whatever it touched, stopped. Stalks went mute. The felt went deaf. The chord died terrace by terrace. The world ran into the Throat, the story says, and the Throat closed over it and warmed it back to life, and that is why the pit is warm, and that is why it sings. They call the falling silence the Dampening, and when clouds cross overhead they watch them the way other peoples watch for omens, and they mean the thing you would call rain.

**What It Is Becoming**

Stand at the rim at dusk, when the chord drops into its minor key and the night-shift of fauna takes over, when the moth-swarms begin to spark faint blue along the floor like wandering embers and the stars come out around the ellipse of the rim like rivet-heads in a drumhead. The basin below is not finished. It is not close to finished. The walls calve, the floor churns, the groves walk, the roots gnaw downward a handspan a year, and every Swell rearranges the furniture of a geography that refuses, on principle, to hold still.

The people say the Throat is the world's first word — that the planet sat silent and dry for an age, and then opened here, and sounded, and has been sounding ever since, and is still only learning what it means to say.

You do not have to believe them. But you will stand there a long time, with the hum in your breastbone and the bronze haze going dark below, and it will not feel like listening to a place.

It will feel like being heard by one.
