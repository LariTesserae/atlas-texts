You come up onto the Shelf from the south scarp — four days of switchbacks through frost-shattered stone, the air thinning and drying until your lips split and your breath stops smoking, because the cold here doesn't trouble itself with fog. It takes your moisture without showing it. Then the rim. Then the first sight.

White, to every horizon. Salt pans, dust pans, basins bright as crockery, the light falling white from a hard high sun and bouncing back off the ground until it seems to come from everywhere at once. Cool light, though — bright without warmth, a glare you can shiver in. And laid across the whiteness, dark lines: cords, some thin as wire at this distance, some thick as roads, running straight for a day or wandering in long lazy curves, joining at nodes that shimmer at the edge of sight. And under all of it, felt in the soles of your feet before it reaches your ears: a beat. Slow. About four seconds apart. Then a second beat, off-tempo, arriving from farther away. The ground here keeps time.

The ground has had time to learn. The Shelf is old past drama. Its mountains were ground down to whaleback ridges so long ago that the gravel of them has itself been polished into ventifacts, faceted and re-faceted by a wind that never gets bored. The basins are filled with their own dissolved mountains, precipitated out as salt — polygon crusts, pressure ridges, all of it settled at rest angles, finished. Erosion has done everything here it intends to do. No stone on the Shelf remembers being wet, and no stone expects to be.

Because there is no water on the Shelf. There is only water *in* the Shelf, and all of it is spoken for.

The cords are the span. One plant — or three, or one body going under three names; nothing has found its edge, and if it has a center, the center is a rumor. It covers the plateau the way a net covers: everywhere, and mostly holes. Its cords are cable-laid bundles of vessels in amber bark, humming faintly, and what runs in them is not sap as you'd know it. It is oil — a thin terpene liquor, resin-blood, the working fluid of everything. The span runs on hydrocarbon. Water it treats as ore: at certain nodes the mirror-leaves stand in vast white arrays, each leaf a small heliostat tracking the glare, and their focused sun falls on heaps of gypsum and hydrated salts in bake-ovens of woven fiber, cooking the structural water out of the stone as steam that never once sees open air — straight into condenser throats, sealed, rationed, spent and recaptured forever. Not a molecule has touched the ground in a million years. The desert is not where water is absent. It is where water is property.

Follow a cord for two days through the ringing empty middle — your own footsteps the loudest thing in the world, the cord humming under your palm like a struck fence-wire — and you come to a node, and the node is industry.

The drums first: vaults of gourd-hard wood over deep chambers, a diaphragm of fiber across the throat, beating by muscle-strand, fed on oil. Pump stations. You feel one in the sternum at ten kilometers; up close your vision jellies with each stroke and your teeth ache in their sockets. The grists next — slow stone mills where the span eats the mountains it sits on, boulders fed into a gizzard of millstones turned by wind-sails the size of ship canvas, a boulder to a meal in a decade, the rock-flour piped in oil-slurry to the render-pools: vats of solvent where stone is digested down to its metals and salts. The span plates its own cords with copper drawn from its grindings. The hum you feel in the wire is not only pumped oil. The cords carry charge. The whole plateau is a living grid.

And on the high ribs of ground, the spark-trees: graphite-hearted spires, bare, black, standing in groves. The dry wind and the dust-storms charge the whole pan like a rubbed plate, and the trees bleed it off — standing arcs, coronae, scheduled lightning in a sky without a cloud. In the arc's heat the thin air's nitrogen burns into nitrates, which fall as a fine ash on waiting mouth-leaves. Here the rain is ash. The thunderstorms bring no water, and the plants are glad of it.

None of this was built. All of it grew. Nothing here thinks — but everything *runs*, on schedules with no scheduler, and you can set your days by it.

The sound deserves its own account, because the Shelf is loud the way other worlds are wide. Near the organs it is not sound so much as weather happening inside your body — the grist's grind arriving through your skeleton, the drum-strokes pressing on your chest. Between nodes, hollow reed-forests stand at every junction, tuned bores of different lengths, and when a storm crosses, the plateau plays: chords the size of weather fronts, organ-bass below hearing, a shriek like torn metal carrying a hundred kilometers through the cold dense basin air. Dry thunder rolls for minutes at a time because the salt pan itself rings like a drumhead. Most of the animals are deaf by their second year and feel the world through their feet. And you navigate by it: every drum keeps its own tempo, held for centuries, so the Shelf is mapped in polyrhythm. You steer by beat the way sailors once steered by stars.

The Shelf makes its own weather and keeps its own frenzied calendar. Dust-and-spore storms come off the pans in static-charged walls. At dusk the whole network of breather-domes inhales the cooling air; at noon it sighs it out — a continental breath riding the forty-degree swing between night and day, and that breathing, through valves, is half the pumping. Nothing on the living layer is permanent. A grist eats a ridge in a century, then is reabsorbed; new stalks rise at a hand-span a day, fast enough that you can sit with your lunch and watch a tendril quest; spent organs slump into their own render-pools and are drunk back down. The Shelf is never finished and never ruined — it is mid-process everywhere, an anthill working at plant speed, visible if you hold still. The only catastrophe is fire: rare, blue-fronted, creeping through the oil-rich tissue after lightning strikes wrong, and the span answers with slag-gates of mineral tissue that slam shut growth by growth, walling the burn off, composting the scar.

The animals are sparse and strange and many-kind. Tap-moths with kite wingspans drill the cords for leaks, and the span tolerates them for the pollen they ferry. Ticker-birds nest inside the drum-vaults and hold their conversations in percussion — a dawn chorus like a foundry starting its shift. Oil-beetles glean the rims of the render-pools. Rime-lickers work the dawn, harvesting the hoarfrost that feathers the stone near the breather-domes — the only ice in the world, the span's own exhaled moisture, and the span reclaims whatever they miss through dawn-wicks before the sun gets high. Grist-hares dance the mills' vibration up through their feet. Something called, by no one, the pan-runner hunts across the crust by seismic sense, and you will likely never see it, which is the usual arrangement.

And there are the longwalkers. Perhaps nine of them alive. One per few hundred kilometers — the sparsest thing in a sparse world. You feel the footfalls three days before the animal comes over the horizon, a pressure in the ankles, a slowness in the beat. It drinks a node for a week. Its back carries dust, spores, a colony of ticker-birds, half the ecology of the last node it left — courier between the organs of a body too large to pollinate itself. Whether the longwalkers serve the span or the span gardens the longwalkers is a question the Shelf does not ask.

Night locks the cold down hard. The glare goes to black and the stars come out like rime. The drums continue — they always continue — and the spark-trees stand crowned in cold blue fire, bleeding the day's charge, and on the horizon silent lightning flickers from storms three days' walk away. You sleep by the beat, or you don't sleep.

At dusk, if the air lies right, the fata morgana builds over the far nodes: towers, scaffolds, whole cold cities of bent light hanging above the pans. They are the only architecture the Shelf permits. Nothing made persists here — the span digests metal, the salt eats the rest, and anything dropped is composted within a season — so the eye, starving for something built, invents it nightly over the horizon and takes it back by dark.

If you must drink, cut a cord. The oil is bitter with salt and it will keep you alive. But drink and move on, because the span answers a wound within the hour — tendrils questing toward the injury, sealing it, building there. It does not distinguish between damage and instruction. Come back in a year and your theft will have become a new organ, working.

Then out onto the pan again, into the ringing white middle, the nearest drum four seconds underfoot and the next one two days ahead, slightly off the beat, and the two rhythms swinging against each other across the emptiness the way the whole place swings — stone on its eons, flesh on its days — a world that was never built and never born, only switched on, long ago, and running ever since.
