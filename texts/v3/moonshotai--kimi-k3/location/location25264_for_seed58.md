The ledger at the shaft-head calls it Condensery Shaft Seven. The chalk inside the door calls it the Still.

You come down through four hundred iron stairs of warming dark, and the place announces itself first as breath: a long, slow intake somewhere below, as if the earth were asleep and dreaming, and the cage of the stair rattles less than it should, because the young rock down there drinks noise. Then the gasketed door, the pressure-sigh as it opens, and the hall.

It is a single vaulted chamber — you can see the far wall; the whole town fits inside one breath of stone. Cathedral scale, two hundred paces end to end, sixty to the crown, and every cubic meter of it occupied. The light is rationed: amber bulbs in wire cages strung at long intervals along the main gallery, the orange seams where furnace doors fail to quite meet their frames, and at the far end, over the condenser fins, one blue-white arc lamp humming faintly — the only cool-colored thing in the world. Between these points the dark is not empty but layered: catwalk over pipe-rack over cable tray, terraces and galleries bolted into the walls like swallows' nests, flues doubling as ladders, walls doubling as plumbing, every surface doing two jobs and wearing a gauge for at least one of them.

The rock is young. Fresh-fractured basalt, glassy, sharp enough to cut a careless palm; the tool-marks on it are still bright. Whether the survey charges opened this void or merely found it, the surveyors still disagree in the canteen's chalk margins — but nothing here has weathered, nothing has slumped or softened, and when you strike the wall it rings, taut from its own making. And yet the place is already accreting. In the wet heat, geology runs fast: dripstone buds over every gutter, pipes fur with scale and must be reamed, and on a ledge above Cistern Row a wrench left behind a season ago is now welded to the stone, half-sheathed in calcite. The tenders use it to teach. The town must be scraped to remain a town.

Heat rises off the floor at the hall's center, where the Breath is. A field of vents in the raw rock, shimmering, too hot to linger over — you do not lean on anything down here without checking it first; the handrails are warm as wrists. Every few hours the floor exhales a long tide of warm, wet air, and then draws back, and the canvas ducting along the vault swells and slackens in answer, a second breath answering the first. Doors are gasketed against the pressure shift. Loose papers migrate with the cycle and are gathered, each time, to different corners. The clocks in the Tally House are set to the Breath, not to the sun. The sun is a rumor from the shaft-head.

Water, here, is never fallen from sky. It is negotiated out of air. When the warm exhale climbs to meet the night-cold that spills down the shaft throat — the Cold Collar — it beads on the fins and the chilled stone, gathers, runs, and for an hour the vault sweats: a soft interior rain, the gutters singing at their different pitches, cistern gauges rising, the tally chalk updating in a hand you have not seen. This is the weather. This is also the harvest. Cistern Row holds it in black riveted tanks, water warm as a held hand, tasting of chalk and iron; and once a cycle the main gives a deep, slow gulp and sends a pulse up the shaft — the town swallowing upward, paying its water-debt to the dry world above. Elsewhere there is dryness: grey dust in the dead corners, desiccated rope. And one pocket of stillness — the Sump, a black warm mirror where the floor dips, where bootprints in the silt lead in and out.

At dawn, on the coldest nights, rime feathers grow on the Collar — the only ice for a thousand meters in any direction. It is scraped into a single bucket. The canteen's one cold cup of the day. It is spoken of the way other towns speak of festivals.

The town itself is organized with the confidence of something built all at once, from a plan: the Trunk running the length of the hall at mid-height; districts by function — the Stack and its furnaces, the Condensery under the arc lamp, Cistern Row, the Tally House with its brass counters and pneumatic message tubes that tick like a resting insect — except there are no insects. A narrow-gauge cart track on the floor. The great flywheel of the pump engine turning unhurried, unhurried, as if it had been promised eternity in writing. Dynamos humming low. Everything cast, riveted, gasketed, gauged. Power is spent on pumps and fans before it is spent on lamps, which is why the light stays at amber points in warm dark. The bunkroom — the Twelve — has twelve beds, all made, some slept in. The flour tin in the canteen is sealed against weevils that do not exist.

What grows here grows without much light and without any appetite after it. Amber-grey felts and long hanging strands in the drip-lines, sipping chemistry instead of sun; a chlorotic halo of moss around the base of the brightest lamp, faintly green, faintly luminous; pale fungal fans shelving from damp timber, perfect and uneaten. And the garden: a window-box trough under a dedicated bulb, a tight bright row of cress, thinned recently, by someone. It is the greenest thing within a thousand meters of rock. You could hold it in both arms.

There is no animal life. Say it as an inventory, because the place keeps inventory: no rats along the Trunk, no mice in the flour, no bats under the vault, no moths at the cages of the bulbs, no silverfish in the ledger, nothing that scurries, flutters, gnaws, or sings. The rope rots only by damp. The quiet is not the hush of things hiding. It is the hush of nothing there to hide. And the young rock drinks sound besides — a shout dies within twenty paces, while small sounds persist and become the whole soundscape: the tick of drips, pitched by the depth of whatever they strike; a valve's soft clunk; a counter advancing one number, like a held breath let go; the flywheel; the distant fan at the Collar; and, if you stand at the Hot End long enough, your own heart, which the hall returns to you unembellished.

Who keeps it is a question the tally chalk declines to answer. The signs are everywhere and the hands are nowhere: fresh figures on the board, the garden newly thinned, a coat on a peg, bootprints in sump-silt ending at a sealed drift door, the rime bucket emptied and set back to dry. The bunks sleep twelve. Whether twelve sleep here, or three, or a rotation that is always elsewhere in the drifts when you are in the hall — the Still maintains itself around the rumor of its crew the way it maintains everything: quietly, on schedule, warm to the touch.

These are the rules the place teaches anyone who stays a cycle. Nothing here is cold except on purpose, at night, at the Collar. Water is owed upward. Stone is owed scraping. Sound stays where you make it. Time belongs to the Breath, not the sun. And nothing is ever lost — it is only adopted, welded gently into the young rock that is still deciding, alongside the bright rivets and the unblistered paint, what all of this is going to become: a town, a machine, a lung, a reservoir.

For now it is all four at once, doing everything a living town does except live — condensing its weather one breath at a time, drip by counted drip, in the warm dim, in the hush of nothing hiding, with the chalk already changing somewhere behind you.
