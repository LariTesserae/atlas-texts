Somewhere in the low country — a moor of young hills, their glacial scratches still fresh as cat-claws, the turf not yet finished knitting itself over the stone — there is a fissure no wider than a finger, hidden under a lip of peat. Rain finds it. That is the only event the outside world ever contributes: rain, finding the crack, shouldering in, and beginning the long fall down.

Inside, the fissure opens into a chamber it has no right to contain. The hill above it is modest; the room is the size of a small bell, a heart, a place four paces across if paces meant anything here — and it exceeds the rock that holds it by a matter of inches, an impossibility so slight the world has not yet noticed. The excess grows. By perhaps the width of a thought, each century.

The walls are newly split stone, conchoidal, glassy, sharp enough to cut a hand that isn't there. Nothing has weathered them. Silt lies on the floor a single grain deep, black and soft as ash, because the crack is young — the chamber opened recently, though recently, in a place this slow, may mean the rain that first entered is still on its way down. Water fills the room completely, stone to stone, with no surface and no sky. It holds itself at the temperature where water is heaviest, the cold of deep cellars, of the undersides of stones, and it sinks with that weight into the earth's inattention.

There is no open water. The thicket allows none. From every wall, from the floor, from the ceiling — which here is only another wall — the vegetation crowds in pale profusion: a felt of colorless filaments furred across the fresh stone; ribbon-fronds translucent as old ice; chains of bladders like strings of unlit pearls, each one sealing itself shut with a faint click when it has drunk its fill. Pigment is pointless in the dark, so the plants have abandoned it, and the whole crowded forest is the color of moonlight on milk — not that moonlight has ever entered. They do not eat light. They eat the thin warmth that seeps up through the young crack from the deeper earth, the gradient itself, the difference between the warm below and the cold above. And — this is the first thing a visitor would get wrong — they eat time. They metabolize duration. The thicket is dense because it drinks the moments passing through the water, and so the fuller the chamber grows, the slower the world inside it runs. Time is thickest at the center of the room, where the fronds are oldest. There, things do not decay so much as persist.

For most of its life the vesicle is perfectly dark. Darkness here is not an absence but a medium, a black thinner than water layered through the water, with a grain to it, a texture a hand could learn. The place is known by touch and taste: iron, cold, a green bitterness. But the thicket keeps one appointment. At the tips of the tallest fronds hang the lanterns — small hooded organs, dark for years at a stretch, and then, on a schedule no one keeps, they light. All of them. Together. A dim blue, weak as breath on glass, lasting perhaps an hour. The room calls this nothing; there is no one to call it anything, but it functions as a kind of noon, a slow noon that comes once in a long while and is the only clock the chamber has. During the lighting the shadows behave oddly — they lean toward the light rather than away, as if curious, as if starved for it. The next lighting is due soon. Soon, here, means within a few decades. A visitor who arrived on the right day would get one blue hour and then years of black to think about it in.

Mostly it is quiet. The young stone ticks now and then as it settles into being stone. A bladder seals. Rain, at the fissure far above, knocks once and is admitted. But the water is so dense, so crowded, that sound does not travel through it cleanly — it gets caught. The felt and the fronds hold noises the way a coat holds burrs, and release them late, out of order, apologetically. A tick the wall made thirty years ago may surface beside your ear tomorrow. The hush is not empty. It is crowded with pending sound, a library of the room's few noises, all waiting their turn.

Almost nothing lives here, and what does lives carefully. There is the scribe: a ribbon of a worm the length of a hand, translucent, unhurried, tracing grooves through the silt of the floor in lines that bend and close and bend again. It has been drawing for as long as the room has existed. There is the gardener: a many-limbed, glassy thing like a pale isopod, which spends its seasons stacking small stones — there is a cairn of six of them near the center, mortared with patience alone, and stacking is easier than it should be, because falling here is slow and negotiable, and a pebble may take a week to decide to rest, and some few choose, instead, the ceiling, where they hover in the felt like a inverse floor. And there are the undecided — a drifting handful of motes, neither spore nor larva, which have been meaning to resolve into one kingdom or the other for centuries and are in no rush. Perhaps there is something else: a coiled pallor half-buried in the silt, thick as an arm, unmoving. It may be a root. The gardener avoids it, or tends it. It is hard to say which, and the room is in no hurry to clarify.

The grooves the scribe draws are a map. This becomes clear only after long looking: the lines describe a shoreline — bays, a point, a shallow reach — the shore of a lake that does not exist. The notches the gardener has worried into the wall at patient intervals are depth marks, a gauge for water levels the chamber has not yet reached. The cairn of six stones stands where an island will be. Everything built here — and nothing here is built with tools, only with instinct and uncountable years — is prospective. The map comes first. The territory is expected.

The laws of the place are local and few, and they bend. The inside exceeds the outside and the excess grows. Falling is a negotiation conducted over seasons. Causes occasionally follow their effects: a frond leans, and then the current arrives to lean it; ripples spread out from a touch that hasn't landed yet. Sound arrives late. Time is thickest in the middle, because the thicket is drinking it, and as the felt thickens the room will slow further, and no one will mind. Nothing here has anywhere to be.

It is a young place. Everything in it is rehearsal. The rain that enters at the fissure falls slowly down through the crowded dark, threading the pearl-chains, tasting of sky, and will reach the floor next year. The scribe's lines lengthen by a hair a season. The lanterns hoard their blue. And in some far, unhurried age the hill will slump, the fissure will widen, the wall will give way to weather, and the vesicle will spill open into a small tarn under the actual sky. The shoreline it finds itself with will be the one already drawn in the silt. The island will stand where the cairn stood. The one held breath of air — the single bubble that rests in a cup of stone near the ceiling, that does not rise, that has waited all this time like a germinal spot in an egg — will lift at last and join the general air, and be ordinary, and be free.

This is how lakes begin. Not as floods, not as gifts of rain, but as dark rooms full of patience, packed to the stone with pale life, rehearsing a shore in the black, counting down the slow centuries to their one blue hour — and then to light.
