# The Bright Well

The plains give no warning. For days they are the same: a cool, pale flatness under a wide white sky, the light falling hard and heatless, the wind too thin to carry anything. No green anywhere — no tree, no grassblade, not even the gray patience of sage. Just mineral ground, salt-pale, and the occasional far flicker that you might mistake for heat-shimmer if there were any heat.

Then the ground rises in a swell so gentle you could miss it, and you are standing at the rim of the Well.

It is a bowl four or five kilometers across, three hundred meters deep, and it is *brighter down there than up here*. The walls are a bone-pale tufa, soft and rounded, and they catch the sky's hard light and hand it downward, wall to wall, bounce by bounce, until the floor of the basin glows like the inside of a lamp. The shadows down there are thin and directionless and slightly blue. Light comes up at you from the paving. Nothing is hidden from it.

And the bowl is full. A city fills it — but a city worn so long against the stone that you cannot tell, from the rim, where construction ends and geology begins. Streets run as canyons between fused walls. Vaulted roofs carry skins of flowstone. Whole districts bulge gently, like rising bread, where tufa has crept over and through abandoned rooms. Everything is rounded. Every stair has been worn toward a ramp, every threshold grooved deep as a finger's width by feet, every handhold polished by ten thousand years of hands. The pebbles in the lanes are the rounded bones of older buildings. The only sharp edges anywhere in the Well are the edges of the tools it gathers with: the dew-fins, the scraping blades, the rims of mirrors.

From up here, at the white hours, the city flickers. Thousands of mirrors on the rim-slopes catch the light and tilt and pass it down, and the basin chatters with crossing flashes — the whole place speaking in heliograph to no one, all day, every day, and has been, longer than anyone's counting.

You should know before you go down: it is silent.

Not quiet — *silent*, in a way that takes a day to understand. The tufa drinks sound. A clap is born and dies in the same spot; there is no echo anywhere in the Well, no ring, no tail on any noise. Voices carry a few meters and are gone. For the first hours you will think you are going deaf. You are not. You are hearing, for the first time, how much of sound was always echo, and what the world is like without it.

So the Well has become a city of the eye. The people — there are perhaps a few thousand, thinly spread through a place built for many more — speak with their hands, in a sign-speech as fluid and fast as water, and keep a whispered register for the cave-dark where hands cannot be seen. They wear slit visors of cleaved selenite against the glare, and to see someone's naked eyes is an intimacy, reserved. The streets carry flags where other cities carry bells. A thin column of dung-smoke rising over the bowl is a sentence of weight — fuel is precious here, fire is rare, and smoke is spent only on what matters: a birth, a death, a debt of light settled.

Because there is no wood. There has never been wood. Understand that and the Well explains itself: no beams, no planks, no charcoal, no carts. The architecture is corbelled stone and tufa block, vault on vault, lanes roofed over into tunnel-streets in the old quarters. The textiles are felt — matted from the gray-gold film that felts itself over any damp stone — and herd-leather, and the pale silk of the spinner-grubs. Tools are stone and bone and a little cold-hammered copper. A single wooden object in this city is an heirloom beyond price; a house with one wooden lintel is rich.

And there is no water. That is, there is water everywhere, and none of it flows.

No stream has run in the Well in longer than the basin remembers. What the city drinks, it harvests. Beneath the bowl, the old cave-system — the Root — breathes: slow exhalations of cool, wet air that pour up through the throat-caves at the turn of morning and evening, when the day's temperature shifts. At the breath-turns, the people crowd the throats with fins of polished slate and spread felt, and the air's moisture beads and runs and is wrung and caught, and the cisterns under every house take their thin tithe. Roofs are water-farms, sloped and finned and guttered. Sometimes a rain comes over the rim, and the whole city tilts its face up — and mostly it falls as virga, gray veils that never arrive, and that is normal, that is expected, and the felt is wrung anyway for what condensed on it while everyone watched. Sometimes a mist pours over the rim at dawn and the entire city weeps at once, every wall sheathed, and then it is all hands, all movement, a festival of wringing and carrying conducted in total hush — the loudest thing in it the soft tick of ten thousand drips, swallowed as they fall.

Water belongs to the roof it condenses on. Light belongs to whom it is granted.

This is the law that runs the Well. On the rim-slopes stand the Mirror Fields: thousands of mirrors, mica and polished stone and selenite, mounted on pivots, driven by sand-clock weights, tended by a constant traffic of climbers. Through the day the mirrors pass beams down into the bowl — to this court for an hour, then that one — and a court granted the beam blooms instantly into labor: the beam-ovens lit for the communal baking, salt-pans angled, fiber laid out to cure, every task that needs brightness or the little warmth the light carries done in its hour and no other. Beam-time is traded in the Beam Court, a bazaar conducted in a rustle of sleeves and flashing hands, small but furious, an anthill node in the long empty arcades. The tenders flip the sand-clocks in waves, and the light-geometry of the city shifts hour by hour, and nothing in the Well is ever lit the same way twice.

Nothing in the Well is ever finished, either. This is custom, or faith, or something older than both. Every house keeps its capstone indoors, displayed, withheld — a wall somewhere always missing its crown, scaffolding always standing somewhere, a vault always half-risen or half-come-down. When someone dies, their household capstone is mortared into place at last and the house is *closed*: sealed, left to the tufa. Whole districts of the city are Closed Rows, streets of sealed houses slowly bulging with internal flowstone, rooms filling with cave, the city growing its own fossils. The oldest layers are so far gone in this that they are simply geology with doorways.

Stand on the rim long enough and you understand what the churn is for. The felt films reclaim every damp wall. The tufa creeps. The mirrors drift. Leave the Well alone for a century and it would close like a fist, return to cave, rejoin the great stillness of the basin — which finished changing long ago, which has settled into its final shape and will wear it now until the world ends. The city is the one unfinished thing in a finished world, and it stays that way on purpose, by ceaseless motion. The scraping, the rebuilding, the wringing, the flipping of clocks, the trading of beams — none of it accumulates. It cancels itself. The Well has churned like this for ten thousand years and looks, on the scale of the basin, exactly the same. The turbulence is how it stays still.

The animals know the rhythm and use it.

There are no plants, so everything eats the felt, or eats what eats the felt. The herds are pale, long-lipped, soft-padded animals the size of cattle, and they make no sound at all — in a world where sound dies anyway and predators hunt by sight, silence was free, and they took it. They move through the Fallow Runs — whole districts left open as grazing rotation, streets of felt-farms and weeping walls — in slow tides, and when a tide comes through the inhabited quarters the street-gates are swung and the lanes become drives and a river of pale backs flows past your knees at dawn, sounding like nothing but slow breathing.

Overhead, the mirror-moths ride the thermals of the light-well, broad wings flashing, and several times a day the swarmers rise — small drinkers that cluster on weeping stone — in sudden silent blooms, sheets of wings gusting through the streets like weather that decided to be alive. The wing-blooms are the Well's storms. The elders read them the way other people read clouds. On the high fins, the stalker waits: tall, still, patient past reason, holding the shape of a dew-fin for days until something with wings believes it, and then one movement, once, and stillness again.

In the cisterns live the keepers — blind white crustaceans the size of a thumb. A cistern without them fouls; a seeded cistern stays sweet for generations. Every household tends its water-keepers like a garden, and a bride carries a jar of them to her new roof, and this is what wealth looks like here: clean water, a full cistern, a beam-debt in your favor, a pane of selenite.

About the panes. In the Root, down the throat-caves where the air turns cold and the last of the light gives out, the city grows its windows. Selenite crystallizes on the wet cave walls, and the people seed it and tend it, and a good pane takes eighty years to reach the thickness that can be cleaved and set. Families tend panes across lifetimes. A window in this city is willed, like land. There are panes in the Well older than the houses they are set in, and through them the borrowed mirror-light comes softened and gold-gray into the cool rooms, and this is considered the proper way to see indoors: by light that your great-grandparents planted.

Go deeper, past the pane-gardens, and the churn dies out of the world. The seep-pools lie black and still under low stone, cold enough to ache, and nothing moves, and the silence down there is the true silence, the basin's own, the stillness the city is holding off with all its flashing and scraping and trading. It is not hostile. It is patient. It has the rest of time.

The Well is not alone in the region. A half-day across the plains lie the Sisters — smaller dolines, smaller wells. Two are lived in, thinly. One is not. The youngest Sister died generations ago and closed, and its people left, and the felt and the tufa took the streets. But one mirror on its rim, mounted loose on a wind-vane, never jammed, and it still swings in the thin wind and still throws its flash across the empty plains, irregular, untended, a lighthouse signaling from a town with no one in it — and on still nights the people of the Well can see it winking, and some of them sign back, out of courtesy, though there is no one there to read it.

At dusk in the Well the breath turns, and the throats exhale, and the felt banners stream at the cave-mouths in the only wind the city has. The mirrors are folded or set to their night-rest; the last beams climb the walls and go out; the stone begins its slow ticking as it cools, swallowed note by note. The herds bed down in the Fallow Runs and the people bed down among them for the warmth, wrapped in felt, visors off at last in the dark. A moth-bloom crosses the bowl above the rooftops, soundless, like a single held breath let go.

From the plains, if you are traveling at night, you will not find it. There is no glow — the light is all borrowed and the loan is called in at dark. You would walk past within a kilometer and never know. But if the wind moves one loose mirror on the rim at the right moment, and a last thread of the white sky's light goes down into the bowl, you may see, once, far off, a flash from the ground itself — the bright Well, the unfinished city, speaking to no one in the only language it has ever needed.
