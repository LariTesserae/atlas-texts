The light comes from below.

There is a sky, technically — a low ceiling of steam, pressed down over the world like a lid — but it is lit from beneath, its underbelly glowing dull amber from the heat of the ground, so that all shadows fall upward and the clouds are brightest where they hang lowest. Somewhere above the steam there may be a sun. Occasionally a pale coin shows through, irrelevant as a coin under ice. The real illumination is the world's own slow burn: a red seam along the horizon where a kiln province is firing, a warm exhalation from every seam between every brick.

The heat is total and wet. The air is thick enough to lean a hand on. It tastes of clay and lime and faintly of iron, and it does not move so much as it stands, sweating. Everything sweats. The bricks sweat. You sweat. Condensation beads on every vertical face and runs down in patient rivulets, and the whole world ticks — softly, everywhere, the small ceramic pinging of things cooling or warming or deciding — beneath a silence that is otherwise nearly complete.

The floor is the world. It is brick, hand-laid, and it runs past every horizon the dimness permits you to reach. The land is low to the point of rumor: relief is measured in courses. A single step up is a hill. A dais of three courses is high country, and from it you can see — a little further into the amber murk — the next punctuation in the emptiness. Because the world is mostly empty. That is its nature and, apparently, its design: vast plazas of bare floor, a league of nothing, and then one chimney standing alone, smoking gently, attached to no structure that has ever existed. A stair of a thousand steps rising to a platform and stopping, awaiting its tower. An arch, perfect and solitary, framing steam. A wall forty leagues long and knee-high — a foundation line, the beginning of something whose scale does not bear thinking about.

Water stands over everything in warm sheets an inch deep, blood-warm, mirror-still, doubling the underlit ceiling so that the world is two amber glows pressed together with you walking on the seam between them.

Every brick is signed. Turn one over — they are warm, always — and there it is: a thumbprint, pressed into the clay before firing. The same thumbprint. On every brick. Billions of bricks, one thumb.

The work never stops and is never seen happening. A wall you passed yesterday has gained two courses overnight. If you are patient and the steam thins, you can watch a brick arrive: first a sheen on the air, a film of wet clay condensing out of the saturated heat, thickening, settling onto the course with a soft, final *clack*. Then a pause. Then the next sheen. At night the hod lines move — single files of loose bricks crossing the open floor at an inch an hour, patient as glaciers, bound for sites where walls are rising. No one carries them. They are simply logistics, the way water is simply rain. Scaffolds stand here and there across the plain, towers of stacked ceramic tube and fired pier, leaning against empty sky, braced for structures not yet decided upon. Tools are found lying in the wet — a trowel on a half-laid course, still warm; a plumb line; a level of glazed clay — and are never where they were left.

Scored into the floor, faint as chalk under the standing water, are the other lines: the ghost plans. Whole cities exist as floorplan only, corridor and courtyard and hall drawn out in shallow grooves, and you can walk their streets ankle-deep in warm water for days. Where stakes of dark ceramic carry strung lines of spun slag-fiber, marking a room not yet built, the air inside the rectangle is already different — a degree cooler, oddly still, with the faint echo of an enclosed space. The room is already true. The walls are a formality.

Sometimes you find the plans themselves: tablets of fired clay, thumbprinted, incised with elevations and courses. The plans are upstream of the world. If a wall is scratched out of a tablet — this has been tried — then somewhere a standing wall receives notice. It does not crumble. Nothing here crumbles; crumbling requires a past, and nothing has one. Instead the wall is *recalled*: the hod lines come, and over nights the bricks leave, one by one, courteously, top course first, until the floor is clean and the score-lines have already faded. Demolition is a form of politeness here.

And there are the ruins that were built as ruins — pre-ruins, laid fresh and sharp-edged, already broken, with rubble mortared permanently into picturesque collapse. The plan called for a ruin, so a ruin was made, new as this morning. Age does not accumulate in this place. Only intention does.

The geometry votes. Count the courses of the great stair going up and count them coming down: the numbers differ, and both are correct. Plumb lines hang a few degrees off the true and the floor accepts their judgment; the bubble in the level sits at the end of its glass, and that is what level means here, locally. There is a walled yard in the middle reaches — forty paces around from outside, two hundred across within. Distances renegotiate between visits. The lone chimney that stood a league off last firing is now beyond the steam, or closer, or behind you.

The weather is masonry. Steam squalls roll through, white and blinding and hot as breath. Sometimes it rains bricks — small ones, falling slow and gentle as snow, stacking themselves in rough courses where they land, so that after a brick-fall the plain has grown a low wall that appears in no plan anyone has found. And there are the firings: the far horizon goes white, and then the whiteness walks toward you at a strolling pace, a front of vitrification crawling across the floor. Behind it lies a glaze desert — the brick fused to rippled black glass, singing as it cools, a chorus of ticking and pinging and sharp pops, flaking into scales that the hod lines will eventually, patiently, harvest.

The water has its own cycle, and the cycle is mortar. Drip-lines feed the lime pans, brick-lined pits where quicklime slakes and the water boils itself milky and caustic — the world's kitchens, where it mixes its own blood. Slurry channels creep away from the pans, gray-green, slow as thought, all of them draining down the world's gentle tilt to the Mortar Sea: a sea of unset mortar, heavy, tidal under some unseen pull, never hardening because something in its depths keeps it stirred. Foundations the size of islands drift half-submerged in it, waiting to be sent for. From the sea the steam rises; the ceiling feeds on it; the ceiling lays the world.

Nothing lives here, but chemistry has ambitions. Where the drip-lines feed a wall long enough, the efflorescence gardens come: feathers of white salt, rosettes of gypsum, lime crusts spreading in slow spirals — the only flora, blooming mineral and blind. Sometimes a hod line carries a rosette away whole, and it is found later, mortared into a new wall like a relic. As for fauna: there is motion, but it belongs to materials. Drip-lines that run uphill when the geometry votes change. A plumb bob in the lee of a pre-ruin that has been swinging for a year though no air moves there. Nothing watches you. If you feel watched, it is by the thumbprints.

Time is not kept in days. It is kept in courses, and in firings. The world is vast enough to have its own weathers and patient enough to have no history — everything crisp-edged, nothing worn, an eternity of first mornings. You are walking across a workbench the size of a planet, and the work is going well. The empty leagues are not unfinished; they are plazas. The silence is not absence; it is the silence of a workshop between tasks, the trowel still warm.

Everything here is going exactly according to plan. No one has found the plan.

Lift a brick. They don't mind; there are always more. Press your thumb into the print.

It fits.
