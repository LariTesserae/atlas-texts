From the lowland haze the range appears without warning — a wall of black glass and pale ash thrust up through the heat shimmer, too young for rivers. The maps call it the Ascendant Range. Everyone who lives there calls it the Young Range, and the city that climbs it they call the Calder, or simply the Works.

The altitude should be cold. It is not. Whatever cold belongs to this height was evicted long ago by the heat rising through the crust, and the air up here is thin, hot, and still, carrying the smell of warm stone and ozone. The light is ordinary — an ordinary sun, an ordinary day — and that ordinariness is part of the strangeness: this place goes about its impossible business under plain white noon.

Nothing on the mountain is rounded. The rock is all fracture and facet, fresh basalt and welded tuff breaking along clean planes, cliffs with the sharpness of broken pottery. There is no soil, no gravel worth the name, no pebble that water has ever softened. Roundness must be manufactured here; the guilds cast their shot and bearings, and a tumbled stone is a christening gift, the most expensive toy in the world. The built city matches its mountain: brick of pressed ash, cast stone, slag ceramic, drawn glass, iron and copper gone verdigris and rust-bloom in the damp heat. No wood anywhere — nothing has ever grown to provide it — so the scaffolds are steel, the ropes are wire, the doors are ceramic slab. Everything is dense and near-touching: tier upon tier up the flanks, staircases threading between foundry walls, flues and cable runs laced overhead until the sky comes in narrow hot ribbons.

The mountain is under construction. That is the first rule of the place, and the one visitors grasp last. In the lowest tiers, down in the Sumps where the heat stands like a hand on your chest, the Lift Engines work: piston houses the size of cathedrals, stone jacks seated against the roots of the range. Their stroke is one per hour. You do not hear them. You stand in a doorway and the doorway leans, slowly, against your shoulder. Terraces are always half-finished; scaffolds put up for a decade become permanent, then load-bearing, then indistinguishable from the architecture, which is itself indistinguishable from the geology. Surveyors publish future maps — the city as it will stand in a hundred years, with addresses at elevations that do not yet exist. You may be born at one altitude and die at another without ever moving house.

Whether the Engines raise the mountain or merely steer a rising that would happen anyway is the city's oldest argument. The Drivers and the Riders agree on nothing except that the work continues.

Water falls upward first. That is the second rule. The young crust breathes steam through a thousand vents, and the city drinks its own breath. Along the eastern ridge stand the Fin Fields — acres of black ceramic vanes, packed as a crop, radiating the day's heat back at the night sky until, in the small hours, they are the only cool surfaces in the world. The steam finds them and becomes water. Dawn is the harvest: for one hour the gutters sing, the only hour the Calder is ever loud, and even that is a quiet song. Condensate carters make their rounds with sealed wagons. Below, in the Drip Vaults, cistern chambers the size of ballrooms collect the city's seepage, each drip with its own note, the darkness full of slow mineral music. Sometimes, when the plume from Stack Row climbs into the thin cold above, the city makes its own weather — a small warm rain that falls nowhere else in the world, on nothing but roofs and fins. No water leaves the Calder if the keepers can help it. Water is wealth; height is wealth; the two are banked together.

The waste steam of the Works is let out through Stack Row — eleven stacks, each a stopped organ pipe, tuned — and it escapes so slowly, so baffled, that what emerges is not noise but a single chord, low enough to feel in the teeth rather than hear. It has not stopped in living memory. The city reads its health in that chord the way you read a sleeper's breath, and the guild of Chord-tuners, who adjust the stacks by fractions over decades, are the closest thing the Calder has to priests. Beyond the chord the city is quiet past all belief for an industrial place. Thin air carries sound poorly. The machines are slow. The current in the copper bus-lines is silent, the arc lamps shaded, the incline trams creeping one cable-length at a time. At noon the heat owns the streets and the hush is total — the hush hour, they call it. What sound there is, is an event: the tick of cooling stone at dusk, the Long Bell rung once a week, and now and then the crack of new rock settling, like far thunder, at which everyone pauses — not in fear, in attention, the way you pause at a word from a sleeping child.

The keepers themselves are broad-chested, slow-speaking people who measure their words the way their machines measure strokes. Their kitchens run on vat cultures — gray broths and pressed cakes grown from simples that even the natural philosophers agree are not plants, seasoned with sulfur-smoked salt. Nothing green has ever grown here. On the copper roofs a blue bloom spreads, a patina the philosophers insist is alive and the chemists insist is not; both bring samples, and the argument has outlasted dynasties. The animals are few and strange. Pipe-foxes — lean, long-tongued, their fur mineralized to a dull wire sheen — climb the Fin Fields at dawn to lick condensate from the vanes' undersides, and seeing one is considered luck. In the deep cisterns live blind eels, pale as tallow, filtering the mineral crusts. That is nearly the whole bestiary. The rarest fauna in the Calder may be its builders.

The dead are buried at street level, and the street rises. A grave is a promotion: in six lifetimes a pauper's marker stands on a spire above Stack Row. The cemeteries are the highest districts of all, and the quietest, and the most visited — families ride the inclines up on rest days to sit with ancestors who are, slowly and unmistakably, ascending.

From the Rim the whole Works lies open at regional scale: the Fin Fields black along the eastern ridge, the Inclines with their creeping cars, districts across the gulf — a full day's travel by span and stair — whose smaller stacks answer the Chord in a lower octave. Cable lines sag between peaks. Below, past the last terraces, the land drops into a heat-haze sea, a white playa emptiness, a world younger still. On the clearest days the horizon shows another smudge of rising rock, and the surveyors say there may be another Works there, or only the mountain practicing alone.

And in the oldest tier there is a channel cut by nothing but condensate — a gutter that has run for two hundred years. It has begun, faintly, to meander. In its bed a few pebbles have started to soften at the edges; the guild has labeled them, the world's first river stones, ahead of their river. Stand there at dusk with the Chord underfoot and the first stars coming out in a violet sky, and you can see the far future of the place: the valley this gutter will become, the watershed it is rehearsing. The city is teaching the mountain to be a mountain.

It will take a while. Nothing here minds a while.
