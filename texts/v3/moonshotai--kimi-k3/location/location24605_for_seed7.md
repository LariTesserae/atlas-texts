Three hundred and fifty meters down, the dark is warm.

That is the first wrongness. You descend the ladder past the dead lift cage expecting the stone-cold of deep places, and instead the air rises to meet you like a held breath — forty degrees, saturated, tasting of wet iron and crushed stems. By the twentieth rung your clothes are damp. By the bottom you understand that here, warmth and darkness are not opposites. They are the same condition, the medium everything in this hall swims in.

The hall is one room, and only one: a barrel-vaulted gallery seventy meters long, twenty across, twelve at the crown, cut from pale rock and lined in shotcrete that still looks freshly sprayed. It is almost empty. That is the thing the eye takes longest to accept — how much of it is nothing. Long reaches of bare warm floor, swept by no one, where the only record is a film of condensate beaded on the excavation dust like dew on a field at dawn. The dew never burns off. Morning never comes.

What light exists arrives in islands. Of forty caged work-lamps bolted along the gantry, three still burn, fed by a trickle of thermoelectric current the room generates from its own deep heat. Their amber pools don't touch; between them lie gaps of total, warm black through which you navigate by drip-sound and the gradient of heat on your skin. Stand under a lamp and you'll notice the strangest absence first: no gnats. No moths. The beam holds nothing but falling water and green. The halo that every night-lamp wears everywhere else in the world — the orbiting cloud of wings — is here made of leaves.

Because the lamps have been found. From the cable tray above each burning fixture, a vine has descended, or climbed — it's impossible to say which — and arranged itself into a ragged crown of small coin-shaped leaves, every blade angled to the wattage with a surveyor's precision, so close to the bulb that the nearest carry brown scorch-kisses at their tips. These are the only truly green things in the hall. Green is expensive here. Green requires light, and light is three lamps and falling.

The rest of the garden has made other arrangements. At the far end of the hall crouches the tap housing — a great bolted dome of flange and steel over the sealed geothermal bore, warm as a loaf from the deep heat bleeding up through its gaskets. Against its flanks, and against every warm pipe that runs from it, the vegetation grows pale: broad, waxy, colorless leaves, thick as tongues, pressed flat to the metal like hands laid on a stove. They do not turn toward the lamps. They turn toward the heat. They have, in some slow evolutionary lurch, traded the sky for the machine, and they are thriving — the housing wears them in overlapping plates, a blind armor of leaves, each one sweating condensate it has no use for. A cream-colored felt, thick as a wrist, sleeves the hot pipework end to end. Where a gasket leaks warmth, the felt thickens. The room's ecology maps its plumbing exactly.

Water arrives the way weather arrives in a place with no sky: late, and transformed. When it storms on the surface, nothing here knows for three or four days. Then the seep wall at the north end — where a young collapse of sharp, crystal-glinting blocks broke through into older wet rock — begins to weep more heavily, a curtain darkening, drips merging into threads. Rain, remembered by stone, delivered as seepage. It gathers in the sump trenches along the floor: pockets of still, black, bath-warm water, perfectly mirror-flat, in one of which a single red standby LED on the control panel reflects as a steady ember. Everything else the water does, it does as condensation. The crown of the vault, touching cooler rock above, grows beads that swell, merge, and release — each fall height its own pitch, so the hall plays a slow, random, well-tuned music: a high tick from the gantry, a low note from the crown, seconds apart, then a minute of nothing.

The soundscape is otherwise an inventory of near-silences. The mains hum, faint and continuous, traveling the cable trays like a held chord. The tick of steel somewhere, expanding or relaxing, once a minute. And then, on a thermostat no one has serviced, the pump: a relay clicks, and the machine takes a single slow stroke — water moving somewhere in the pipe run with a deep swallow — and stops. It pumps condensate from the sump into a return line that was capped before commissioning. The water goes nowhere. The pump performs this liturgy regardless, a heartbeat keeping time for no body, and the room's water travels its closed, leaking loop: seep to sump, sump to pipe, pipe to weep, wall to air, air to crown, crown to floor.

Nothing here was finished, and nothing has had time to age. The galvanizing on the cable tray is still bright. Rust exists only as a first orange blush on cut edges, unlayered, like a child's first sunburn. The enamel signs are perfectly legible — GEOTHERMAL TAP T-3, CONDENSATE RETURN, an arrow and the word LIFT — stencils over paint that has not chalked. At the breach, the fractured rebar shows unoxidized silver, and beneath the fallen blocks the cave has only just begun to reassert itself over the wound: the first generation of soda-straw stalactites, hair-fine, two centimeters long, a geology restarting from zero on a foundation of rock bolts and mesh. A single ore cart stands chocked on its short rail, its load of spoil still aboard, waiting for a shift that ended thirty years ago.

And the absence of animals has its own texture. No webs in the corners. No droppings, no tracks in the dust but yours, no gnaw marks on the leaf-felt. Which means the fallen leaves are never carried off, never eaten; they accumulate where they drop and compost by microbe alone into soft black beds, knee-deep against the tap housing, faintly warm with their own digestion. It also means the flowers are a tragedy on a schedule. Several times a year, buds that the pale heat-leaves produce for reasons of pure inheritance open into pale, heavy, trumpet-shaped blooms, spurred and scented, engineered in every detail for a pollinator that has never once existed here. They open into absolute darkness, exhale a sweetness so thick it pools in the still air — the strongest smell in the hall, a ripe floral rot with no receiver — and wilt, seedless, into the litter. The scent outlasts the flower by days. It answers nothing.

The room keeps time in four slow cycles. Once a day, the barometric pump of the surface breathes through the single narrow shaft — too deep and too bent to admit a photon, but open to pressure — and the hall exhales and inhales, felt only as a faint flexing of the eardrums and a brief quickening of the drips. On its thermostat, the pump strokes. With the seasons far above, the seep wall swells and fades on its days-late delay. And, slowest of all, the lamps die. Their filaments are rated in years, and one by one they go — there were nine burning when the place was sealed, there are three now — and each death orphans a crown of green. You can read the history on the floor: pale, dried runner-lines crossing meters of bare dark concrete, the tracks of vines that felt a light go out and set out, a centimeter a week, across the void toward the next glow. Some of the pilgrim lines reach a lamp. Some stop short in the dark and end in a curled, colorless tendril, still pointing.

That is the trajectory of the place, legible as a sign: the heat will outlast the light by ten thousand years. The deep bore doesn't fade on any human schedule; the filaments do. Lamp by lamp the green will give way, and the hall is slowly becoming a blind garden — colorless leaves pressed to warm steel, the felt thickening on the pipes, the pump stroking, the flowers opening for no one in a dark that will eventually be complete. It is not dying. It is settling into what it is: a small sealed engine at the bottom of the world that grows a garden as its exhaust, ruminates its own water, breathes once a day, and asks nothing.

Stand long enough between the lamps and your eyes give up entirely. Then the hall is only touch and time: warm film on your skin, a drip's high note, the far-off swallow of the pump, and somewhere in the black, quietly, at no one's bidding and for no one's eyes, the faint tearing sound of another flower coming open.
