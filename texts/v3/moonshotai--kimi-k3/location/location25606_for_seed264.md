You hear it before you see it, and you misunderstand it first.

Coming over the low rim — hardly more than a long, worn shoulder of hill, rounded like a step in an old stair — the sound reaches you as something between surf and industry: a deep slow breathing under a scatter of rhythmic concussions, and beneath it all a chord, held, faintly sour, like an organ note leaned on in a distant cathedral. You might think: a factory town in the next valley. A storm against a coast. Then you crest the rim and there is no town, no coast, no storm. There is a basin, perhaps forty kilometres across the long way, floored in pale crust, holding a thin warm haze — and standing in that enormous floor, far apart from one another like furniture in a too-large room, a handful of presences you can count on two hands. A whaleback ridge of dark stone. A line of terraces with something moving on them, rising and falling. A sunken field smoking gently. Still ponds like dropped coins. Emptiness between them, wide and singing.

The floor is warm. Not hot — warm as skin, warm as a sleeping animal's flank. It is crust, a knotted gray-cream sinter laid down in layers over millennia, and in places it sounds hollow underfoot, because you are walking on the roof of plumbing. On the open flats the crust breaks into salt polygons, their seams dark with damp, and the seams shine. Everything shines a little. There is no dust, no rubble, no fresh-broken edge anywhere. Every stone is seated in its socket like a tooth. Every surface is polished, not by hands — there have never been hands — but by condensation: by warm moisture beading, running, drying, beading again, uncountably many times, on a schedule older than the hills that contain it.

This is the first thing to understand about the basin: nothing in it was made, and all of it works.

The sound has parts, and each part has a place. In the sunken field — call it the Bellows — the whole valley breathes. Every eleven minutes the many low vents draw in together, a long, many-throated inhalation, cool air rushing past your ankles toward the warm shafts; then the exhale, a damp mineral wind pushed up out of the earth, smelling of wet stone and something faintly sweet-metallic, like rain on hot pavement in a world without pavement. The haze over the basin is this breath, hanging between cycles. And the breath is why the basin is damp at all: no stream enters, none leaves. The valley inhales its own exhaled water back. The same water has been here the whole time, rising warm through the rock, condensing, dripping, pooling, rising again. You will be damp the entire time you are here. The valley will breathe some of its water back off your skin.

On the terraces stand the Stamps. Seven flat, round stones, each table-sized, each seated in a wet socket over a steam throat. Pressure builds; a stone lifts on a gasp of vapor, hangs a half-instant, and drops — a concussion you feel in your teeth and through the soles of your feet. They fire in a fixed order, always the same order, the full pattern every forty-one seconds, and the intervals are exact enough that after an hour you have stopped hearing seven stones and started hearing one sentence, repeated. The stones are rounded by their own lifting. Nothing about them has worn down or sped up in longer than anyone could measure. They are a stamp mill milling nothing, forever.

The whaleback ridge is the Pipes: fused stone tubes, their mouths worn smooth by their own voice, and when the Bellows exhales, the ridge plays — three notes, one slightly flat, an unresolved chord that has been resolving toward nothing for a million years. Near it, conversation is impossible. You communicate by touch, or you wait. In grottoes at the basin's shallow edge, condensation drips into echoing chambers, each grotto a metronome at its own period, all of them phase-locked into the larger rhythm, so that the whole valley is a single composite beat: the breath beneath, the chord over it, the Stamps for a backbeat, the drips like a high, patient ticking. It is loud. You will raise your voice to speak. After a day or two your heart entrains; you begin to keep valley time, measuring your walk in stamp-patterns, your rest in breaths. You will learn to sleep inside it. Silence, here, would be the emergency.

There is no plant life. What covers the warm damp stone in veined sheets — bottle-green, oil-sheened, edged where it thickens into agate banding — is something simpler and harder to name. The film. It is one continuous skin, miles of it, following the damp seams and the warm shoulders of every feature, and it does not grow. It repairs. Press a footprint into it and within a few long breaths the seam has wicked flat again; scratch it and it reknits by the next day, the same thickness, the same extent, as if the valley has exactly one state and returns to it the way water returns to level. Whether the film is alive is a question it declines to answer. It eats warmth and wet stone. It has always been exactly this large.

Things eat the film, a little, at exactly the rate it repairs. On the still ponds — the Mirrors, water so level and unmoving that they hold the sky precisely, warm as bath water — skate small glassy creatures, hand-sized, translucent, sipping. When the Bellows cycle peaks, standing waves cross the Mirrors in a star-pattern, the same star every time, and the skaters ride its nodes, and you can only hear their faint rustle in the pocket of quiet between stamp-falls. Above the largest Mirror, at the same point in every cycle, a cloud forms: the same shape, in the same place, dissolving on schedule. The valley has one cloud, and it is always this one.

And there is the custodian. You may not see it; seeing it is an event. It is low and broad and plated in sinter, so that standing still it is indistinguishable from a hummock of crust, and it carries a garden of the film across its own back. It walks a circuit of the entire basin — a faint trackway, polished into the crust, that is the only road here and also an animal's path and also a feature of the erosion, all three at once. It grazes the film without ever diminishing it. Its footfalls are the one sound in the valley that does not fall on the beat: they land between the stamp-falls, a slow syncopation, the same syncopation always. Everything else here is the engine. The custodian is the passenger — the basin's single wild thing, and even its wildness keeps time.

Because nothing here changes. That is the deep rule, the one all the others serve. Long ago — the rock says — a body of heat cooled beneath this floor, groundwater found it, and the plumbing began to tune itself the way geyser systems do, throats finding their periods, deposition balancing dissolution grain for grain. At some point the oscillations locked. The valley finished becoming and began merely continuing. Seasons do not reach it. Marks do not last in it. The sun crosses and the Mirrors trade sky for stars and the chord holds and the Stamps fall and the breath turns and nothing accumulates, nothing erodes further, nothing is born, nothing is spent. It is not dead. It is not waiting. It is idling — an engine at rest, warm, enormous, perfectly tuned, running at full voice to go nowhere, forever.

Stand in the middle of the empty crust at night, the far features known to you only as directions of sound, and feel the next breath begin somewhere under your feet. The chord swells. A stone lifts, hangs, drops. Far off, unhurried, between the beats, something patient takes one more step.
