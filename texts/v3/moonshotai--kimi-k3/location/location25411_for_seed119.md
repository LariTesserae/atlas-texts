The plain is a floor.

For a day's walk in every direction the ground is made ground: brick, crushed brick, lime, the razed fabric of a city pressed flat under the weight of its own years. Nothing here is where water or wind put it. Everything is where hands put it. Dig anywhere and you do not find soil; you find older courses, then older ones, then rubble fill, then the first impatient hands of all. The water table stands in buried cellars. The rain is stored in the plans of rooms.

The city was taken down so long ago that the taking-down has become a landform. Wall-lines stand ankle-high, waist-high, slumped and glued by their own leached lime into low levees, and between the levees the rooms remain — not as walls but as basins, shallow pans that hold the sky's ration. Water stands in the plans of houses, in the plans of lanes, in the plan of a hall broad enough to hold weather. After rain the whole plain is a marsh made of architecture, and the frogs come up out of the brick-mud and sing from every room at once, loud as a city that still has people in it.

The sky is a grey lid, low and cool, dim at noon, dimming slowly toward a dusk that arrives like a held breath. Nothing absorbs sound here. Any single glance finds nothing — a heron a mile off, standing in someone's former kitchen; dragonflies working a lane; one thin line of sedge along a levee. But any patient hour finds everything, because the plain holds its creatures the way a hall holds candles: few, and far apart, and each one multiplied by the dark around it.

There is exactly one vertical thing. A willow, old and slow in the cool, the plain's only tree, drinking from the one pan that never shrinks. Beneath it is a curb of stone, drum-round, waist-high, fluted on the side toward the willow by a thousand years of rope.

This is the Jar. When it sings, people say the Bell is ringing.

It breathes. At dusk it exhales cool air that smells of lime, guano, and black water, and the willow's leaves lift all at once. At dawn it draws back in. And when the wind swings to the wet quarter and crosses the mouth at the right angle, the whole buried vessel sounds its note — a bottle-flute the size of a chapel, a tone you feel in your teeth before you hear it, carried for miles across the Flats on still nights. The frogs, they say, fall quiet to listen. They also say the frogs join in. Both things have been said for a long time.

Inside: a stair. Hand-moulded bricks no longer than a forearm, laid in thick mortar beds where thumbprints still show. Waterproof plaster the color of old flesh — crushed brick beaten into lime — patched, wept through, and healed again by a slow skin of new mineral that grows over everything like ice forming on the inside of the world. Square holes step up the walls where scaffold poles once sat; swifts nest in them now, generations deep, and their screaming in the shaft at first light is the chamber's morning bell. At the third step there is a niche, and soot on the vault above it. The rule is older than anyone's memory of the rule: light the lamp there and carry it down, and if it gutters and won't hold low on the stair, go back up. The chamber makes its own weather, and some of it is unbreathable. Beside the niche, tally marks. Generations of them. Someone has always been counting something down here.

Down to the fifth step, there is moss, a fern at the lip, a thin green band that follows the light. Below the moss line nothing is green. The web down here is not fed by light.

The stair enters the water and keeps going. You can watch it descend through the black, astonishingly clear water to a paved floor no foot has touched since before the razing. The level has stood at the same mark forever — within a hand's breadth, wettest winter to driest year — fed by weep-points in the walls where the plain's made water-table leaks into the vessel, lost to a drain at the far side that no living person has seen, that still works, that the otter uses as a door.

The chamber itself is almost nothing, and the almost-nothing is the artifact. A dozen paces across, corbelled courses closing overhead like a fist to the bright coin of the mouth. In it: water, a column of daylight, the drip-lines, and one fallen roof-chunk that came down so long ago the lime has welded it to the floor — an island now, with a back worn smooth. The emptiness returns everything. Each drip falls as a note with a long silver tail; the vault multiplies it; after rain the tempo climbs toward clatter. Wings are thunder. A handclap comes back as a chord. A voice comes back changed, and you lower it without deciding to.

The crown is full of bats. Hundreds. Up in the last dark the ceiling seethes softly, a sound like a slow fire, and all night they work the pans of the Flats, harvesting the insect haze above the standing rooms, and come home belly-full of sky. Their rain falls on the ledges and the water; pale mats of it are grazed by amphipods and larvae; the whirligigs write and erase their circles; the backswimmers hang like punctuation. Big water frogs boom from the margin. Small wall-frogs tick like cooling metal from pockets in the plaster. The snake — thick as a wrist, patterned like wet rope — owns the island, takes frogs by night, and lies by day digesting in the light-patch when the light-patch reaches it. This is the economy of the Jar: the night feeds the water.

There are pulses. An owl knows the shaft is a gauntlet and takes bats on the wing at the mouth. On certain nights the otter comes up the drain, and every frog goes silent at once — the chamber's whole loudness switched off, nothing left but drips — and then it is gone, and the chorus restarts section by section, like a crowd remembering the words. Outside, a kingfisher works the overflow pan where the drain daylights, a struck spark in the gloom, the one bright color in a landscape of grey and brick.

Through the day the shaft of light slides its coin across the water and climbs the far wall; on certain dates it stands at noon exactly on the last dry step. The mouth was placed to do this. That is what the tallies were counting. Why — no one says. The saying was lost with the city's name, and the city has no name.

Nothing here is falling down anymore. What was going to fall, fell, and is an island. What wears away is matched by what grows: the lime-skin thickens a fingernail's thickness a decade; the stalactite straws under the weep-points are young enough to remember someone's grandmother. Wear and accretion, inflow and drain, breath in and breath out — the Jar has finished becoming. It persists.

And the rope-flutes on the curb are still bright. Someone comes — rarely, at first light — stands between the willow and the mouth, and draws three buckets by hand. The splash stops the frogs; the third splash starts them again, louder. A crust of bread is left on the curb, and the swifts take the crumbs. Whether it is one person, or a line of people keeping an appointment whose reason is gone, no one has asked. The plain is big, and questions travel badly here.

What is true here:

- Everything is carried. There is no stone that hands did not place.
- Water is never absent, and never rises past its mark.
- Light the lamp at the third step. Trust it over your lungs.
- Sound does not end; it comes back. Speak accordingly.
- Below the moss line, nothing is green. The night feeds the water.
- What falls, stays. The lime takes it and keeps it.
- When the wind sits in the wet quarter, the Jar speaks. It is not speaking to you.

Come at dusk in the singing season. The outflight goes up like a rope uncoiling through the mouth, hundreds of bats threading the owl's gauntlet into the grey; the swifts make their last screaming circuits of the willow; and then the frogs begin — the chamber first, then every room-pan on the Flats answering, the whole razed city loud as traffic and empty as a held breath. If the wind turns then, the note rises underneath it all, and the keepers say — and no one has yet disproved — that the choruses across the plain slide toward it, and lock, and for a while the city of water and brick sings in a single key.
