The glacier gave this ground back so recently that nothing on it has decided what to be yet. A low outwash plain, flat as a poured floor, stitched with braided meltwater the color of skim milk from the rock flour it carries. Sleet comes and goes in fine needles. The sky hangs low and grey and close, and the cold is the wet kind — a degree above freezing, the kind that gets into cuffs and stays. Nothing grows here taller than a thumbnail of moss. The gravel is angular, fresh-crushed; the striations on the exposed bedrock are sharp enough to catch a fingernail. It is a landscape still smelling of the mill that made it.

You find the place through your feet first. A thump in the soles, regular, patient, like a pile-driver working far underground. Anyone who found it twice would call it the Knock.

The entrance is nothing — a seam where the glacier's rotting edge leans against its own moraine, low enough that you have to lie down in the wet gravel to see in. It breathes. A slow inhale of cold metallic air, a pause, then a hard wet exhale of mist, on a rhythm you can count. Condensation beads the rock lintel and drops fall in a line like beads on a string. The cold that comes out of it smells of crushed stone, of standing water, of something faintly sharp — the smell a soldering iron leaves in air, though there is nothing here to solder.

Inside, the room. It is small — a chapel's worth of space, a pocket of air kept open inside the ice — and it is almost entirely empty. That is the first thing. The middle of the chamber is nothing: dark hollow air, a space held for the sound. Everything that exists here presses itself to the edges, so the room reads as one long sentence of emptiness with a few words of punctuation. The walls on two sides are glacier — deep blue, banded, debris layers running through them like sediment in a slowed river, the blue darkening toward the thick ice until it is nearly black. The third wall is bedrock, raw and scalloped, weeping. The floor is a shallow black lake no deeper than a boot, crossed by a stream that enters from somewhere under the ice wall and leaves through the slot you came in by, shouldering small berms of slush ahead of it. Light comes dim and blue through the ice itself, plus one pale seam of daylight at the door. Your eyes adjust in stages. The drips adjust faster.

Then the knock. Every eleven seconds or so — it varies; later you learn it varies with the tide — the whole chamber throbs, one deep hydraulic beat, and the lake surface shivers into rings and the gravel ticks once against itself. It is water hammer in the glacier's own plumbing: meltwater driven through englacial conduits under pressure, valves of ice closing somewhere in the dark, the shock arriving in the floor like a piston stroke. The flue in the ceiling answers a half-beat later with a hard chuff of mist, a steam exhaust venting, then the slow draw of the inhale. Against the back wall the glacier extrudes a tongue of ice, patient as a conveyor, and every few hours a slab calves off with a crack like a rifle and drops into the lake, and the stream takes it and ships it out the door — bergs made and delivered at pocket scale. In the streambed a single rounded boulder spins in a pothole, grinding, milling the hole deeper by the week. Nothing here was built. All of it is machinery.

And it is electrified. The grinding ice and the wind-driven crystals strip charge from the air, and on still nights the icicle tips and the quartz points in the fresh rock wear faint blue coronas, and the room crackles to itself, and your hair lifts, and there is that sharp metal taste on the tongue. Between the knocks, under everything, runs a hum you feel in your teeth more than hear — the chamber has a note, and when wind crosses the flue the whole glacier sounds it, a low organ tone with miles of ice for a pipe.

The life is all at the margins, and at the margins it is abundant to the point of frenzy. The slush berms are threaded with ice worms, thin as thread, black as commas, rising at dusk in their thousands to graze the algal dust. The pools are peppered with springtails that raft the surface tension in gray moving drifts. The stream runs thick with small dark crustaceans, pulsing along in swarms, and working that stream is a dipper — a slate-colored bird that nests on a ledge behind a curtain of meltwater and hunts by walking, absurdly, along the bottom, and whose song is loud and fluting and precisely placed in the troughs between knocks, as if it has learned the machine's cycle and sings in the gaps. Where a slightly warmer mineral seep stains the rock wall there is the green: a collar of moss hummocks and flattened liverworts, lush the way an oasis is lush — lavish within its edge, nothing beyond it — and from it the midges hatch in pulses, timed, impossibly, to the thump, so that every eleven seconds a new breath of wings comes off the seep. In the ice walls themselves, flecks of dark cryoconite dust have melted their own tiny cells, each one a pocket within the pocket, each holding a drop of water and its whole microscopic citizenry. Outside the slot, in the mornings, there are fox prints: something comes and sits and listens to the engine, waiting for whatever the engine flushes out.

The place runs on overlapping clocks. Afternoon melt swells the conduits and the pulse quickens and slabs calve off the back wall; night freeze locks the plumbing and the rifle-cracks begin and the coronas bloom and the worms come up. The stream reroutes itself in the dark; gravel bars migrate; a pool that held a swarm yesterday is a dry black dish today. And beneath the diurnal beat there is a slower one — piped through miles of ice conduit comes a faint, continuous hiss that is the sea, three miles off, acoustically guided down the glacier's arteries, so that the ocean is always audible in the room like surf in a shell. The tide swells the subglacial pressure, and the knock answers the moon. The engine keeps lunar time.

It will not last. That is its final rule, and the thing that makes it most itself. The room exists on loan from the same forces running it: a few warm seasons will thin the roof and let the sky in; a surge of the glacier will close it like a fist. The mosses on the seep wall are the first of their kind here; there is no soil for a successor. Everything in the chamber is the first draft of a landscape, being manufactured at industrial pace by a machine that is simultaneously manufacturing the draft's erasure. Stand in the middle of all that holy empty space, hair lifted by the static, the drip-line ticking, the flue drawing breath — and wait for the knock. It is never late. Nothing made it, and it is never late.
