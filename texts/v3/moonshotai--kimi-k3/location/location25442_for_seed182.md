Above, the land is dry enough to split lips — a dust-town of cinder terraces and shuttered windows, where the weathercocks hang still for weeks. The stair down begins behind a riveted iron door at the edge of town, and it goes down through rock that still remembers the pick. Every face is fresh: combed with tool-marks, glittering with mica not yet dulled, edges sharp enough to take the leather off a boot toe. The heat meets you on the third flight, rising up the shaft like something breathing out. The wetness meets you on the fifth. This is the wrong way around for caves, which are supposed to be cold and damp. The Kettle is hot and damp — a greenhouse of the deep.

On the newest-cut faces grows a lichen that gives off a faint green light, the color of the inside of a pea pod. The tenders use it as a calendar. It only colonizes stone cut within living memory, and when its glow finally leaves a face, the rock is considered settled — done deciding what shape it means to be. Most of the vault below is still lit. The place is young, and it glows with it.

The vault itself is one chamber, learnable in an afternoon: a nave of raw stone perhaps sixty paces long, its ceiling lost in a gloom that beads and glitters, its far end narrowing toward the furnace throat and the cutting face. You could pace its bounds before your eyes finished adjusting. People spend lifetimes here learning its slowness.

The physics are patient. Heat comes up through the floor — not furnace heat, though there is a furnace, brick-throated and red-eyed; the furnace only borrows. The real warmth is the planet's own, arriving through a sill of dark rock, warming the sumps and the engine beds and the black standing water. Warm wet air rises and meets the ceiling, which is the one cool thing down here, and the ceiling takes the moisture out of it. The vault sweats in reverse: the air gives up its water. Beads gather on the stone overhead, creep along slopes too slight to see, merge, hang, and let go. There is no stream anywhere in the Kettle. There is sweat, and there are pockets — sumps like black mirrors set between the engine beds, water standing in the cupped leaves of the cistern vines, water trapped in pipe elbows that must be tapped with a wrench before they'll run. Rain is an event, not a presence. More on that shortly.

The engines are slow the way the sky is slow. A flywheel the size of a millstone turns once in the time it takes to breathe twice; you can watch a single spoke make its whole journey. The walking beam rocks like a metronome set by someone with infinite patience. The gradient that drives all of it — hot floor, cool ceiling — is too gentle for hurry, and the machinery has been tuned down over the decades to match the pace of everything else in the vault, for reasons that turned out to be practical.

Twice an hour, the works breathe. Intake: a long cool sigh drawn down the stair, the gallery doors leaning on their latches. Exhaust: a warm wet hush up the vent flues, carrying leaf-smell and machine-oil to the world above, where the dust-town's weathercocks twitch at it and fall still again. The tenders keep time by the breath the way other towns keep time by bells.

Nothing down here is green the way the surface is green. The leaves are nearly black — bottle, oxblood, the deep gloss of a wet horse's flank — because light is rationed and must be swallowed whole. They hold themselves toward the caged amber lamps and the furnace grates the way faces turn to a fire, and they do turn; prune a frond away from its lamp and within days it has leaned back. The first of them came as seed in packing straw, as spores in the creosote of crate timber, as one uncrated fern forgotten on a landing. The vault did the rest. Now there is no bare surface in the Kettle. Every pipe wears its moss. Every rail carries its fern. Every gear-guard has a fringe, every walkway edge a pelt. Roots find the pipe joints the way water finds a thread, and the copper and the vine have been down here together so long that the tenders no longer say which of them is plumbing. A burst main, years back, was bridged with a trained cistern-vine; it carries the water as meekly as copper ever did, and flowers besides, and the tapping of it is a small ceremony — the cut, the cup, the wait.

This is the arrangement at the heart of the place, unplanned and now deliberate: the piston glands of the great pump are packed with moss, because nothing else holds steam so gently; the garden keeps the engines sealed, and the engines keep the garden warm and watered, and the tenders keep the boundary between them — which is not a wall but a pruning line, walked daily with hook and basket. Whatever crosses the line toward the machinery is cut back and carried to the kettle room as greens. Whatever crosses it toward the garden is left, mostly. The line has drifted, over the years, in the garden's favor.

Light is a local event. It comes in three kinds, none of them bright. The amber of the caged work-lamps, spaced far apart along the galleries, each with its standing crop of dark leaves angled up at it. The red of the furnace grates, which breathes with the engines and is the nearest thing the vault has to a hearth. And the green of the lichen on the young stone, faint as breath on glass. At noon, if the sky above is clear, the shaft admits a single coin of daylight that lies on the spoil floor like something lost. The moths gather to it. The tenders step around it, out of an old unreasoned courtesy no ledger records.

Mostly, it is quiet. The quiet has furniture. A drip into the near sump, middle C, every four seconds. Another off the walking beam, higher, less regular. The tick of hot iron remembering its shape. When a drop lands on one of the big cistern leaves there is a soft clap, then the smaller patter of its overflow running leaf to leaf down to the floor. Twice an hour, the breath, like a sleeper turning. The tenders speak low, the way people do in libraries and sickrooms, though no rule says they must. Perhaps the hush is catching. Perhaps it is simply that loud noise shakes the ceiling beads loose before their time, and the vault prefers to release its water on its own schedule.

Which it does. Every few days the ceiling's load gets ahead of its patience and lets go all at once: the weather turn, the tenders call it — an indoor rain that can last an afternoon, falling from a sky of cut stone. Then every vessel in the vault is stood out, transplants are done in the soft hours, the newt is fed, and the moths fly drunk through the falling water, wings heavy, caroming gently off the lamps. Afterward the air is clearer and the engines run easier for a while, as if the place has sighed.

The hamlet hangs in the vault's upper third, where the air is coolest and the condensation gathers: riveted galleries stacked in three tiers, iron stairs, walkways swaying slightly underfoot, bunk nooks curtained in waxed canvas, a kettle room, slate ledgers chalked with the vault's slow news — *breath holding steady; sump two up a finger; glow out on the east rib; moth brood, small.* Twelve people, sometimes fourteen. They are born to it now. They go up the stair squinting and come down gratefully, and their children learn the drip-notes before their letters. The town above drinks what the pump sends up, and sends down lamp-oil, canvas, salt, and the occasional unwise cat, which never stays; there is nothing for a cat here, and the dark leaves bore it.

Animals are few, and all of them pale. Condensation moths, white as wet paper, sip the ceiling beads with uncoiled tongues and are the vault's only music in flight — a whisper, a paper-soft touch against a lamp cage. Pale isopods graze the moss like slow sheep. And in the deepest sump lives the newt: one newt, as far as anyone knows, black-eyed, old beyond reasonable newt years, which the tenders call the Prior. Sightings of it are entered in the ledger months apart, in careful hands, and it is fed at every weather turn, and its sump may not be drained for any reason whatsoever. This is the closest thing the Kettle has to law.

At the far end, past the furnace throat, the boring head still turns. It advances a finger's width a year into rock warm as bread, and the spoil comes back along a slow conveyor to be tamped into the floor paths, which crunch and settle and crunch again, never quite finished. No one working will see the next chamber. They cut for grandchildren, or for the garden, which thinks in longer units than either.

Meanwhile the calcite works from the other direction — glazing the oldest pipes, skinning the rails, millimetre by slow millimetre turning the works toward stone. Give it centuries and the Kettle will be a grotto encrusted around its machines, garden and engine sealed mid-breath in mineral, a lung stopped between sighs. Or the garden will win first, and the flywheels will stand in green to their hubs, turning slower and slower under their pelts of moss. Or — and the tenders believe this, though their ledgers don't say it — the two have already come to terms, and what looks like a slow contest is actually a slow handshake.

Twice an hour, the breath. Every four seconds, the drip. The dark leaves angled to the furnace glow like faces to a fire, and at noon the coin of daylight lying untouched on the floor.
