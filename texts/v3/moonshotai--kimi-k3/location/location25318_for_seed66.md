# Sinter

*A city on the rim of the High Shelf, where nothing has ever grown a leaf.*

---

## The Shelf

From two hundred kilometers out, the High Shelf looks like a bank of weather that has decided to keep its shape — a long pale tableland shouldering up out of a sea of cloud, its escarpment catching the morning light in rust and bone and wet slate. The cloud sea below is warm. It breathes against the cliff face all day, and the cliff drinks, and along the rim, strung for three thousand kilometers like beads on a drip, lies the city.

Sinter has no center. It has an edge.

The plateau beneath it is ancient basalt, long past any violence, riddled with warm springs that have been laying down mineral crust for longer than anyone has kept count — terrace upon terrace of sinter, pale and poured-looking, stairs made by patient water. Everything here is old enough to have stopped changing its mind. The talus slopes below the rim have been still for ten thousand years. The city has been the same size for a thousand.

The first thing a traveler notices is the palette. There is no green. There has never been green. The world is rust-red, bone-white, sulfur-yellow, wet gunmetal, the oily pearl of wet chitin — and above it all an ordinary blue sky with an ordinary sun, which somehow makes the absence stranger. Green exists in Sinter only as verdigris, the corrosion of copper, and it is treated the way other cities treat a rare pigment: ground, traded, locked away. Children learn it as the color of sickness in metal. Nothing here has ever grown a leaf.

## The Water

The Shelf is high, and there are no rivers worth the name, and yet everything drips.

The wind comes off the cloud sea laden, and the city sieves it. Along every ridge and avenue stand the fog-fans — tall stalked colonies of animals, jointed like reeds, each crowned with an array of vanes and veils that turn slowly to face the weather. They are the city's waterworks and its only shade. Mist condenses on their membranes in beads, runs down their stalks, and is tapped at the base into gutters, cisterns, the long ceramic throats of the condensaries. A mature fan-street gives more water than a well.

So the city weeps, constantly, gently. Walls seep. Eaves tick. The underside of every stair is wet. Warm air and wet skin and condensing mist become indistinguishable — you are never sure whether you are sweating or being rained on upward. Certain streets, beneath the standing cloud the city exhales each afternoon, receive their rain at the same hour every day, and are named for it.

At the escarpment the loop closes. The gathered water of ten thousand gutters pours off the rim in white threads — and does not land. The upwind off the cliff catches the falls and carries them apart into mist, and the mist rises, and the fog-fans drink it again. What falls off the edge comes back as breath. Everyone learns this before they learn letters.

## The Reef

Lean against a wall in Sinter and the wall leans back, faintly: warmth, a slow pulse, a dry tick like a cooling engine. The wall is alive. Nearly every wall is.

The masons came first — colonial encrusters, each polyp no bigger than a grain of rice, building in stacked crusts of iron-stained lime the color of dried blood and old ivory. They poured themselves over the warm sinter terraces long before any person arrived, and when the founders came, chasing the deep heat and the water, they built their city onto a reef and the reef simply continued. The city's second masonry is animal. Cut a cross-section through any old district and you find strata: brick, crust, brick, crust, the felled reefs of past centuries quarried into reefstone and laid again as walls that the masons recolonized within a generation. Every brick was once alive. The city is built of its own dead, and it says so nowhere; it is simply known.

The masons click — a snap-crackle like fat frying, audible through any quiet wall. Over them crawl the grazers: stone-lice the size of a thumb, armored, unhurried, the true pigeons of Sinter, underfoot everywhere, scraping the rust-red films that are the first flesh of the world. Over the lice move the reef-cattle — plated, many-legged, the size of oxen and nearly as placid, driven in slow herds along the rooftop ramps by children with click-sticks, cropped from wall to leased wall.

And overhead, the sky is full.

At dusk comes the sift: the swarm-rise of the sieve-moths, broad-winged filter feeders lifting off the towers in their millions to strain the evening air of the microbes the reef exhales. For an hour the sky is a colander. Their wing-hum merges with the fan-whistle and the engine thrum into a single enormous chord, and grit falls softly the whole time — cast plates, spent shells, a dry percussion on the rooftops that the sweepers collect by morning and sell to the lime kilns. At noon, instead, the kites are up: great soaring scavengers riding the thermals that the foundries and the warm reef throw off, turning in their hundreds over the hottest districts.

For every human body in Sinter there is, by the wardens' reckoning, something like four hundred times its weight in other animals. You are never the majority in any room. You are never out of sight of thousands of lives. Stare at any surface for ten seconds and it moves.

## The Web

There are no plants, so the world starts with stone.

At the bottom of everything are the films — rust-mats and sulfur veils and black iron-breath, simple life, growing wherever warmth and water and mineral meet. They eat the rock. They eat the deep heat's chemistry rising through the boreholes. They eat, candidly, the city: its effluent, its warm runoff, its exhaust. The engines of Sinter are geothermal — the city does not burn much; it boils — and every foundry, every condensary, every rendering works leaks warmth and broth into the walls, and the films thicken there, and the masons and the lice and the cattle graze the films, and the moths sift what the reef lofts into the air, and the people eat everything above the bottom rung.

It is a hot spring made of engines, and it feeds on itself.

The wind completes the loop. The city drinks the wind so thoroughly that the lee of the Shelf is a desert — a thousand kilometers of bare sinter terraces and dust devils, dead as the moon, existing only because the rim reef takes the moisture first. The dust that blows off that desert feeds the fog-fans. The reef eats the desert that the reef creates.

Human agriculture is a matter of walls and vats. The staple is rustbread, pressed and baked from cultured film raised on leased wall-faces and in the steaming vat-houses of the underflow. There is reef-meat and crackling lice-shell, eggs from the moth towers, milk and milk-wine from the cattle, blood-broth, drip-spirit distilled from who-knows-what in the sumps. Nothing is planted. Everything is set — seeded onto a surface and kept.

## The Built City

There is no wood in Sinter. There has never been wood. Beams are iron or reefstone or the laminated stalks of felled fans; cloth is spun chitin and leather; books are vellum; fuel is rendered oil and the deep heat itself. The architecture is dense and vertical and layered — narrow streets canyoned between crusted walls, buildings stacked on the strata of their own ancestors, scaffolding lashed around growth that the wardens have decided, for now, to keep.

The stack runs deep. At the top: the roofscape of herds and fan-rows and pigeon-creche moth towers. Below: the streets, loud and wet, trams ringing their way through crowds of people and cattle and indifferent lice. Below that: the underflow, where the pipes are half alive — sump-eels and pipe-worms husbanded like plumbing, because they are plumbing, keeping the channels scoured — and where the films glow faintly, a dim rot-light, in the oldest cisterns.

Every district has a Heart: a condensary tower where the deep heat surfaces and the reef grows thickest, meters of crust in hanging organ-pipe shapes, warm rain falling perpetually inside from the tower's own breath. The Hearts are the oldest structures in Sinter and the only ones never cut back. People go to them the way people elsewhere go to cathedrals — to stand in the warm loud dripping dark and feel the scale of the thing they live inside.

Once or twice a century, a district is felled. The wardens declare it, the scaffolding goes up for years, and the reef is cut to the brick — harvested, rendered, quarried, the walls hosed down to bare ancient masonry — and the district stands naked for a decade, raw and echoing, while the films creep back. It is the only festival Sinter keeps. The children of the fellers rarely see the regrowth complete.

## The Voice

Sinter is loud, and most of the voices are not human.

The base layer is the engines: a thrum felt in the teeth, steam and dynamo and the long groan of the cliff-lifts. Over it, the fan-choirs — the tall fans whistle in chord when the wind is right, whole avenues singing in slow intervals, so that a change of weather arrives first as a change of key. Over that, the rasp: ten thousand grazers scraping ten thousand walls, a sound like very distant surf that never breaks. The mason-click. The tram bells. The ticking rain of shell-fall. The dusk sift, when the wing-hum of millions rises and the whole city tilts its face up.

People here sleep deeply and cannot sleep elsewhere. Silence is the only bad omen Sinter has — a street that goes quiet is a street where the reef is dying, and wardens are sent at a run.

The light, for all of this, is perfectly ordinary. A normal sun crosses a normal sky. It strikes wet chitin and wet lime and comes back in glare, hard-edged, without a soft leaf anywhere to diffuse it — until the afternoon, when the city's exhaled cloud caps the Shelf and the light goes pearl-gray and even, and the rain-schedule streets take their appointed hour.

## The Long Patience

Everything in Sinter is loud, and almost nothing in Sinter is fast.

The reef advances a fingernail's width a year. The city plans in felling-cycles; leases are written in generations; maps are redrawn by grandchildren. The cloud sea itself rises and falls against the escarpment across spans no one alive witnesses whole, drowning the talus docks and giving them back.

Out along the rim walk the Slow Ones — the last things a traveler sees, and the first thing locals point out. Reefs that decided, some age ago, to walk: vast column-legged filter feeders, centuries old, meters of crust fanned across their backs, moving along the escarpment at the pace of erosion, each one a hill that has somewhere to be. They are older than the districts they pass. Their positions are marked on the long maps. A marriage, a mortgage, a reign may be dated by where the nearest Slow One was when it began.

Visitors notice, after about a week, that they have stopped itching. The people of Sinter host their passengers, as every large animal here does — a film of grazers too small to feel that keeps the skin. It is not mentioned. It is simply what it means to be taken in.

## What Is Known Here

*What falls off the edge comes back as breath.*

*The wall is not yours. You keep it for the reef.*

*Cut slow.*

*Never kill a fan on a water street.*

*If the street goes quiet, run for the warden.*

*The city does not grow. It thickens, and is cut, and thickens.*

---

At dusk, stand on the rim. Below, the cloud sea glows. Behind you, the sift is rising — millions of wings — and the fan-avenues are changing key as the evening wind comes round, and the cattle are being walked down off the roofs with their slow bells, and the gutters are ticking, and every wall you can see is faintly, patiently, alive. Far along the escarpment, a Slow One crosses the last of the light, fanned and ancient, going nowhere you will live to see it reach.

The city breathes out. The mist rises. Tomorrow the reef will be wider by less than a hair, and that will be, as it has been for a thousand years, exactly enough.
