Long before you see the Youngs, you hear them. The sound comes up through the soles of your feet on the climb, through the cold rails of the lower tramway, through your teeth when you stop to breathe — a standing hum, low and even, that never entirely stops. The locals call it the Note and argue about its source the way other people argue about weather: the dynamos, the wind bowing across the cable-spans, the mountain itself. Your grandfather heard it a semitone higher. Nobody finds this strange. The range is still growing, and a growing instrument deepens.

The Youngs are mountains the way a charcoal sketch is a portrait. Nothing about them has been softened. No rounded shoulders, no worn-down ridges, no talus that has had time to settle into respectability — every edge is raw, every scree slope loose and trigger-ready, every gorge cut so recently that its walls still sweat. The range rises a finger's width every year, patiently, groaning as it goes, and you can read the growth in the cliffs: old anchor-bolts from abandoned tramway moorings climb the rock faces in rusted ladders, each row a dated ring, marking where the ground used to be.

The stone is the strangest thing, and the foundation of everything else. Fresh stone — green stone, the quarrymen call it — comes out of the seams soft enough to cut with a saw, soft enough to spade. So the city is built of a material that was once malleable: blocks cut like cheese, poured into forms, carved while forgiving. Then the curing begins, and it takes decades. A young wall is faintly warm to the touch for its first twenty years; in winter you see people leaning against the newer buildings with their eyes closed. A wall sweats as it sets. A hall in the lower Spread, the Slow Hall, was loaded gently for sixty years, propped and shimmed and re-shimmed, and only last decade took its full roof-weight; the guild held a ceremony. Every doorway in the city is a diary — sills stacked with thin wedge-stones, each one a year's re-leveling, penciled with a date.

And sometimes the saws open a seam and there are shapes inside. Casts. Impressions. A fern frond of a species that grows nowhere. A bird's skull with a beak no bird here carries. Last year a cutter working the High Seam found the mold of a fish, which no one has explained, because the tarns hold no fish. The guild sends these to the Catalogue of Arrivals, a ledger kept in the dynamo hall where it's warm, and the pages go back four hundred years, and the disturbing thing — the thing everyone here has made their peace with — is that the Catalogue is accurate. The creatures arrive. On a schedule measured in decades, the molds in the stone walk out of the mist and take up their lives. A pale soft-shelled thing emerged from a fissure in the east gorge when I was young, dried and darkened over a summer into something boot-sized and beetleish and harmless, and matched a casting found forty years before. The fish has not arrived yet. People leave the tarns alone now, or watch them.

The city — the Spread — is not a city in one place. It is a constellation strung across three valleys and the long tilted bench of the Apron: a smelter here, a cistern-house there, a row of workers' cottages around a warm dynamo hall, a crushing works in the gorge below. An hour of empty moor separates one lamp-string from the next. What binds it is the String itself — the cable tramways sagging across the gorges on riveted iron pylons, ore-buckets and passenger-cages crawling along them at the pace of a walking man. On damp nights the current crawls too: a blue corona halos every wire, drips hissing off the lines, a phenomenon the wire-tenders call wire-glow and treat as a mild nuisance. Construction here is layered like geology: rotting timber cribbing under old stone under riveted iron under fresh poured green-stone, four generations of holding-on. The smelters run on hydroelectric, which is its own adventure, because the drainage of the Youngs has not been decided. Streams change their minds. A gorge that ran loud for thirty years goes quiet in a night, its water swallowed by some new fissure, and the watershed clerks walk the hills with their maps and reroute the grid around the mountain's indecision.

Water, for all the mist, is scarce — it exists as pockets in a larger dryness. The city lives inside the condensation band, a middle altitude where the rising damp meets the cold and gives up: fog-veils, enormous industrial nets, stand along the ridges harvesting the passing cloud, each veil feathered with frost at dawn, each one someone's legal property under the fog-rights. Every roof is guttered, every cable weeps a slow rain of drips, every windowsill beads by morning. Below the band the slopes are dusty and dry; above it, drier still, a desert of raw rock. Between them, this damp skin of the world: cold tarns sitting in undecided basins — a tarn can vanish overnight, drunk by the mountain — drip-caves ticking in the dark, and occasionally, a few times a season, real rain, violent and brief, sending young streams roaring down courses they haven't finished carving.

Nothing here has soil yet. That is the quiet miracle under everything: you can watch the first soil in the world being made. Lichen grinds the new rock a grain at a time; the clock-lichen of the high pastures spreads a finger's width a year, faintly luminous, and doubles as the calendar — herders reckon dates by its edges. Moth-dust, guano, patience: soil is built in trays and beds like a treasury, pampered, inherited. Wherever water lingers, the green arrives in force — moss-forests knee-deep in the drip-zones, ferns fountaining under every ledge and lintel and bridge-joint, cushion-plates dense as upholstery on the sheltered benches. The Spread is furred with green in every shadowed seam, lush in pockets exactly, half garden and half bare young stone.

The light never quite commits to day. A permanent high overcast turns the sun into a tarnished coin; dawn takes an hour, dusk takes an hour, and between them lies a long pewter twilight in which shadows barely bother. From the High Seam, above the cloud deck, the sky thins further — at midday you can pick out bright stars near the zenith. So the Spread is defined by its lamps: arc-light burning blue-white in the mist, lamp-strings sagging across the gorges, a sparse constellation seen from above with great intervals of darkness between the lights. The darkness is not empty. It belongs to the herds.

The animals are everywhere, and they are unbothered. The herds are the center of it — broad, slow beasts, shagged to the ground against the cold, pale-eyed, their breath steaming in the lamp-light, their horns crusted with lichen. They graze the clock-lichen, slowly erasing the calendar while the calendar regrows behind them, and their muzzles come away faintly luminous, so at night the herds drift through the dark with glowing faces, lowing in long held chords that thread through the Note. Wirebirds nest by the thousand in the rivet-holes of the pylons and pour off the lines like smoke; they have learned to mimic the rhythm of the crushers. Hand-sized moths pile in soft drifts beneath the arc lamps and are swept up each morning into the soil trays. And there are the arrivals — the fissure creatures, the catalogued ones — and the oldest arrival of all, the one the ledgers record first, centuries back: the inspector, low and long and silent, pony-sized, leaving tracks like two hands pressed together in the frost. It follows the herds through the streets on winter nights. You step off the path for it. You do not meet its eyes.

The loudness is a compound thing, layers you learn to read: the Note underneath, the crushers slow as a heartbeat, cable-song, water moving underground, the herd-chords, the long-interval bells. And then there is the playback. Young stone keeps sound — takes it in while it cures and gives it back slowly over the years. A quarry face murmurs with last year's thunder on a still day. A foundation repeats a dropped tool's clatter for a decade, at odd hours, like an apology. Workers here speak up without noticing; some go noise-soft from the years of it and are prized as deep listeners, the ones who put an ear to a fresh-cut face and say what the stone remembers. Nobody shouts near a ripe slope anyway — young scree is eager — but there is courtesy in it too: fresh stone repeats what it hears, and no one wants their fear played back to strangers for years.

Above everything, the Unsettled Line. It is marked with cairns and iron poles, resurveyed each year at the Reckoning, and it creeps upslope as the range grows, because the Line is the contour above which the world is not finished. Up there the sky is too dark at the wrong hours. Distances drift — the walk between two cairns is longer this year and nobody's legs are wrong; the Ledger of Distances is revised annually, a thick bureaucratic scripture of corrections. Tools left above the Line come back wrong: rusted through decades in a season, or found un-aged under the snow with the frost of the day they were lost. People who sleep up there wake with their memories misfiled, or carrying small memories of things that haven't happened yet. The feeling, travelers say, is of being unremembered by the world — thin, provisional, sketched in. Only the High Seam crews work above the Line, chasing the richest green stone, and they come down before dark, and they do not sleep there.

So the rules of the place are simple and old, and they are all one rule, which is that the mountain is a rough draft and you must live at the pace of its revision. Re-level your house and log your wedges. Water belongs to the net that catches it. Never kill a lamp-line at night. Record every arrival and harm none until the Catalogue has its say. Speak carefully near fresh stone. Don't trust a distance you haven't walked this year. Don't sleep above the Line. Haste is the local heresy; the great beam engine in the lower gorge takes a full minute to complete a stroke, and it has outlasted every machine built to replace it.

At night, from a height, the Spread is a sparse constellation burning in the mist, the lamp-strings trembling with wire-glow, the herds moving through the dark intervals with their faint lit faces, chord after slow chord rising out of the gorges. The Note holds under it all, a semitone lower than your grandfather heard. Somewhere in a seam of curing stone, pressed like a signature into the soft rock, is the shape of a fish — patient, exact, scheduled — waiting for the mountain to finish deciding where the water will be.
