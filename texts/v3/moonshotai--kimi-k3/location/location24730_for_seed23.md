# The Kettle

You come up through the cloud-sea the way everyone does: three days inside wet gray, hands numb on the fixed ropes, and then — in the last hundred paces — the world opens. You climb out of the fog the way a swimmer climbs out of a lake. Above you, light. Below you, a white ocean pouring itself slowly against the shoulders of the range. And there, in a notch of the ridge, cupped like water in two hands: the Bowl.

The mapmakers of the low country write it as *the Kettle*. The people who live there do not write, and do not say it either. Their name for it is a sign: two cupped hands, tipped gently forward. Pouring.

The whole of it is perhaps four hundred paces across — a glacial cirque old beyond reckoning, its floor and walls polished long ago and kept polished since. You can stand at the rim and see everything the place contains: the icefall wall at the back, blue and layered like a cut bank; the scatter of black pools connected by seeping veins; the town stacked against the east wall like cells in a wasp nest; the slot at the far lip where the outflow slides over into the cloud. A strong throw from any edge to anywhere. The entire world, cupped.

## Light

The first thing that hurts is the light. It comes from three directions at once — direct from the sun, up from the cloud-sea, and sideways off every rimed and dripping surface — and it has no warmth in it at all. It passes through you like light through ice. Shadows here are pale things, and they come from several directions, none of them confident. The people wear goggles of carved bone with hairline slits, and they squint anyway, all of them, their whole lives, so that a lifetime of squinting is the normal shape of a face. Outsiders' first lesson is the burn: snow-blindness by the second day if you're careless, and everyone is careless, once.

## Cold, and the water that listens

The cold is absolute and it is permanent. Cold air drains down the peaks at night and pools in the Bowl and never leaves; the sun blazes and warms nothing. Your breath does not rise in steam. It falls — a fine dust of ice settling out of the air in front of your face.

And yet the Bowl is wet. This is the central fact, the thing everything else obeys. Springs rise through the old fracture lines at two full degrees above freezing — which here passes for summer — and spread across the floor in black veins, and as they spread they cool, and they do not freeze. They go on running, glassy and reluctant, well below the point at which water should be stone. The whole floor of the Kettle is water holding its breath.

Until it is disturbed. Any sharp vibration — a dropped pot, a stamping foot, a clap of cold-delighted hands — and the supercooled water nucleates: ice races across a pool in a heartbeat with a sound like a fingernail drawn along glass, and locks solid to the bottom. Every outsider claps, once, on the first day, and watches a pool die, and is gently and firmly shown the sign for *sorry*. It is the most-used sign newcomers know.

A shout can freeze a spring-channel for a day. The town's one bell — lowland copper, precious, kept wrapped in felt in the elder-house — has been rung three times in living memory, and each time the water withdrew its goodwill for a season. It will not be rung a fourth time.

So the silence of the Kettle is not emptiness. It is discipline. Six hundred people speak with their hands, or in whispers in felt-lined rooms. Tools are wrapped; mallets have hide heads; everyone goes soft-shod. The standing sounds of the place are almost nothing: seep and trickle, the tick of ice deciding, breath, cloth, a stone set gently on a stone. And beneath all of it, at the very edge of hearing, a faint continuous hiss, everywhere and nowhere — water, freezing in films too thin to tick.

There is one room, deep in the icefall wall and lined in layered felt, where a person may laugh out loud, weep, sing the old loud songs. There is often a queue.

## What lives

Almost nothing grows. Ochre and black lichens map the lee sides of stones. At the spring-heads, where the two-degree water first breaks surface, there spread the mats — thin films of red and emerald, half-life, that everything else eats. These are the Kettle's fields. Mat-harvesters scrape them in slabs and dry them on racks like felt, and the dried mat feeds the pelt-rats, and the pelt-rats — dense-furred, fat, stupid, commensal, living in pens on the roofs and burrows under the floors — feed everything else. Fat is the economy: fat is food, fuel, lamp-light, currency. All warmth in the Kettle is borrowed from animals, because nothing burns here but what once moved.

The rest of the fauna is abundant past reason. Thread-worms swarm in the wet ice by the million; where a film flash-freezes underfoot it locks them mid-motion, and the Kettle is scattered with *worm-glass* — panes of ice gone black with frozen threads that, warmed in a bare palm, remember they are alive. Snow-fleas drift like pepper at every melt-margin, hopping without sound. Pale efts knot in the pools and are scooped in sinew nets; their oil burns in every lamp, bright and almost heatless, like the sun. And overhead, riding the still air, the mutes: ten thousand soft-fringed white birds who have no voices at all, who feed on the worm-blooms when the springs pulse, and who turn all at once over the Bowl like a single slow snowflake.

The predator is called the hush. It is white, and you never hear it, and you know it has passed only by the absence of something that was there.

The rim has shaggy goats that eat lichen and are mostly rumor. Below, the cloud-sea has — shapes. Sometimes it bulges. The people slide offerings over the lip: frozen bundles, fat, once a copper bracelet. They do not sign about what they are for.

## The town

The town has no streets. It is stacked against the cirque wall and against itself, roof on roof on roof, and you cross it by walking over other people's houses, entering your own through a hatch, descending by ladder into warmth. Walls are fitted rubble mortared with poured ice; every exterior is glazed in clear ice a hand deep, renewed by the bucket, so the whole warren gleams like one wet object. Windows are ice-lenses, thick and clear, ground on sandstone — the town's famous export — and the deep rooms glow blue, lit through their own walls. Beneath and between the lived rooms run the crawl-passages, the Underneath, where the pelt-rats burrow and children go to be briefly, deliciously lost.

It is dense in every sense. Rooms nest in rooms: smoke-hole above hearth above eft-pen above drying-rack. Bodies sleep piled for warmth. Nothing is an arm's length from anything else, including, usually, another person.

## The churn

Do not mistake the hush for stillness. The Kettle is frantic.

The springs pulse on no schedule anyone has learned, and when they pulse, pools brim and lock and overflow and melt back; rime grows visibly on every wet surface and sublimes and grows again; films of ice race across the floor and retreat. Every morning, crews pick open the doorways and channels that froze in the night — the town is in daily negotiation with its own freezing, and the negotiation never ends. Stone is quarried by the world's own habit: fill the drill-hole with water, agitate it, wait for the split. The market runs dawn to dark at full bazaar fever, conducted entirely in the hands — runners, sledges of fat and bone and lens-blanks on the ice-slicks, arguments in flashing fingers, not a voice raised. The mutes come down in their thousands and go up again. The grey hours arrive when the cloud-sea rises and pours through the notch in slow soundless waterfalls of fog, and then everything accretes an inch of rime and the town goes interior and the Kettle holds its breath inside its breath.

Once or twice a generation the icefall wall cracks — a deep groan, muffled by a hundred feet of ice, the only loud natural sound the place makes. They call it the Speaking. When it happens, everyone stops, mid-gesture, mid-trade, and faces the wall.

## What the wall remembers

The icefall is the archive. Behind its clear face you can see the strata of older towns: rooms, roof-beams, a hearth with pots still on it, sealed in accreted ice century by century as the wall grew over the settlement's rear edge. And in the shallowest, clearest layers, the dead. The people of the Kettle are not buried; they are glazed in — laid in a cut in the ice and poured over, generation above generation, until the back wall of the world is full of them, visible in good light, many with their hands still raised in the farewell sign. The town lives with its back against all of its ancestors.

At the lip, the outflow slides over into the cloud past a bone-midden delta a thousand years deep, past the organ — a colonnade of icicles the size of tree trunks that sounds, faintly fluted, only in the rarest wind. The one music nobody makes. There are elders who have heard it twice.

## The guest ledge

Outsiders are made to wait on a ledge below the rim — a day, two — to acclimatize: to the cold, to the glare, and above all to the quiet, which is the hardest of the three. They come for the ice-lenses, for eft-oil, for felt that sheds water like a bird. Lately they come, more and more, for the silence itself: the noise-sick of the low country, climbing up out of the cloud with their jaws clenched, flinching at everything, and going down again — if they go down — speaking softly for the rest of their lives.

What they carry away is not quite the place. The place is this: a cold that never lifts and a light that never warms and water that will not make up its mind, held in a stone hand above the clouds; six hundred people and ten thousand birds and a million worms, all moving, all the time, in a hush they keep the way other towns keep a fire.

Stand still long enough and you will hear it — the faint hiss at the edge of hearing, everywhere, patient as weather. Water, deciding.

And then deciding again.
