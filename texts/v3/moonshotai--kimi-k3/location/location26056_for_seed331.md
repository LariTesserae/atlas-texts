From the plain, it is nothing. The old lowland runs to the horizon in long waves of grass, worn as a bar of soap, and the rise ahead is only the gentlest interruption — a swell a child could climb without breathing harder. Everything in this country has been eroding for so long that erosion has stopped being a process and become a condition. The hills are finished. The plain is finished. Nothing here is becoming anything else.

Then, halfway up the swell, the sound stops.

Not fades — stops. Within a single pace the wind unhands you. A kite crosses the line overhead with its wings laboring and makes no sound at all, and neither does the grass it should be stirring. Behind you the world still murmurs; ahead, nothing. The boundary is exact as a tideline. You could straddle it: one ear in the world, one in the hush.

From the lip you can see the whole of it. The bowl is perhaps half a mile across — you could walk its rim in an hour — and shallow, the lip standing only forty paces above the floor. It is too smooth for chance, though nothing in it is straight; a hundred thousand centuries have buttered every edge. But water remembers what the eye forgives: the pools below align in long concentric arcs, like ripples that never dispersed. The floor is a meadow of sedge and moss gone silver-green, broken by open water and by trees. Few trees. A few dozen, each alone, no two crowns touching — pale trunks fluted like the muscles of some great limb, buttressed roots standing knee-deep in black water, crowns high and thin so that light comes through everywhere. It has the proportions of a room. It has the stillness of a nave. And it is full — you will understand this only slowly — of animals.

The light has no direction. Warm mist scatters it; the pools throw it back; it arrives from the whole sky at once, silver-gold, nearly shadowless, and it comes from below as well as above — the water throws light up into the trees, so that even the shade here is lit from beneath. At dawn, if the night has made dew, the bowl is ruled in lines: hair-fine filaments strung from tree to tree and lens to lens, beaded with water, each bead a small sun. By midmorning they are invisible again, and you locate them only by the dragonflies perched along them in rows, resting on what cannot possibly hold them.

No stream enters the bowl. The water comes up from below, filtered through whatever the floor is made of, and it is warm as blood and it never rests. The pools migrate — over days a pond slumps sideways through the meadow, stranding mud on one shore, drowning grass on the other. Every nine days or so the whole water table stands higher; the pools swell and join, and the bowl gleams like a held thought. Then it breathes out. The rocks along the rim weep constantly. The leaves drip at noon. Nothing is ever dry, and nothing is ever quite flooded.

Set flush into the meadow and the pool-floors are the lenses: dark ovals, a dozen of them, perhaps two, some no bigger than a plate, some you could lie down on, made of a substance that is not stone and not metal and has not, in all this time, learned to weather. In a world filmed with algae and silt they are spotless. Silt settles on them and is gone by morning — not washed away, gone. They are warm. Dragonflies bask on them in the cool of dawn, and an enormous soft-shelled turtle, moss-backed and boulder-eyed, hauls itself onto the largest and stays for days. Stand on one and the hush deepens past belief: your footsteps leave you, your breath leaves you, your pulse is swallowed at the wrist. Speak, and your voice goes out like a candle — not muffled, deleted. The animals treat the lenses as furniture, as rocks, as facts.

One thing still stands. Near the rim, a fin of the same dark substance leans out of the slope, eroded at last to a smooth blade. Beneath the clearest pool, an arc of darker floor curves away under the silt — a buried geometry that the fish honor, schooling along it in slow parabolas, tracing the hidden shape like a readout. On one exposed slab at the weep-line there are marks: parallel striations a hand's breadth wide, too regular for any geology, tool-marks made by machines for machines.

It is all one thing. That is what you come to understand. The bowl, the lenses, the filaments, the fin — one device, mostly buried, older than the plain that has grown up around it. A dish. An ear. It was made to hear something very faint, and its first function — the one it has never once stopped performing — is to subtract every local sound. The hush is not an absence. The hush is a task, being done.

And into this held silence, life has poured itself in numbers past counting. By every law of wet warm places this bowl should roar — frog-chorus, insect-whine, bird-clatter — and instead the frogs, dozens on every bank, hunt and mate and leap in muteness; the crickets abandoned the fiddle for the semaphore long ago, signaling in wing-flashes and in drummed forelegs felt only through the stems; even the splash of a fish is swallowed within an arm's length. Over deep time the loud simply wasted themselves — the ear drank their voices before any mate could hear — and so the bowl evolved toward light and scent and touch.

The result stands around you in layers. In the water: clouds of fry turning as one body, glass shrimp, diving beetles falling like dropped coins. In the air: dragonflies in strata, each species owning its altitude — amber ones skimming at the knee, blue darners working at the waist, great gliders planing at head height, hundreds upon hundreds, their wings the loudest sound in the bowl, a rustle like cellophane, like someone far away turning pages. On the mudbanks: butterflies puddling in drifts of sulphur and blue, rising in sheets around your knees as you wade, almost weightless, almost soundless. Among the sedges: sundews no bigger than coins, each tentacle tipped with a jewel, and fat pitchers half-full of rainwater and the quiet dead. At the pool margins, heron-things stand like fixtures and strike without noise. Mowing the floating mats, the grazer makes its rounds — broad as a boat, skin like wet slate, patient as geology — leaving mown trails through the green, its breath rising in slow silver bubbles, the only sound it makes, and the ear takes that too.

Within an hour you are infrastructure. Dragonflies perch on your shoulders; butterflies gather at your wrist to taste the salt of you; nothing here can be alarmed by noise, because no noise survives to alarm. That is the overwhelming thing — not menace, there is none, but the sheer number of eyes, the density of quiet occupation, the sense that you are the single largest and least-noticed event in a room full of ten thousand small intentions.

Dusk brings the change of shift. Bats cross like thrown gloves. Moths come up out of the sedges, and under the pools and along the lens-rims the machine's lights come on: faint, green-amber, sourceless, status-lights for an errand nobody remembers. Beetles answer them in flashes. Patterns cross the bowl — inquiries, replies — a conversation in light that goes on until dawn.

The rules of the place are simple, and you learn them by staying. Speak at arm's length, or touch. Camp nowhere; the water will come, and your footprints drown within hours. Do not trouble the lenses; they keep themselves. Nothing echoes. Storms that cross the outer plain arrive at the rim as silent cinema — you watch rain-gray march across the grass, watch lightning stand in the far clouds, and not one drop-sound, not one rumble, crosses the lip. And once in a decade a great tree flowers, and the whole bowl smells of honey, and the moths come down like blizzards, and the ear, presumably, hears none of it.

Whether it has heard anything at all in the last hundred thousand years is not knowable from the surface. But if you lie on one of the lenses at night, very still, you feel it — not hear it, feel it, in the bones of your back: a single slow contraction, once every seven minutes or so. The scan. The listening. The held breath of a machine that has been holding its breath since before the hills were hills.

Leave at dusk, then, climbing the rim through the warm rising mist. At the lip the world returns all at once — the plain's evening chorus hits like surf, crickets and wind and your own sudden blood-roar — and you turn and look back at the bowl lying bright and still below, its pools catching the last light, its lights coming on under the water. It says nothing. It is not waiting for you. It never was. Somewhere under the meadow, in the dark of its buried body, the ear is open, and the silence it keeps is the oldest thing you have ever stood inside.
