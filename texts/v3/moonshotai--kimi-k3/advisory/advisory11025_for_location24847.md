# Preparing for the Plateau of the Line

## First, understand what you are visiting

This is not a wilderness and not quite a town. It is a measurement that people live inside. Every drop of water on the plateau is accounted for at dawn; every fallen leaf is carried away by hand because no creature will do it; the population, the gardens, the cisterns, the noise — all fixed, all deliberate. You, arriving from the multiverse with your appetites and your clatter, are an unbudgeted quantity in a place where unbudgeted quantities are the only kind of disaster they have.

So the first preparation is not physical. It is accepting that you will visit as a guest inside someone's arithmetic. You will be assigned water. You will be assigned days. You will be fed from stores that were planned without you in mind. Honor the numbers you are given exactly — not approximately, exactly — because the people watching the water stand at the Line every morning have staked their whole civilization on the idea that exactly is achievable.

## Arranging the visit

Do not arrive unannounced. Send word ahead through whatever lowland contact, runner, or signal arrangement serves — ask in the warm country below the escarpment, where someone will know the protocol, because visitors are rare but not unknown. You will be told when to come, by which route, and how long you may stay. Accept the number of days. A roof will be standing empty for you — that is how this place works: the emptiness comes first, the occupant second.

If you are ill — anything respiratory, anything feverish — wait at the base until you are well. This is a small, fixed, isolated population. Your head cold is a historical event waiting to happen. Quarantine is not an insult here; it is courtesy of the highest local kind.

Leave every animal in the lowlands. No pack beast, no dog, no caged bird, nothing. There is no dung on this plateau — the smell of it has possibly never occurred there — and nothing eats what it cannot reach. A mule on the moss would be a profanation you could not undo. You carry your own load up the cliff, and you carry it all back down.

## The ascent

The climb is the dangerous part of the journey, and it is dangerous in a specific, young way. The rock has not been rubbed down. The basalt stands in fresh hexagonal columns with edges that take skin; the talus at the base is bare, angular, unsettled — it clicks and rings underfoot, and it will shift. Bring real approach boots with ankle support and leather gloves for the rock. Tape or cloth-wrap every piece of metal on your kit — buckles, carabiners, zippers — partly for noise, partly because you are about to enter a world without metal and there is no reason to arrive jingling.

Budget two days minimum: a night at the talus base to acclimatize — the plateau sits at the altitude where rising air surrenders its water, high enough to notice — and the climb itself. The upper cliff weeps water along its bedding planes, so expect slick rock under the cloud hem. If your route passes through the great notch, you will climb directly into the cloud's front door: whiteout, condensation beading on every surface, and the tall frames of the fog-comb dripping overhead. Do not touch the comb fibers. That is the town's drinking water condensing, and your hands are not clean by local standards — nothing's are.

Know this about your arrival: the mountain will announce you. Every footstep on the talus rings up the cliff face. You cannot sneak onto this plateau, and you should not want to. The whole town will hear you coming, softly, the way they hear the one sanctioned hammer. Climb in the morning, unhurried, on the day they expect you.

## Your body

Start toughening your feet weeks in advance. Boots are for the cliff; the plateau itself is walked barefoot — heel-light, moss-first, unhurried — over cushion moss a forearm thick and lawns wet at every hour. Your boots, once on top, are both a noise and a statement about you. Go barefoot in the wards as soon as your feet allow. Watch for the bare tuff aprons and any worked basalt, which can be sharp, and learn that wet moss is nearly frictionless — it is slick enough to drag loaded sledges across — so walk as they walk: weight down through the heel, no shoving off with the toes, no hurry. There is no hurry here at all. Nothing on the plateau startles, and you should try not to either.

Everything will be damp for the entire visit. The air is warm and wet; mist condenses on you; the houses drip from their living turf roofs. Dress in wool or soft brushed cloth that stays warm and quiet when wet — nothing swishy, no hard rustling shells, no Velcro anywhere on your person (a Velcro rip in that hush would turn heads in every ward). Bring a warm layer for evenings: when the cloud sinks below the rim, the plateau stands in clear gold air at altitude, and wet clothing chills fast in that dry windless hour. Bring sun protection for that same hour — it is the one time the sun has any force.

Care for your feet and skin against the constant wet: dry them at every opportunity, treat any hot spot or fungal itch early. Care for cuts immediately and seriously — the young rock opens skin easily, and you are far from anything but sedge-fiber and patience.

And prepare yourself for the loudest thing on the plateau: you. In a hush so complete that people hear their own warmth, you will hear your heartbeat, your breath whistling faintly in your nose, your stomach, your joints. You will discover you have tinnitus, or you will invent sounds your brain expects and isn't receiving. Some visitors find this euphoric within a day. Some find it frightening for longer. Both reactions are normal. If the silence begins to ring at you, sit by a runnel — the small water-sounds are the plateau's mercy: condensation ticking off a lintel, sheet-flow whispering, the seep-hiss inside a tuff wall.

Sleep may be difficult at first, not from noise but from its absence. Lie still under the turf roof and let the drip-line outside the eaves set your rhythm. It will. Within days your pulse will synchronize to the daily one: dawn reading, cook-fires, thick silver midday, gold evening. That pulse stands in for seasons there, and it will stand in for yours.

## Your kit

Strip your gear down and quiet everything. Soft pack, cord closures, no jingle — take the keys and coins off your ring now; money means nothing here and metal is the local absence. De-blister any medicines into cloth rolls, because a blister pack's crackle carries across a ward. No cans, no foil, nothing that crinkles. Nothing that beeps — no alarms, no watches with chimes, no electronics at all; they are useless and obscene here in equal measure.

Carry water in a soft flask or gourd. Bring a waxed or waterproof notebook if you must write — paper will not survive the damp — or better, learn to keep your days their way, as knots in a sedge cord. It doubles as a gesture they will understand in their bones.

On light: light is language on this plateau. The wards talk in small silent flashes from polished obsidian disks. A camera flash would be a shout of gibberish across the whole town; a signal mirror is a telephone you don't know how to use; even shiny jewelry glints like muttering. Remove reflective objects or cover them. If you bring a lamp for the night, make it dim, shielded, and used only indoors — a bright beam sweeping a turf-roofed ward after dark would be their version of fireworks in a library. Ask your hosts to teach you two or three flash-phrases — greeting, thanks, the equivalent of "all well" — and use nothing beyond them.

The trash doctrine is absolute. Nothing on the plateau eats what it cannot reach, and there are no mouths but human ones. Anything you drop stays where it falls, visibly, essentially forever, until a leaf-walker — a person whose whole work is carrying away what a beetle would have handled anywhere else — has to deal with it. An apple core on that moss is a monument to your carelessness. Pack out everything, including organic waste, including anything you trim, peel, or shed. Leave exactly as much as the mist leaves: nothing that wasn't already water.

Fire is forbidden to you, full stop. The soil is a hand deep of half-peat over tuff — the plateau's entire history compressed into a layer one spade-thrust thick — and an ember could smolder through all of it. Cook-fires are lit once, at dawn, all at once, by people who know exactly what they are doing. Eat when they eat.

For gifts: ask in the lowlands what is wanted rather than guessing. Salt is a fair bet on a cloud-fed mountaintop; fine lowland cord or cloth, plainly colored, is unlikely to offend. Bring no metal, no seeds — nothing alive, ever, nothing that could take root or crawl — and think carefully before gifting anything as exotic as a feather: this is a people for whom a single bird is a generation's event, and you don't know what you're handing them. The best gifts are news from below, offered quietly, and your labor.

## Water and food

The water will be the cleanest you have ever tasted — nothing lives in it, no dung has ever touched it, it fell from a cloud that morning. It is also rationed to the drop. Never drink from a cistern, runnel, or basin uninvited. Never wash in the Mirror or any cistern; ask where bathing is done — there will be a designated place, downstream of the intakes. Ask where the latrine arrangements are and follow them to the letter; you are inside the loop, and the loop is closed. What the comb yields, the seep-fields are permitted to return — and you are now a small pipe in that plumbing. Be a clean one.

The food is entirely of the wet-gardens: taro-leaf, water-celery, the staple rush root, grain sedge cut with obsidian. No meat, no dairy, no egg — there has never been any. It will sustain you fine for the length of a visit. If you habitually salt your food heavily, use your own supply discreetly rather than draining theirs.

## Sound

This is the etiquette that matters most, and the one you will fail at first. Speak low and close. No shouting, ever — a shout here is not rude, it is a kind of disaster, a thing people will reference for years. Do not whistle. Do not laugh at full volume until you have heard how they laugh (softly, through the nose, like their songs). Cough and sneeze into cloth. If you must do anything loud, ask when the sanctioned hour is — the one hour when one worker makes the plateau's single permitted noise — and do it then, at the designated site, or not at all.

Learn the barefoot walk before you arrive if you can: practice on wet grass at home, in the dark, trying to make no sound at all. It is harder than it sounds and they learned it as children.

If you sing, hum through the nose. If you weep — some do, at the comb at dawn, hearing hundreds of small drips at different heights and pitches, the nearest thing this world has to an organ — weep quietly and without embarrassment. It is the correct response, and they will understand it.

## Participating

Attend the dawn reading at the Mirror. Stand back from the basalt pavement, out of the reader's sightline, and watch the water stand exactly at the inlaid Line as it has through tens of thousands of recorded knots. Go to the comb at dawn at least once; sit in the warm wet and listen to your hosts' water being harvested out of the air.

Pay for your stay the only honest way: work. Ask to join the leaf-walkers — it is humble, endless, essential labor and the fastest way to understand the covenant — or take a turn on the comb maintenance round, renewing fibers fiber by fiber. Work quietly, at their pace. Nothing here is renewed in a hurry.

Do not pick the flowers. The rosette-trees bloom for no one — there are no bees, there is no audience — and their pale wands opening to the empty air are the plateau's own private gesture, not a bouquet. If, by some grace of wind, a bird comes up out of the lowlands while you are there: stop whatever you are doing, stand in the open as everyone does, and listen to the single voice. You will be present at an event people describe to children not yet born. Say nothing until long after it ends.

Navigation warning, plainly: at midday the cloud comes in thick, the light goes silver-grey, and a flat, featureless plateau of lumpy turf-roofed wards becomes a whiteout beside a fresh-fractured rim. Stay in the wards during the thick hours, or follow the polished dark-green sled-way tracks, and never wander toward the edge in cloud. The rim is a young fracture with unpatinated edges; keep back from it even in clear evening light.

## Leaving

Carry everything out, including your waste. Climb down on a day they know about. And then — this is real, plan for it — schedule two or three quiet days in the low country before rejoining your world, because re-entry is the hardest part of the journey. After the plateau, the lowlands will assault you: insect whine, bird-chaos, dung-smell, musk, rustling, your own voice booming like a stranger's. Ordinary civilization, when you reach it, will seem for a while like a place where everyone is screaming. This fades. What doesn't fade is the calibration.

Because you will have seen it: the basin full to its mark, the drip, the one slow ring going out, the surface returning to the Line and staying there. That is the standard for your visit, and the souvenir. Be the drip. Raise your one slow ring — they will absorb it, they have practiced absorbing small dissonances for ten thousand readings — and then let the surface come back to its mark, and stay there, with a few more ordinary knots in the Ledger-house that happen, quietly, to be yours.
