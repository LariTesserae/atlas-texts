# Preparing for the Long Morning

First, understand what kind of expedition this is, because it is not the kind you have trained for. Every other wilderness negotiates with you — weather turning, water hiding, daylight spending itself against your progress. The Long Morning negotiates nothing. Its conditions are fixed and known to the centimeter: one temperature, one light, one silence, forever. Nothing there will help you. Nothing there will hinder you. Nothing there will forgive you. Every variable in the equation is you — your lungs, your water, your feet, your discipline, your mind. Preparation for the Long Morning is therefore almost entirely self-preparation. You are packing character more than kit. Pack both.

## Before the climb

**Acclimatize.** The plateau sits at extreme altitude; the sky is violet because there is not much air between you and the dark. Spend ten days to two weeks staging at height below the notch before the final ascent. Learn the signs of altitude sickness and take them seriously, because the only cure is descent, and descent means all the way back down the climb — and because the light up there will never tell you to hurry. There is no storm coming, no night falling, no window closing. The mountain will give you no reason to turn around. You must bring your own reasons, written down, in advance.

**Decide everything at the bottom.** Your length of stay. Your route. Your rules. The person who will wait for you and come to the notch to shout if you overdue yourself (sound carries fine in the moving world; it is only up top that voices die at arm's length). On the plateau, nothing will ever remind you of anything. There are no deadlines in a place without a future. Every decision you fail to make in advance, you will make later in a trance, badly.

**Choose a companion.** Two to four is right. Not for the dangers — there are almost none in the usual sense — but for the calf, and for the long look, and for the moment one of you stops walking and doesn't notice. Give each other permission, in writing if you must, to take one another's arm and walk each other onward.

## The kit

**Time, redundantly.** Two watches minimum, one of them spring-wound. There is no external time on the plateau — no sun movement, no stars, no shadow creep, nothing. If your timekeeping fails, you will not know whether you have been out three days or nine, and your sense of duration will begin lying to you within two sleep cycles. Keep a tally as the keepers did: one scratch per sleep, on the wall of whatever tally-house shelters you. Sleeps are your days now. The tallies are the tradition — counts, not confessions — and joining them is permitted. Note that all the old counts end mid-stroke. The plateau charges admission in your own years: you age there; it does not. Budget your life deliberately.

**Sun protection, absolute.** Permanent mid-morning at that altitude, doubled off the white salt. Glacier goggles with side shields, not sunglasses. Sunscreen on every surface including under the chin — the light comes up at you off the pan. Lip protection, draped hat, long sleeves, sun gloves. You will not get one cloudy minute. There is no shade anywhere except the bothies and your own shadow, and your shadow will be the same length your entire stay, so you cannot use the sun's height to judge how long you've been out. Use the clock.

**One perfect outfit.** The place keeps a single cool, mild hour, so once you've felt it you can dress for it exactly — no rain gear, no midnight parka. This is a strange luxury. But the climb up and down crosses a moving world with its own moods; pack for the approach separately. Broken-in boots and gaiters — the salt crunch underfoot will be your constant companion, and salt in a blister is cruel. Pack extra socks; whether anything of yours ever dries up there is between you and your own physics, and the place is under no obligation to help.

**Water, and the discipline of the bowls.** There is no water on the plateau. None. The rivers carried their last water before the oldest living thing was born; nothing falls, nothing melts, nothing seeps. The only water in the world stands in open bowls in the tally-houses, spaced a day's walk apart, kept full across the ages by the restraint of everyone who has ever passed. The bowls are ledgers: they record each traveler's thirst and each traveler's restraint, and the difference, in millimeters, forever. So carry your own — three to four liters per walking day — and plan as if the bowls do not exist. Then, when you pass one, you may make the only deposit the place accepts: pour a little in. The level you leave is the level the next traveler finds, in a year or in ten thousand years. Drink from a bowl only in true need, and understand that the need will be public forever. The real preparation is engineering away the temptation before it arrives.

**Food, all of it carried.** Nothing is forageable. The herds are warm to the touch and they are not food — they are not statues, and what you cut from them you would be cutting forever, in front of everyone. The one convenience: nothing rots. Your supplies keep indefinitely; no preservation, no pests — the bee is occupied. Still, practice crumb discipline: eat over a cloth, pack out every scrap. A dropped almond is a monument.

**A solar cooker.** The sun is always up; a panel or parabolic cooker works whenever you stop, leaves no ash, consumes nothing, marks nothing. If you must have flame, use the bothy hearths with your own fuel, sparingly — the stacked dung is finite forever, and every stick burned is irreplaceable. Never light an open fire out on the pan. Your smoke will rise, stop, and hang exactly where it stopped — the only cloud in the world, signed with your name. "You are the only weather here" is not a metaphor. Ventilate the bothy if you use the hearth; still air clears nothing on its own.

**The small strange things.** A contoured eye mask — you will sleep in permanent daylight, and the dim bothies are your bedrooms. A signal mirror, useless for rescue but excellent between companions across a pan. A bright garment, so your partner can pick your moving shape out of ten thousand standing ones. A tether line for the herd density. A full medical kit and blister kit — evacuation is self-rescue only. Wag bags. This needs saying plainly: nothing rots, which includes everything you excrete. There is no honorable alternative to carrying it all out. A latrine dug in salt is a permanent installation.

Leave behind: pets, weapons (the cat is occupied, forever), star charts, anything disposable, and anything you are not willing to carry the whole way — the place will not take your trash burden from you, ever.

## Moving through

**The sun is a fixed compass.** Note its azimuth at the notch; it will hold that bearing for your entire stay. Every shadow on the plateau points the same direction forever — the cat's, the lammergeier's oval lying on the salt, your own. You can orient anywhere by any shadow. Carry a magnetic compass too and cross-check; if they disagree, trust the shadows.

**Stay on the cairned corridors.** This is survival, not just courtesy. The Congregation is packed flank to flank; you cannot push through it without moving bodies, and what you move stays moved — a person would spend the rest of their life remembering the exact posture in which they left someone. The paths exist so that you never have to. If you lose the path, stop, find your own footprints, and follow them out. Your outbound line is your way home; walk it cleanly. You will also find older lines, pressed into the salt, readable forever. Some end at tally-houses and never leave. Some go to the rim. Do not follow any line to the rim.

**Sound dies within meters.** No echo off the ridges, nothing. Your breathing will be the loudest thing in the world. So: line of sight, always. Hand signals agreed in advance. Rally at every tally-house. If separated, do not shout — look.

## The rules of the record

Move nothing. Touch the way you would touch a sleeping person, and prepare for the warmth — the flank that yields, the thin musk the air holds without releasing — because it will undo you the first time, and it is meant to. Take no souvenirs: not a ventifact, not a lichen stone, not the copper awl worn to its nub. Make no marks except your tallies and your footprints — and your footprints are not optional, so make them honest. They are the only biography the world permits, and they will outlast the mountains' patience. Everyone who comes after you will be able to read where you walked, what you detoured around, what you approached, whether you were kind.

Act as if the herders can see you. In the only tense the plateau has, they can.

## The calf

Have this section settled before you reach the pan — at the notch, not at the pan's edge. The path passes close enough that you can see the calf's eye. You will want to push the cat aside, carry the calf to its mother. Understand precisely what that would be. It would not be rescue: there is no future in which rescue could unfold, and the calf will be warm and unbreathing wherever you put it. The cat is equally paused, and mercy laid on one of them is a hand laid on the other. Your intervention would change nothing; it would only sign everything — your mercy rendered as permanent sculpture under a sun that will never set to forgive it. The cairn-builders thought about it too. The paths are their answer. Give yourself permission to grieve without acting. Give your companion permission to walk you on.

## The mind

The silence has no floor — your voice will die at arm's length and nothing will ever answer, not even an echo. Some travelers bloom in it; some unravel. Bring music if you need it (solar charging works forever; it is the one bottomless resource). Talking to the herders is human and harmless. Do not begin to hear replies.

Beware the long look — the trance that comes standing before the old woman's turned face, the child's reach, the calf's eye. And beware the deeper seduction underneath it: it is a *good* hour the place keeps, useful and honest and mild, and it is always now, and nothing will ever make leaving easier than staying. The place does not keep you. You keep you. The counts that run long all end mid-stroke. Hold to the date you set at the bottom.

If you meet another traveler up there, the first gift to exchange is the time. Synchronize watches. It is the only news there is.

## If things go wrong

No rescue exists. No one lives there; no weather will ever erase your SOS; the next reader of any message you leave may come years or millennia after you write it. Leave word at the notch — it is the one mailbox the place has. Treat altitude illness with immediate descent. And if you die on the Long Morning, you join the record: unweathered, newborn, forever. Let that make you careful rather than afraid.

Do not go down at the rim. Not to check, not to look, not for anyone. The people who went down to check whether the sun moves in the lower country did not come back up to say. Return the way you came.

## Coming down

Budget for re-entry. The first wind will feel like being touched. The first sunset will be a wound and a mercy at once. Echoes will startle you; rotting food will seem profligate; the sheer *noise* of the moving world, its smells, its hurry, will take days to stop feeling violent. Keep your clock discipline a little while. You earn the name at the notch, but you become it on the way down.

And at the threshold, look at your boots. The salt clinging to them, carried below the notch, will be the first dust of that place to move in longer than the mountains remember. Brush them at the boundary, or carry it down knowingly into a world where it can finally blow away. Either way — decide. That is what the place teaches, in the end: that you are weather, that you are a record being written, that care is a choice made in advance.

The compact is five lines. Drink little. Walk the corridors. Move nothing. Count your sleeps. Come back down.
