# Preparing for Sinter: A Traveler's Guide

## First, Understand What Kind of Visit This Is

You are not visiting a city. You are applying to join one — and the application will be processed by the city itself, not by anyone in it.

Every other place you've traveled, you remained yourself: a sealed body moving through an inert environment. Sinter does not permit this. The walls are alive and will lean back when you touch them. The water loop passes through living fans. The air at dusk is strained through a million wings. Within a week, your skin will host a resident population of grazers that the locals will never mention to you, because to them it is not a fact worth mentioning — it is simply what *being there* means. Four hundred times your weight in animal life shares every room you enter. You will be colonized, gently and completely, by sound, by wetness, by microbes, and by a timescale that makes your longest plans look like a sneeze.

The good news: humans thrive in Sinter. Compatibility is proven. The bad news: your body, your gear, and your instincts were all calibrated for a world with plants, silence, and dead walls. Prepare accordingly.

## Medical Preparation

**Get allergy-tested before you commit.** This is the single most important item on this list. Sinter is made of exoskeleton. The cloth is spun chitin, the food is chitin, the grit that rains at dusk is chitin, the dust is chitin. If you have a shellfish allergy, a dust-mite allergy, or chitin-sensitive asthma, you must know it *before* you arrive, not during your first sift. Bring antihistamines regardless; bring epinephrine if you have any history at all. If the tests come back bad, seriously consider not going. There is no antihistamine strong enough to make a chitin world safe for the chitin-allergic.

**Train your skin, then protect it.** Arrive with intact skin — no open wounds, no fresh tattoos, no healing blisters. In Sinter, a scrape is a vacant lot, and the city develops vacant lots overnight. Trim your nails short (you'll understand why below). Bring an occlusive wound kit: liquid bandage, hydrocolloid dressings, antiseptic. If you get cut, seal it immediately and keep it sealed. Do not let the reef lease your injuries.

**Expect the Itch.** Sometime around day two or three, you will begin to itch — a low, distributed, unscratchable itch, worst at night. This is your skin being audited. The films test you first; then the grazers arrive to keep you, the way they keep every large animal in the city, and around day six or seven the itch simply stops. Do not fight this process. Do not scrub, do not soak in antiseptic, do not scratch yourself raw. Scratching tears skin, torn skin gets colonized by worse things, and you'll reset the whole negotiation. Take an antihistamine, keep your nails short, and wait. The day you stop itching, you have been — there is no other word for it — *taken in*. Locals will not congratulate you. It would not occur to them. But they will notice you stopped complaining, and something in their manner toward you will settle.

**Anticipate the gut week.** Everything you'll eat sits above a microbial food web your ancestors never met. Expect several days of digestive negotiation. Bring oral rehydration salts, bismuth, and emergency loperamide, but understand that the cure is time, not chemistry. Your antibiotics from home may do nothing at all against Sinter's microbiology — your real defenses are intact skin, cooked food, and the local healers, who have been managing foreign bodies for a thousand years. Find one early. Be polite. Pay well.

**Supplements for long stays.** Nothing green grows here, ever. Vitamin C comes only from fresh meat, milk, and eggs — adequate for locals, marginal for you. Bring a multivitamin and vitamin C for any stay over a month. Bring fiber. Your gut was built by plants and will miss them in ways you'll feel within ten days.

**Acclimatize to heat and wet.** Sinter is warm, and everything is wet — the walls seep, the eaves tick, the mist condenses on you, and after a day you genuinely cannot tell sweat from condensation. Two weeks of sauna or hot-humid exercise before departure will save you a miserable first week. Bring antifungal powder and use it daily, especially on your feet. Trench foot and fungal infections are the quiet killers of Sinter itineraries. Waterproof boots with aggressive tread, synthetic socks, and dry them every night.

**Prepare your ears.** This is not metaphor. Sinter is loud in layers — engine thrum you feel in your teeth, the rasp of ten thousand grazers like surf that never breaks, mason-click through the walls, tram bells, and the fan-choirs whistling whole avenues in slow chord. Bring foam earplugs and a tapering schedule: full plugs nights one through four, one plug for a few nights after, none by the end of week two. You are not escaping the sound; you are titrating into it. The goal is fluency — the point where the chord is your silence, and its absence wakes you bolt upright. Because absence is the alarm:

**Practice the silence drill until it's reflex.** If a street's sound thins — clicking stops, rasp dies — you do not investigate. You do not linger to finish your sentence. You walk fast toward noise and find a warden, and if the quiet spreads, you run. Silence means a dying reef. It is the only alarm bell Sinter has, and it rings by subtraction. Every local learned this before letters. Learn it before arrival.

## Mental Preparation

**The absence of green is a physiological event.** You think you understand "no plants." You do not. No grass, no moss, no weed in a crack, no leaf, no herb, no wooden beam, no cotton thread. Rust-red, bone-white, sulfur-yellow, wet gunmetal — under a completely ordinary blue sky with a completely ordinary sun, which somehow makes it worse, because the sky keeps promising a normal world and the ground keeps declining. Travelers report a specific grief arriving around day three or four — a chromatic homesickness. Prepare for it the way you'd prepare for altitude: acknowledge it's coming, don't panic when it arrives, let it pass through.

A warning attached to this: your phone wallpaper is probably a forest. In Sinter, that image is an object of power. Green exists there only as verdigris — ground, traded, *locked away*, taught to children as the color of sickness in metal. Your photograph of a meadow may draw a crowd. A child may be brought to look at it. Decide in advance how you feel about being a traveling shrine to a color, and never, ever flash it carelessly. And leave your green clothing at home — wearing it isn't offensive, exactly, but it's like walking into a cathedral dressed in relics. People will stare, trying to decide whether you're holy or ill.

**Desensitize to swarm.** If insects on your skin unsettle you, begin exposure work now: aquarium touch tanks with giant isopods (they are almost exactly stone-lice), a bat emergence at dusk (the closest thing on most worlds to the sift), hot-spring terraces for the palette and the sulfur smell. Surfaces in Sinter move when you stare at them for ten seconds. That is not a warning about dangerous surfaces. That is all surfaces. You will never be the majority in any room, never out of sight of thousands of lives. If you need to be alone to feel safe, Sinter will grind you down in a week. If you can learn to feel *accompanied* instead, it will feel like the warmest place you've ever been.

**Recalibrate your sense of time.** The reef advances a fingernail's width per year. Leases are written in generations. The city has been the same size for a thousand years. Your deadlines, your itinerary, your two-week window — hold them loosely. If your exit from this universe depends on anyone in Sinter signing anything, building anything, or approving anything, redesign your exit. The only clock that matters in Sinter is yours.

## Packing

**The governing principle: anything organic you bring is food.** The films eat rock, effluent, and chemistry; the grazers eat the films; everything eats, eventually, everything. Your cotton shirts, paper notebook, leather journal, wooden toothbrush, wool socks — all of it will be assessed, colonized, and consumed on a timescale of weeks. Pack as if for a slow acid bath:

- **Synthetics only.** Nylon, polyester, silicone, sealed technical fabrics. Quick-dry everything — nothing ever fully dries, so "quick" is relative.
- **A stone-paper notebook and mechanical pencil.** Stone paper — calcium carbonate sheets, no wood pulp — is nearly inedible, waterproof, and thematically perfect. Locals will appreciate the joke without smiling. Your tree-paper journal will be read by lice before your friends ever see it.
- **Waterproof everything electronic, or leave it home.** Warm, wet, sulfur-touched air corrodes contacts and condenses inside casings. IP68 minimum, dry bags, silica gel packets, and accept losses. Power banks and a small solar panel (the sun is ordinary and strong until the afternoon cloud cap).
- **Polarized sunglasses and a brimmed hat with a strap.** The glare off wet lime and wet chitin is hard-edged and leafless until the afternoon cap closes over. The strap is for the rim.
- **A light veil or mask for dusk.** The sift is millions of moths overhead and a soft, constant rain of cast plates and shell grit. It's magnificent. Watch it the way you'd watch a ticker-tape parade from below, not with your mouth open.
- **All personal consumables.** Menstrual supplies, contact lens solution (or better, glasses — grit and mist make contacts miserable), medications, specific toiletries. Nothing analogous exists. There is no pharmacy for your kind.

**Gifts and trade goods.** Bring what Sinter has never had, sealed against what Sinter does to things. Honey is the perfect gift: it keeps forever, weighs little, and is the distilled biography of a meadow — flowers, which have never existed there. Present it sealed and expect to explain it slowly. Distilled spirits, refined sugar, spices, sealed wooden objects, silk — all unimaginable, all safe if processed and sealed. Copper is a special case: it corrodes into verdigris, the locked-away pigment, so bright copper goods are effectively unrefined luxury and may attract regulation. Ask a warden before displaying any.

**What you must not bring.** No seeds. No bulbs, no cuttings, no hopeful potted herb as a hostess gift. A leaf that wants to happen is the single most obscene and dangerous object you could carry through the gate — biosecurity catastrophe and cultural blasphemy in one terracotta pot. This is not a gray area.

## Arrival and the First Week

However your route delivers you, the approach is the same: a pale tableland shouldering out of a warm cloud sea, looking from two hundred kilometers like weather that decided to keep its shape. Then the rim, and the city strung along it, dripping.

Expect to feel briefly ill — heat, wet, noise, strange water, stranger protein. The locals likely have a name for the visitor's adjustment; whatever it is, budget three to five days for it and schedule nothing. Drink the water — it's condensed fog, clean by any standard, merely mineral and alive in ways your gut will need to meet. If you're cautious, ask a healer or warden for the visitor's protocol rather than secretly boiling everything; boiling their sacred loop in your room is technically fine and spiritually clumsy.

Learn your street's rain-hour on day one. Certain streets receive their rain at the same hour daily and are named for it; "meet me after Third-Rain" is a complete address. Learn the sift as your dusk. Learn to hear the fan-avenues: when a whole street changes key, the weather is changing, and after a fortnight you'll hear a front arrive before you feel it on your skin.

Hire a guide, and hire a sweeper if you can. Sweepers are out at dawn collecting shell-fall for the lime kilns; they see every street at its most honest and know its mood. Pay in trade goods and attention.

At the rim, respect the edge. The updraft there unpicks waterfalls — carries them apart into mist before they land. It can take your hat, your scarf, your balance. Stand back. If you drop something over, don't mourn it; it isn't gone. It comes back as breath, and a fan drinks it. This is the first thing local children learn. Let it be one of your first things too.

## The Rules

- **The wall is not yours. You keep it for the reef.** Don't carve, don't scrape, don't lean bare-skinned on warm crusts for long. Walls are leased; grazing rights are contracts; that crust is somebody's livelihood and somebody's ancestor both.
- **Never kill a fan on a water street.** The fog-fans are the waterworks and the only shade. Killing one is poisoning a well and felling a sacred tree simultaneously, in a culture with no word for tree. Just don't touch the fans at all.
- **Quiet street → warden. At a run.** Already covered. Covered again.
- **Don't sign anything long.** Leases run in generations; you have no grandchildren here and a good landlord will tell you so. Short-cycle traveler arrangements exist. They cost more and mean less, like everything temporary in that city.
- **Don't mention the itch.** It's not mentioned. Complaining about it marks you as not-yet-taken-in; enduring it quietly is your naturalization ceremony.
- **At a Heart, behave as you would in a cathedral.** The condensary towers are the oldest structures in the city, never cut back, filled with warm rain that has never been weather. Go in your first week, while the scale can still knock you sideways.
- **Yield to the herds, respect the click-sticks.** Reef-cattle are placid and ox-sized, driven along rooftop ramps by children who are working, not playing. Mass is mass. Step aside.
- **Don't ask for anything green to eat.** Not offensive — just impossible, and faintly uncanny, like asking a fishmonger for clouds.

## Eating and Drinking

Start with rustbread — baked, cultured film, the safest entry point, dense and iron-dark and unlike anything with a grain in it. Move outward: crackling lice-shell (bar food, shrimp-adjacent, excellent), moth-tower eggs, reef-meat, blood-broth, milk and milk-wine from the cattle. Everything is set, never planted — seeded onto a surface and kept — and the vat-houses of the underflow steam with it.

Drip-spirit: distilled in the sumps from, in the text's own words, who-knows-what. Treat it exactly as you'd treat moonshine anywhere — a reputable house, a small glass, a second glass only if the first treated you well. At noon in the hot foundry districts, eat with one eye on the sky: the kites are up on the thermals, indifferent to you, extremely interested in what you're carrying.

## What Not to Miss

- **The sift, from the rim, at dusk.** Millions of wings rising off the towers, the fan-avenues changing key as the evening wind comes round, the cattle bells descending from the roofs, grit ticking softly on every surface. This is the city's daily masterpiece. Be at the rim for it at least once, mouth closed, eyes open.
- **A Slow One, and then the long maps.** Walk the rim to the nearest walking reef — a hill with somewhere to be, centuries old, moving at the pace of erosion. Then ask a warden to show you the long maps: where it stood the day you arrived. The difference will be less than a handspan. Date your visit by it. This is how the city will remember you — not your name, but where the hill was when you came.
- **A rain-schedule street at its hour.** Stand in a street that receives its rain on schedule, in a city that has no rivers. Then check whether the talus docks below are in a drowned era or a given-back one before planning any descent on the cliff-lifts.
- **The underflow, once, with a guide.** The half-alive pipes, the husbanded eels, the faint rot-light on the oldest cisterns. Go down, see it, come back up. Warm wet low places pool heavy air, and the pipes bite the way plumbing bites — impersonally, and only when ignorant hands are on them.
- **A felling, if the century offers you one.** Once or twice in a hundred years per district. If the long maps show a declaration near, rearrange your life. It is the only festival Sinter keeps.
- **The lee desert, if the noise breaks you.** A thousand kilometers of bare terrace behind the Shelf, dead as the moon — the only silence within reach, made by the reef drinking the wind first. Go for a day. You will find that silence now sounds like a dying street, and you will walk back toward the chord with something like relief. That's how you know the city has you.

## Coming Home

Your luggage will come home alive. Decide before you open it what you owe both worlds: sterilize thoroughly, release nothing. A microbial web that eats rock and outcompetes everything at the bottom of its food chain is not a souvenir; it's an invasion waiting for a damp corner. Incinerate or quarantine what you can't clean.

Your skin's keepers will die in your dry home air within a fortnight whatever you do. Expect to miss them, the way you'd miss a cat that slept on your chest. Your skin will feel unkept for a while — like a district hosed to brick.

Record the sift before you leave. Record the fan-choir, the rasp, the ticking gutters. You will sleep to them for months, because silence will have become your bad omen too, and your own world's nights are nothing but.

And the first green thing you see on return — a lawn, a pine, moss in a sidewalk crack — will hit you like a key change in a fan-avenue. You'll stand there longer than the moment warrants. Let yourself. You now know why they lock the verdigris away.

*Cut slow.* It applies to travelers too.
