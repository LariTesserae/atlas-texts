# Preparing for the Sea on the Roof of the World

## First, understand what kind of place this is

This is not a wilderness in the usual sense. Wildernesses are alive with accident — weather, decay, chance. This place is a *held note*. It is an artifact that has outlived its makers: eleven thousand kilometers of near-freezing fresh water, filtered to optical emptiness by machines that no one tends, balanced so precisely that the waterline has not moved off an etched mark in living memory. You are not visiting an ecosystem. You are visiting a *condition*.

That has two practical consequences. First: nothing here will rescue you, because nothing here responds to you. There is no weather to wait out, no seasons to plan around, no locals, no rescue service, no random event that might break your way. Second: everything you do will be visible forever. Nothing rots. Nothing erodes. A dropped tool stays bright on the bottom for geological time. Your mistakes are permanent in a way that is true nowhere else.

Prepare accordingly.

## The altitude comes first, before the water

You will feel the plateau before you see the sea: thin, cold, still air that does not carry sound. Treat arrival as landing at extreme elevation — plan for the equivalent of five thousand meters or more, because that is what "too thin to carry a shout" implies.

- Acclimatize before you do anything strenuous. If your method of arrival allows a staged ascent, take it. If you arrive directly at the plateau, plan three to seven days of near-idleness at your base camp before any diving, climbing, or heavy boat work. Bring acetazolamide, a pulse oximeter, and supplemental oxygen for the first days and for emergencies.
- Know the signs of pulmonary and cerebral edema, and treat "we should descend" as a decision you make *before* it feels necessary. There is no clinic. The nearest hyperbaric chamber is in another universe.
- Sound will not work as a safety tool above water. Whistles and shouts die within a stone's throw. Establish buddy procedures that are visual or radio-based. Hand signals agreed in advance, written down, practiced.
- Bring a pressure cooker. Water boils far below 100°C up here; ordinary cooking becomes unreliable. Matches and lighters burn poorly in thin oxygen — pressurized canister stoves and ferro rods.
- The UV is ferocious: thin air, high sun, snow, and water glare. Glacier glasses (side-shielded), serious sunscreen, and lip protection. Snow blindness here, alone, days from your shelter, is a life-threatening event.

## The four-minute rule

The surface water holds one to two degrees above freezing. Immersion kills in about four minutes, and the first ninety seconds are the cruelest: cold shock, the involuntary gasp, the heart doing something strange, your muscles abandoning you while your mind is still clear. Hypothermia gets the name, but cold shock does the work.

Therefore:

- **On any craft, at any distance from shore, you wear an insulated immersion survival suit with integrated hood, gloves, and flotation. Always.** Not stowed — worn. This is not clothing; it is your hull. The water is so clear and so calm-looking that you will be tempted to treat a boat deck like a sidewalk. Do not. The rule is absolute because the failure mode is instant.
- Practice self-rescue in the suit before you come: getting back into a small boat from the water, alone, cold, in gloves. If you can't do it in a warm pool, you can't do it here.
- Travel the water in pairs of vessels when possible. A surfacing ridge-grazer can capsize a small boat without ever knowing you existed — they are the size of ships and they do not look up. Your survival suit buys you four minutes times some margin, not a rescue.
- Choose boats that are stable and ice-tolerant. On calm nights, frazil ice forms at the skin; thin ice complicates propellers and rudders. Anchor thoughtfully — the bottom is young black basalt, sharp enough to cut a hand, and it holds anchors poorly on the steep flanks.
- One quiet hazard unique to this place: the rock exhales a micron-thin mineral film onto *every* surface, including your hull, and film is the only pasture in the world. An anchored boat is a slow-growing meal. Check your hull. If a grazer takes an interest, move. You will have days of warning — nothing here is fast except the cold.

## The sound: you cannot prepare, so rehearse

Everything you have read about the chord is true and still understates it. Sound in this water travels four times faster than in air and essentially without loss, and the engines have locked into resonance with each other and with the Rim's white-noise floor. The result is a world-sized chord — three notes over the shallow bottom, five over the deep — that arrives from all directions at once, plus infrasonic grazer voices below the engine band that you feel as a hand pressed flat on your chest from fifty kilometers away.

Prepare like this:

- **Above water**, the thin air muffles the airborne component, but the chord comes up through rock, through boat hulls, through the ground under your tent. Earplugs help with the high content; nothing stops the lows, which are bone-conducted. Bring the best ear defenders you can for sleeping, bring masking audio of your own, bring sleep medication you have tested before. Expect a week of bad sleep. Most people adapt. Some don't, and there is no shame in being one who doesn't — there is only danger in refusing to admit it.
- **Underwater**, abandon any expectation of quiet and any instinct of acoustic direction. The chord has no source; it is the medium. Diver-to-diver audio comms will be swamped — use hardwired comms, tethers, and prearranged tactile signals. Remember that every loud thing you do underwater — a tank banged, a sonar ping, a dropped wrench — is heard by every animal in the basin. Be acoustically polite. You are a guest in an instrument.
- **Near an intake, the pressure of the sound can bruise.** Keep real distance — hundreds of meters minimum, more if you feel it in your chest wall. Headache, nausea, disorientation, or a vibrating vision are your cues to withdraw. Log them.
- **Infrasound does things to human minds.** Unease, awe, the unshakable sense of a presence, tears without cause. When a grazer sounds and you feel it arrive through your sternum, you may feel watched, called, or judged. Know in advance that this is physiology — low-frequency pressure acting on your organs — and also know that knowing this will not entirely protect you from it. Keep a daily log of your moods. It will help you sort the place's effects from your own state, and it will be the most interesting document you bring home.

## The clarity will lie to you

There is nothing in the water — no plankton, no silt, no pollen, no dust. Light passes invisibly; you see only sources and surfaces. This produces specific, predictable failures of human perception:

- **Depth deception.** An arête thirty meters down looks three meters down. The drowned mountains show through the surface like a relief map under glass, sharp as torn metal — and they *are* sharp, and some of them reach nearly to the surface. Charts don't exist. Run a forward-looking depth sounder at all times, move slowly, and treat every visual depth estimate as wrong. The one marked route — the buoy chain running nine hundred kilometers over the trench — is worth using where it serves you.
- **Distance deception.** You can see the bright back of an animal a kilometer away through the water, and engine-lights across six hundred kilometers at night. Your intuition about "near" and "far" will be wrong in both directions for weeks. Trust instruments, log lines, and measured bearings. Never trust your eyes.
- **Vertigo.** Underwater, with no particles to give you a reference, you are not swimming — you are *flying over mountains*. Down looks exactly like up with better scenery. Divers get the swooping, falling sensation here that no other world produces. Always dive with a reference: a shot line, a wall in arm's reach, a measured distance to your buddy. If you feel yourself falling, look at your gauges, hold the line, and wait. It passes.
- **Your lights are lanterns, not torches.** A beam needs something to strike, and here there is nothing. Underwater you will see only lit surfaces and light sources — no beam in transit. Signaling with a light still works (your buddy sees the source), but sweeping a beam to "find" something in open water does not. On the bright side: underwater photography with zero backscatter. The pictures will look fake. They're not.

## If you dive — and you will want to

The whole meaning of the place is underwater: the engines' flanges and crawlers, the drowned ridges, the stair that descends past sight, the white bones on black basalt. So dive, but dive like it's polar altitude diving, because it is:

- Active-heated drysuit, not passive insulation — at one to two degrees, passive buys you minutes of useful work, not hours. Full-face mask, redundant regulators, environmentally sealed against frazil ice (regulator freeze is a real night-and-surface hazard here). Avoid diving when frazil is forming.
- **Altitude decompression is its own discipline.** Standard tables assume sea level. Use a computer with an altitude mode, set it correctly, and cut your profiles conservatively — then cut them again. There is no chamber.
- **Plan your exit before your first dive.** Whatever carries you home — aircraft, portal, stranger means — treat it as a pressure event. Finish all diving at least several days before departure. If your way home involves anything instantaneous, make it a week. This is the easiest way to die here and the most avoidable.
- Intakes *drink the sea*. Approach currents are real even at distance. Learn each platform's layout from afar before closing; stay out of the intake side entirely; the warm outflow parishes — where the glint-fry turn in metallic clouds — are the safe and beautiful side.
- If you meet a grazer: give way, keep kilometers not meters, do not touch. They are slow, patient, and eat stone film; they will not attack you. But their voices at close range, in water, are a pressure event, and a hand on your chest from fifty kilometers implies something you do not want to feel from fifty meters.

## The engines: rules of conduct

They are industrial in the old heavy sense — riveted towers, turbine halls, gantries, penstocks like road tunnels — and they are *live*. Governor-locked, self-repairing, tended by welding crawlers. They hold the sea liquid and empty. Whoever built them is gone.

- Board platforms in pairs, tethered, treating every valve, line, and hatch as pressurized and every crawler as unpredictable heavy machinery. The crawlers repair faults; do not let one decide you are either a fault or material.
- Do not shelter in the stack exhaust. Warm, yes; breathable, unknown. Do not touch governors, controls, or anything that looks like it could be adjusted.
- Do not repair, retune, restart, or "improve" anything. This is not merely caution about your safety. The engines' waste heat is exactly what holds this world above ice, and the chord is exactly what every animal in it orients by. If the pitch ever changed, it would be the first event in millennia, and everything alive in the basin would turn toward it — including, perhaps, toward you. **You do not want to be the event.** This world is finished. Do not become its next chapter.

## Travel, distance, and navigation

The sea is large enough to have its own weather, and it has exactly one: permanent standing gyres that move engine-warmth in fixed loops. Learn the gyres; they are the roads. Everything else about movement is arithmetic and patience.

- Fuel math dominates. Engines stand three to eight hundred kilometers apart; islands are rare; distances are measured in days. Carry double your planned fuel for heat and propulsion, then add margin. There is nothing to burn locally — no wood, no peat, nothing organic on the rock at all. Every calorie of heat arrives with you.
- Navigate by sextant (there is one in the hut, but bring your own), by engine-light bearings at night, and by the buoy chain where it runs. Treat magnetic compasses as advisory only — young basalt, magnetite-plated grazers, and enormous iron engines make nonsense of needles.
- The Rim's permanent cloud ring stands at the horizon in every direction, and that is your one great fixed reference: **it marks the edge of the world, and you do not go toward it.** The whole sea pours over that lip, the fall's bottom has never been seen, and spray rises upward in slow columns through drumming rock. No boat. No aerial survey into the cloud — it is a permanent icing wall with unknown drafts. Accept that the question "what is below" is unanswerable. It is the oldest unanswered question here, and it will outlast you too.
- Water: melt snow for drinking — it is distilled Rim water, the cleanest thing in a clean world. The sea itself tastes faintly of metal; test it before relying on it. Food: bring all of it. The local biochemistry is silicate and mineral film; the glint-fry are not provisions except in true extremity, and probably not even then.

## The hut, the gauge, and the stair

On one island there is a hut with its door open: a table, a sextant, a brass gauge, a mark etched at water level annotated in a script no one reads, and a stair of two hundred precise steps descending into the water and continuing down the flank, out of sight.

Make this your base if you can. Someone human-scaled kept this sea, and the hut is the only place on the roof of the world that was made for a creature like you. But treat it as a borrowed station, not a find:

- Use the instruments; leave them. Photograph and copy the script; add nothing to the wall. Log the gauge daily — it trembles when a grazer surfaces near the island, and learning its baseline is learning your neighborhood. Check the water against the etched mark each morning. It will be at the mark. It is always at the mark. **If it is ever not at the mark, leave. That is the only news this world can print, and it will not be good news.**
- The stair is the single strongest temptation you will face. It was cut to be descended, and it does not end. Set your limit before arrival, in writing, with your companion as witness — a depth, a gas reserve, a turnaround — and know that on site, standing on those steps with the chord coming up through the stone, the limit will feel arbitrary and cowardly. It is neither. Survey by remote means first if you possibly can. Whatever the stair was for, it has waited this long; it will wait for a second expedition.

## The ethics of a finished world

Nothing here decays. That reframes every rule of travel you know. Litter anywhere else is a sin against time; litter here is a *permanent lie* — a bright dropped canister will sit on black basalt looking brand-new for longer than your civilization will last. Pack out everything. Scratch nothing. Take nothing — not a bone, not a rivet, not a step's worth of stone dust. The bones of the grazers lie white on black where they fell, unrotting, and they are not souvenirs; they are the only history this place writes, and it is written at a rate of roughly one word per millennium.

## Your mind

Budget for this as seriously as for fuel. The combination — deafening emptiness, changelessness, spacing, infrasonic voices, the sense of being inside a note struck at the beginning of time — acts on people. Some find it sublime. Some unravel. Most do both, in alternation.

- Do not come alone. Bring someone whose judgment you trust more than your own, and give each other explicit authority to call the expedition over.
- Keep a strict routine and a dated log. In a world where nothing happens, the calendar is yours to maintain or lose, and losing it is the first symptom.
- Watch each other for the specific obsessions this place breeds: the stair, the Rim, the urge to touch an engine, the urge to *find out where the chord goes*. And watch for the gentlest, most dangerous thought of all — the one that arrives around week three, in the open-doored hut, beside the working gauge: *someone should keep this sea, and it could be me.* The hut invites it. That may be what the hut is for. Decide before you arrive whether you are the kind of person who answers open doors, and if you are, bring someone who will close it behind you.
- Hard triggers for immediate departure, agreed in advance: the chord changes pitch; the water leaves the mark; the crawlers change their behavior toward you; either of you stops sleeping for a fortnight; either of you proposes, on site, lowering a rule you set at home.

## The kit, compressed

Polar immersion suits worn on the water, always. Heated drysuits, sealed regs, hardline comms, altitude-rated computers if diving. Oxygen, pulse oximeter, altitude and frostbite pharmacology. Expedition shelter and bags even if you plan for the hut. Pressurized stoves, ferro rods, pressure cooker, all food, snow-melting capacity. Glacier glasses, sunscreen, gloves you never take off on the rock. Radios, mirrors, flares, a sextant, paper charts you draw yourself, forward sonar, a log line. Ear defenders, masking audio, sleep aids, books, music — your own sound, to remember what yours is. Cameras and a journal. Double fuel, then margin. And whatever carries you home, kept near, fueled, above the waterline, never dependent on anything that floats.

## What it will actually be like

You will spend the first days dizzy, sunburned, sleeping badly, feeling the chord in your sternum and mistaking your own heartbeat for an engine. You will see no animal for a week and hear all of them constantly — the sea empty of neighbors and full of company. Snow will fall straight down through still air, the only soft sound in the world, hissing faintly where it strikes the water, while underneath the surface a second snow of frazil ice falls inward and melts in the four-degree dark. A grazer will cross your entire field of view over three days, and you will find yourself scheduling your mornings around its progress. At night the engine-lights will come on across six hundred kilometers of empty water, and the glint-fry will turn in their warm parishes, and somewhere a single low note will arrive through the bones of every living thing in the basin — answered, hours later, from beyond the curve of the world.

And then, eventually, you will come home, and home will be the strange place: loud with decay, cluttered, unfinished, everything happening at once and nothing held. You will catch yourself listening for the chord in quiet rooms. You will understand that the sea did not notice you, does not miss you, and is exactly as you left it, down to the millimeter of its waterline — and that this, somehow, is the thing you will miss most.

Go prepared. Go slowly. Leave nothing, change nothing, and let the note be held by whatever struck it.
