## Preparing for the Chordlands

### First, understand what you are visiting

Most places, you prepare for the terrain. Here the terrain is finished — erosion's final answer, stone that cannot be further worn — and what you actually prepare for is the *air*. The country's danger is invisible. The sky above it is honest, gold, equatorial, and it will not warn you of anything. Every warning here is audible: storms arrive as chord-changes, hazards announce themselves as muffled notes, safety is a lull. You are used to watching weather. In the Chordlands you will *hear* it, and your ears are simultaneously your instrument panel and your most vulnerable organ. Everything in your preparation follows from that.

### Your ears

This is not a place where hearing protection is advisable. It is a place where hearing protection is the difference between a journey and an injury.

- Bring plugs in quantity — foam, silicone, custom-molded if you can get them — plus a pair of over-ear defenders for the noon white chord. But also plan to adopt the local standard within your first day: waxed moss on a cord around the neck. The cord is the point. You will take plugs out during lulls to speak, and a plug that lives in your pack is a plug you will lose.
- Rule one: **plugs live on your neck, not in your pack.** Rule two: before noon, you check your companion's plugs and they check yours. Nobody travels unplugged outside a lull.
- Learn to fear the *coughs*. When fog plugs a vent, pressure builds until it bursts free — a bass-drum concussion that staggers a person at half a mile. The practical sign: a tower whose note has gone muffled and laboring in thick fog is a tower about to cough. If a familiar voice of the land chokes, move away from that face, put in both plugs, and open your mouth to equalize the pressure wave.
- Expect the sound to get *inside* you. The bass drone lives in your sternum and the roots of your teeth. Strong low-frequency sound causes nausea, dizziness, tooth-ache, chest pressure, and a strange, sourceless dread. Know this in advance: that dread is physiology, not prophecy. When it comes, retreat to the lee face of a tower — the quiet side, bare bone, no moss — and rest until it passes. The lee is the country's refuge, always.
- In the sudden silences your ears will ring. That's normal here. If they're still ringing a week after you've left the country, you were careless. Don't be careless.

### Water: the country drinks on a clock

There are no rivers. Let that restructure your thinking. You cannot follow water downhill, because there is no downhill water. There is a *schedule* instead:

- **Morning:** fog climbs the rim and pours between the towers. Moss and vine-veils drip. Collect from the windward faces.
- **Noon:** the chord-change, the coughing, then ten minutes of near-vertical rain. Ten minutes is all. Every container you own should be open and deployed in under a minute — practice this before you arrive. A fast tarp is worth more than a second water skin.
- **Afternoon:** everything drips; the resurrection moss blazes emerald; the vine-veils are heavy. Best harvesting of the day.
- **Dusk and night:** the fog drains. Dry.

The reliable sources are the cistern-pools in basins on the tower-tops, which hold for days. Get the cistern map from the first people you meet — a hermit-tuner knows every pool within hearing of his cluster, and this knowledge is worth more than any trade good you carry. The hour-pools on the chasm floors are a gift, never a plan. Carry two days' water minimum — double what you'd carry in a green country — and a mesh or oiled-cloth fog sheet to angle into the wind overnight.

And memorize the country's first law, because it is a survival rule: **the green follows the loud.** Sound shakes the fog's droplets together until they fall. If you are ever desperate for water, climb toward the sound, to the windward face of the loudest tower, where the moss fur grows an arm thick. Follow the loud and you will find the drip.

### The vertical problem

Stand at the rim, look into a chasm whose floor you cannot see, and be honest with yourself about what your legs do. Much of this country is empty air — gulfs you could lose a cathedral in — and there is no shame in staying rim-side. Many do.

If you go down and in: harness, thirty or forty meters of line, slings, gloves, and soft-soled boots. Soft soles matter twice — they let you feel the stone's buzz, and they don't chip the handhold stairways that someone spent years cutting. Cross the rope bridges one at a time, clipped in, and wait out gusts rather than arguing with them. Do not cross in the white chord; swaying deaf over a void is for people born to it.

Remember that a singing tower is a *vibrating* tower. Grip fatigue on a full-voiced spire comes fast and strange. The fat keeps are the friendly climbs; the thin blades shriek at the top of the range and should be admired from a distance. And watch for the gut-strings stretched across chasm-mouths — nearly invisible, humming with signal-notes, someone's message line. Treat them like live wires: never pluck, never cut, look before you throw a rope.

One more: stay off the chasm floors at noon. The whole land drinks at once, hour-pools form fast, and the dune-faces at the chasm mouths boom and slide under storm wind. Be on a ledge, catchment deployed, when the key shifts.

### Reading the weather by ear

Your eyes will lie by omission. Retrain yourself on these signs:

- **The key shifts** — a warm storm is minutes away. Deploy catchment, double your plugs, get off anything exposed.
- **Coughing starts** — pressure is clearing somewhere near. Move away from vented faces.
- **The drone rises** — wind is rising. Finish your bridge crossing or don't start it.
- **Silence drops like a cloth** — the wind has veered. Regroup, talk fast while speech works, and expect anything.
- **Bell-crickets ring** — a true lull. Travel quickly, speak, make your decisions.

Plan your days to the land's clock: walk midmorning, shelter and harvest at noon, collect water through the dripping afternoon, find people in the evening lulls, sleep to the night drone with your plugs on their cord.

### Navigation: the map you hear

Towers have notes, and notes carry for days. Your route is a chord chart. Before you leave any camp, fix the notes of where you're going and where you came from; write them down — a tuning fork or pitch pipe lets you log them properly, and your notebook will become half map, half music book. In fog — and there is fog daily, pouring through the chasms like slow rapids — sound is your only sense of place. Triangulate on two known notes, keep a line between members of your party, and drum-tap check-ins through the stone.

Know that **maps here expire**. When the chord-seasons turn, towers go mute and silent ones wake, and your auditory landmarks rearrange themselves. If you stay through a season-change, budget days to re-learn your country — and count yourself lucky, because you'll hear notes nobody has heard in years.

If you are lost: stop. Lee side. Water from the moss. Wait for the evening lull and drum a slow distress rhythm on resonant stone — sound carries farther in this country than walking does.

### The people

Two days' travel, one thread of smoke. You must be self-sufficient, and you must also understand that the sparse families and hermit-tuners are your only real safety net. Approach dwellings by drumming — your voice will not arrive before you do — and wait for an answering rhythm.

Learn thirty signs before you come: *water, storm, bridge, trade, friend, help, food, sleep, name, thank you.* Bring trade goods suited to a country of stone, wood, fiber, bone, and hide: metal above all. Worked copper or bronze — the cold-hammered bell is their heirloom prestige object — plus needles, fine tools, hardwood billets for tuning wedges, blocks of wax, strong cord. A set of tuning forks will make you a tuner-friend for life.

Three rules of respect:

1. **Never touch the work.** Not the hardwood wedges, not the gut-strings, not the ancient petrified plugs or baffle-walls. Tuning a tower is a life's work, and the old stone is load-bearing in ways you cannot hear until it's too late.
2. **At the echo-wells, keep your voice low and leave a small offering.** Speak only what you would be glad to hear again in thirty years. The locals are matter-of-fact about shouts coming home changed, and you should be too. If a voice comes *out* of a well while you're near, remember the words and repeat them at the next fire — it may be a message arriving.
3. **If invited to sing the tower up, sing.** Pitching your voice against the drone is the country's one true hospitality. You will be bad at it. That is fine. Everyone is, at first.

And if an ancient plug fails in a storm and a tower wakes after centuries of silence — drop your plans and walk to it with everyone else. It is the rarest event the country offers.

### The kit

- Plugs in quantity, wax, moss, neck cords; over-ear defenders
- Two days' water capacity, fog sheet, funnel, fast-deploy tarp
- Harness, line, slings, gloves, soft boots, warm layer for the cold rim nights, sun protection for the honest equatorial light
- Slate and chalk, a hand drum, notebook and pencil, tuning forks
- Trade goods and food for the long gaps between hearths
- Small offerings: beads, carved wood, a written song

Leave behind anything that relies on being heard, any pet, and any habit of making noise for fun. Sound here is landscape and law.

### When to go

Come during the **Long Note** — the weeks-long stable chord when the scattered families converge on the Bowl for the year's one market, the marriages, the news. You will find guides, trade, teachers, and the country at its most welcoming. Avoid the **Fretful Weeks**, when the winds fight and the land is arrhythmic percussion; nothing is reliable, not even the notes. A chord-season is a wonder for a return visit.

And time your arrival for the grey hour before dawn, at the rim. Let your first experience be the pre-dawn Quiet — the only true silence, under ordinary good stars — and then watch the sun strike the lowlands and the whole fossil city wake and begin to play. That ordering matters. It is the country's true introduction, and it only happens once per visit.

### What it does to your head

Prepare for contrast. Deafening totality, then a silence so complete you hear water ticking off stone and a single wingbeat. The lulls unsettle more travelers than the chord does. Prepare for scale — hours spent watching one windray ride the air-rivers because nothing else moves. Prepare for loneliness, and bring either a companion or a good relationship with your own.

And prepare for afterward. When you leave, ordinary silence will feel wrong — empty, unoccupied, a landscape with its weather missing. You will catch yourself listening for a drone that isn't there. This is normal. The mountains are gone and their weather never found out; once you've stood inside that argument, some part of you keeps listening for it. Pack for the sky, respect the stone, keep your plugs on their cord — and choose carefully what you say to the wells.
