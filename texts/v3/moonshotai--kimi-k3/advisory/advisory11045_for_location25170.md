# Preparing for the Bench Above the Notch

## First, understand what you're walking into

Everything about this place — the violet sky at noon, the rime that skips liquid on its way out of the world, the light that arrives unsoftened — tells you the same thing: **there is almost no air**. Treat the bench as the equivalent of a 6,000–7,500 meter col on Earth, possibly higher. The closest terrestrial analogs are the high Atacama, the Antarctic Dry Valleys, the dead zone above the last Sherpa villages — but those places teem compared to this. Here there is exactly one living thing, and it is glass, and it grows a hair's breadth a year.

If you arrive by multiverse means rather than on foot, verify the air before you commit: pressure, oxygen fraction, contaminants. A violet noon sky means thin, but thin is not the same as breathable. Spend your first hour measuring, not sightseeing — how long is the day, how fast does the shadow walk, does your watch agree with the pool's slow tick. The place runs on a schedule. Learn the schedule before you build your plans on it.

Assume no rescue exists. Nothing with wings crosses this sky, and no one is coming.

## Get the body ready, then get the blood ready

Two separate preparations:

**Conditioning.** Months of loaded hill work, stairs, long days on your feet. The final climb through the notch is done "with your breath counted out" — that is literal instruction, not poetry. You will rest-step. You will pressure-breathe. You will count.

**Acclimatization.** If the route allows, come up over a week or more, sleeping at staged elevations below the ridge. If you can only arrive directly at altitude, keep the first visit short, carry supplemental oxygen for the notch crossing, and know that your margin is thin and getting thinner every hour you stay. Carry a pulse oximeter and check it morning and evening. Carry acetazolamide; carry emergency dexamethasone and nifedipine and know what they're for. Set turnaround rules before you leave and obey them when your altitude-dulled mind suggests negotiating: any headache that worsens, any ataxia, any cough with foam — down, through the notch, immediately. Note that even retreat requires a few steps *up* to the notch first. At this altitude, a few steps up is a real cost. Keep that reserve.

## Time the door

The notch is the only entrance, and it is a wind instrument — the gap was quarried by wind, and wind remembers the way through. Cross it in the morning calm. By afternoon the cloud-breath pours through it in slow rivers, the world turns to milk, and a blade ridge with a long drop on one side is no place to be feeling for holds. Expect venturi gusts through the gap at any hour; tether your gloves, cinch your hood, keep nothing loose.

If fog seals the notch while you're on the bench: **stay put**. The white pebble line from notch to headwall was laid by the old visitors for exactly these milk-world hours — it is infrastructure, and it still works — but the better move is to wait. The fog always withdraws the same afternoon, leaving everything rinsed. Carry an emergency bothy bag or bivy so that waiting is survivable even at the rim in wind.

## Dress for two weathers in one hour

The bench runs a temperature swing of 30–40 degrees across a single moving line, and the line is not the sunset — **the freeze comes at the shadow's touch.** You will be stripping layers in still, blazing noon air and fighting for your shell ninety minutes later as the shadow walks toward you cell by cell. Build a system you can change fast:

- Full layer stack: wicking base, active insulation, heavy parka, wind shell. The afternoon fog beads everything, then withdraws — dry your shell before the shadow arrives or you'll wear ice armor.
- Two pairs of gloves, liner and shell, tethered. Losing a glove here means frostbite and a piece of litter that outlasts you.
- **Two pairs of glacier goggles, Category 4, side-shielded**, plus clear lenses for fog hours. The light has weight; the snowfield hurts; at noon the rising vapor doubles the glare and it comes at you from below as well as above. Snow blindness on this bench, with that notch as your only exit, is a life-threatening emergency, not an inconvenience. The spare pair is not optional.
- Full skin coverage, nose guard, high-SPF on everything exposed including under the chin. The bloom melts pits in frost with thumbnail lenses. Your skin is a softer target.
- Stiff, insulated boots with enough edge precision to walk stone rims — because you will walk the rims all day — plus trekking poles with the discipline to plant tips on stone, never in the cells.

## Water: bring every drop

Water here keeps strict hours and none of them are yours. The rime vanishes to air by midmorning. The noon melt beads appear one at a time, wink, and dry. The pool in the snowfield's lee is the size of a cupped hand, lidded at night, open a few hours — it is a clock and perhaps a shrine, and it belongs to the bench. Do not drink from it. Do not touch it.

Carry three to four liters, insulated, one of them a thermos. Sleep with your bottles. Plan food that needs little water, and accept that at this pressure your boil is tepid and your rehydrated meals stay chewy — bring a stove that works in cold and thin air (liquid-feed canister or white gas), because no fuel has ever existed on this bench and you must not be its first hearth. The snowfield is your emergency reserve only: melt from its edge, filter, take little, and know that every scoop scars a thing that has outlasted every summer anyone knows of.

## Walk like a guest in an archive

The cold here is an archivist. Your footprints in the silt will remain legible for years. This is not a reason to stay home; it is a reason to move correctly, and the correct movement is also the practical one:

**Walk rim to rim.** The cell centers are soft flour-fine silt that puffs dust in the dry hours and stands in brittle needle-ice fur in the cold ones, collapsing underfoot like meringue — bad footing regardless. But the centers are also filmed with the bloom's taut skin, and the sheltered faces of the rim-stones are sheeted with it, and the lee of the old wall is hung with threads of it that took centuries to lay down. The rims are the durable path, the intended path, the kind path. Pole tips on stone. Kneel on a pad, not on a cell.

**Take nothing.** The flakes in the knapping hollow are as sharp as the hour they were struck — they will cut you to the tendon if you rummage, so look with your eyes — but more than that, they are someone's afternoon. A preform rests half-finished where a person set it down in good light, meaning to come back. You will want to finish it. You will want to pocket one black glittering flake. Do neither. Leave it in its light.

**Change nothing.** The cairns are nine, spaced like teeth — a count, not a suggestion. Add no tenth. The quartz pebbles are a trail for whiteout hours, not souvenirs. The rings themselves are sorted with a patience that is almost intention; a single displaced rim-stone undoes centuries of frost work. The windbreak against the headwall still works, and sitting in it at dusk, facing the field and the long west, is arguably what it is *for* — you may sit. Add no stone, move no stone, and rise gently.

## The daily round, as your itinerary

If you can manage one night — and you should try, because half the bench's speech happens after dark — here is the shape of it:

- **Midmorning arrival.** Cross the notch in the calm. The dawn's rime feathers are already subliming off the lee of every stone; you'll catch the last of them vanishing, which is worth seeing once.
- **Noon.** The lensing hour. Every bead focuses the brutal light into the frost and the whole field breathes cold vapor — the ground smoking gently, glare doubled, shadows black as drilled holes. Watch from a rim, goggles on. This is the worst hour for your eyes and the best hour for understanding what the bloom *is*.
- **Early afternoon.** The cloud-breath. Fog pours through the notch in slow rivers and the world turns to milk. Be near your shelter and the quartz line. Everything beads, the bloom threads with moisture, the trail-pebbles shine — then it all withdraws, rinsed. Dry your gear now.
- **Low sun.** Go to the pecked boulder above the windbreak. All day the carved rings are only dimples, but the bloom grows dense and amber in worked stone, and at evening angle the whole drawing warms to dark gold and appears — the underdrawing of the field, the old people's answer to the mountain's geometry. You don't need to solve it. Just be there when it shows.
- **The shadow walk.** When the straight-edged shadow steps out from the headwall side, dress *before* it reaches you — not at sunset, not when you feel cold. The freeze follows the line within moments.
- **The ticking.** Then the bench speaks its dusk speech: thousands of tiny snaps and creaks as bead-shells contract, cell by cell, perhaps half an hour, like the first drops of rain on a roof that never rains. Sit on a rim. Record it if you brought audio — you should bring audio — but mostly listen. **Stay until the ticking stops.** This is the essential minimum of a visit, the thing the place asks of you.
- **Dark.** No lights. Give your eyes forty minutes and the field will give back its tithe: a faint gray-green afterglow rising through the rings, dim as breath on glass, gone by midnight. Above it the stars come down close enough to snag on the cairns. If the world has a moon, plan your visit for its dark phase.
- **Small hours.** Needle ice combs the silt upward, creaking just below hearing; the pool lids itself. You'll hear your own pulse. This is normal. Nothing walks through it — nothing ever does.
- **Dawn.** Stillness so complete the rime grows almost audibly, feather by feather, into the wind. Then sunrise strikes the headwall's crest and the light pours down it like liquid. Have your camera put away by then. Some hours are for watching.
- **Midmorning, out.** Pack as the frost sublimes, police the site twice, count nine cairns, and cross the notch before the afternoon breath.

## The overnight, honestly assessed

A night on the bench means the coldest hours of a very cold place, lying on permafrost, at an altitude where sleep is shallow and your breathing may stutter in the Cheyne-Stokes pattern (know it in advance so it doesn't panic you at 3 a.m.). It is worth it anyway, with the right kit: a bag rated to −25°C or beyond, *two* insulated pads because that ground conducts like the stone it is, a four-season shelter pitched **only** on bare outcrop near the notch or at the snowfield's margin — never on the rings, never on bloom. Guy out everything against venturi gusts. The needle ice will heave and crunch beneath you all night; that is the bench turning in its sleep, not a problem.

If you cannot overnight: visit twice, or stay from midmorning until the ticking stops and descend by headlamp only if your party is strong, the notch calm, and the route wanded in your memory. The bivouac is the honest recommendation.

## The small print

- **Obsidian.** Never kneel in the knapping hollow, never steady yourself with a bare hand there. Bring a real wound kit and closure strips; flake cuts are clean, deep, and immediate.
- **Dust.** Flour-fine, in everything. Dry bags and zip pouches for electronics; a buff for the dry hours; saline and ointment for the nosebleeds the dry air will give you.
- **The rim.** The bench is a slow conveyor delivering itself over its own edge; the outermost cells will go over in coming centuries, stone by stone. Admire the drop from a stride back. Do not use the cairns as anchors — they are ancient, frost-heaved, and half pattern themselves.
- **The bloom.** Don't eat it, don't rub your eyes after touching it, don't pocket a bead. It is the only life in a world of mineral, its chemistry is unknown, and the oldest crusts are centuries of layered glass. Staring into the glints is like staring into tiny suns — the goggles apply here too.
- **Waste.** Everything packs out, everything, including the obvious and the freeze-dried. Pee on one chosen bare stone near the notch, away from bloom and pool. The archivist keeps even that; keep your entries minimal.
- **Instruments.** Pencil, not pen — ink freezes. A watch, so you can start keeping the bench's hours: first melt-bead, fog arrival, shadow line, ticking start and stop, afterglow fade. You will end the visit as its clock-watcher, and your notebook will be the only record that leaves.
- **Your mind.** The silence here is not empty, but it has no wings in it — no bird, no rustle, no track, nothing that moves except light, fog, shadow, and glass. Some travelers find this ecstatic. Some find the deep time casually displayed — agate layers of centuries, a blade set down one afternoon by someone who never returned — to be more altitude than their meaning-making can breathe. If it hits you, sit on a rim until it passes. It passes into something good.

## What not to bring

Fire. Drones. Speakers. Pets. Bright white light after dark. More than two companions — the bench is a few hundred paces across, and a crowd would trample in an hour what frost spent millennia sorting. Any stay longer than two nights; altitude degrades you daily and your water is on your back. Any expectation of wildlife. Any plan to take anything home except recordings, notes, and the memory.

## Leaving

Do a final slow lap on the rims. Check the ground where you slept, where you cooked, where you sat — the cold forgets nothing, and the only acceptable trace is faint prints on stone that the wind will scrub in a season. At the notch, look back once: the nine cairns against the west, the quartz line shining faintly, the glittering hollow holding its half-finished blade in the good light. Then climb through, counting your breath, and be below the ridge before the fog rises.

The bench will not remember you the way it remembers — it barely registers a day. But you will have heard it speak twice, once in vapor and once in ticks, and that is the whole of what a visitor may honestly take.
