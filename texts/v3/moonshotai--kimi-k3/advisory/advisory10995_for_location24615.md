# Preparing for the Lower Reservoir

## First, understand what you are visiting

This is not a ruin and it is not wilderness. It is a machine that is currently running. The Lower Reservoir is working infrastructure — flood buffer, pumped-storage basin, cooling sink — and the people who own it experience it as a gauge reading and a power price on a screen four hundred meters above your head. Nothing down there knows you exist. The fish will scatter from the vibration of your footsteps; the pumps will not notice you at all. That indifference is the single most important thing to pack for. There are no staff down there, no patrols, no rescue. There is an elevator, a schedule, and you.

The good news: nothing here requires heroics. The dangers are cold, moving water, and machinery — the unglamorous kind that kill ordinary people through ordinary inattention. Prepare for those, and what you get in return is one of the strangest sights available to a human being: a lit, loud, silvered, numbered sea that has never once been weather, holding itself in its first decade like a tool that hasn't yet learned what it is.

## Permission

The stencil on the door — NO DIVING WITHOUT PERMIT — is your first piece of intelligence. It means two things: diving is possible, and a process exists. Do not try to sneak in. This is critical power-grid infrastructure; the owners are wary by profession, and honestly, you want them to know you're down there anyway.

Write to the operating utility. Your best angle is the accidental ecosystem: eleven years old, unplanned, unstudied, gin-clear, and running a complete food chain in four hundred kilometers of bay. Marine biologists would trade years of their lives for access. If you can attach yourself to a research proposal — or simply write a patient, credible letter about the eels — you have a real chance. The eels are the story that opens doors: thousands of silver eels pressing against the outlet screens each autumn, trying to reach an ocean they can't possibly know about. That is a conservation problem someone on the surface should be forced to think about, and "visiting researcher documenting the eel impingement" is a reason to be there that a utility's environmental office can approve.

Failing the dive permit, ask to ride a maintenance inspection. Even the gallery at BAY 000 — the railing, the water going flat and faintly steaming to the horizon, the recession lines of lamps burning in long perspective — is worth the trip on its own.

## Timing: the tide is a price curve

Forget everything you know about tides. This one is set by the electricity market. The rule of the place is simple: **the water rises by day and falls by night** — six vertical meters over roughly eight hours — because cheap nighttime power pumps water out and expensive daytime power lets it back in through the turbines.

Before you descend, ask the control room for the pumping and generation schedule, and check the weather *on the surface* — weather comes down the sun wells. Rain on the surface means a column of rain ringing the water beneath each well, four hundred meters underground. In autumn it means leaves arriving one at a time, sinking through forty meters of lit water like mail delivered to an empty building. In summer, warm river inflow slides over the cold basin water in a shimmering false surface, and the air-gap vaults fill with actual fog — banks of it, drifting indoors across the waterline. You can be fog-bound in a basement. Plan for it.

Season matters. Autumn gives you the leaves *and* the eels at the screens — the one moment the place shows its teeth as a boundary rather than a world. Summer gives you the fog and the shimmering layer. There is no bad season; the cold holds at seven degrees regardless.

## The descent

Four hundred meters in a maintenance elevator. Your ears will pop. Bring everything with you — there are no facilities, no shop, no spares, no phone signal below. When the door opens on LEVEL −400, the sound arrives before the view does: the drone of pump-turbines forty kilometers away, coming through water and concrete as a single continuous note you feel in your sternum before you hear it. Then the gallery, the railing, and water to the horizon under a ceiling so distant it reads as weather.

Two things to expect in your body. The air near the water can be colder than the water — the surface steams faintly with cold, mist rising off seven-degree water — so dress warmer than the number suggests. And the sameness is genuinely disorienting. Every bay looks like every other bay. The ceiling lamps never flicker and never go off. Bring a watch and trust it, because the place keeps its own time and will happily take yours.

## The cold is the boss

Seven degrees Celsius, year-round. This is the hazard that organizes everything else.

- **Wear a PFD any time you are on or near the water.** A fall from a catwalk or gallery into seven-degree water in heavy clothes is a survival problem measured in minutes — cold shock first, then swimming failure. The water is heartbreakingly clear and you will see the painted floor forty meters below you, which makes it easy to forget it will kill you gently and quickly.
- **If you boat, carry an immersion suit or full drysuit in the boat**, plus a sealed dry bag with a complete change of warm clothes, a bivy, and a thermos. Getting wet is the emergency; everything else is inconvenience.
- **If you dive, it's drysuit territory**, with proper thermal underlayers, hood, and gloves, and regulators rated for cold water (freeflow is a real risk at this temperature).
- Wool and synthetics only. Waterproof shell — in the air-gap vaults, condensation rains steadily from eighty meters up, and the drip arrives with eleven seconds of echo behind it.

## Getting around

You will not walk far. Bays are a kilometer wide; machines appear every few kilometers; pump halls every forty. Bring a boat — a packraft or small dinghy that fits in the elevator, with an **electric motor only** (combustion fumes in an enclosed volume, however vast, are a bad idea, and the drone will mask your approach to hazards anyway).

Navigation rules:

- **Get the column-numbering schema before you descend.** The columns are numbered in black paint at the waterline — BAY 114 COL 22, BAY 114 COL 23 — and that grid is the only map you will have on the water. No GPS, no cell signal, no landmarks except numbers repeating into the green distance. Carry a printed bay atlas and binoculars to read digits at range. Count columns aloud as you pass them. People get lost in identical places not by walking wrong but by stopping paying attention.
- **Tie off above the black wet band.** Every column wears a six-meter intertidal stripe of mussel crust — the tide's signature. It's slippery, it's sharp, and anything moored within it will be hung up or swamped twice a day.
- **When you hear the knock, note your position.** Water hammer — a gate closing somewhere in the system — rolls through the bays at the speed of sound in water, a thunderclap with a long concrete tail, and the whole numbered world rings once like a struck hull. That sound is information: a decision has just been made by the body of water you are floating on. Within minutes, a surge front may arrive — not a wave but a slow wall of current carrying drifting leaves and the smell of distant inflow. It's mostly benign in open bays. It is not benign near screens and intakes.
- **Give the turbine halls and outlet structures a wide, multi-kilometer berth.** Near the machines the water funnels and roars and the floor thrums. The fish give those bays a wide, remembered berth. Copy the fish. Current pinning you against an intake screen is the classic industrial death here, and it is entirely avoidable.

## If you dive

Treat it as what it is: cold, overhead, industrial diving in a space the size of a province.

- Full cave-diving discipline in any stretch where the water meets the ceiling — reel and guideline, gas planning, no exceptions. In bays with air overhead, it's open water, but stay oriented; the floor helps you. It's painted concrete, forty meters down, with lane markings, rail lines, and yellow no-park hashes still legible — you can literally navigate by road markings that have never been driven on.
- Two lights minimum per diver, though the place is lit: rows of submerged lamp cages burn around the clock, and the upper water column glows like a swimming pool at night, except the pool is the size of a province. Colors read true to thirty meters. Visibility runs forty meters, thanks to the mussels — a billion small pumps working for free.
- When the knock hits underwater, hold your depth, equalize, and let it pass. Everything alive down there flinches and resumes. Do the same.
- Don't touch the lamp cages or cable trenches (electrical, and the eels live in the conduit mouths). Don't pry at the zinc anodes bolted along every column — they corrode so the steel won't; they are the world's only planned metabolism, and they are not souvenirs.
- Lanyard everything. This matters more here than anywhere you will ever dive, for reasons below.

## Leave no trace — literally, forever

There is no sediment. Nothing buries anything, and at seven degrees, decay hesitates. A wrench dropped from a catwalk a decade ago lies exactly where it fell, in focus, on a floor that will never silt. The place cannot absorb your mistakes. A dropped bottle, a clipped glove, a food wrapper — it will sit on the painted floor, sharp-edged and legible, for years, catalogued by its fall. Pack out absolutely everything, including organic waste. And don't drink the water, however gin-clear it looks: it's a machine's working fluid, filtered by mussels but steeped in zinc, paint, and eleven years of anode sacrifice. Bring every liter you need.

## The inhabitants

Almost nothing here can hurt you, and almost everything is worth your attention. The mussel reefs near the flows are the foundation of everything — crusted black shells, sharp enough to cut, so wear gloves on ladders and lines. Amphipods swarm them like static. Pale crayfish work the column bases at night, one claw always larger. The midwater belongs to the schools — millions of silver fish per bay, grazing the algae halos that grow on the lamp cages' own light — and where there are schools there are the hunters: two-meter catfish drifting out from under the submerged gantry cranes at dusk, unhurried and harmless; pike holding station in the sun wells where the light gathers prey. Don't dangle your fingers in a sun well; otherwise, float and watch.

In the air vaults, swifts nest on ledges below the wells, their cries thrown back eleven-fold. Their guano spots the ledges — the only soil in the entire world, if it can be called that. Don't scrape ledges; guano dust is not your friend.

And the eels. If you come in autumn, you will see them at the outlet screens in their thousands, gone silver, pressing against the grating. Do not interfere, and do not touch the screens — suction aside, this is not yours to fix. Witness it. Photograph it. Carry the story up the elevator and tell it on the surface, where the decisions get made. That is the one thing you are allowed to take.

## Kit list

- Drysuit with thermal underlayers (dive) / immersion suit (boat); PFD
- Packraft or dinghy, electric motor, paddle backup
- Headlamp, handheld, and a spare light per person; batteries carried warm against your body
- Printed bay/column atlas, compass, wristwatch, binoculars
- Earplugs (the knock indoors is a thunderclap with an eleven-second tail) and an eye mask — the lamps never go off, and if you overnight on a catwalk platform, you will need darkness you bring yourself
- Warm hat, waterproof shell, mussel-proof gloves
- Thermos, dense food, all your water
- Dry bags, full spare clothing layer, bivy and hypothermia wrap, first aid kit, throw line, whistle
- Camera with a fast lens — the light is dim by daylight standards but true
- A filed plan with the control room, agreed check-in times, and a topside contact who knows when you're due up

## One good day

Descend after breakfast with the generation schedule in your pocket. Launch at BAY 000 and motor out a few bays in the morning, while the water stands high and rising on its daily breath. Read the column numbers as you go. At midday, be under a sun well — lie back in the boat and watch the pillar of real daylight stand in the water with dust and fish turning in it, while rain or sun or falling leaves arrive down eight hundred meters of fluted concrete. Tie to a column above the black band, eat lunch, listen to the drone. When the knock comes, feel it in your sternum, note the current's slow decision, and let the surge carry the leaf-fall past you. In the long artificial evening, drift near a submerged gantry crane and wait for the catfish to come out, unhurried, from under the steel. Ascend before the night pumping cycle pulls the tide down its six meters. Sleep on the surface.

You will come up with damp gear, photographs nobody will believe, and the eel story. Leave everything else exactly where it fell.
