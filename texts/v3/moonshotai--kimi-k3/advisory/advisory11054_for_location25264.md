# Preparing for the Still (Condensery Shaft Seven)

*Notes for an ordinary traveler, written the way the place teaches: quietly, and in order.*

## Before you commit

Get your legs ready. There is no lift. Four hundred iron stairs down through warming dark, and — the part everyone forgets while descending — four hundred back up at the end of your stay, with a pack, after days of wet heat. Train on stairs for a few weeks beforehand. Your knees will forgive you.

Spend the week before in heat if you can: saunas, hot baths, midday walks. The hall is warm as a held hand and humid enough that sweat stops being a mechanism and becomes a condition. Arrive hydrated and stay that way.

Get a tetanus booster. The rock is young, glassy, fresh-fractured, sharp enough to open a careless palm, and everything metal down there is warm and damp. Bring your vaccinations and your common sense up to date.

And do not go if you are sick — not even a cold. There is no animal life down there, which means no shared immunity, no background microbial noise, and a crew, however many they are, who live sealed inside it. You would be the only disease vector in a thousand meters of rock. That is not a guest's gift.

Plan to stay at least two full Breaths, and ask for a cold night. One cycle teaches you the rules. The second lets you notice you’ve learned them.

## What to carry (down, and remember, back up)

**Clothing.** Wicking base layers, light long sleeves and trousers — you want skin covered not for cold but for sharp rock and warm metal. Everything quick-dry; nothing cotton-heavy. One genuinely warm layer, sealed in a dry bag, for the Collar at dawn — the only cold in the world happens there, on purpose, at night. Boots already broken in, with soles that grip wet glass. A brimmed hat or light hood, because for one hour each cycle it rains indoors. Two pairs of gloves: a leather work pair for rock and scale-scraping, a thin pair for general handling. Keep your sleep clothes vacuum-bagged and bone dry; a dry bunk in a wet world is a luxury you manufacture yourself.

**Light.** A headlamp with a warm or amber mode, a backup light, spare batteries in a dry bag. The dark between the caged bulbs is not empty — it is catwalk over pipe-rack over cable tray, and it is total. Do not bring some blue-white flood lamp. The arc lamp over the condenser fins is the only cool-colored thing in that world, and it earned the position. Keep your light warm, pointed down, and humble.

**Papers and recording.** A waterproof notebook and a pencil — pencil, because ink runs in that air. Clip or tether everything. Every few hours the floor exhales and draws back, and loose papers migrate with the Breath and are gathered, each time, to different corners. If your map is loose, it will be adopted.

**Health kit.** Electrolyte salts — essential, because the water is warm and you will sweat constantly, and warm water never feels like it’s replacing what you lose. Wound care for basalt cuts, burn gel for the rail you will eventually touch without checking, blister supplies, antifungal powder for feet, ear drops, chafe balm (hot, humid, four hundred stairs — do the arithmetic), and any personal medication, double-bagged. If mold spores bother you, bring a mask and antihistamines; the drip-lines are felted with life.

**Food and gifts.** Bring nothing that melts. Bring bright, sharp flavors — salt, citrus, anything sour or acid — because everything down there tastes of chalk and iron and warmth, and the canteen will make you crave contrast. As gifts for absent hosts: seeds (leave them on the canteen table with a note; do not plant them yourself — the garden is someone’s), good soap, a whetstone, watch oil, chalk, dried fruit. Nothing living besides seeds. No pets, ever, under any circumstances.

**A watch.** Bring it. Then stop consulting it. The clocks in the Tally House are set to the Breath, not the sun, and within a day yours will be too.

## The descent and the door

Sign the ledger at the shaft-head. Write your name, your intended duration, and where you mean to spend your time. This is not bureaucracy; it is the only introduction the place requires, and if you vanish into a side gallery and get gently welded into the young rock, the ledger is how anyone will know to scrape you free.

Take the stairs slowly. Notice the cage rattling less than it should as you go down — the rock drinks sound, and you are entering a quiet that has mass. Swallow and work your jaw as the warmth rises.

At the gasketed door: wait for the pressure-sigh. Never force a sealed door against the cycle. It will open when the Breath allows. Step through, read the chalk, and accept that you are no longer in Shaft Seven. You are in the Still.

## Living by the Breath

The town inhales and exhales every few hours, and everything in it is organized around that fact except you. Learn the phases. When the floor exhales its long tide of warm wet air, the canvas ducting overhead swells like a second lung answering the first, doors hold against the pressure shift, and your ears pop. When the exhale climbs to meet the night-cold spilling down from the Collar, the vault sweats: a soft interior rain for an hour, the gutters singing at their different pitches, cistern gauges rising. That rain is the weather and the harvest both. Decide ahead of time where you want to be for it — under cover, or standing in it with your face up. Both are correct. And once a cycle the main gives a deep slow gulp and sends a pulse up the shaft: the town paying its water-debt upward to the dry world. Don’t be alarmed. Be impressed.

Set your internal clock to all of this. Count cycles, not days.

## Water discipline

Drink freely from Cistern Row — the water is warm, safe, and tastes like a held hand tastes of coins — but take none away. Filling bottles for the surface is theft from the tally, and the tally keeps count in a hand you will not see. Water is owed upward. You are a guest inside a negotiation between air and stone; drink your share and no more.

The Sump is not water. It is a black warm mirror where bootprints lead in and out. Do not drink from it, do not wade in it, and do not follow the prints to where they end.

If you are offered the dawn rime cup — ice scraped from feathers on the Collar, the one cold cup of the day — accept it with both hands and drink it slowly. It is spoken of the way other towns speak of festivals, because it is one. Do not ask for it. Do not ask for ice at any other time. Nothing here is cold except on purpose, and the purpose is not you.

## Touch protocol and moving around

Assume every surface is warm until proven otherwise, because it is. Hover the back of your hand near any rail, pipe, or valve before gripping — the handrails are warm as wrists, and some are warmer than that. You do not lean on anything down here without checking it first; make this a reflex within your first hour or the Still will teach it to you blisteringly.

The basalt cuts. Gloves on for any scrambling, and even then, test holds. The drip-lines are slick with chemosynthetic felt — treat every wet surface as ice. On catwalks in the amber dim, three points of contact, always. Do not linger over the vent field at the hall’s center; the shimmer there is not a mirage.

## Sound, and being heard

Understand this clearly, because it is a safety fact disguised as poetry: a shout dies within twenty paces. You cannot call for help across the hall. Stay within sight of your companions, agree on light signals, and if you wander into a side gallery, leave a note in the canteen chalk margin saying where you’ve gone. The margin is where people talk; the surveyors’ argument about the void’s origin is still running there.

Otherwise, speak at conversation volume. Small sounds persist and become the whole soundscape — drips pitched by the depth of what they strike, a valve’s clunk, a brass counter advancing, the unhurried flywheel, your own heart at the Hot End, returned to you unembellished. You will find yourself lowering your voice without deciding to. And know that sound stays where you make it: this is the best place in several universes to say a true thing out loud.

## The living things

Do not eat anything that grows here. The fungal fans are perfect and uneaten, and in a place that keeps inventory, *uneaten* is information. Do not stir the felts or shake the hanging strands. Admire the chlorotic moss haloing the brightest lamp from a respectful distance.

The garden — the window-box of cress under its dedicated bulb, the greenest thing within a thousand meters, small enough to hold in both arms — is the closest thing this town has to a sacred object. Do not touch it. Do not thin it. Someone does. Stand there a while, though. You’ll understand why someone does.

And remember: down there, *you* are the wildlife. You are the only thing that scurries, the loudest warm body in the hush of nothing hiding. Conduct yourself like an animal that knows it is being tallied.

## The keepers

You will almost certainly not meet them. The signs will be everywhere — fresh chalk figures, a coat on a peg, a garden newly thinned, the rime bucket emptied and set back to dry — and the hands nowhere. The bunks sleep twelve; whether twelve sleep there, or three, or a rotation that is always elsewhere when you are present, the tally chalk declines to say.

Do not try to solve this. Do not lurk, do not hide to catch them at their work, and absolutely do not follow bootprints to the sealed drift door or unseal anything that is sealed. The Still maintains itself around the rumor of its crew, and the rumor is load-bearing. A good guest leaves the mystery intact and instead becomes a good rumor in return: sign the ledger, write in the chalk margins, sleep in a bunk that is made and not slept-in, remake it when you leave.

Pay for your stay in labor. Stone is owed scraping — dripstone buds over every gutter, pipes fur with scale, and the town must be scraped to remain a town. Ask the chalk margin what needs doing, or simply take a scraper to the gutters and work until the Breath tells you to stop. Gather migrated papers to their corner after each cycle. Leave the tally board itself alone; that hand is not yours.

## Health while you’re there

Dry your feet at least once per cycle and powder them; warm wet skin macerates fast. Watch for heat exhaustion — headache, nausea, chills in a place where nothing is cold — and treat it by resting near (not under) the Collar’s spill. Keep wounds covered and clean. Sleep in the Twelve is surprisingly easy: the flywheel turns unhurried, the dynamos hum low, and most travelers report sleeping like something promised eternity in writing.

## Leaving

Do a full kit sweep, twice. Nothing is ever lost down there — it is only adopted. A wrench left on a ledge is half-sheathed in calcite within a season, and the tenders use it to teach. If you do not want your belongings educating future apprentices, pack them out, along with every scrap of trash.

Sign out in the ledger. Drink deeply before the ascent — you will be dehydrated despite the humidity, you always are — and climb slowly from warming dark into cooling dark. At the top, the surface will feel wrong for a while: dry, bright, loud, rustling with creatures. The first cold drink will taste like nothing. You will have had the rime.

You will come up weighing the same as you went down. It will not feel that way. You spent days inside a lung, breathing its weather, leaving your sweat in its cisterns and your voice exactly where you made it, drip by counted drip — and somewhere behind you, even now, the chalk is changing.
