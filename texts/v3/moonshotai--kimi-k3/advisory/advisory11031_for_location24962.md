# Preparing for the Array: A Traveler's Briefing

Start with the counterintuitive part: **nothing here wants to hurt you.** No predators, no storms, no hostility of any kind — the array is a factory with the temperament of a lichen, and you are beneath its notice. Rank your preparations accordingly. The actual dangers are glare, edges, silence, disorientation, and patience — yours and its. Pack for those.

## How Long to Stay

This is the first decision, and it's a safety calculation, not an aesthetic one. The array precipitates copies of things that hold still in warm places, and accretion takes weeks. That gives you a safe window, but not an unlimited one:

- **Plan for five to ten days.** Set a hard ceiling of two weeks and treat it as non-negotiable.
- **Arrive midmorning,** when the fog is lifting, shadows are behaving roughly on time, and time itself is running thin and normal. Do not arrive at dawn (thick time, diffracted light, a disorienting first hour) or at dusk (your first impression should not be the drains murmuring yesterday at you).
- Weather is a solved problem: the array makes a small closed loop of damp and never a storm. The forecast is always *cool, dry on deck, dripping below, very bright.*

## Body Preparation

- **Altitude.** The dark cobalt zenith means thin air. If your transit method allows staging, spend two days acclimatizing at the rim. Know your headache protocol. The cool air (breath visible until midmorning) is pleasant but means real cold at night — bring a bag rated below freezing; the deck gives the day back as *light*, not heat.
- **Eyes — the critical item.** Ten thousand mirror-fins under a high thin sun is a snow-blindness machine. Glacier goggles with side shields, **plus a spare pair.** Losing them mid-array is an emergency, not an inconvenience. You will also sunburn in your own shadow — the fins throw light from every angle including below. Full coverage, serious sunblock, lip protection.
- **Hands and skin.** Every edge is fresh and nothing has had time to dull. Cut-resistant gloves for finfield travel, leather for the talus slopes. Pack wound closure supplies — strips, sutures, glue, antiseptic, tweezers for foil-scale splinters.
- **Lungs.** When the wind makes that sound like distant applause, that is a flurry of sharp molten-thin laminate scale on the move. Shield your eyes and breathe through a respirator or tight scarf. The deck dust is foil-scale mixed with dried droppings — treat it as you would any fine mineral dust.

## What to Pack

- **Sleep kit:** an opaque mask (the night never completes — the fins release stored daylight at one part in a hundred, and puddles hold yesterday's sun for hours) and earplugs (the drip metronome, the thermal ticking, and the dusk murmuration from the drains; many travelers cannot sleep to the sound of their own yesterday).
- **Water: carry almost none.** This is the great inversion — the only open water for a hundred miles is *here*, beading on cold metal. Bring a filter and two small bottles and refill constantly. Take from running channels, not the still cisterns (algae, verdigris bloom, and the faint glow, which is probably harmless, and you should filter anyway). **Never drink from the warm shallow pans. Never touch them recreationally at all.**
- **Food: every calorie, packed in.** Do not hunt. Some of the animals were born and some of them precipitated, and after a season of weathering no one can tell which — and neither can your stomach, *probably*, but that word is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Foil-feathered birds and frogs that tick like cooling metal are not provisions.
- **Navigation:** two mechanical watches, synchronized daily (if they disagree after a thick dawn, believe the average and stop scheduling anything precise for dawn). A magnetic compass is unreliable among forty miles of alloy. Cord and physical tags — **tie your markers, never stick them**; the skin molts, and anything adhered peels away with it. A notebook and pencil: sketch cistern arrangements, count corridors between turns.
- **Signaling:** sound dies here. A whistle carries ten paces before the fins drink it. Carry a signal mirror — light is the one resource the array overproduces — and agree on flash codes and rope-tug signals with companions *before* entering the finfield. Keep line of sight at all times; the hush swallows a separation in seconds and returns it to you, unhelpfully, a day later.
- **Shelter:** freestanding — the deck takes no stakes. Low profile against the flurries.
- **Waste:** there is no soil. It is machine all the way down, and anything you leave gets recycled — by moths, by flurry, or by the works. Seal it and pack it out, unless you want it incorporated.

## The Two Rules

These are the only local law, and everything that lives there observes them:

**1. Do not answer the drains.** Sound condenses here; the finfields drink the day's noise and release it later, gently, through culverts and seams at hours of their own choosing. You will hear your own footsteps from this morning. A heron's cry from three days ago. Possibly, on a return trip, your own laugh from last year. Understand: **the drains repeat; they never converse.** It is playback, not presence. If you answer, you teach the array new material in your voice — and the only known way the array acquires more of you is if you give it. If a drain ever says the *new* thing, the responsive thing — leave that district calmly and do not camp downwind of it.

**2. Keep moving.** Stillness in warmth is how the array reads you. Never camp twice in one place. Don't nap beside the pans. Don't establish a favorite cistern or a usual seat — routines of place are just slow standing-still. And here is the subtle part: the array is easy to love. The quiet, the patience, the two herons at the cistern. **Fondness is the trap.** When you notice you love a spot, that is your cue to leave it.

Derived courtesies: speak sparingly. Every word is archived and replayed for strangers at dusk, and the culverts have no discretion — speak no secrets aloud. Keep a written journal instead; it doubles as a verification tool. At dusk, when the drains murmur, read what you said today and check the entries off. Yesterday's footstep is only yesterday's footstep.

## Navigating the Finfield

The uniform glittering plain is the array's cruelest practical joke: you can see forty miles and barely turn around. Corridor-counting plus tower fixes is the whole art — climb a condenser tower at least once daily to reorient, and camp within sight of a climbable tower. Use the Front (the glitter and heat-haze at the growing edge) and the aqueduct lines as compass points.

Three anomalies to handle calmly:

- **Grating corridors.** When the fin ranks align, reality diffracts: one drip falling in a hundred channels, the same swift crossing the same gap in triplicate. Not dangerous in itself, but nauseating, and it lies to your eyes and instruments. Stop, close one eye, back out along your entry line, and tag the corridor. Go around, never through.
- **Lagging shadows.** Near rims and talus, do not trust a shadow that arrives late; trust your boot. Stand back from cliff edges in wind — the faces shed foil sheets — and never linger at the base of the rim.
- **Thick time.** Near dawn and brightfall, moments dilute; a drip's descent takes as long as a sunset. This is not a hazard so much as a schedule change: do not attempt mileage, knotwork, or arguments at dawn. Be stationary for it. It's the best show in the world anyway — that's not consolation, it's just true.

Fog mornings: knee-deep, slow, and hopeless for footwork. Wait them out on a tower with breakfast.

## Precipitates

You will see them: the bird with foil feathers, the cistern frog ticking like cooling metal, the rat with an extra stillness. Observe, sketch, do not touch, do not feed, **do not collect specimens** — a specimen of a precipitate is a specimen of the array's intentions, and you do not want to carry those.

Now the hard paragraph. If you find a human shape accreting in a warm pan — decide your policy *now*, on the rim, while it is abstract. Some travelers disrupt an accretion; some cannot bring themselves to; everyone agrees that lingering to watch it day after day is how a person ends up staying long enough to need the policy twice. **If the shape resembles you, your visit is over.** Leave within the day, by the straightest corridor, and say nothing aloud as you go.

## Don't Bother

- The under-deck is the array's body — its rivers, its memory, its warmth. Peer in from the cistern rims; do not traverse it.
- The daily clunk of the press is harmless. Do not go looking for the works.
- The aqueducts are scenery, not roads, unless you came equipped for an exposed highline over a hundred-meter gulf. The sister arrays will still be there next expedition.
- Feeding the animals is standing still with extra steps.

## Leaving

Leave on schedule. The array's hospitality is asymptotic — it will never ask you to stay and never ask you to go, and that is precisely the mechanism. Build external commitments you cannot slide.

Expect after-effects: ordinary silence will sound loud for a while. You'll catch yourself checking puddles for stored sunlight. You'll pause at drains. And accept the toll: a thin layer of you stays behind — your footsteps archived in a culvert, your voice replayed for the next traveler at dusk, at one part in a hundred. It is cheap at the price.

## Why Go

Because of the long dawn from a condenser tower: the ground brightening before the sky, brightfall sifting almost invisibly into the channels, ten thousand drips descending unhurried, the deck ticking awake — and at a cistern below you, two herons, identical except that one is slightly brighter and slightly newer, both of them drinking, neither of them minding. After you see that, the two rules will stop feeling like restrictions. They'll feel like good manners in someone else's young, patient world.
