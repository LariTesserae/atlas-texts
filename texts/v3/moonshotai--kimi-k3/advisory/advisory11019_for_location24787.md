# Preparing for the Singing Basin

You will pay for this visit in three currencies: water, hearing, and voice. There is no fourth currency and there is no negotiating. Everything below is about spending all three deliberately, on your own terms, instead of having them taken from you on the basin's.

## First: understand what the place is

You are not visiting a city that has sound in it. You are visiting a sound that has a city in it. The roar is the weather, the agriculture, the economy, and the city wall, all at once. Every practical problem of the visit — hydration, communication, sleep, safety, sanity — runs through that one fact. Travelers who treat the noise as scenery come back damaged. Travelers who treat it as the terrain itself come back changed, which is the best available outcome.

## The hearing decision (make it before you leave home)

The keepers are deaf by degrees and by choice; they consider hearing a childhood thing, outgrown like milk teeth. You are not a keeper, and you presumably have other worlds to walk where birdsong and conversation still matter. So decide now, in the quiet of your own room, how much hearing you are willing to spend.

- **With discipline** — plugs in before the rim, felt hood at midday, sleeping up at the rim, a stay of two or three nights — expect a temporary dullness and a ringing like surf for a day or two after. Full recovery is likely.
- **Without discipline**, permanent loss begins within days, starting at the high end. The basin will not get quieter as you go deaf. You will simply lose the ability to prove how loud it is.
- **The middle path:** some travelers deliberately take one unprotected hour at full noon, once, kneeling, as the true toll — letting the chord become a condition, breath and heartbeat entrained, vision gone soft at the edges. If you choose this, choose it in advance, tell your companion, and have them watch your eyes for the moment to pull you out.

Do not make this decision in the moment. The chord at noon is very persuasive, and it does not give refunds.

## The crossing: the Hush

The last two days are across a salt pan the maps leave blank. This is the most dangerous part of the trip, and the danger is *silence*.

**Water.** There is no water in the basin. Not a drop. The plants make a thin sweet oil; the cisterns hold sound; the one jar of actual water is a sealed shrine and is not a resource, ever, at any level of desperation. Carry everything: a gallon per person per day minimum, more if you must walk at midday. For a week out, that is eight to ten gallons per head. Cache half of it at your first noon camp for the return leg, and mark the cache by bearing and star sight, because the pan has no features to mark it by. If you have a pack animal, remember that it drinks too.

**Travel by dawn and dusk.** Rest under a tarp at noon. The glare off the pan is blinding in the literal sense — bring slitted goggles or smoked lenses, a veil, full-coverage pale clothing, and salts for your water. You will sweat white.

**Day one gives you nothing.** No note, no thread, only the Hush turning your ear inward until your own blood is the loudest thing in the world. Some travelers turn back on day one, certain they've been misled. They weren't. The thread comes on the second day — felt in the molars and the breastbone before it is heard. Pack faith alongside the water, and ration your doubt.

**The fence rule applies to you.** In the deep sand, things hunt by vibration, and silence is how they know something has died. The basin's roar tells them *alive, keep moving* — and your job, out in the Hush, is to say the same thing about yourself. Bells on the pack. Songs while you walk. A drum at every rest stop. Never a silent camp; rotate a singing watch at night. This is safety equipment, not entertainment. If the ground ever vibrates in a way that is neither you nor the distant Drone, move. Do not investigate. Do not dig.

**The rim comes without warning.** The plain shows you nothing until your boot is at the edge and the whole basin opens below at once. When the chord swells from sound into pressure, slow down, probe ahead, rope yourself to your companion. And when it opens: kneel. Take a knee and let your eyes and your bones catch up with each other. The moment is large, the vertigo is real, and the edge is too.

## The kit

- **Ear protection:** two sets of wax-and-fiber plugs (the keepers make excellent ones, but you need yours before you ever reach them), a felt-lined hood, a headband to hold everything in place at noon.
- **Communication:** a wax tablet and stylus; a dozen hand signals agreed with your companions *before* the crossing — stop, water, rest, danger, direction, going up, going down, enough. If prior travelers recorded the keepers' signs, learn the twenty commonest. A bone tuning fork, if you can obtain one: pressed to the teeth, it lets a plugged ear hear the floor-drums.
- **Noise:** frame drum, bells, a rattle, and at least ten songs memorized. See above.
- **Navigation:** charts of the local sky, a compass (test it before the last settlement; lodestones do not answer in every world), a knotted pacing cord, and stakes or salt blocks for cairns.
- **Shelter:** a noon tarp, and a genuinely warm night layer — the basin breathes in the cold after dark, and the pan is colder.
- **Food:** all of it. The keepers may offer oil and seed-cake; accept a taste as courtesy, but do not plan on a garden that drinks sound to water a creature that drinks water.
- **Gifts:** beeswax, felt, tanned hide, hardwood, bone blanks, resin, fine fiber — the materials of their technology. Above all: new songs, and news of the watered world. Leave metal trinkets behind; there is no metal in the basin and no want of it.
- **Medical:** salts, sun salve, something for pressure-headaches, bandages, your own necessities. If your heart is unreliable, weigh the trip carefully — the Drone sets the rhythm your heartbeat eventually takes, and it does not ask permission.

At the last settlement before the blank, ask two questions: *is the season still or windy*, and *is it moth season*. A still season means a thinner chord, a hungry garden, and twenty voices holding the fence alone — your visit will cost them more, so come ready to work, or come another time. Moth season means the Shimmer grows wings at dusk, and that is worth timing a life around.

## Arrival and the toll

You will not find the keepers. Your footsteps on the salt announce you, and they come up from the city to meet you at the rim. Wait. Don't shout — sign, or show your empty hands and your full waterskins.

Everyone who enters owes the city a voice, sung into the intake horn at the head of the stair. Choose your song **before you arrive**. It must meet three conditions:

1. **You can hold it steady** — a long note, a plain verse, nothing fancy in the din.
2. **It does not resolve.** No final cadence, no closing note, no ending. The chord never resolves; endings are not permitted; this is the one law they will check at the door. Practice ending on the question, not the answer.
3. **You are willing to leave it behind.** Your voice will keep sounding in the towers for a week after you've gone. If you sing your mother's lullaby, your mother will be singing in that city while you walk away across the salt. Some travelers find that beautiful. Some find it unbearable. Know which one you are.

After the toll is paid, they will sign you the entire history of the place in one sentence. Do not expect archives. One sentence is the whole of it, and it is enough.

## Living in the chord

**Sleep at the rim**, in the felt-lined dwellings where the keepers raise their children; the roar arrives there as a friendly tremor through the floor. Descend by day, and acclimate the way you would to altitude — short descents the first day, longer after. Expect your breath and heartbeat to sync to the Drone. Don't fight it. It fades two days out.

**The day has a shape; plan around it.** Dawn is the anxious hour, when the keepers drum the gardens awake — go watch, or lend a hand. Morning is for walking the stair-streets and the terraces with a keeper; stay on the stairs and never step into the rows, because every leaf is both an instrument and a meal. Noon is the furnace and the full voice: retreat to the rim, or take your one deliberate surrender, protected and witnessed. In the great towers at noon you can see the chord — salt haze sorted into glowing stacked discs along the standing waves. The keepers stop work to look at it every single day. Stop with them. Dusk brings the modulation down the scale and, in season, the moths. Night is the Drone alone and the cisterns breathing out their stored afternoon while the garden ticks softly in the dark, digesting.

**Speech is confiscated** from midmorning on — not discouraged, *confiscated*. Slate, signs, touch. A keeper's hand on your forearm is a full sentence; receive it as one. If you need help and no one is looking, drum on the floor or stamp — they will feel it, and they will come. Never separate from your companions in the terraces at midday. You cannot call for help, and neither can they.

**If the wind fails while you are there**, the city becomes labor: mirror-stoked tower-mouths, hide bellows, twenty voices down in the Throat holding the chord through the dangerous quiet. You will be asked to sing or pump. Say yes. It is the rent, and it is the honest kind — you will be feeding the fields and holding the fence with the same breath.

## The rules, in traveler terms

- **Never resolve the chord.** Not into the horn, not into a tower, not into the Throat. If your homesick skull aches for a cadence — and it will, by day two, like a tooth — hum it into your bedroll at the rim, into felt, into nothing that resonates.
- **The caps and plugs on the sound-canals are irrigation.** Touch nothing. An opened sluice floods a row with sound; a wrongly closed one starves it. You would not re-dig a stranger's ditches. Do not re-tune a stranger's city.
- **The cisterns hold trapped afternoons.** A charged echo-vault is a bell you can stand inside. Enter only when invited, only when the keepers sign that it is spent.
- **The jar in the rim shrine** holds the last water of the lake, older than the city. Do not open it, do not ask to, do not joke about it, do not look at it as a resource even in extremity. It is not water. It is a memory in the shape of water — the way a bell remembers the mold.
- **Do not grieve at the deafness.** They are not diminished; they listen with the bones of their feet and the palms of their hands, and they felt you coming long before you saw the stair. Don't shout. Don't offer cures. Don't make a show of your waterskin — though if a keeper asks to hold it and feel the slosh, hand it over. That is a wonder you can afford to give.
- **When you hear rain at two in the afternoon** and look up into a merciless, empty sky — that is the garden, ten thousand drum-leaves applauding. Travelers weep at it before they understand. Let it happen. Then drink actual water; tears on a salt pan are a water expense.

## Leaving

Do the water math again before you go; you leave with what your cache holds, no more. Mark your exit line with a cairn and a star bearing, because two steps past the rim the basin closes behind you like an eyelid, and travelers have walked past their own destination on the return leg by trusting their eyes.

Trust the thread instead. The Shimmer stays in your ears like surf for a day; the Drone rides your breastbone, faint but present, for two. While it is in you, you can still correct your course — stop, stand still, and listen with your bones. This is how the pilgrims navigate. This is how the moths cross a hundred miles of featureless white. You will be carrying the same compass.

Keep the noise discipline all the way out. The last day, when the Drone has finally faded from your chest, is the exposed leg — you are back in the Hush proper, hoarse and ringing and sung out. Sing anyway.

And know this: your voice stays behind for a week, added to the Standing Chord. If you meet inbound pilgrims on the pan, they may have been hearing you in the towers before your dust ever rose on their horizon. Greet them kindly. You are, in a small way, already part of the fence they are walking toward.

---

Carry all your water. Decide about your hearing at home, in the quiet. Bring a song you can bear to leave behind, and never — not once, not for an hour — be silent in the Hush. The basin is the loudest thing in a silent world, and there is only ever one direction to walk.
