# Preparing for the Bowl

## First, understand what you are

Before any gear list or training regimen, accept the reframe the place demands. You are not a visitor entering a landscape. You are an anomaly arriving at a resident: a warm, wet, ringing body in a world that is mineral, dry, and silent by law. Everything about your preparation flows from three facts:

1. **There is no water.** Every drop you will have, you carry on your back up a dead volcano through a rainless country. Your visit is bounded by liters. The bowl's memory is bounded by nothing. That asymmetry is the whole relationship.
2. **The floor hears.** Not metaphorically. The Felt is a drumhead with teeth, and beneath it are things that have spent eons learning to eat anything that makes a sound. Your primary survival skill is not strength, navigation, or first aid. It is stillness.
3. **Light is a word with two meanings.** Food, and the thing that eats what seeks food. You will go there dependent on vision, and you must unlearn that dependency before you arrive, because every photon you emit is a sentence spoken in a language you don't understand.

You will have two budgets on this journey: a water budget and a noise budget. Both are spent irreversibly. Most travelers who fail in places like this fail by spending the second one carelessly, once.

## Should you go at all

Be honest with yourself before the place is honest with you.

**Temperament matters more than fitness.** Can you stand motionless for an hour while something you cannot see investigates you? Can you be startled — genuinely, viscerally startled — and not flinch? The human startle response is the predator's trigger here. If a book dropped behind you in a quiet room makes you jump, you are not ready. If you have tinnitus, know that in true mineral silence your own ears will generate sounds, and you must learn to recognize them as internal or you will spend the visit chasing ghosts. If silence and absolute darkness unsettle you after an hour, they will break you after two days.

**Body.** You need heat tolerance, altitude tolerance, and load-bearing capacity simultaneously — a rare and miserable combination. You will carry thirty to forty kilograms of water and gear uphill through black clinkstone talus under a white sun, then live at altitude in fever-heat on thin, poisoned air. Heart conditions, lung conditions, pregnancy, and any tendency toward panic attacks are disqualifying. This is not gatekeeping; it is arithmetic.

**No rescue exists.** Write that down and sit with it. No one can come get you. A rescue team would be a dozen loud, fast bodies on the drumhead — they could not reach you even if they tried. Every problem that happens under the Roof is solved by you, alone, slowly, quietly, or it is not solved. If you die there, the Felt digests its fallen from below and builds the next layer of the world out of them. Some travelers find that thought not entirely terrible. Decide how you feel about it before you go, and write your will accordingly.

## Training: the months before

**Kill your flinch.** This is the single most important preparation. Have friends drop heavy books behind you on night walks, bang pots while you concentrate, shout from hiding — until your reflex to sudden sound is not flight, not even turning, but *freezing*. You are training against millions of years of evolution; it takes months of repetition. In the bowl, the creature that startles and bolts is dinner. The creature that startles and becomes stone is weather.

**Learn to fall.** You will fall at least once — the Felt gives underfoot like flesh. Practice on gym mats: fall, and then *do nothing*. Do not scramble up, do not flail, do not curse. A fall is one loud event; flailing is twenty. Fall, freeze, let the floor forget you, then rise at the pace of growing things.

**Slow-walking.** Walk everywhere at half speed for a month, then quarter speed. Practice the sleeping-house test: cross a room full of sleeping people or animals in full kit, at night. If anything stirs, repack and repeat. Your gait must become: place the foot, settle it, transfer weight, pause. Nothing here hurries. Match the tempo or announce yourself as prey.

**Noise-proof your kit at home.** Stretch a drumhead — an actual drum, or a taut trampoline — in a quiet garage. Walk beside it in full gear. If it rings, find the rattle and eliminate it: tape the buckles, wrap the metal, felt the hard plastic, remove or secure every dangling item. Then repackage *everything*. No crinkly wrappers, no snap-lids, no Velcro — Velcro's rip is a scream. Food goes into soft silicone or cloth pouches. Every item opens with a slow, silent draw.

**Darkness adaptation.** Spend a weekend in a lightless room before you go. Do night walks without a lamp until you can move through broken country by feel, starlight, and hearing. You are re-ranking your senses, because the bowl will do it for you if you don't: touch and vibration first, hearing second, smell third — the air tells you the calendar and the hazards; ammonia means back away, rising sweetness means the Seeding nears — and sight a distant, nearly useless fourth. The makers knew this. Look at their glyphs: small figures with no eyes and great outsized ears. They are not depicting the bowl's natives. They are depicting the correct posture for a guest. Go there as an ear.

**Heat and altitude.** Two weeks minimum of heat acclimation, plus a staged ascent plan with days spent camping at intermediate elevations. Carry your full water load on training hikes. Learn your body's heat-illness signals and altitude-illness signals — and learn that they overlap cruelly: headache, nausea, dizziness, confusion mean *something is wrong*, and under the Roof you will not have the margin to diagnose the difference.

## The kit

Annotated, because every item exists in answer to something specific.

- **Water, all of it.** Budget six liters per day under the Roof, more for the approach. Wide-mouth, soft-sided, indestructible containers — nothing that can be dropped and burst. A dropped water bag is not merely a lost supply: it is a *spilling*, and the bowl's one law above all laws is that everything converges to lap at a spilling. Spill your water and the bowl comes to your feet — grazers, mites, and the killers themselves, in truce, around your boots. Guard your water like a heart, twice over: for its value, and for its announcement.
- **Respirator, multi-gas cartridges, and spares on a strict change schedule.** Sulfur compounds and ammonia are the known hazards; the ammonia concentrates near the vent-spires. Carry a real gas detector if you can source one, because your nose lies to you: olfactory fatigue means the sulfur smell fading tells you nothing. Trust the instrument. Practice slow breathing through the respirator's resistance until it calms rather than panics you.
- **Thermal imager.** This is your eyes. The natives see by warmth — the ash-kites steer by ember-threads — and through a thermal lens you will see the bowl as it sees itself: ember-cracks glowing, heat-columns shimmering above the vents, cool spills sliding dark down the rim, your own body a blazing anomaly. Learn its palette on your first short excursion before relying on it.
- **One red-filtered light, dimmable, pointed down, used sparingly.** Red is the color of geology here — the ember-light in the cracks — and the only safe light to imitate. Blue-white is the color of food and of the liar. **Tape over every LED on every device you own.** Cover every luminous watch dial. A blinking blue status light on a camera makes *you* the glimmer, and something out there eats the glimmer's seekers.
- **Soft-soled, thick-insulated boots** — moccasin-flexible above, furnace-proof below. The pooled heat at knee height is real; the Felt itself is fever-warm. Add gaiters: ash sifts down constantly, and you do not want pore-mouths exploring your ankle.
- **Full-coverage clothing, matte, dark, nothing reflective or phosphorescent.** Brimmed hood against the ash-fall. Sealed goggles for the spire zones and heavy ash. Gloves — though you will take one off often, deliberately, to lay a bare hand flat on the ground, because the drumming is why you came.
- **A stethoscope or contact microphone** if you want to hear the bowl's hidden music properly. The bowl is only quiet in the air. Through the ground it is full of courtship, territorial drumming, duets sustained for days between mates a field apart. Your hand will do; the instrument will break your heart.
- **Camp gear for stone, not soil:** a sleeping pad (the cell's stone is hot; insulate from below), a tarp with cord — to re-roof the dry-stone cell, the first roof it has had in ages. Weight the tarp with stones placed gently. Nothing is ever hammered, staked, or snapped.
- **Cold rations.** No fire. Fire is light, heat, and smoke — every forbidden thing at once — and there is nothing to burn except the resident. Soft, mild, low-odor food in silent packaging. No cooking under the Roof.
- **Pencil and notebook.** Pencil scratch is quiet; pen clicks are not. You will keep a log, copy the glyphs, and count tally-marks. This is not sentiment; it is calendar-keeping.
- **A small vessel, dedicated, for the libation.** You will understand when you stand on the threshold.
- **A satellite beacon for the approach only.** The rainless country is empty and the desert leg can kill you conventionally. From under the Roof, do not count on anything. Rescue is not coming into the bowl regardless.
- **No weapons beyond a utility knife.** Nothing here can be fought. Your defense is stillness, and stillness weighs nothing.

## The approach

Cross the desert at night; the white sun on black clinkstone is an oven by day. Cache water along your route and at your staging camp. The final talus slopes are clinkstone — rock that rings like slack bells when walked on — so treat the ascent as your final silence exam: if you can climb that slope quietly, you are ready for the floor.

Spend at least two days at a staging camp for the altitude. Then make the final climb and see it: the Roof — a standing canopy of ash and fume held aloft by the mountain's own breath, permanent night balanced on a column of warm air. It has never cleared. You are about to walk out of the world of weather.

Walk the rim first — it takes an hour. Read the cairns; note that some are toppled, and that shakes happen. Find the notch, the one wound in the rim where stars show through. Find the stair: some four hundred steps worn into scoops by feet, descending. Find the cell at its foot, roofless, and at its threshold the cup-shaped hollow worn smooth in the stone.

## The rim camp and the libation

The makers built nothing below the rim — nothing on the hide. The cairns, the stair, the cell, the seat: all stone, all above or clinging to the wall. This is not absence. This is the map of where humans may be, drawn by a people who spent generations learning the bowl's terms. Follow their zoning exactly. Stone is neutral ground: the stair, the cell, the seat. The silence law binds on the drumhead; the rock does not carry your footsteps to the listeners the way the Felt does. Reverence or good sense — here they are the same thing, which is the deepest thing the makers can teach you.

Re-roof the cell with your tarp. Cache your reserve water there, lidded with a flat stone. Copy every glyph at every landing — the concentric circles, the tessellated Felt-pattern, the eyeless ears — and count the tally-marks precisely. They may be the breath-cycle calendar. Compare them against what you observe. If they match, you hold the makers' reckoning of time.

Then, before you ever step onto the Felt, perform the libation. Fill the threshold dish from your own carried water — water you hauled up a volcano, the most precious thing you own — and pour. Step back onto stone. Be still. Watch: small things will come to lap it, quickly, in truce, before the hot ground drinks the rest. This is the only rain this place has ever known, brief and caught between bodies, and you have just made it. What the libation buys you is not protection. It is posture. You arrive as a guest who knows the terms.

## The rules of the floor

Here is the refinement a first-timer needs, because the silence law is subtler than "be quiet." The bowl has a standing sound — the herds' combined rasping, soft and continuous, like rain that isn't. Soft airborne sound is tolerated; everything here breathes and rasps and lives. What the bowl punishes is **suddenness and ground-transmitted sharpness**: a clicked shell-edge, a turned pebble, a stride too quick, a dropped tool, a tap. The drumhead carries these like bells to things that have waited days without moving and can wait days more.

So: you may breathe, whisper rarely, move cloth. You may not click, snap, scrape, tap, or thud. Walk at the pace of growth. Lanyard everything; if something falls anyway, let it lie — retrieve it slowly, later. You cannot hide your shape; the listeners read vibration, not silhouette. You can only hide your motion. Stillness is your camouflage and your only armor.

At every stop, take off a glove and lay your hand flat on the Felt. It will flinch — you will flinch it ten thousand times on this journey; make each one gentle, as apology — and through your palm you will feel the faint continuous drumming, the pulse of something too large to see. Read it the way the makers did. Herd movement. Distant strikes. The tempo of the breath. Do this often enough and the floor begins to tell you things before they happen.

Do not tap back. The drumming is a language, and you do not speak it. An improvised tap might mean *food*, or *rival*, or *mate*, and you will not enjoy any of the three conversations.

Expect the herds to lean slowly away from you — a wake of stillness traveling with you through the crowds. Everything will know where you are. In a world of constant subterranean music, your bubble of hush is itself a signature, and things that hunt may track the silence as easily as the sound. Accept it: you are known. Expect the mites to find you — you are the wettest thing that has walked here in ages. Small things will come to lap your sweat. Stand still, keep your mouth closed and eyes protected, and let them. It is the truce at small scale, and it is, if you let it be, an honor.

Hard rules, memorized before descent:

- **Do not enter at high breath.** When the vent-flow surges, the herds pack until the floor moves as one fabric and there is truly no bare ground — no you-shaped space anywhere in the bowl. Descend at low breath, when the Felt pales and tightens and waits, and the gas runs thinnest. Learn to read the phase from the rim: flushed and thick is high; pale and taut is low.
- **Give the vent-spires a wide berth.** Ammonia concentrates near them, the rising columns above them are the kites' roads and the hottest, foulest air in the bowl, and the spires *breathe*. Never put your face near a breathing thing.
- **Never step into a crack.** Ember-threads glow down there; the crust is thin; and a cracking crust is the loudest sound imaginable.
- **Every hole is someone's mouth or home.** Do not enter the Fallen Chimney. Do not reach into crevices. Do not lift the edge of anything.
- **If something near you is taken** — the floor blooming upward, the wet thump, the brief crackle — freeze. The herds lean away; the shroud-moths will arrive within the hour. You resume moving when the bowl does.
- **If shroud-moths begin attending *you***, stop and ask why. Check for a dead thing in your gear, an unnoticed wound, an infection. The moths are the bowl's readers of approaching death. Do not ignore their review of your prospects.
- **Do not kill anything.** Not for food, not for water, not in fear. The cistern-beasts are the bowl's only wells, slow armored wineskins waddling between the sweet-stone veins, and the water economy here is a closed sacrament that passes mouth to mouth and is never set down. You are not in it. Do not crack one. Do not drink one.
- **If you witness a spilling, freeze and do not intrude.** The truce is theirs. Those few seconds when nothing hunts are the safest moments in the bowl — some travelers say the only moments you could move quickly and be forgiven — but do not test whether the amnesty extends to you. It is seconds long, red as rain, and not for you.
- **Do not kneel in the deep bowl.** The worst heat pools at knee height, and kneeling doubles your contact with the drumhead. Crouch briefly on both feet if you must, or stand.

## Light, yours and theirs

Navigate by thermal imager, by the notch's stars, by memorized landmarks — the Fallen Chimney, the tallest spire — and by counting steps. Do not plan to retrace your footprints: the Felt closes over every print within the hour. The bowl erases your passage behind you while you are still inside. You will walk a path that exists only once, forward.

If you see a cold, bluish light winking in the dark: freeze. Fix its bearing. Withdraw at walking pace, never toward it, never hurriedly. Light means food, or the thing that eats what seeks food, and the glimmer is patient in a way you are not built to outlast. It can wait longer than you. Do not walk toward a light here — the oldest rule, and the one most likely to be tested, because everything in a human body wants to walk toward light in the dark.

And if grazers begin drifting toward *your* red lamp, mistaking it: kill the light and freeze. Better blind than interesting.

## The calendar

There is no day and no night. Time here is kept by breath and low breath, by the Seeding, by the blade of light through the notch — and you must keep it too. Log the Felt's color and turgidity at every visit to the floor. Note the density of the rasping. Smell the air: the faint seasonal sweetness rising is the Seeding approaching. Watch the moon slide past the notch from the seat; the same moon pulls the mountain's breathing, and the notch is the makers' observatory window. Bring a star chart for that narrow slice of sky and a lunar calendar. Count everything. Make your own tally-marks. The habit begins on the stair: four hundred scoops, descended one patient foot at a time.

If you can possibly arrange it, time the whole expedition to end at the Blade. Two or three times a year the geometry lines up — the full moon, or the sun at its seasonal extreme, crossing the notch — and a single band of light no wider than a door leans over the rim and slides across the bowl: up the far wall, across the spires, over the backs of the herds, gone in minutes. Nothing here has eyes for it. The Felt tightens under its touch; the falling ash shows silver within it; the kites cross and cross it like shuttles on a loom. At the head of the stair, facing the notch, there is a seat: a hollow worn into stone by long sitting. Sit in it. Arrive days early. Bring water up from the cell. Wait the way the makers waited, through the whole of the bowl's patient breathing, for the few minutes when the world's one opening admits the world's one visitor from the sky. You may be the first sighted witness in longer than the reef-ground is deep. If the Blade is the sun's, have your dark goggles ready even for those minutes. If it is the moon's, let your eyes have it.

The Seeding, if you catch one: witness it from the notch the first time. The Felt releases its spores and for days the bowl admits its only standing light, a slow snow of faint glowing motes drifting up through the dark until the air itself seems made of dim sparks. It is the most beautiful thing you will ever see and a season of heightened everything — feeding herds, active glimmer, spores in every breath, which is why the respirator never comes off. You will be dusted with dim sparks. Brush down thoroughly before sleeping. You do not want to glow.

## Emergencies

- **Vent flare.** Rare, but the bowl absorbs catastrophe as routine and will not pause it for you. If the gas surges: move to the stair — fast now, ringing like a bell, and that is acceptable; on stone you are safe — and up. Expect a heavy ash-fall off the Roof. Goggles, hood, respirator, and wait it out above the rim.
- **Respirator failure.** The cooler air spilling down the rim is your refuge. Retreat to the fringe, then to the rim. Bail out of the bowl entirely if you cannot breathe the deep air. There is no shame in the fringe; it is the nursery slope, and it is enough for a whole visit if it must be.
- **Injury.** Splint, bind, and get to stone at whatever pace you can manage. Your companion — bring exactly one, trained as you are trained, fluent in your pre-arranged hand signals and touch codes — is your entire medical system. The four hundred scooped steps of the stair are your corridor of grace. Every other direction is drumhead.
- **Lost.** Stop. Hand flat to the ground. Find the notch's stars overhead or through the thermal lens; the cool spills on the rim show dark. Never chase your footprints — they are gone. Stand still until you are calm; nothing here hurries, and for the length of one water bottle, neither may you.
- **Panic.** Stop. Breathe four-counts through the resistance of the mask. Hand to the ground. The drumming has kept time for longer than the reef is deep; lend it your pulse until your pulse agrees to slow down.

## Leaving

Pour a second libation at the threshold — you will want to — and ascend the stair slowly, counting. Through the notch, and the world hits you like a hammer: white sun, hard shadow, *sky*. After days in the mineral dark your eyes cannot take it; have the dark goggles ready before you step through, keep your skin covered, and time the exit for outside dusk or night if your water allows.

Before you descend the far side, brush down every item of outer clothing and bag it. You are a door between universes, and the Seeding's motes drift upward on the warm column; do not be the wind that carries them to another world. Wipe your feet at the threshold of reality. Quarantine runs both directions — and spare a thought for who you tell about this place. The bowl has no defenses against the curious except its remoteness and its terms. Hold its location the way the makers held their buildings: off the floor.

Take no souvenirs. Not a shell, not a stone from the reef-ground, not a fallen spore. The bowl keeps its own dead and builds the next world out of them; nothing there is unclaimed, and everything there is history. What you take is the tally in your notebook, the makers' glyphs copied true, and the drumming remembered in the palm of your hand.

## What it will do to you

The first time you speak aloud after leaving, your own voice will startle you. That passes. What doesn't: you will come home to floors that don't flinch and find they feel like a lie. You will set a glass of water down on a table — casually, without ceremony, an act no creature of the bowl has ever performed — and remember the truce at a spilling, and your hand will hesitate. You will walk through your loud, bright, wet world for the rest of your life listening through the soles of your feet.

You went in as the anomaly — warm, wet, ringing, trailing a wake of stillness through the herds. You come out as a witness. That is the only rank the bowl confers, and it is given to anyone who keeps the terms: not loud, never toward the light, water poured and never spilled, the mountain's calendar kept. The stillness closes behind you like a held breath let go. The rasp of the herds rises again, soft as rain that isn't. The spires exhale. And the bowl resumes its long breathing, which is all the weather it has ever needed — and which, now that you have felt it through your open hand, is a weather you will carry in you, faint and continuous, for the rest of your life.
