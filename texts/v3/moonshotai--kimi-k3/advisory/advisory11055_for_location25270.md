You are not visiting a ruin, a garden, or a machine. You are visiting something that finished becoming itself a long time ago — a pump that grew a body, or a body that happens to pump — and the single most important preparation is internal: understand that nothing in the Kettle needs you. Most places on most routes want something from a traveler. This one is complete. It will tolerate you the way a sleeping animal tolerates a fly that has the sense to keep still. Pack and behave accordingly.

### Timing and dose

Arrive in early afternoon. This gives you hours to get your bearings and your balance before dusk, which is the one scheduled event the place offers — the moths, timed to the beat, rising into the stack's plume to live their single night. Plan a first visit of afternoon plus dusk, or at most one overnight. You will know by midnight whether you can sleep inside a heartbeat, and there is no way to learn this except by trying.

Season matters to you, not to it. The Kettle keeps its level and its temperature through drought and flood alike, which is itself worth seeing — go in a dry year, when the ditches outside are cracking, and stand in a place that has declined to notice. Winter gives you the frost line: the sharpest climate border you will ever step across, and a real hazard on the way out, which I'll come back to.

A note on who should think twice: if heartbeat sounds distress you, if you cannot sleep through rhythmic noise, if dense wet green presses on you — this may not be your stop. And if you are the kind of traveler who cannot keep from picking flowers, stay outside the dike entirely. I'll explain why below.

### The quarantine, both ways

This is the part most travelers skip and shouldn't, especially those of us who cross between worlds.

**Clean before you enter.** The Kettle grafts everything into everything — root to root, species to species, until the whole basin is arguably one individual. A spore or seed riding your boot treads from your last world could be *adopted*. Brush and wash your gear before you cross the fields. Note that the place keeps almost no animals and appears to have no biting insects at all — a warm swamp with no mosquitoes is its own quiet miracle. Do not be the traveler who ends that sentence.

**Clean after you leave, harder.** The plume-blooms grow nowhere else anyone has found, in any world. Keep that true. Brush the mat-felt from your treads, clean your pole tip, and dry everything for days; gear that comes out of saturated warmth will smell of silage for a week, and some of it will try to grow things. Consider dedicating a kit to this place and quarantining it permanently. This is the price of being allowed inside a closed loop.

### What to pack

- **No flame. This is absolute.** Gas domes wander beneath the mat like slow cattle, and the stack breathes sweet gas. No stove, no lantern, no lighter "just in case." Go all-electric, and accept cold food or self-heating rations. The joke writes itself: the one place called the Kettle where you may not boil anything.
- **A stout probe pole, chest-high.** Your most important tool. The mat lies about its own depth — knee-deep here, bottomless there.
- **Footwear that drains.** Boots or trail shoes you accept soaking to the knee. Not waders: if you punch through the mat, waders fill and you don't come back up. Bring three pairs of dry socks and foot powder; warm-water immersion foot is real even at bath temperature.
- **No cotton, ever.** Quick-dry everything, dry bags inside your pack, and a warm dry layer sealed away for the exit in cold seasons.
- **Sleeping gear if you stay:** a small free-standing tent with a full fly and generous ventilation. Do not hang hammocks or lines from the canopy. It is all one organism, and it is holding the engines together.
- **Headlamp, backup light, spare batteries in waterproof bags.** The one intact lamp is not infrastructure; it is a relic with a job, and it is not yours.
- **All the water you'll drink.** The Kettle is made of water and none of it is for you. If you must refill, take it at the outflow sluice and purify chemically or with a battery UV — remember, no boiling.
- **Earplugs and over-ear muffs.** They will not stop the beat — it arrives through your sternum and the soles of your feet — but they take the edge off the drip-patter and the long hiss of the stack.
- **A watch with alarms.** The polyrhythm of three engines slightly out of phase is too gradual to hear changing, and it eats hours whole. If you have obligations, set alarms.
- **A simple mask** if you're asthmatic or spore-sensitive, for dusk near the plume. Antihistamine. Small first aid. Tetanus current — there is still iron under all that green.
- **If traveling paired, 10–15 meters of line.** A whistle. A waterproof notebook and a pencil — ink runs, and lenses fog when cold gear meets saturated air, so seal cameras in bags until they warm to the interior.
- **Optional:** an audio recorder. Know in advance that no recording captures the part that matters. That part you carry out in your ribcage.

Leave behind: pets, plants, fishing gear (there is one eel; it is *the* eel), drones, gifts, offerings, and any intention of leaving a mark. The Kettle accepts no donations.

### Crossing the berm

The border is one step wide. A few degrees of warmth, total saturation, and the sound going from *heard* to *carried in your body*. Pause on the crown of the dike. Let your lenses sweat inside their sealed bags. Fix your exit landmarks — the enamel sign, the stack above the canopy — because the green erases sightlines within ten paces, and the basin is small but it is not simple.

Then do the central thing deliberately, once: stop on the mat and stand through a full cycle. The settling. The gathering pause. The beat, and the whole basin lifting a finger's width beneath you, and a second later the shaken canopy releasing its brief private rain. Do it on purpose the first time. After that, it simply happens to you, on schedule, for as long as you stay.

### Walking the mat

Probe every step. The mat gives like a chest even where it holds, and you will develop what sailors would call Kettle legs within the hour — until then, pause mid-stride when a beat is due, because that finger's width of lift is exactly enough to take your balance while your weight is between feet.

Avoid the smooth traveling hills — those are gas domes migrating, and a firm rise in one month is a sighing hollow the next. Avoid any green noticeably richer and darker than its neighbors; that is where the mat is thin and well-fed. Do not walk the pipes. They are sleeved in living root and they are working; follow them with your eyes all you like — you will not find the seam where iron ends and plant begins, because there isn't one — but keep your weight off them. And keep fingers, hair, and pack straps clear of the flywheels. Three meters of iron turning at the pace of a minute hand invites touching. It is still three meters of iron.

If you punch through: do not thrash. The water is warm, the dark is full of roots, and the mat closes over you like a mouth. Spread your arms, catch the edge, roll back the way you came. With a companion on a line this is an anecdote; solo, it is why you told someone in the ordinary fields where you were going.

The sump is not for swimming. It is the one open pool because the pumps drink from it — there is suction, there is warm still water (which everywhere in the multiverse carries things that want your sinuses), and there is an old eel the color of the water, working. Admire from the bank.

One reassurance: unlike every ruin you've ever walked, nothing here is about to fall on you. The entropy finished long ago. The hazards of the Kettle are wet, gas, and rhythm — never gravity.

### Living with the beat

Low frequency does things to a human body that are hard to predict. Some travelers weep at hour two without knowing why. Some feel a calm they describe, gropingly, as prenatal. The drip-rain arrives a second after each beat, and you can set your expectations by it: you will never be dry, and the sooner you accept that, the sooner it becomes almost pleasant.

Sleep is the referendum. By midnight you will know whether the beat has become your own pulse — in which case you will sleep like something in a womb — or whether it is a fist on a door, forever. If it is the fist: pack up and sleep outside the dike in the field. The thud carries out across the dark fields softened to a rumor, a heart audible from outside the body, and some travelers honestly prefer the Kettle from there. No shame in being an outside listener.

Notice, too, the shaped hole in the sound: no birdsong. Nothing with a spine but you. It is one of the strangest silences available anywhere, and it unnerves some people more than the noise does.

### The rules of the house

**Everything here is load-bearing.** That is the whole law. The census is three, and each member forbids something: do not touch the grease-cups or housings — the pale slugs in them *are* the lubricant, and have been longer than the gauges remember (wash your hands after touching anything, before eating). Do not wade into the plume at dusk — the moths get one night each and it is not yours. Do not feed, fish for, or "just check whether it's real" the eel. It is real, and it is working.

The plants are arguably a single individual, which makes picking a flower closer to pulling a tooth. No souvenirs. Photographs, recordings, and the memory in your sternum are the only things you may take.

**No repairs.** The needles tremble, once per beat, against dials stenciled *NORMAL*. Believe them. Around hour three, a specific temptation comes over mechanically minded travelers — the urge to tap a gauge, tighten a fitting, adjust the half-living belt. Recognize it as the place testing your manners, and put your hands in your pockets.

**Leave nothing.** Anything dropped gets processed to a stable form — there is a hut folded gently into the mat with the tools still hanging inside, and a chair set facing the largest engine. That is what the Kettle does with leavings: keeps them, tidies them, makes them permanent. Your wrappers and tape would be kept too. Pack out everything, including organic waste; the mat would reclaim it in days, but your microbes are foreign to a closed loop.

The chair: you may sit in it. It is stable — it has been stabilized. Face the engine. Stay a while. Do not move it, do not carve anything, and do not expect an explanation. Someone watched. Now someone is watching. That is the entire sacrament this place offers, and it is enough.

And don't clean the sign. The algae film is the current coat of paint.

### Dusk, and leaving

Be near the stack at dusk, at a respectful side-distance, out of the plume, masked if you're sensitive. One species of pale moth, timed to the beat, rising into the exhaust to spend its only night among flowers that exist nowhere else in any world. The lamp that glowed pointlessly all day stops being pointless. This is the performance. There is no other, and there needn't be.

When you go: in cold weather, change into your dry, warm layer *before* crossing the dike — frost begins at the berm like a wall, and wet clothes plus that step is how travelers get stupid. Set whatever return arrangement you use out in the ordinary fields; the Kettle is a poor place to be between worlds, like leaving mid-heartbeat.

Within fifty paces of the sluice, the ordinary world entirely reasserts itself, and you should plan for the specific grief this causes. Sit in the field a while. Listen to the thud carry across the darkening flats. Many travelers end up spending their last night outside the berm on purpose, just to hear it from the dark.

For a day or two afterward you will feel the rhythm in your own chest at roughly half-minute intervals and check your pulse. It fades. Some people go back annually.

The sign says *PUMPING STATION No. 9*. Whether there is a No. 8 anywhere on your route, the Kettle has no opinion. It has no opinions about anything — and that, in the end, is the rare luxury it offers an ordinary traveler: an hour or a night inside something that is running perfectly, that arrived long ago, and that does not need you. There are few rests in the multiverse like being entirely unnecessary. Prepare well, so you can afford to enjoy it.
