# Preparing for the Catch

The first thing to understand is that this trip is an exercise in subtraction. Most multiverse travel prep is about what you add — gear, currency, language, defenses. The Catch asks the opposite of you. You are visiting a town of forty people balanced on a single ancient organism in a cold, dry basin, where water is public accounting, the floor is fuel, the floor is memory, and the floor is also, in some sense, the host. Your job is to arrive with everything you need and take almost nothing from the place — not water beyond your ration, not warmth beyond your share, not a cutting, not even, ideally, much of their attention.

Prepare to be quiet, cold, thirsty, and strangely at peace. In that order, at first.

## Timing your visit

**Season matters enormously.** In dry weeks the fleece is grey and the basin is at its starkest — beautiful, but the fire discipline must be absolute (more on this below; it is the single most important safety fact of your trip). Just after rain, the fleece turns olive, the drown-hollows fill, the sealed snails emerge by the hundred, and the town is at its most social and generous. If your travel office has any way to flag a **bloom year** — the whole basin gilding at once, moths in clouds, honey and creosote on the air — rearrange your life for it. It happens perhaps once in a generation. The elders remember two. You would be witnessing the event by which the town counts its history.

**Arrive at the rim in mid-afternoon.** The walk down is an easy hour over dry gravel and pale scrub, and descending in daylight matters: in a town where footsteps make no sound, approaching *visibly* is a courtesy. You do not want your first interaction to be a startled keeper who never heard you coming. A dawn arrival means walking into the pooled cold of the basin at its deepest; save the dawn for the water slate on your first full morning.

**Plan on three to five days.** Less than two and all you get is the silence. More than a week and you begin pressing habitual lanes into the floor like a resident, which is a commitment you haven't earned.

**Arrange lodging before you go.** There is no inn. There are perhaps thirty structures, all of them working buildings or keepers' homes. Your downhill contact — whoever holds the charter over the Works — should be able to arrange a bed with a keeper family. Bring letters of introduction. Bring a bedroll anyway; heat in the Catch is local and scarce, clustered near the Works' waste warmth, and spare bedding is not guaranteed.

## What to pack

- **Water, or a water arrangement.** This is the heart of your preparation. Either carry your full supply (roughly 3–4 liters a day; remember the return walk is an hour *up* loose gravel, so weight matters) or, better, pre-arrange a daily ration through the Works and carry a reserve. Understand what you're agreeing to: the cisterns are gauged every morning and the numbers are chalked publicly on the slate outside the engine-house. If you draw water, the town will know the stranger drank. Be sparing. Never pour water out on the ground where anyone can see it — you would be pouring out the rarest thing they own.
- **Wool layers, insulation over windproofing.** The Catch's cold is not windy cold; it is *pooling* cold. Cool air drains down the basin slopes all night and collects in the bowl like water in a sink. At midnight you can feel it as an invisible river flowing around your ankles toward the lowest point, and your breath fogs at knee height. Frost comes some nights. Warm boots, warm hat, gloves you can write in.
- **No tent stakes. Nothing that goes into the ground.** This cannot be overstated. The oldest rule in the basin, older than the Works, is that no one drills the fleece. Its deep interior holds water accumulated over centuries and that water is its own; the town takes only what falls and what condenses. A tent stake is a drill. If you're not lodged indoors, bring a freestanding shelter or a ground cloth weighted with stones, and set it on gravel, not on the bed.
- **Cold rations, or a plan to eat with hosts.** No open flame, ever — see below. If you need hot food, ask the engine-keeper whether a pot can sit near the Works' waste heat. Ask. Don't improvise.
- **No fire of any kind.** Not a stove, not a candle, not a spark striker, nothing smoked. The fleece is resinous — that is why nothing eats it — and resin is what the town dries for a year in the long sheds and burns in the boiler. You will be walking on the town's fuel supply. In a dry week, treat every step as if you are walking across a stocked hearth that happens to be alive. For light, bring a shielded, enclosed lantern and keep it low and warm-toned; the town runs a handful of bulbs off a small dynamo and a harsh white light would be its own kind of rudeness.
- **A notebook and pencil.** The place rewards slow looking. Also bring your own food packaging out with you — there is no waste loop here for foreign material. Ash from the boiler goes back to the cut sites; your trash goes back in your pack.
- **Everything medical you'll need.** There is no clinic. The engine-keeper likely has a kit; the evacuation route is the rim trail, an hour uphill in loose gravel. Don't get hurt. The one mercy: the floor itself is ankle-to-knee-deep cushion. It is a forgiving place to fall.
- **Gifts.** Non-living, non-flammable, useful. Good chalk for the water slate — it is chalked every single morning and the town runs on it. A small mirror is a genuinely thoughtful choice: this is a town with almost no standing water, where people come out after rain specifically to look at reflections in the drown-hollows. And bring news from downhill. They have little of it.

## What to leave behind

- **The botanists' question.** Whether the fleece is one plant or ten thousand fused has been argued downhill for a century. The town's opinion is that the question is rude. Do not ask it. Do not take a cutting to check.
- **Any thought of samples.** No cuttings, no resin, no ash, no snails, no moths, no seeds, no water beyond your ration. This is partly etiquette and partly cross-world quarantine, and the quarantine is not a formality: cuttings from anywhere in the Catch root identically. A fragment of this organism in another universe — one with no ring-moth, no cold basin, no warden of the margins — is either a dead fragment or an existential problem. Neither is acceptable.
- **Loudness.** Leave the speaker, the drone, the project voice. The fleece eats sound the way fresh snow does, permanently. Bells struck at the Works come out clipped, tails gone. Voices carry a little way and then stop. You will learn to speak at the volume of the place, which is low.
- **The boathouse joke.** There is a painted boathouse on the fleece, used to store pipe fittings, a day's walk from any water a boat could float in, under shorelines that predate memory. Nobody finds it funny anymore. Let it be what it is.

## Arriving

From the rim it looks like a puddle that forgot to evaporate — a grey-green pool with two thin stacks and a thread of steam. Walk down. The silence arrives before the town does; notice your footsteps vanish from the soundscape while the ground is still gravel, then fleece.

Present yourself at the engine-house — it's the civic heart: small, brick gone soft-edged, breathing steam that smells of green smoke. Read the slate outside. Those chalked numbers are the town's whole condition, updated each dawn, and reading them tells you more than any introduction. Ask for the **warden of the margins** and the **gauger**; the warden is the person most invested in where your feet and your gear have been, and it's wise to meet them early.

That first evening, go out and stand in the cold. Feel it flowing around your ankles, downhill, toward the lowest point. Then sit on the fleece. It is warm — compost warmth from its dense interior — and sitting on it in the cold air feels like sitting on the back of some great sleeping animal. Visitors make that comparison exactly once, quietly. You will too. Make it, and then let it go.

## How to be there

**Attend the morning slate at first light at least once.** The gauger walks out, reads the cisterns, chalks the numbers; the dew-frames tick as they dry; the boiler breathes — you feel it through your soles as much as hear it — and the heron lifts its head. This is the town's pulse. Everything else is commentary.

**Walk the pressed lanes.** The fleece remembers weight. A habitual path becomes a pale pressed lane that lasts seasons; a cart left standing a month leaves a dent for a year. The townspeople read the floor without meaning to — who walked where, at what hour, toward whose door. Accept this completely. Everywhere you go in the Catch is legible by morning. Use the existing lanes, walk openly, and do not creep; creeping just leaves sneaky lanes. Don't stand or sit in one spot on the bed for hours, and never leave gear resting on the fleece — set packs on the swept gravel margins or on stone. Small objects dropped on the bed are slowly swallowed. A shed abandoned a century ago is now a green hummock with a chimney poking out like a periscope, and the town points children at it. Do not become a smaller version of that lesson.

**Offer to sweep a margin.** Around every house, cistern, and pipe junction runs a bare gravel strip a hand's width wide, and keeping the fleece back from it is daily, unglamorous, absolute work. An hour of sweeping is the fastest way to stop being a visitor and start being a guest. It is also, not coincidentally, the most peaceful hour you'll spend there.

**Approach people in their line of sight.** Your footsteps are silent. Everyone's are. Wave. Be seen.

**Do not touch the dew-frames at dawn** — the angled glass and blackened fins are doing the town's drinking then — and don't shade them, don't lean on cisterns, and don't try to out-stare the cistern-heron. There is exactly one heron. It has the air of an inspector, it is tolerated, half considered an omen, and full considered a colleague. Treat it as staff.

**Give yourself one small task.** Copy a map. Learn the margin work. Sketch the frames. Because the thing that will press on you by day two or three is not the silence — it is the *settledness*. Nothing in the Catch is becoming anything else. The town is not growing and not dying; it takes what regrows, drinks what falls, and waits. Time is measured in boilers (the Works is on its sixth; each lasts about a human life), in cuttings (a person sees two or three), in blooms (before and after). Some travelers find this the deepest rest of their lives. Some find it unbearable. A small daily task gives the stillness a shape you can hold.

## If it rains

Drop everything. The basin darkens within minutes and releases a green medicinal scent, and this is the loudest and best day of the season — the whole town goes out to stand in it. Secure paper and anything water-sensitive first, fast, then go out and stand in it too, on the warm floor, in the cool air. In the first hour the sealed snails appear by the hundred — watch your step near stones — and the drown-hollows begin to fill, and people come to the edges of them to look at reflections. For two or three days the Catch has wetlands, and animals, and mirrors. Then it dries, and goes back to the long patient gleaning of the air, and you will have been there for the thing everyone waits for.

## Leaving

Inspect everything. Boots, cuffs, pack seams, anything that sat on stone — the snails glue themselves shut against hard surfaces and a pack set down carelessly is a hard surface. If you visited anywhere near a bloom, clean the seed-ash out of everything daily and again at departure. You take no part of the fleece out of this universe. That is the one law your travel office should enforce with real consequences.

Settle your accounts precisely — water, food, lodging. This is a town whose nearest thing to priests insist they are only accountants; precise payment is a form of respect, not a transaction. Walk your sit-spots and your camp spot and collect every foreign object. The pressed lanes you leave behind are acceptable — the floor is a palimpsest, and your passing is now part of what it says — but nothing you carried in should remain.

Say goodbye at the slate, in the morning, in a low voice. Walk up out of the cold pool with the sun on the gravel. And expect this: for weeks afterward, in noisier worlds, you'll find yourself listening for the tick of condensate at dawn, and speaking more quietly than the room requires, and thinking about a town in the bottom of a bowl that has everything it needs because it decided, long ago, what enough was.
