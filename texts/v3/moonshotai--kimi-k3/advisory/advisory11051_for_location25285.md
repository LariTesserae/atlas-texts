# Preparing for the Finished Room

Most places, you visit. This one, you *happen to*. Understand that before anything else: the room has had perhaps one true event in all of memory — the stillness of the Eel, once, recorded in molts — and your arrival will be the second. You are not a guest. You are an occurrence on the scale of their calendar, and everything you pack, everything you practice, every hour you spend inside should be organized around that fact.

Here is how to prepare.

## 1. Rehearse Your Scale

The people stand a hand high. Their city — nine hundred lives, stacked niches, granary-shelves, cord-bridges, cisterns, ancestors sealed in clay — is a crust on the walls, dense as coral, and to you it is at the scale of a wasp nest. Your knee is a neighborhood. Your leaning shoulder is a landslide. Your dropped glove is a roof that never lifts.

So train before you go, literally: hours of slow-motion movement, kneeling and rising over minutes, placing hands and feet as though the floor were thin ice over something sleeping. Practice never reaching for anything quickly. Speed is violence in a slow world. Nothing in that room has ever moved as fast as a startled human hand, and nothing should.

Better yet: plan to keep your body out of the room entirely. The best berth is the threshold of the last water-choke — the crawl where the passage opens — where you can lie prone, chin at the waterline, and let the chamber hold you in its acoustics without holding your weight. A full visit conducted from the threshold is not a lesser visit. It is the only polite one.

## 2. The Approach

Do not attempt to reach the room under your own power. The route floods to the ceiling; the sumps have never been sounded. That is not an approach, it is a disappearance, and it would be read by the people in the worst possible way — because the only thing that comes up through the sumps is the Eel. Arriving through the floor would make you either a blasphemy or a second exception, and the room's law has room for exactly one.

Arrange your transit to set you down at the last dry crawl, on the mountain side of the water-choke. Expect the final meters to be full immersion in eight-degree water, in absolute darkness, in a space the size of a coffin. If you cannot hold your breath and your nerve through that, practice cold immersion and confinement until you can — or do not go.

## 3. Your Breath Is Weather

This is the preparation most travelers will resist and the one that matters most.

The room has never had weather. The air sits at eight degrees, saturated, unchanging, and the stone is *finished* — the seep-water carries exactly the mineral load of the water already present, so nothing deposits and nothing dissolves. The chamber exists in perfect chemical standstill, and that standstill is the deepest fact of the place.

You are a warm animal exhaling carbon dioxide. Every breath you release into that chamber is its first wind, its first climate, a chemical perturbation in a room whose entire meaning is equilibrium. The calcite will notice, on a timescale you won't live to see — but the acolytes tending the Chord will notice sooner, because a warm body in a cold still room moves air, and moved air moves sound.

Wear a closed-circuit rebreather or a CO₂-scrubbing mask for the duration of your visit. Yes, for the whole visit. Yes, it will be uncomfortable. You are not entitled to exhale on a finished world.

## 4. Your Heat and Your Light

You are a hundred-watt furnace in an eight-degree room, and the room's oldest duty is a fence of fire. The veils — the iron-ochre and salt-white bacterial films the people farm, the slow white hunger that creeps along every seep-line — will not cross a warm, lit threshold. The lamp-line holds the border.

Which means: **if you stand near a wall, the veils will retreat from your warmth.** You will push the border back like a lamp the size of a city district, disrupting a farm rotation that has run for ten thousand years, and it will be read as miracle or assault depending on which wall you chose. Stay away from the walls. Stay at the threshold, where your heat spills back into the mountain and not into the room.

Bring no lamp. Their hundreds of trimmed grease-flames, ember-glow shattering across calcite and doubled in the black floor, are their noon — and your headlamp would be a second sun, blinding eyes that drink dimness, and worse, it would function as a weaponized border against the veils wherever you swept it. Use a low-gain night-vision device instead, the passive kind that adds no photons to the room. Let your eyes adapt to their noon. It takes twenty minutes. Spend them lying still at the threshold, which you should be doing anyway.

Dress for wet cold under all of this: eight degrees, saturated air, condensation forming on you as it forms on the stone. You will be chilled to the bone by hour two and you must not shiver — shivering is motion, and motion is noise.

## 5. Your Voice

The Chord fills the room the way water fills a sump — completely, to the ceiling, without pause. Hundreds of drips, each with its interval and pitch, folded by the stone into a single standing note. For the people it is the medium of existence, tended like the lamps, censused like the dead.

Your ordinary speaking voice, released into that acoustics, is a thunderclap that goes on happening. It will shake pegged molts off their rows. It will drown the catch-basins' notes. It may be the loudest thing that has ever occurred in the world.

So: do not speak. Agree with any companions beforehand — total silence within the room — or better, go alone. Because here is the other thing: the room is a whispering gallery. A breath shaped against one wall arrives intact at the far end. There is no privacy for you there; privacy in that city is quietness, and you are not quiet, and never will be. Assume total audit. Every murmur you make will be received, by nine hundred listeners, in full.

If you must signal, learn to do it in their register: a single soft tap, fitted into a gap in the Chord, repeated at long intervals. One pattern, always the same, meaning *I alter nothing on purpose*. Practice it against the rhythm of falling water before you arrive. It is the only sentence you need.

And prepare your own mind for the Chord. After an hour, the human brain begins to hear voices in a standing chord — speech, choirs, your name. This is pareidolia, not revelation. Expect it, welcome it, don't act on it. Bring soft earplugs for rest periods if you stay past a few hours, but spend most of your visit listening. Listening is the visit.

## 6. The Nine Hundred

The number does not change. Do not let anything you do change it. This means: no medicines offered, no interventions in an accident you witness, no rescue that is not already their law. A death that occurs while you are present will be attributed to you regardless of cause — you are a walking sign, and signs do not get to argue causality. Accept this going in.

Practical protocol:

- **Never touch one of them.** Your fingertip is a warm, oily slab the size of their doorway. If an exchange is ever invited, offer a clean, cold, wet slate shard as an intermediary surface, placed down slowly and left for them to approach.
- **Let them set the distance.** Coracles will come to the floor's edge. Dark eyes will gather at the niche-mouths. The acolytes will come nearest of all, because they must measure what you have done to the Chord.
- **Accept correction.** If small hands guide you — a pressure against your sleeve, a cord strung to redirect you — you are being moved off a drip-line, out of an acoustic node, away from a wall of ancestors. Being tuned by the acolytes is not hostility. It is the deepest hospitality the room has: they are treating you as an element of the instrument, something that can be brought into agreement, rather than as weather to be survived.
- **The walls are not walls.** They are dwellings, granaries, archives of molts, and sealed family dead, and they are faintly resonant — knock anywhere and the knock comes back slightly answered. Do not knock. Do not lean. Do not rest a hand on any surface of the city, ever.
- **If the Eel passes, stop.** Everyone stops. Work ceases, coracles hold their poles, and the people stand in the slow displacement of it, feeling the longest unit of their year move beneath the floor. You will feel it too — a pressure in the water, a roundness in the foot. Stand in it. Do not speak of it afterward; there is nothing you could say that would add to it.

## 7. The Law of Matter

Two rules, absolute:

**Leave nothing.** The geology is finished. Nothing erodes, nothing buries, nothing dissolves. A dropped crumb does not rot away — it sits on the finished stone for ten thousand years, an eternal foreign body, and it is also an ecological bomb in a nutrient-starved system, and it is also, to the people, an object the size of a granary that appeared from nowhere. Control your lint. Tie your hair. Count your gear in and count it out. If you lose something, you do not leave until it is found.

**Take nothing.** Not a molt, not a veil-thread, not a cupped palm of the black water, not a chip of calcite. The veils in particular must never leave the room — the slow white hunger, given a dark wet corner of another world, is a multiversal contamination event wearing the face of a souvenir. Decontaminate every surface of your kit before transit home, including the soles of your feet.

If you feel the need to give — and you will, standing small before all that completeness — understand that material gifts are only scaled-down versions of the same catastrophe. The one currency the room accepts is memory. Their storage is pegged molts, sealed niches, and recitation. So give something recitable: a rhythm tapped into the Chord's gaps, a pattern they can keep without mass or cost. Teach them your name as a rhythm. It will outlast anything you could have carried in.

## 8. Conduct Yourself Like the Eel

The room has exactly one conceptual slot for "a thing that comes from elsewhere and returns": the Eel. Their single word means room, and world, and now — you arrive from a place their language cannot hold. Expect to be filed, semantically, as Eel-kind.

So take the template seriously, because it is the only etiquette for visitors that their world has ever generated:

- **Be slow.** The Eel's circuit takes a generation. Nothing about you should hurry.
- **Be felt, not heard.** Low presence, minimal sound, pressure rather than announcement.
- **Be rare.** Come once. Do not return on your own initiative — a second visit would demand a theology, and they cannot send you an invitation. Let the molts close over the memory of you at the natural pace of the room.
- **Pass through and leave the note unchanged.** When the acolytes recite the census of drops after your departure, let every fall be what fell.

## 9. The Kit

- Closed-circuit rebreather or CO₂-scrubbing mask, tested, with duration margin
- Passive night-vision device (no illuminator)
- Dry layers and insulation for 8°C saturated air; nothing that sheds lint
- Soft, sterile, decontaminated footwear — cleaned *both* directions
- No food, no drink containers that open inside the room, nothing organic
- Soft earplugs (for you, in rest periods only)
- One slate shard, clean and cold, if an exchange surface is needed
- A small waterproof tally of everything carried in, for the count-out
- Nothing else. If it isn't on this list, it doesn't go.

## 10. Duration and Departure

Stay hours, not days. Every hour is an hour of warm breath scrubbed or not, of absorbed sound, of the room accommodating you. Two hours at the threshold, moving almost not at all, is a full visit. Four is generous. Leave before you are comfortable — comfort is the moment you start treating a world as scenery.

Leave as you arrived: through the water-choke, never toward the sump-mouths in the floor, which are their only doors and whose law you must honor even in reverse. Count your gear. Check your seams and boot-treads for veil-filament. Let your last act inside be the long stillness you began with.

## 11. Prepare for the Return

This is the part no one believes in advance.

You will come home to a world of weather, and it will feel loud and provisional and *unfinished* — because it is. Rooms will feel temporary. Silence will feel empty in a way you won't be able to explain, because you have heard what a room sounds like when silence is not merely absent but unimaginable. For weeks, you will catch yourself listening for a chord in the dripping of a gutter. You will tap rhythms into the gaps of ordinary noise without deciding to.

That is normal. You were recited into the memory of a finished world — pegged, in a sense, alongside the molts of a generation — and some part of you stayed pegged. Give it time. Sit in the sun, which they cannot imagine, and let your own world come back into tune.

And if anyone asks you what it was like, remember the whispering gallery and answer the way the room taught you: quietly, once, and only what the whole world may hear.
