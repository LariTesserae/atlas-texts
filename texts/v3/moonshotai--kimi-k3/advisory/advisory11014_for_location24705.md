# Preparing for Anchor 7: A Traveler's Briefing

The first thing to understand is that you are not visiting a mountain. You are visiting an event — a very slow one, currently a finger's width taller than it was last year, ringed by the youngest ground in its world. Everything about your preparation follows from this: the place is not finished, it is not stable, and it is not indifferent to you in the way old stone is indifferent. It hums. It rings. On a schedule no one has cracked, it reminds you what is holding it up.

Prepare for two journeys: the one that gets you to the top, and the one the top itself is on.

---

## 1. Getting There Is the Real Expedition

The plateau is the easy part — flat, walkable in under an hour, with a bench to camp on. The hard part is that the top of a sheer, wet, glass-sharp, actively growing stone column stands between the ground and you.

**If your transit lets you choose your arrival point,** arrive on the plateau itself — on bare slab near the field station bench, in mid-morning, never at dusk (the cloud collar spills over and the world goes silver-blind) and never at night. Arrive anchored: clip yourself to your pack before you materialize, because you do not yet know whether you'll arrive on a calm day or into a quickening.

**If you must come up from the lowland,** budget days for the black-water channels — slow boat travel through reeds and steam, standard swamp discipline: guide, pole, water, insect protection, and patience. Then stop. **Do not plan to climb the column.** The faces are sheer, seeping, algae-slicked in rust and jade, sharp enough to cut rope-sheath and skin, and they calve without warning — you will hear the daily rifle-crack of the rock growing, and each crack is a reminder that the wall is in motion. The people who built this place came by air; the notch in the rim with its fused-stone pads is where things docked. If your frame of travel permits charter or lift of any kind, that notch is your landing zone. Rotor wash and the rim's permanent updraft argue for a morning drop, before the collar builds.

**Arrange your extraction before you arrive,** with a fixed date, a backup date, and a handler who treats a missed check-in as a trigger, not an oversight. The newer camp — patched tarp, tins, familiar insufficient gear — tells you the truth about rescue here: someone still comes. *Not often.* You must be prepared to be unfindable for weeks.

---

## 2. Altitude and Heat: The Double Bind

The summit sits above its own weather, in thin hot air. Prepare as you would for 3,500–4,500 meters, and for a furnace, simultaneously — an unusual pairing.

- **Pre-acclimatize.** If your transit is instantaneous, spend a week at altitude somewhere else first. Bring acetazolamide if you use it, a pulse oximeter, and treat your first 48 hours on top as rest days. There is no "descend to sleep" option; the plateau is one altitude.
- **The rock burns.** By noon the black stone is painful to touch. Carry light leather gloves for any midday contact with stone, test slabs with the back of your hand before kneeling or sitting, and pack burn gel alongside your cold-care — because the filament at the center drinks heat and can take skin the other way. You can be burned hot and cold within the same hundred meters.
- **Run a tropical-mountain schedule:** active from dawn to mid-morning and from late afternoon to dark. From roughly eleven to three, you sit under your tarp — pitched on open slab, **never under the cornices, never beneath the figs** — and you drink.
- **Sun:** UV at this height, in this latitude, is a daily dose. Long light-colored clothing, wide-brimmed hat *with a chin strap* (the rim updraft collects hats), glacier glasses, SPF 50 reapplied against the constant warm drip.

## 3. Water, Feet, and the Warm Wet World

Everything up there is wet and warm — blood-warm seeps, bath-warm marsh, warm rain, morning steam. This is a strange luxury with specific costs.

- **Drink the seeps, not the standing water.** The keepers' rule. The seep-water rises through the column's grain and arrives clean enough that generations of keepers drank it; filter or purify it anyway, because you are not a generation of keepers. The marsh is the filament's footprint — silt, algae, bladderwort threads, and the stage for the dome. Do not drink it, do not soak wounds in it.
- **Carry 3 liters of capacity and refill morning and evening.** In that heat you'll want 4–6 liters a day. Warm water is demoralizing to drink; bring electrolyte powder and something tart. There is no cold water on the plateau. Accept this.
- **Your feet are the weak point.** Constant warm moisture means immersion foot within days if you're careless. Rotate two pairs of socks minimum, dry your feet completely at midday, use antifungal powder from day one as prevention, and bring camp sandals. The ground itself radiates a slow vegetable warmth through your boot soles — pleasant, and a warning that nothing ever dries on its own up there. Dry bags for everything; nothing cotton.

## 4. The Keepers' Four Rules — and the Ones You Must Add

Someone stood by that station door for years, scratching tautenings into a slab in neat columns, thousands of them. The four rules implied by the description are the distilled survival knowledge of that vigil. Treat them as scripture:

1. **Don't shelter under the cornices.** The rim is knife-edged, young, and calves without warning — fresh scars prove it. Stay a full body's length back from the edge at all times, farther after you hear the daily crack, and never camp, nap, or take shade beneath any overhang, anywhere, including at the base.
2. **When the hum quickens, tie down your gear and stand back from the marsh.** Better: *never untie your gear.* Everything you own lives lashed, every day, because the tautening comes on no schedule. When you feel the stillness gather, you should have nothing to do but step back and sit down.
3. **Drink the seeps, not the standing water.** (Above.)
4. **Don't try to follow the droplets with your eyes for too long.** Note the gentleness of the phrasing — this is a rule written by someone who watched a visitor fail at it. The droplets climb the filament in a slow unbroken procession, against everything your inner ear believes, and something about tracking them costs people more than attention. Whatever the mechanism — vertigo, hypnosis, time-slippage — the defense is identical: glance, note, look down at your feet. Set a timer if you're observing. Through binoculars or a camera screen is *worse*, not safer. If a companion says your name twice and you didn't hear the first one, you're done at the center for the day.

Now the rules the keepers were too close to write down:

5. **Never touch the filament with skin.** It drinks heat in a landscape of warmth. Cold that patient will take skin on contact. Gloved, brief, once — or not at all. Not at all is better.
6. **Time-box the center.** Within twenty meters the hum becomes a pressure in sternum and teeth, cycling every ninety seconds, and the standing ripples breathe with it. Fifteen minutes inside the seep-lines, then out. Log how you feel after. Infrasound of that constancy wears on mood and sleep in ways you won't notice happening.
7. **Sleep where the keepers slept:** the bench above the marsh, near the station. It is above the dome's reach, off the seep-lines, back from the rim, and out of the strongest of the hum. Every element of that camp placement is a lesson.
8. **Do not test the artifacts.** The dark thread on its spool has never been cut by any tool brought against it, and your tools will not be the exception — everyone tries; the spool shows no marks; your knife will lose. Don't press the keypad's unalphabetic keys. Don't climb the pylons or drop pebbles into their sockets; a sedge lives in each one now.
9. **Write your departure date down before you arrive, and keep it.** More on this below. It is the most important rule on this list.
10. **Leave nothing, and I mean nothing.** The soil on that plateau is an inch thick and thirty years old. You cannot dig a cathole in a world that young — bring wag bags and pack out everything. No fires, ever: the moss, the peat, the sedge are not fuel, they are *the world so far*. Cook on a stove, on bare slab.

## 5. The Tautening Drill

Every few days, on no schedule anyone has found: the hum quickens, a stillness gathers, the column rings once like a struck bell through a wall, the marsh stands up in a slow shallow dome, holds, and settles — and near the center, for a moment, things fall *wrong*. Leaves at half speed. Pebbles uphill.

Rehearse this until it is boring:

- **You will feel it before anything happens.** A quickening in the chest-pressure, a gathering quiet. Trust it immediately; do not wait for confirmation.
- **Step back from the marsh edge,** away from the center, away from the rim, onto open bare slab. If you are inside the seep-ring when it starts, walk out steadily — do not run on algae-slick young rock.
- **Sit down. Hold your pack** (it is already lashed, because it is always lashed). Wait.
- **Expect the bell, the dome, and a few seconds of local wrongness** if you are anywhere near the center. Half-speed falling feels like a dream and is over before you can be frightened by it — which is exactly why you rehearse: startlement, not physics, is the danger. A startled person steps backward without looking, and on that plateau the wrong step ends at a knife-edge.
- **Afterward:** check your guy lines, scan the rim from a safe distance for fresh scars, expect renewed dripping, and — this matters — **log the date and time.** The keeper's tally is the only long record this place has. Continue it *in your own notebook*. Do not add scratches to the slab; that is one person's life written in stone, and your marks in it would be forgery.

If the pattern ever breaks — repeated ringing, a dome that holds, a hum that shifts off its ninety-second baseline for more than an hour — that is undocumented weather. Pack, move to the notch, activate your beacon, and document everything. Curiosity is what the tally slab is *for*; it is not what your body is for.

## 6. Campcraft on Ground That Is Still Arriving

- **Pitch on the bench or on bare slab.** Stakes won't hold in glassy fracture and you must not drive them into seep-lines or peat — use guylines tied to rock-filled stuff sacks. Low-profile tent; the wind combs steadily over the rim and the updraft hunts loose objects.
- **Rig a handline** between tent, water point, and the station. When the collar spills over in the evening, the plateau goes inside its own weather — silver light, zero visibility, everything dripping. People have walked off edges in fog on friendlier mountains. In whiteout conditions: line, whistle, stay put.
- **The daily crack** — once a day, like a distant rifle shot — is the column growing. You will jump every time. Everyone does. It is also your cue to mentally re-check what is overhead and what is underfoot.
- **The loose grit walks inward** toward the filament over the years. Yours will too — crumbs, threads, anything small you drop. Pack with this in mind. Leave no offerings; the place does not need them and is already collecting.
- **Step only on bare rock and the fused-stone pads.** The moss cushions are years of growth; the sundews are small and jeweled and everywhere you want to kneel; the black ferns grip cracks that didn't exist when their spores landed. Check the ground before every knee and hand. The seven figs you will count without meaning to are not shade, not food, not firewood — they are the entire forest. Walk around them. The one dark frog you will likely never see is busy being the only amphibian on a world of stone; leave it to its single evening knock. The swifts own the updraft and at dusk they ride the strange lift along the filament itself, up after the climbing droplets until they vanish — watch them do it, but from the bench, and do not fly anything of your own up that line.

## 7. The Body's Kit (Condensed)

- **Wounds:** the rock is broken-glass sharp — closure strips, tourniquet, antiseptic, trauma shears (they won't cut the builders' thread; they will cut your gear free), tweezers.
- **Burns both ways:** gel for noon rock, and treat any filament contact as a cold injury.
- **Altitude:** acetazolamide, antiemetic, pulse oximeter. Note that the hum's ninety-second pulse can confound your felt sense of your own heartbeat — the oximeter is your ground truth.
- **Heat:** electrolytes in quantity, salts, antifungal powder, foot care kit, and more water-treatment redundancy than you think you need.
- **Mind:** a journal and pencils (pens hate warm wet), a watch for timing the pulse and tautenings, earplugs — not because they block the hum (it's in your sternum, not your ears) but because they take the edge off the dripping nights.
- **Comms:** satellite messenger or beacon, scheduled check-ins, whistle, signal mirror, headlamps ×2, compass for the fog (GPS is fine until the collar is inside your hood).

## 8. The Mind — the Hazard Nobody Packs For

Be honest with yourself about what prolonged exposure means. You will sleep within earshot — within *chest-shot* — of a pressure that pulses every ninety seconds, in silence so complete your own breath is often the loudest thing on the plateau. Infrasound at that constancy produces unease, poor sleep, and awe in unpredictable proportions. The place encourages ritual: the grit walks inward, the tallies accumulate, you will count the seven figs every day without deciding to. Counting is how humans cope here, and it works — until coping quietly becomes *staying*.

The keeper who scratched thousands of tallies into that slab arrived, presumably, planning to leave.

So: **precommit.** Fixed departure date, written down before arrival, shared with your handler, non-negotiable from inside the experience. If you travel with a companion — recommended — give each other explicit authority to call the trip for the other. If solo, treat your check-in schedule as a hard contract. Watch yourself for the specific drift this place induces: "just one more tautening," "the hum is different when I'm near it," "someone should stay and keep the count." Someone did. The slab is full.

That said — do not armor yourself so thoroughly that you miss it.

---

## 9. What You Are Actually Going For

Arrive at dawn. Watch the seeps breathe faint threads of steam off the warm stone while the light comes up gold through the haze. Walk the rim circuit — under an hour, a body's length back — and understand with your legs that the summit sits above its own weather, the cloud collar churning below you like a ceiling that can never quite rise. Count the figs. Find the frog's sedge. Photograph the ballooning spiders' webs at morning, every strand beaded with water creeping slowly upward along the silk — walk the pylon ring with a hand raised, and take the picture, not the silk.

At noon, retreat under the tarp while the black rock shimmers and the marsh gives off its slow vegetable warmth. Listen for the crack. Nap badly in the heat; everyone does.

Then stay for the evening, because evening is when it becomes most itself. The collar rises at last and spills over the rim, and for an hour you are inside the plateau's own weather — silver light, everything dripping, the figs gone to gray ghosts. The swifts come down the line in long spirals. The frog knocks once, a stone dropped into a deep well. And on the filament — dim, cold, patient, a line darker than the sky ruling the world in half — the water of your small wet summit beads, trembles, and begins its long climb back up.

You will not follow it with your eyes for long. That is the rule, and you will finally understand it, because you will feel the pull to watch and keep watching, and you will look down at your feet instead — at moss an inch deep on rock younger than you are, the youngest ground in the world, still being pulled up by its roots, in no hurry at all.

Become the kind of traveler who isn't either. Go home on the day you wrote down. If the place is still calling — and it will be — it will also still be there, a finger's width taller, whenever you are ready to be counted among those who came, and left, and came again.
