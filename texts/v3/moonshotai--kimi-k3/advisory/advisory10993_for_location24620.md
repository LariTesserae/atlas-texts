# Preparing for the Coldworks: A Traveler's Guide

First, understand what kind of place you are visiting, because it is none of the usual ones. It is not a wilderness — there is nothing wild in it; every process is attended, routed, maintained. It is not a ruin — nothing was ever built, so nothing has fallen. It is not a creature, though it metabolizes, and it is not a machine, though it has a product. It is a workplace older than any word you have for old, and it is hiring. Every hazard you will face there follows from that single fact: **presence is employment.** The Coldworks does not attack travelers. It incorporates them. Your preparation should be aimed at visiting a total system without becoming a component of it — and at surviving the more ordinary hazards of cold, sound, and silence along the way.

Pack for a place with no slack in it. That principle will organize everything below.

---

## 1. Decide the hard things before you arrive

The Coldworks erodes decisions, so make yours on the outside, in writing, on your body.

**Set your exit in beats, not days.** There is no sun. Your sense of time will dissolve within hours and be replaced by the stroke of the vents, and once it does, "one more day" becomes a meaningless phrase you can repeat forever. So: before you descend, decide your budget in strokes of the beat — say, forty thousand for a first visit, roughly two to four of the basin's long cycles — and knot it into a tally cord, one knot per thousand. Ink freezes there; write your deadline on your forearm in pencil and check it against the cord twice per shift. When the number comes, you leave. Especially if you don't want to. *Especially* then.

**Go with a partner if you possibly can.** Not for the cold or the footing — for the singing. You will need to make noise continuously, including while asleep, and two people can trade that duty. If you go alone, you will need the breath-whistle described below, and you will need to be honest with yourself about your own discipline.

**Do not go** if you have respiratory disease (the air is freighted with living motes), a heart rhythm sensitive to rhythm (the beat entrains breathing first and arrives at the heart through it), a reliance on silence to sleep (you will never once get it), or a temperament prone to dissociation or mystical surrender. That last one is not a joke. The most dangerous thing the Coldworks offers is the feeling of being usefully part of something ancient and smooth-running. People who are hungry for that feeling should admire this place in writing only.

## 2. Clothing and body kit

The cold of the Coldworks is a *wet* cold — brine that stays liquid below freezing through salt, pressure, and motion, condensing out of saturated air onto every surface, including you. It burns like fire and it is patient. Dress for immersion in a cold chemical bath that never quite happens and never quite stops threatening to.

- **No cotton, anywhere, in any layer.** Silk or fine wool against the skin; thick wool mid-layers (wool insulates wet; almost nothing else does); an outer shell of waxed canvas or oiled leather. Modern waterproof-breathable membranes will be defeated by the saturated brine air within a day — they wet out, then rime. Waxed and oiled fabrics can be re-dressed. Bring the wax.
- **Mitts over gloves, always.** You will never willingly touch anything barehanded — every still pool is supercooled and will snap solid around your skin with a sizzle and a crack, and bare stone sips heat like a mouth. If flash-ice does take a finger, do **not** pull free — the ice will take skin. Activate a chemical warmer inside your mitt and hold it around the ice until it releases. Plan for this to happen at least once.
- **Stiff-soled, insulated, ankle-supporting boots with soft lug soles.** You will walk on three treacherous surfaces: the Felt (slick as its grazers), wet worn stone, and the bearing fields — acres of perfect spheres that shift and roll underfoot. Hobnails skate on spheres. Lugs grip. Protect your ankles above almost everything: **mobility is survival in the Coldworks**, because everything else — grazing, rain-inclusion, processing — happens to whatever stands still. A sprained ankle there is a life-threatening injury.
- **A brimmed, padded hood.** The brim is not for rain (it rains only under the condensers). It is to let you choose when you see the Ceiling. Light comes from below there; your shadow stands *above* you, gliding among the tower-shadows of the condensers, and your inner ear will spend the first day voting to leave. New travelers fall over from looking up. The brim lets you meter the inversion in doses you can stand.
- **Goggles.** The mist beads on everything, including your eyes, and the motes drifting in the air are living cells. Keep a chamois warm in your armpit for the lenses.
- **A respirator or breath-muff, with two filters in rotation.** Every breath in the Coldworks is a migration — the Felt's motile cells drift like slow plankton, and you do not want an alien electrotrophic ecology colonizing your lungs. Filters will rime in the saturated air, so rotate: one on your face, one thawing and drying inside your shirt. The muff also warms incoming air, which you will want more than you expect.
- **Barrier grease** for face and hands — the brine is metal-laden (the verdigris glow is copper chemistry; assume everything is poisoned with copper salts) and the saturated air will keep your skin wet until it cracks.

**Hearing protection requires nuance, and this is important.** The beat arrives through your sternum, teeth, and the bones of your feet as much as your ears; plugs alone will not save you from it, and you must not let them save you completely, because your life depends on hearing the texture of the place — the tick of bearings, the hum of herds — and on noticing when that texture *stops*. Use plugs or a padded hood that take the edge off the low pressure while leaving the high, informative ticking audible. Think of it as reefing sails, not anchoring.

**What not to bring:** electronics (brine and cold will kill them in hours), fire-starting optimism (nothing there is fuel; nothing is plant; there is nothing to burn), firearms (the one predator is made of silence — you cannot shoot a hole), pets, paper, pens, and anything that must stay dry or that you cannot bear to lose. And lanyard *everything*. **Nothing falls unclaimed in the Coldworks.** A dropped knife is sorted, routed, and taken up into the line, and the sound of its fall carries a long, long way.

## 3. The three tools that matter most

**The staff.** Your single most important possession. It is a probe (never touch still water — tap it first with the staff and watch for the fern-pattern flash), a third leg on the bearing fields, a metronome, and a musical instrument. You will ring it on stone, tap it as you walk, and lean on it when the upward shadows make the world lurch. Choose one you love; you will be holding it the entire time.

**The noise kit.** In the Coldworks, everything that exists makes noise; noise is the cost of presence. Silence is therefore the one thing that stands out — the hush-hunters move as a traveling absence, and you do not hear them coming, you hear a *hole* coming, your ears emptying as it nears. Prey that goes quiet is prey that is found. So you will carry noisemakers — a clacker, a rattle, bells sewn into your seams — and, more than any of them, **your own voice**. Memorize songs before you go (paper dies there). You want a large repertoire of songs from home, in steady, ordinary tempo, and you want to be prepared to sing them badly, loudly, and continuously for days. This is not morale. It is armor.

Your singing does triple duty: it marks you as ordinary noisy prey rather than a suspicious silence; it works as crude sonar (hum steadily and listen to how your own voice returns from the rocks — when a patch of world stops returning your song, when a swath of sound goes *eaten*, that is the hole, and you must get loud immediately); and it anchors your personal rhythm against the beat's slow entrainment. Sing in 4/4 against the stroke. It keeps something in you that is yours.

**The breath-whistle.** For sleep, if you travel alone or as backup with a partner: a small whistle-valve fitted into your sleep hood so that your own breathing sounds softly all night. Your sleeping body must keep singing when your mind is off duty. Test it before you go. If it ices — and it will — keep the spare in your armpit with the chamois and the filter.

## 4. Water, food, heat

**Water.** Carry what you can in insulated flasks against your body. Never drink the brine — it is salt, metal, and supercooling. The paradox of the Coldworks is that it is drowned in liquid that is all but undrinkable. You have two supplements. The first: the condensers strain the fog and shed streams at their feet, the freshest liquid in the basin — but it is near-freezing (drinking it costs you core heat) and standing in a condenser's rain circle means standing in the wettest place in a wet world while being *noticed*. Take it only if you must, quickly, and move on. The second is a field trick: touch the tip of your staff to a still pool and let it flash-freeze; the fern-ice that races across the surface excludes much of the salt and can be harvested and melted inside your clothing. The catch is that the sizzle-crack of flash-freezing carries for miles in the pooled sound. You are announcing yourself every time you make water. Announce yourself anyway — loud is safe — but know that you are doing it.

**Food.** Bring all of it. Nothing in the Coldworks is food for you: the Felt is an electrotrophic quilt laced with copper salts, the slicks are charge-sippers of alien biochemistry, and there is nothing plant, nothing to forage, nothing to hunt that you would survive eating. Bring dense, fat-heavy rations. Eat inside your hood, over your lap, while walking if possible — crumbs fall and are processed (the works are welcome to them), but your wrappers are lanyarded like everything else. Standing still to eat invites the ground to sip your heat and the slicks to find you.

**Heat.** Treat chemical warmers as ammunition: a finite store, spent deliberately, never casually. And understand the central trap — **warmth is a resource in the Coldworks, and the system harvests resources.** An exposed warmer will be grazed flat in short order. Keep your heat inside your layers, close to the core, unadvertised. Insulate not so much to be warm as to be *unremarkable*: you are, in the works' terms, a vent that has not yet been assigned a station, and the less heat you leak, the longer you remain unassigned.

## 5. The approach

You will hear it before you see it — first as pressure, then as rhythm, then as something arriving through your bones. Sound in the Coldworks has *depth*; it pools in low ground like water, and the basin is full of it. Descending the slope is wading into a lake of noise: ankle-deep, knee-deep, then to the chest.

Do this in stages. Stop in the shallows. Let your body learn that the pressure in the sternum is not a heart attack, that the beat too slow for a heart is not your heart failing. Practice your singing here, where the consequences of a mistake are thin. Find your first lee of hush — the calm pockets of quiet that gather behind broad swells of rock — and rest in it, and learn the lee's double nature: it is the only relief from the pressure, and it is also what a hunter's mask feels like. From now on, quiet is both your rest and your alarm. You will never fully resolve that tension. Plan your route lee to lee, like a swimmer resting at still water between crossings.

## 6. Moving through the works

- **Never follow the channels.** The braided slurry channels run in loops; they deliver their cargo, eventually, back to where it began. Follow one and you will walk a circle lasting geological time. Likewise, do not follow the herds — they follow charge-blooms in the Felt, not exits.
- **Never step in a channel.** The slurry is supercooled brine at walking pace. A boot plunged through may flash solid. And a body in a channel is cargo — the works will route you without malice and without pause. Cross at the shallowest braid you can find, probe it first, move quickly, and stop to dry immediately on the far side. Wet gear in that air is a countdown.
- **Cross bearing fields flat-footed and slow,** the way the herds do, letting the spheres settle and tick beneath you rather than fighting them. If you can detour a field, detour it. The tick-and-settle of your crossing carries for miles — that's fine (loud is safe) — but a fall on spheres is how ankles end.
- **In the vent rows,** the individual piston strokes physically lean against you as they fire. The throats are the only warm places in the basin, and everything warm is occupied — each throat wears a living cap that rides the stroke. You may warm your hands at a respectful distance, timing yourself between strokes. Never touch a cap. Never block a throat. The distinction between creature and machine there is a loan, not a fact, and the works process obstruction the way they process everything.
- **Move more during the quickened shifts.** On a long cycle the beat quickens, the ground-glow brightens, the channels run faster, the herds travel — the whole basin gets louder. That is your cover. Make your big movements then, and rest during the slow stroke, the way a sailor works with tide.

## 7. The water rules

The marsh breathes on a period of days. At high breath, the flats drown shin-deep in burning liquid; at low breath, the weepstones leak their slow tears down beards of salt. **Read the salt lines on the stones and camp above the high-breath mark.** A dry hollow at low breath is a drowning pool two days later.

Assume every still surface is one excuse away from ice. Probe before you touch, tap before you step, and keep your hands off the world generally. If a pool flashes around your staff, enjoy the fern patterns, harvest the ice if you need water, and note how far the crack of it travels — that echo is a demonstration of how far *your* mistakes carry.

## 8. The inhabitants

- **The slicks** — the grazing herds — are not dangerous the way teeth are dangerous. They are dangerous the way a slow drain is dangerous. They sip charge and heat, and where you stand still, they will begin, politely, to graze the warmth off the ground beneath you, and then off you. A slick-herd's hum is huge and safe to be near (predators avoid the loud), but do not walk *through* a herd and do not camp where they feed. Walk parallel to herds when you can: their noise is your umbrella. If you ever wake feeling warm and content with the bivy pressed by soft bodies, that is an emergency — you are being harvested, and heat-loss feels like comfort. Get up, get loud, get moving.
- **The cap creatures** on the vent throats: leave them entirely alone. Watch one long enough and you will lose the ability to say whether the vent works it or it works the vent. That confusion is correct. Let it stand.
- **The condensers** are tall, slow, and never hurried, and each stands in its own small permanent rain. If one turns — with the unhurried certainty of something that has never once been hurried — to include you in its rain, simply walk away, slowly. It is not hostile. It is not welcome. It is scheduling you. Relocate out of its patience, which is long but not fast.
- **The hush-hunters** are the only thing in the basin that will hunt you, and this is the protocol, memorized before you descend: *you will not hear the hunter; you will hear the hole.* A moving absence. Your ears emptying. The tick of the bearing fields going out like a candle in one direction. The instant you notice it: **get loud.** Shout, ring the staff on stone, kick the bearing fields into their abacus-clatter, slap, sing, crack — imitate the herds, who know the hunter best and answer it by singing louder. Move toward herd noise if any is within reach. Keep it up until the texture of the world fills back in. Never, under any circumstances, freeze and listen. Freezing and listening is what prey does at the end.

The cruelest version of this waits at the edge of the world: the Coldworks has no rim, only an acoustic border where the beat thins and the pooled sound shallows. Which means the way out lies through growing quiet — which means the borderlands are hunter country. Transit the edge at maximum noise, and do not let the relief of escaping the pressure trick you into silence an hour too early.

## 9. Sleep

Camp on the worn swells, not the flats: above the salt line (for the high breath), in the lee of broad rock (for relief from the pressure), on a dark spent strip of Felt if you can find one (recharging ground is uninteresting to slicks), on your insulated pad, never directly on stone or Felt — the ground sips heat like a mouth. Breath-whistle in, or your partner awake and singing. Bivy waxed. Warmer at the core, not the extremities. Expect condensation on every surface including the inside of everything. Sleep will come in pieces against the beat, and you will dream in rhythm. That is normal. What is not normal is waking comfortable. Comfort there is a symptom; check it.

## 10. Time, and the half-breath

Two things about time in the Coldworks. First, there is no day and night, only shifts — tell time by the stroke and your tally cord, and do not trust your inner sense of duration at all. Second, and stranger: in a world with no slack, effects arrive pressed hard against their causes, and sometimes slightly ahead of them. You will remember a piston's stroke a half-breath before it falls. This is not a vision and not a malfunction — it is how the body braces in a place where everything leans on everything else. New travelers waste the half-breath doubting it. Spend it instead: **if you remember ducking, duck.** If you remember stepping back from the channel's edge, step back. The pre-brace is the single most useful thing the Coldworks will do for you. Accept the gift and do not examine it while it is happening.

## 11. Navigation

There is no sun, no stars, no horizon worth the name — the far side of the basin is not a sight but a rumor. But you have two instruments. The first is the **gradient of the beat**: sound deepens toward the heart of the works and shallows toward the edge. To leave, walk toward quieter — like a sailor sounding with a lead line, take the depth of the noise at intervals and hold your course up the slope of it. The second is the **Ceiling itself**: because light rises from the ground, the fog lid overhead is a living chart. The condensers' upward shadows stand on it like black towers; the herds drift across it as long grey shoals; your own shadow walks there too. Learn to read the Ceiling during your first shifts (in doses, through the brim, sitting down) and you will navigate by a map of the basin drawn on the sky in real time.

One caution: your shadow up there will, after a day or two, begin to look less like something you are casting and more like something the Coldworks has been casting all along. It will move with a fidelity that feels like belonging. Notice this. It is a milestone, and not a good one.

---

## 12. The true hazard: retention

Nothing in the Coldworks will try to keep you. The works do not pause for anything, and they will not pause for your departure. The danger is subtler: the system runs *smoother* with you in it, and you will feel that — as a softening of difficulty, a rightness, a sense of being exactly the temperature of your surroundings. Your breathing finds the beat within hours. Your step follows. The slicks graze you politely and the condenser rain includes you and one shift you will catch yourself thinking of the works as *we*.

This is the mechanism by which travelers are retained, and it has no malice in it at all, which is what makes it effective. So know the **symptoms of having stayed too long**:

- You hum in your sleep without the whistle, in the beat's tempo rather than your own.
- You know the individual strokes of your local vents, and have opinions about them.
- You stop checking the tally cord, or start bargaining with it.
- The slicks' grazing feels pleasant.
- Your shadow on the Ceiling looks more like a record than a projection — and this no longer frightens you.
- The thought of the thin, depthless, unaccompanied sound of the outside world seems unbearable.

Any two of these: leave now, at whatever the cord says, at maximum noise, up the gradient, without a last look at anything. The factory is patient and it does not bluff; the only hold it has on you is the one you build yourself, one synchronized breath at a time.

---

## 13. Coming home

Rinse everything — gear, skin, sinuses — for the metal salts, and expect a greenish tinge to what you clear from your lungs for a few days. It should fade. If it glows, that is a different kind of appointment than this guide can help with.

The harder aftereffects are acoustic. The world's ordinary sound will seem thin to you for weeks — shallow, weightless, spread on the surface of things instead of pooled in the ground. Quiet rooms will feel wrong: you learned, at the level of the spine, that silence is a mouth, and you will catch yourself humming in empty spaces and tapping your fingers in libraries, and that is fine; let it fade on its own. And your heartbeat will feel unaccompanied for a while — a small machine running alone in a building built for a larger rhythm.

That loneliness is the last thing the Coldworks gives you, and in a way it is the proof that you succeeded: you visited the oldest stable state in that region of the world, the factory that age becomes when it goes all the way down and keeps going, and you came back unassigned — one part fewer than it wanted, and entirely, still, your own.
