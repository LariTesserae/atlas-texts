# Preparing for the Well

## The first thing to understand: you are cargo the city would like to harvest

Everything else on this list is secondary to one piece of physics. The basin sits at forty-five degrees with the air nearly saturated, which means the wet-bulb temperature — the number that decides whether a human body can shed heat — is essentially forty-five degrees. The ceiling for human survival is about thirty-five. The people who built this place didn't leave because it was unpleasant. They left because it crossed a line that cannot be trained around, toughened out, or acclimatized past. You will not adapt to the Well. Nobody did.

And here is the detail that should govern all your planning: at forty-five degrees saturated, the air is *warmer than your skin*. Heat stops flowing out of you and starts flowing in. Worse — you are one of the coldest surfaces in the valley, which means the air will condense on you. You will drip, like the pipes drip. Every droplet that forms on your skin delivers its latent heat directly into you. In the Well, you are a condenser fin with opinions, and the city is very, very good at its job.

Unprotected, at rest, you have perhaps an hour before confusion and collapse, less if you exert yourself. So the entire philosophy of the visit is this: **the rim is your shore, and the basin is a dive.** You do not travel to the Well. You stage above it and descend for sorties.

## Staging

Camp on the rim road, above the thermal wall. You will feel the boundary physically — the heat leans out over the edge like something with weight. Your camp needs three things:

- **A cooled or at least dehumidified refuge.** A vehicle with air conditioning, or a sealed shelter with a powered dehumidifier. This is not a luxury; it is your recompression chamber. You will sleep here — you cannot sleep in the basin, your core temperature won't allow it — and you will recover here between descents.
- **A freezer or ice supply.** Your cooling gear rotates through it.
- **A dry box.** Airtight, with desiccant. Everything you own that can rust, fog, or mold lives in it when not in use. Nothing dries in the Well. Nothing.

Bring a route card and a check-in schedule with whoever stays topside, or a beacon if you travel alone. There is no one down there to find you. The one live screen in the control room updates for no one, and it will not update for you.

## The cooling rig

- **Ice vest or phase-change vest, with at least three rotations of inserts.** This is your primary life support. A fresh insert buys you roughly an hour to ninety minutes of working time, depending on exertion.
- **A liquid-cooled undergarment if you can get one**, worn under light full-coverage clothing. Full coverage anyway — not for the heat but for the felt, the webs, and the centipedes.
- **Waterproof boots, gaiters, gloves.** The low districts stand at ankle depth. The water carries rat-borne leptospira, hydrocarbons, and the felt's spores. No bare skin in the water, ever, and cover every cut before you descend.
- **A head net** for dusk near the lamps, when the midges ball up and the moth cones boil.
- **A P100 respirator**, carried but not necessarily worn — breathing through a mask in that air is real work. Use it when you enter enclosed warehouses (spore load, midge balls) or if you disturb the felt.

## Fieldcraft

**Plan your route cold-spot to cold-spot.** The basin is full of refrigerated surfaces — the fins themselves, the condensate gutters at their bases, the cold pipes sweating their rivulets. The fresh runoff at the fin rows is the cleanest water in the city, and it is cold. Kneel, put your forearms and wrists in it, press your palms to a fin. This is shade-to-shade desert travel, inverted: you hop between cold instead of shadow.

**Walk the lee of the fin fields.** Air that has passed through the ranks has given up its water. It comes off the fans warmer but *drier* — and dry air, even hot dry air, lets your sweat work again. The discharge side of a fin field is the only place in the basin where your body can cool itself. If you feel heat strain coming on, make for the downwind side of the nearest ranks and stand in the fan wash.

**Turnaround discipline.** Set a hard time limit before you descend — ninety minutes maximum with full cooling, forty-five without — and honor it the way a diver honors a tank. Heat illness begins with exactly the poor judgment that would make you stretch the limit. Headache, chills, cramps, nausea, the sense that the place is *interesting enough to stay a little longer*: that last one is a symptom too.

**Navigate deliberately.** There is no sun — a brighter coin of gray slides overhead at noon and dissolves, useless. The avenues run kilometer-straight and identical in the permanent dusk, and half the sodium lamps are dead in no pattern. Mark your route with paint, not chalk (chalk washes; everything washes). Count cross streets. Learn the pump-thud — it's subsonic, felt in the ribs before it's heard, and it's your beacon home. Stack Four has the owl; use the stacks as you would use mountains.

**Respect the crawler.** The freight crawler runs a buried schedule with no driver and no reason to stop. Its route is your lifeline to the rim station, and you can ride it if you time it — but treat its path as live rail. Listen for it. In a city that deadens sound, the machine you don't hear is the one that matters.

**Footing.** The felt is slick. The silt is fine and sorted and hides everything. Calthemite under the bridges is polished slime pretending to be stone. The buildings stand with open doors and skeletonized interiors — the wood is long eaten, the stairs are suggestions, the railings are stabilized rust. Stay out of the upper floors. The open doors are invitations the city doesn't mean.

**Do not feed the canal.** Drop anything edible and the surface boils. Manage your crumbs, your wrappers, your waste like you're in bear country, because you are in something more thorough than bear country. Everything organic you shed joins the food web within hours. Eat at base. Pack out everything, including — especially — your own waste.

## The quiet is a local ordinance

The Well's hunters hunt by ear, and generations of selection have made the whole city soft-footed. You are too big to be prey, but quiet is still the rule, for three reasons. First, your safety depends on your hearing: changes in the pump rhythm, rising water, the crawler. Second, the felt-upholstered acoustics will already have you half-convinced you've gone deaf — your footsteps come back wrong, swallowed — and adding your own noise makes navigation by ear impossible. Third, it's simply the custom of the place. The dogs barked themselves out of existence. Speak low, step soft, and don't bring a dog.

Bring a headlamp with a red mode, and accept what happens when you use it: every lamp in the Well wears a cone of moths hundreds deep, and yours will too, instantly. Light is an event here. Use it sparingly, enjoy the spectacle, and don't stand under a burning sodium lamp at dusk unless you want to be inside the moth storm when the bat river arrives to work it.

## The citizens

Almost nothing in the Well wants to hurt you, which is lucky, because it is the densest animal life you will ever walk through. The real hazards are small and specific:

- **Hand-long centipedes** in the gutters — a genuinely bad bite. Gloves, boots, no reaching into crevices.
- **Sheet-web spiders** at every right angle ever built. Walk the centers of corridors.
- **Rats** — not aggressive, but they are the inheriting class and their water is their toilet. The leptospirosis risk is the main reason for the boots and the post-sortie wash.
- **The felt itself.** Don't touch it bare-handed, don't sit on it, don't lean on walls. It's faintly, metabolically warm, and it lives on grease and hydrocarbons — which is to say, on things like your skin oils and your sweat. Over days it would happily colonize your gear. Carry a closed-cell pad if you must rest.

Everything else is spectacle, and you should budget time for it the way you'd budget for museums: the gecko-freckled ceilings (every ceiling, every one), the slug caravans drying to silver calligraphy, the catfish kissing the surface of the black water, the toads that hum instead of call, felt in the soles of your feet. At dusk — the one real event the sky offers — get to a vantage near a stack flue and watch the bats come out in columns like smoke running backward, tens of thousands, carving through the moth storms. Watch for the lean cats working the polished rat-runs, and the cat on the warm rail over the water, doing its ancient job. Leave them all alone. You are the tourist; they are the citizenry.

## Weather

Every few weeks the deck drops and it truly rains, warm and straight down. If you're in the basin when it starts, get to high ground and stay out of the low districts: the gutters roar, the sumps rise, the pumps quicken until you feel it through your shoes. Then, if you can safely stay for the aftermath, do — the felt blooms, the moth emergences come in waves, the catfish frenzy can be heard two streets off. It is the Well's version of spring, compressed into days.

## The pumps

You will probably find your way to the control room — everyone does; a wall of dead consoles and one live screen is a kind of shrine. Look. Read what's updating. But the rule is absolute: **switch nothing, take nothing, adjust nothing.** The plant runs unattended and has run unattended longer than anyone's records. The water still ships, over the rim in its covered aqueduct, to the thirsty, whoever they are. You don't know who takes delivery, and you don't get to find out by experiment. If the pumps ever stop, the basin takes the city back within a season — but that is not yours to hasten, delay, or fix. The Well is settled. It reached its final form and found it durable. Your footprint should be zero.

## Your mind

Prepare for the dim as a physiological problem, not just an ambiance. With no real day, your circadian rhythm will drift within forty-eight hours — keep a strict clock at camp and use a bright light on waking to give your body the sun the deck won't. Expect the anechoic streets to make you aware of your own heartbeat, your own breath, the wet sounds of your own body, and expect the emptiness to feel specific: not abandoned, but *vacated*, a hole exactly the size of one species. Some travelers find the Well the most peaceful place they've ever been. Some last one sortie. Both reactions are correct.

## Coming home

Decontaminate at the rim like you're leaving a quarantine zone, because you are. Scrub and dry everything; bake or freeze textiles if you can; check every seam and fold for roach egg cases, springtails, and felt spores. The felt needs no light, no soil, nothing but grease and damp — it is precisely the organism that would love to ride a traveler's kit into another world. Don't be the person who seeds it.

## The short list

Ice vest ×3 rotations · liquid-cooled underlayer · waterproof boots + gaiters · gloves · head net · P100 · headlamp with red mode + batteries in desiccant · paper chart + paint markers · pencil (ink runs) · synthetic notebook · 2L water + electrolytes per sortie · sealed hard food containers · heat-illness kit + thermometer · antifungal powder · doxycycline prophylaxis (ask your doctor about leptospirosis) · closed-cell sit pad · dry boxes · beacon · camera acclimated in a sealed bag before you open it, or it will weep like everything else.

## Why go

Because at dusk on the rim road, with the lamp-constellation burning below and each lamp crowned in boiling moths, with the bat river bending soundless overhead and the pump-thud coming up through the roadbed into your ribs steady as a sleeping animal — you will understand something that is hard to learn anywhere else: that a world doesn't need you in it to be full. The Well kept breathing after its people left. It keeps the water moving for strangers it will never see. Go quietly, stay cold, touch nothing, and let it show you how.
