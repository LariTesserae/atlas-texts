# Preparing for the Underkiln: A Traveler's Briefing

Understand first that this is two expeditions wearing one name. The crossing of the salt pan is the harder half; the city is the reward. Most of what can kill you lives above ground. Below, the dangers are slower and stranger, and almost all of them are things you bring with you.

Nothing in the Underkiln will attack you. Nothing has ever attacked anything there. But nothing will help you, either — no water, no food, no rescue, no one. The city is complete, and its completeness is exactly what makes a visitor fragile. Plan as if you are visiting the inside of a finished instrument that will not notice you, because you are.

## The First Fact: There Is No Water

Not "water is scarce." Water has never existed here. This single fact dictates everything else.

- **Carry every drop you will drink, for the entire trip, plus reserve.** The dew is oil. The sap is warm thin tar. The gourd seeds are built for fire and a tar-based biochemistry — assume they are inedible-to-toxic. The reservoirs hold oil. There is nothing to purify, so leave the filter and purification tablets at home; they are dead weight with no referent.
- **Do the math honestly.** In air "dry past dryness," sweat evaporates before you feel it — you will not feel sweaty, and you will be dehydrating the whole time. Drink by schedule, not thirst. Figure 3–4 liters per day below with modest exertion, 6–8 liters for each pan crossing. A visit of four days below plus two night crossings means roughly 25–30 liters — that is 25–30 kilograms of water, and it is the true hard limit on how long the city will host you. Most travelers should plan a *short* visit: two to three days below.
- **Cache.** Stage water at the pan's edge if you can, and again at the base of the shaft. Never haul your full supply on day trips into the terraces.
- **If your home world's kit includes closed-loop water reclaimers** (breath and urine recycling, the kind used for long desert or orbital work), bring one. It converts this from a desperate sprint into a measured visit.
- Accept the indignities up front: no washing, no cooking with water, spit your toothpaste into a bottle and pack it out. You are a closed system visiting a closed system.

## Breathing

The air supports combustion — the gas-jets burn — so there is oxygen. But the city's breath carries tar vapor, sulfur compounds (the copper is black with it), hot dust, and something almond-bitter and metallic.

- **Do not trust your nose.** The almond note may be cyanide compounds, and the ability to smell cyanide is genetic — a large minority of people simply cannot. Your nose is not an instrument. Bring a **four-gas monitor**: oxygen, lower explosive limit, hydrogen sulfide, hydrogen cyanide. After water, this is the most important object you carry. Clip it on at the rim and let it stay on.
- **Respirator strategy.** A half-mask with organic-vapor/acid-gas cartridges for general use; change cartridges on schedule, not when you taste breakthrough — heat and contaminant load shorten cartridge life brutally. Seal them in bags when not in use. If your monitor shows clean air in the upper terraces, give your lungs rest periods unmasked, but mask near the seep, the kiln-hearts, the reservoirs, and in any low pocket of the city.
- **The city breathes on a cycle.** By day the shafts exhale warm used air; at night the towers draw fresh surface air down. This is your ventilation schedule and should also be your travel schedule: descend at night, when the shaft inhales and you are climbing *with* the airflow into freshening air.

## Fire Discipline

The city is a kiln that has, on a schedule of decades to centuries, flooded whole districts with gas and lit them. You cannot learn where any district is in that cycle. Therefore:

- **You will carry no flame and make no spark.** No stove, no lighter, no ferro rod, no brushed-motor anything. All lights intrinsically safe (explosion-proof rated). All food no-cook.
- **Mind static.** The air is dry past dryness; synthetics will crackle. Wear cotton, linen, or wool head to toe. No fleece. Tape or pad metal-on-metal contact points on your gear.
- **Learn the warning signs and set one standing rule before you descend: if the sound changes, leave.** The city is an instrument and its telemetry is acoustic. A new note in a district — doorways whistling at a new pitch, the gourds rattling out of schedule, the crossroad jets burning taller or guttering — may mean valves have opened somewhere. Do not investigate. Move toward the shaft, uphill, and reassess from the crown. The likeliest truth is that you'll never see a firing; the schedule is too long. But you have no way to verify that, so behave as if the decision could be made for you at any time.
- Never touch valves, nozzles, flue-doors, or anything mechanical. Do not block a vent. Do not close anything that is open.

## The Crossing

The pan is a white sea with one knee-high landmark at its exact center. Treat it like open ocean.

- **Cross at night.** The daytime heat is "a hand laid flat on everything," and the white salt throws the sun back up at you from below. Walk in darkness, rest by day under a reflective tarp pitched as a sunshade.
- **Navigate by instruments, not eyes.** Mirages will lie to you continuously. Compass, stars, sun-angle — and the pan's own geometry: the chimney sits at the lowest point, so a sensitive barometric altimeter or simple slope-sense pulls you inward. As you near center, listen; the sustained note carries over flat salt, especially in the still hours. If you have a thermal monocular, the warm exhalation plume is a lighthouse in the cold night pan.
- **Protect your eyes absolutely.** Salt glare is snow-blindness with a different accent. Glacier goggles with side shields, and a backup pair.
- **Foot care and salt care.** The crust is hard and can be sharp; gaiters help. Salt is corrosive — wipe down metal gear daily (dry cloth; there is nothing to rinse with anyway).
- **Avoid dust season.** You have no calendar for it, so watch the sky: rising wind on the pan means abort or shelter. Below ground, dust season means slow waterfalls of sand pouring down the light-wells — spectacular, and safe if you stay out from under the well-mouths, but the crossing in blowing salt-dust is a navigational death.

## The Descent

- Pause at the rim. Take a glove off and lay your bare hand on the clay — it is warm, and smoothed by centuries of breath, and this is the only handshake the place offers. Put your ear to the opening before you ever climb in. The chord has a shape. You are beginning your real preparation, which is learning to listen to this city the way sailors learn a coastline.
- **Rig a rope.** The fired-clay rungs have held for ages and may hold for ages more, but you are not the load they were tested for. Bring a deadman anchor (a stuff-sack you bury in the salt — nothing on that pan will hold a stake), a static line long enough for a deep shaft, harness, descender, and ascenders. Test every rung before trusting it, and stay clipped in.
- Descend at night, with the inhale. Expect the air to warm as you go — the wrong way around, and the first of many corrections. Carry your pan layers; the night surface may be cold even while the city below runs warm.
- If you must climb out during the day, you'll be ascending against the hot exhalation. It is unpleasant, not impossible. Pace it.

## Living Below

- **Light.** The city lives at chapel-dusk: moving blades of gold from the mica-lined light-wells, amber gas-jets at the crossroads. The blades wander over hours like a hand moving in sleep — if you are reading, cooking, or navigating by one, it *will* walk away from you. Carry your own light at all times and treat the jets as your fixed landmarks.
- **Sound.** Midday is organ-full and felt in the ribs; it is glorious, and it is also a noise-exposure event. Bring foam plugs and a pair of musician's filtered plugs, and use judgment — you need enough hearing to catch telemetry changes, so modulate rather than block entirely. At night the chord inverts, higher and thinner. Under everything is the tick and ping of clay thinking about its temperature; you will come to read it as a pulse.
- **Know the infrasound effect before you feel it.** Sustained low-frequency sound produces unease, nausea, and a powerful sense of presence — of being watched. It will happen to you. Name it when it does: it is the chord, not a ghost. (The watchers, such as they are, are pottery. Both things are true; only one of them is dangerous.)
- **The floors are drums.** The plazas are vaulted thin over resonance pits; your footsteps will boom, fallen seeds will skitter and roll at your step, and the city will quite literally play you — sowing its garden with your passage. This is not damage; this is the design working as intended. Walk, don't jump. Test floors, don't trust reed bridges without clipping in, and don't lean on etched walls — the lichen lace crumbles at a touch, and midday clay can burn skin. Test surfaces before you sit.
- **Night brings the dew of oil.** Thousands of ceramic cupped hands will begin offering their collected drip to the channels, and the channels carry it to the trees. Do not stand under the gutter-spouts; do not let yourself be anointed. The oil is a skin and inhalation hazard, it ruins gear, and — more importantly — it is not yours. Wear a hood at night in the terraces, and camp off the drip-lines.
- **Sleep.** Pick a side room or arcade off the drum-plazas (seeds roll at night, and each one is a small boom). A thick pad decouples you from some of the bass, but not all — you cannot escape the chord, and you should stop trying by the second night. Many travelers find it becomes womb-like. Some cannot sleep inside it at all. If you are the second kind, shorten the visit; there is no fixing it.
- **Pack out everything.** Every wrapper, every battery, every wag bag, every bandage. If you are cut on the glaze, you clean up your own blood — it is water and foreign life, and both are contraband here.

## You Are the Contamination

This is the section most travelers skip, and it is the one that matters most to the place itself.

Nothing here has ever competed. No animal has ever existed; no immigrant seed has ever landed; the ecology runs on fire, sound, oil, and time, and its slowest worker — the etch-lichen — measures its progress in centuries. A single mold spore from your world, delivered on a boot tread into an oil-dew gutter, would be the first invasive organism this world has ever known. Nothing here has any defense. You could end a finished thing and never see it happen.

So: **arrive clean.** Brush and wash every piece of gear at your last stop before the pan. Change into clean clothes at the rim. Carry no seeds, no soil, no food with viable grain. Add no fire, no water, no life. Take nothing — not a shard, not a seed, not a glaze-flake. The seeds wake only in fire and the bowls are made of the dead; there is no souvenir here that is not theft from a working system or a grave.

## Among the Dead

The population of the city is pottery, and you will feel the truth of it by the second day. Every dwelling holds one bowl, centered, glazed with the ash of the person who emptied the room. The doors were left open deliberately — the builders' final custom — so sheltering in a room is arguably the hospitality of the dead, offered in advance. Accept it gently if you wish: move nothing, and especially do not move the bowl from its center. Leave every door as you found it.

In the highest terrace, in the smallest room, is one bowl of unfired clay, slumped where it dried. The last of the builders had no one left to fire her. Go up there once, near the end of your stay. Do not touch it. Do not, under any circumstances, entertain the thought of completing the firing — you carry no flame, and it is not your rite to perform. You may be the first person to stand in that room in an age beyond counting. The correct response is to witness, and to leave.

And if you are moved to make sound — hum, sing, speak — do it. Sound is the only offering this place can accept without being changed by it. Your voice sows seeds on the drum-plazas the same as any footstep. It is the one participation the design invites.

## Rhythm and Abort Criteria

Let the chord be your clock: the low rising breath of morning, the full loudness of midday, the thin inverted instrument of night. Learn your district's baseline on day one so that anomalies mean something. Distinguish the benign cycles — the daily reversal, the seasonal deepening of the mother tree's bass note when the seep's slow tide turns — from the local and new: a changed note, a strange jet, an out-of-season surge of rattling gourds.

Turn back without debate when: the gas monitor alarms; a district's sound changes; your water hits the reserve line; cartridges taste of breakthrough; or anyone shows heat illness — confusion, cessation of sweating, stumbling. Note that last one: **do not go alone.** Two travelers double the water load and halve the ways to die. The city will not notice you dying, which is the precise definition of a place where you need a partner.

## Who Should Not Go

Anyone with respiratory or cardiac disease; anyone who cannot sleep in noise; anyone badly claustrophobic (you are inside a bell, underground, in a kiln); anyone who cannot carry their own water; and — honestly — anyone who suspects they would pocket one small bowl-shard, just one, because it is beautiful and no one is using it. That person will harm the Underkiln more than the Underkiln will ever harm them.

## What You Get

On your last day, if the timing allows, stand at midday in the great empty plaza with your bare hand flat on the warm drum of the floor while the chord swells to its fullest and the seeds roll and the gourds rattle and the blades of dusty gold lean slowly across the ember-colored walls. The loudness will fill the emptiness exactly, and you will understand the thing the place has been telling you since the rim: that it is not lonely, that its song is not a cry for company, that a finished thing sounds like this.

Then climb out with the evening, touch the warm rim once more, cross the white pan under stars, and leave no evidence you were ever there. That is not a consolation prize. It is the whole etiquette of visiting something complete: your absence is the gift you give it back.
