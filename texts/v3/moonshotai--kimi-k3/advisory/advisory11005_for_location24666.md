# Preparing for the Basin of Stairs

## First, understand where you are going

Everywhere else you have ever been, the past erodes. Here it deposits. The town's history is not lost — it is *below*, furnished, intact, accreting a skin of stone a season at a time, and the living walk on a roof that is also the street of the house above them, above a column of drowned centuries. Time in the basin runs downward, and everything — water, mountains, rind, memory — is still rising.

The second thing to understand is the pace. Nothing in the basin moves quickly: not the water, not the fish, not the floating meadows, not the conversations. You will be tempted to call this slowness. It is not slowness. It is the local speed, and your first and most important preparation is to internalize it, because the basin notices anything that moves quickly — and whether that is ecology, etiquette, or something with attention, you should behave as if all three are true.

## Before you leave home

**Train your legs.** The count is kept in stairs, and so will your visit be. There is not one flat path in the basin. Everything you bring rides on your own back up wet staircases, forever. If your knees are questionable, reconsider, or plan to be slow in a place that honors slowness.

**Respect the altitude even though it doesn't feel like altitude.** The warm mist lies to you. The air feels lowland-soft, bath-warm, breathable — but the pressure is still mountain-thin, and the stairs will find the lie in your lungs within an hour. Arrive rested. Spend your first days at the waterline doing almost nothing. Conveniently, doing almost nothing is socially seamless here.

**If you hope to dive, train for months — but learn that their technique inverts everything you know.** The water is too dense; you float with your chest and shoulders riding above the surface, and the only way down is to *exhale fully* and haul yourself down a rope on empty lungs. Your margins are reversed: the urge to breathe arrives late and blackout arrives first. Practice breath discipline, learn the signs of shallow-water blackout in yourself, and accept now that a local's judgment about which descents you'll be permitted is law, not suggestion.

**Prepare your relationship with damp.** There is a permanent roof of cloud over the basin. Nothing dries in the sun because there is no sun. You will be damp for the entire stay, and the art is managing damp rather than avoiding it. Wool and grease, not cotton. Rotate footwear. Bring antifungal foot powder in a sealed jar and an acid rinse — vinegar or citric crystals — because the water leaves itself on you: hair dries stiff and pale as rope after every swim, skin goes chalky, and you'll want to strip the rind from yourself weekly even as the locals simply comb it out with pod-wood and wear it. Your teeth will not fleck white in a season; that's the work of years, a passport you haven't earned.

## What to pack

The principles matter more than the list:

- **No metal you care about.** Metal goes to stone faster than anything near that water. Zippers, hinges, knife pivots — assume they seize. The exception is copper, which you should bring not as gear but as *currency*: it's rare there, reserved for knives and knocking-rods, and a bar of it buys more goodwill than gold.
- **Rope. As much as you can carry.** Rot-resistant cordage is the single best gift in the basin — their deepest descents are literally limited by rope length. One caution: do not assume a longer rope makes you a benefactor. The descents stop where they stop for reasons that are not only rope and lungs, and you should not be the person who tests that.
- **No paper.** Ink runs in the mist; pages mold within weeks. Write on slate, wax, or fired clay — or learn their way, and keep your records in memory and arrangement. There is a reason all their archives are underwater masonry: stone is the only medium that survives the damp. It took me an embarrassingly long time to realize the archive isn't *despite* the water. It's *because* of it.
- **Sealed, greased, redundant.** Medicines double-jarred. Vitamin D for any stay longer than a season. Ear rinse for after dives. One genuinely warm layer, which the locals will tell you is unnecessary — they are wrong, rarely but seriously, and I'll explain below.
- **Leave behind:** earplugs (you *need* to hear this town, and muffling yourself is both insult and hazard), bright lamps (at night, light and wake are visible for miles), anything fast, and any living thing. An ecology where nothing moves quickly is defenseless against a fast weed or a fast germ. Scrub your gear before you arrive. Quarantine yourself if you're ill. This may be the most consequential item on the list.

Expect that everything you own will come home paler than it left, if it comes home at all.

## Getting in

There is no river route. The water comes from below, not from the sky, so no valley leads you in. You arrive over the rim or through the Gap.

**The rim** is young mountain — raw fracture, unweathered edges, no soil, rockfall as a weather condition. The cloud cap means you descend blind into mist. Go in the morning, and navigate *by ear*: the soft tok-tok of mallets knocking thin stone off boat hulls is the basin's lighthouse, the first sound of every day, and it will guide you down when your eyes are useless. Arrange a contact in the town beforehand. Your first act in this world should not be an unannounced wake on dark water.

**The Gap** is the one notch in the rim, where the mist pours endlessly out and where, when the cap thins, you can hear white noise beyond — spilling, or somewhere dry. It is a door that is sometimes a waterfall. It is also the only place cold comes from: now and then a thread of genuinely cold air slides down from it, the mist races, and a town that is never cold remembers it has no defense. That warm layer you packed is for this. Do not attempt the Gap without a local reading of the season.

## Arriving and behaving

Modulate everything downward. Speak low — a normal voice carries an entire terrace, and the whole town runs at the volume of one long held conversation; join it at its level. Move slowly. Never splash, above all at night, when a paddle-stroke writes green fire on the surface that is beautiful and visible for miles.

Learn these quickly:

- **Mornings begin with the hull-knocking.** Join in. It's how the day is announced, and it's your rent for the boat you'll inevitably borrow.
- **Time is told in courses and jars.** People introduce themselves by height — "four up," born on the fourth terrace above the water. Know your own lodging's number. Day and night are read off the tending of the glow-moss jars; you'll be given or trade for one, and it is *alive*. Learn to feed it. A dead jar is a tool lost and a small death both.
- **You will work at the construction edge.** Everyone takes a turn at the top terrace, growing walls — lattices of wood and rubble with spring water led through them, which the water itself mortars over ten years. A person is measured by the courses they helped raise. This is not exploitation; it is the only currency that matters, and the tick of your adze carrying across the water is your introduction to everyone in earshot.
- **Knock-code is infrastructure.** Three blows on drowned masonry, a pause, an answer an hour later, and nobody finds the delay strange. Never tap idly on submerged stone — it is the equivalent of shouting into the telephone. And if you ever hear knocks coming up from *below* the deepest known course — three slow blows, or five — do not answer. Ascend calmly. Report it to the elders. Accept the explanation you are given ("the mountain is growing" is true in general, whatever it explains) and do not press. This is the one piece of advice in this guide I cannot fully justify. Give it anyway.
- **Do not mourn drowned things.** When someone tells you they've given a room to the water, the right response is respect, even congratulation. A house is not finished when you move in; it is finished when the water takes it, and finished is not a sad word here. Check your pity at the rim. It is the fastest way to mark yourself as someone who doesn't understand.
- **Fire is precious and portable.** Hearth-coals are carried up in clay pots when the water takes a floor. Never waste fuel. If you are entrusted with a hearth, do not let it die.

## Daily life

You'll sleep on damp stone under woven fiber, and your bedding will never be quite dry — manage, don't fight. Houses tune their eaves over reeds and hollow stones so the roof's dripping plays a private music; you can know a house by its dripping the way you know a person by their step. Do not stand under an eave blocking the drip. You'd be laying a hand over someone's instrument.

Drink what they drink. It tastes like licking a warm stone; your gut will adjust within days. Eat what the pad-meadows give — starchy pad-hearts, stalk-fiber — and when someone poles out to harvest a meadow, go along. A meadow takes a season to drift across the basin, and riding one home like a slow ferry is the local pleasure cruise and genuinely one of the finest afternoons available to you anywhere. The blind white fish in the sunken cisterns are omens, not dinner. Don't tap the cisterns. Their health is read, and a visitor who stresses the fish will also be read.

Learn the sound-map, because it is the only map: the morning tok-tok, the adze-tick at the construction edge where someone is always working late by jar-light, the drip-signatures of the houses, the faint strung constellations across the dark bowl — the Rust Stair to the east where the springs run orange, the Far Steps half a day's pole away. At night, from a *moored* boat, look down: the town exists twice, once above the waterline and once inverted below, where feral glow-moss smolders green down the old stairwells to the edge of sight. See it from stillness. Moving writes fire on you for miles.

Two events override all schedules. When the white bird calls from somewhere in the cloud cap — the only wild voice in the basin — the whole town goes still, and you go still with it; don't ask where it nests, because nobody knows and that's the point. And a few times a season, the sun threads a gap in the rim and the entire bowl turns to hammered gold for an hour. The Opening. Work stops. Whatever you are doing is less important. Get to a roof-deck, turn your wet face up, and don't talk.

## Going down

Only by invitation, only with a partner, only on a rope. Passing the growing line — that white band of fresh rind at the water's edge — you enter the drowned town, and it will be *furnished*. Pots on shelves. Looms strung with stone cord. A doll on a windowsill under slow folds of flowstone. This is not abandonment; custom requires that the water receive the house whole, and so the archive is complete. Therefore the rule is absolute: **nothing below the growing line is a souvenir.** Not a cup, not a shard, not a chip of rind. Taking anything is theft from the water, from the archive, and from the dead.

The eleventh terrace down belongs to the dead, seated in niches, and you go only with a family, at their pace. If you are invited to hold a white stone hand, hold it gently and be the one to let go first. Don't clear algae from a face unless asked. The locals themselves disagree about whether the water is revealing the oldest dead or erasing them; listen to the argument and don't settle it.

The air bells under the vaulted ceilings are three generations unbreathed. If your guide demonstrates, take one breath — then back to the rope. Treat each bell as a room's last words; the bells shrink every year, and when the last one is gone the room is finished. Do not hurry a room toward finished. If your glow-jar dims inside a bell, or you feel dizzy, the air is thin. Leave.

And the rope ends where it ends. The deepest reached course is built in a masonry style no living person recognizes; the town is older than its own memory. Do not ask to go below the marker. The official reasons are rope and lungs. There are other reasons, and if an elder changes the subject, the subject is changed.

## Hazards, briefly

Falls on wet stairs (bare feet or felt soles; go slow, which is the law anyway). Entrapment — drowning in open water is oddly hard when you float like a cork; the danger is *under* things: ceilings, stairwells, terraces. Blackout on exhale-dives. The deep spring throats, where the water enters the world — heat, gas, upwelling, and the knocks; admire from a distance. Rockfall on the rim. Cold drafts from the Gap. Ears and feet in the permanent damp. And the quiet hazard no one lists: a dim, sourceless, shadowless world with no day-markers will blur your sense of time and can press on low moods. Keep rituals. Let the town's sounds be your clock. The Opening, when it comes, will do the rest.

## Leaving

Choose your exit date and route *before you arrive* — not because you'll be in danger, but because you won't want to go. The basin keeps what stays, and more than one visitor has presumably become, in time, a course of the town. Finished is not a sad word here, but decide while the decision is still yours whether finishing is what you came for.

Take only gifts given forward: commission a grown token — lower a small object on a cord for a season and receive it back white — or buy calcified flowers, which keep their color a season and then fade, which is their honesty. Take nothing the water already has. Return your jar or pass it on living, finish your stint at the construction edge, send your thanks by knock-code to any settlement that hosted you, and leave every meter of your rope behind. It is the best tip in the basin.

Then climb out slowly — everything there is slow — over the rim in the morning, with the tok-tok behind you. Expect to turn at the cloud cap for a last look at the roof of that world sealing itself shut.

You will be, at most, a finger's width of the basin's history. Make the deposit a good one: a course helped raised, a jar kept alive, nothing taken, nothing broken, a few houses known by their dripping. That is how the place remembers — layer on layer, downward into the green.
