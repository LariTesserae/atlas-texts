# Preparing for the Sounding Plateau

The first thing to understand is that this place will not try to kill you the way most wild places do — with weather, with terrain, with chaos. The Plateau is *finished*. Nothing about it varies. Its dangers are all constant, which means they are all plannable — and its one genuine predator is the only silent thing in the loudest country you will ever stand in. Your preparation is mostly about three things: thin air, your own mind, and learning to think with your ears while remembering that your advantage is your eyes.

## Before You Go

**Get altitude-ready before you ever see the cloud-sea.** The climb out of the clouds takes under a day, which is far too fast to acclimatize on the way. If your home world permits it, spend a week or two at elevation beforehand. Bring whatever your people's medicine offers for altitude sickness, and treat headache plus nausea as an order to descend, not a complaint. The air up there is thin enough that shouting barely carries, sound arrives through your boot soles, and every exertion costs double.

**Practice with silence and with timelessness.** This sounds like a luxury. It is not. The silt basins swallow the chorus until the loudest thing is your own pulse, and visitors find this genuinely intolerable — people have panicked, fled the basins, made bad decisions in the roaring open. If you can, spend time in an anechoic or flotation environment before you go. Learn what your heartbeat sounds like when it's the only sound. Separately: practice sleeping by clock rather than by light. The sun does not move. Your body will receive no dusk, ever, and within a few clock-days the word "day" will start to feel like a superstition you brought with you. That's normal. It happens to everyone. Plan for it instead of discovering it.

**Never go solo.** Minimum two, better three or four. You will need a watcher system — more on that below — and the only evacuation route is a full day's descent through whiteout cloud with whatever casualty you're carrying. Keep the party small anyway: nothing on the Plateau erases a footprint, and a large group writes itself permanently into the silt.

**Set a hard return date before you leave, and tell someone at home.** Food is the hard limit — there is nothing you should hunt and almost nothing you can gather — but the soft limit is stranger. Travelers linger. The place exerts a gravitational pull on your sense of urgency, because nothing there is urgent, nothing there changes, and after a while your deadline starts to feel like someone else's idea. It isn't. Pack for a fixed number of clock-days (four to six is right for a first visit), carry a small reserve, and treat the turn-around alarm as unbreakable.

## What to Pack

Beyond ordinary expedition kit, the Plateau demands some idiosyncratic things:

- **Two watches, at least one solar.** Solar power never dies here — the sun stands still forever, so you can mount a panel at one fixed angle and never touch it again. Keep batteries warm against your body or on the lit side of your pack.
- **Hearing protection in layers.** Musician's attenuating plugs for general wear — you must still hear the *pattern* of the place — plus heavy muffs for the rim, where the chorus is a physical pressure that rearranges your breathing. Understand: plugs will not save you from the stone chord. The ground is the medium. It comes up through your bones. You are not escaping it; you are only turning it down.
- **Glacier goggles and serious sun protection — for one side of you.** The light is fierce, white-blue, and it comes from a single direction, forever. You will tan and burn asymmetrically. Everyone comes home two-toned, like the stones. Consider a brim arranged for your sunward side only.
- **An eyeshade and whatever sleep aid your physiology accepts.** You will be sleeping in full perpetual daylight.
- **Soft-soled boots, and felt or leather to wrap your trekking-pole tips.** Footing is law here, explained below.
- **Water capacity of at least four liters per person, plus purification and a dark cloth or bag.** You'll harvest water by melting frost on sunlit stone — the eternal sun makes this reliable — and by wicking seep from the melt-rims.
- **A signal mirror.** Superb here: endless fierce light, and since nothing on the Plateau hunts or mates by sight, a mirror flash disturbs no ecology at all. Whistles, by contrast, are nearly useless — the thin air eats them.
- **A notebook.** More important than it sounds. See the section on time, below.
- **Everything you will eat, and containers to carry out everything, including all waste.** Nothing decomposes on the Plateau. Whatever you leave, it varnishes and keeps. Forever. There is no "biodegradable" in a place where the unit of patience is lichen.

Leave behind: tents with bright colors (fine, actually — nothing watches), fires of any kind (there is nothing to burn but lichen that is older than your civilization, and burning it is arson against deep time), and any plan involving a lamp. More on the lamp later.

## The Approach

The climb comes up through the cloud-sea: wet, featureless whiteout for the better part of a day. Rope up, navigate by instrument and ridge-line, and accept that you will arrive damp. This matters less than you'd think — the sun never stops, thin dry air wicks fast, and anything laid lit-side-up on the pale stone dries in hours.

For the last hour, the light arrives first, soaking down through the thinning mist. Then your head breaks the surface and the sound arrives all at once, like stepping inside a bell. **Have your goggles and plugs ready before that moment.** First exposure — altitude, glare, violet sky with two stars in it, and that wall of sound — genuinely overwhelms people. Plan a slow first camp near the rim. Breathe by count; the low frequencies will try to set your breathing for you, and letting them is fine, but do it on purpose.

Do not descend into the cloud-sea to explore. What lies beneath it, the Plateau does not say, and the swifts feeding on spores rising from the unseen world below are the only commuters. That road is not yours.

## How to Move

This is the central discipline of the visit. **The ground remembers every sound.** Step on bare stone and the bedrock telegraphs it for miles — to the hares, to the hornbacks, and to the one thing you care about. The only quiet footing is lichen mat and silt basin.

So you will travel the way everything here lives: along the contour lines. Every shadow is hemmed with a damp melt-rim threaded with rust-and-black lichen, and these embroidered margins are the roads of the country — quiet, relatively warm, near water, and stunningly beautiful. Plan routes hem to hem, basin to basin.

When you must cross open pavement — and you will — do it like this:

1. Wait for the gap after a hornback's note ends. The hush times its rush to the *swell* of a note, using the sound as a curtain. The troughs between notes are when it can't hide, and when you'll hear best.
2. Cross quickly, directly, no dawdling, accepting that every step is a broadcast.
3. Never cross alone. One walks; one watches.

And manage your own shadow. In a world of fixed light, your shadow is a permanent cold zone attached to your body. Water bottles on your shaded side will freeze; batteries will die; your shadow-side hand will go numb while your sun-side hand sweats. Dress and pack accordingly.

## The Hush

Leopard-sized, pale as lit stone, soft-footed, ear-valved, and the only silent thing in the country. It dens in the dead basins — the same quiet basins where everything sleeps, where you will want to sleep — and it hunts on the roaring open pavement. It hears you. You will never hear it. That is the whole asymmetry, and everything in your safety protocol follows from it:

- **Nothing here watches; everything listens. You are the only visual animal in the country. That is your edge.** Keep a watcher whenever the party moves or sleeps on or near open ground. Scan for the thing that is pale-on-pale and *moving wrong* — the stones don't move, the shadows don't move, so motion itself is the tell.
- **Learn the hares' drumming.** The knock-hares read the bedrock with their chins pressed flat, and their pulses travel for miles. Their ordinary traffic has a texture; when it changes — when drumming stops in a district, or turns urgent — the country is telling you something is moving through it. The Plateau will tell you where its lion is, if you learn its language. Spend your first day just listening to the drum-traffic so you know what normal sounds like.
- **Camp in lit silt basins** — warm, silent, soft-floored — and read the silt before you commit. Fresh soft-padded track means choose another basin. Sleep near the basin's edge, in shifts, watcher awake.
- If you believe one is near: get onto a hem or into a basin (quiet footing), stay visually calm, group up, and do not run on stone — a panicked gallop across pavement is a dinner bell with your location attached.

## Water and Food

Carry most of your water. Supplement it two ways: wick the damp melt-rims with cloth and purify it (that margin is half cricket by bulk — filter well, and take gently, because it's also the lichen-hem and the hornbacks' only drink), or scoop hoarfrost from a shadow's white heart *from the lit side, with a long tool, without stepping into the shadow* — every shadow is a cold trap radiating killing cold, and you treat them like thin ice over deep water. Melt the frost on dark cloth in the sun. The sun will not let you down. It has never once let anything down.

## Sound, Sleep, and Sanity

You will habituate faster than you expect — the cricket-hiss gets edited out by your brain within the hour — and that habituation is itself a hazard, because the layers you stop hearing are the ones carrying information. Once a clock-day, stop everything and deliberately re-listen to the full stack: the stone chord underfoot (different in every district — you genuinely can navigate blindfold by harmony, and it's worth practicing), the hiss, the drumming, the swifts, the long bowed notes of the hornbacks.

For sleep: lit silt basin, eyeshade, plugs if you need them — though in the basins the silence itself becomes the problem. First night, expect to lie awake listening to your own pulse and disliking it intensely. This is the Plateau showing you the one sound you bring with you. It passes. Mostly.

Keep rituals by the clock: meals at fixed hours, tea at a dawn you invent, journal entries numbered consecutively. The word "day" will wear out in your mouth — the description says visitors bring it with them and wear it out, and you will — and the journal is your anchor. Write down what happened and when. *Events happen here; nothing changes.* You will need proof that you are still a creature of sequence.

## The Traces, and the Lamp

At perhaps a dozen sites you'll find knapping floors — struck flakes, hand-axes, pale granite, varnished to the same finish as the Plateau's own skin, hemmed by lichen exactly like everything else. Sit with them. Touch nothing. Take nothing. Someone came up out of the cloud with stone in their hands, once, and the country's only comment is to treat their leavings as its own body. Whatever you leave behind will receive the same treatment, which should inform how you walk: no cairns (moving a stone here is violence — a kicked pebble is the first landslide in a thousand years), no fire-rings, no marks. Walk like a rumor.

And the lamp. No one has ever seen the dark face of any stone on the Plateau. You could carry a light into a shadow and be the first. No one ever has. I won't tell you it's forbidden — nothing here forbids — but I will tell you to sit with the question of *why no one has* before you do it. Also note the practical half of the answer: the dark faces live inside the cold traps. The Plateau's secrets are kept at killing temperature.

## Leaving

Descend with margin — food, daylight by your own clock, and emotional steadiness — because the cloud-sea whiteout on the way down is worse than on the way up: wet, cold, and the sound fading layer by layer behind you like something being taken away.

Then brace for home. A moving sun will look wrong for a while — sunsets will seem violent, theatrical, faintly alarming. Your world's air will feel thick as soup and loud in all the wrong ways. You will find yourself pressing your palm flat to the ground sometimes, expecting an answer.

And underneath it all will be the thing the Plateau actually gives you, the reason to go and the reason some travelers don't come back right: *it is still exactly like that, right now, and whenever you return, it will still be true.* Some people find that the most consoling fact they've ever carried. Some find it annihilating. You won't know which kind of person you are until you're standing at the rim, two stars overhead at noon, four animals the size of punctuation marks, and the whole country ringing like the inside of a bell.

Prepare for everything above. But prepare for that most of all.
