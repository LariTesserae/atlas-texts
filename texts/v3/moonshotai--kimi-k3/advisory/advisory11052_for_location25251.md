# Preparing for the Bowl Above the Falls

## First, understand what you are visiting

You are going to a place where dryness is not a condition but an activity — something performed continuously, around hearths, by people who have been doing it for a thousand years. Every piece of advice below follows from one fact: **there is no dry there, only degrees of wet managed around islands of heat.** If you plan as though this were an ordinary rainy destination, you will be miserable by the second night and possibly in trouble by the third. If you plan for it as the locals live it — wet as the baseline, warmth as the resource, maintenance as the way of being — it may become one of the places you measure all other places against.

## The approach

The only way in is up: switchbacks, then ladders, then the last hundred meters climbing beside the falls themselves, in their breath. Prepare accordingly.

- **Train for ladders, not just hiking.** You'll be climbing wet rungs with a pack, one hand on a rope handline, mist soaking everything. Practice down-climbing too — the descent is worse, and most accidents on trails like this happen on the way out, on tired legs, in afternoon cloud.
- **Hire a guide from the warm valley below.** They know the current state of the ladders (things rot; a rung that was sound in someone's memory may not be sound now), and the valley town is also where you should buy what you'll trade — more on that below.
- **Time the falls section for the clear morning hour.** By late morning the cloud pours over the rim and the world shrinks to a few dripping meters. You do not want to be on ladders beside a waterfall in that. Start the final climb early, cold, in the silver-gold, with the falls smoking off the lip above you.
- **Accept the soaking.** Between the mist and the effort you will arrive at the rim wet through. Have a wind layer reachable at the top of your pack — the rim wind is cold, the trees up there are bent black by it, and wet-plus-wind is how a magical arrival becomes a shivering one. Then you descend into the bowl's shelter, and the temperature gentles, and the sound changes from the falls' hiss to the denser sound of water staying. That transition is worth arriving awake for.

## What to pack

Everything must go up a ladder on your back, and everything you bring will begin rotting the moment it arrives. Both facts should discipline you.

**Carry:**
- **A small pack, internally dry-bagged in compartments.** External waterproofing is a lie here; the wet comes from the air itself, condensing on every surface. Assume the outside of everything is soaked; protect the inside.
- **Wool, not cotton, nothing cotton.** Layers you can rotate: a wet set and a town set, the town set stored near a hearth whenever you're under roof.
- **Footwear that drains, not boots that fill.** Grippy soles suited to slick boardwalk and mossy stone. Bring or acquire woven gaiters — everyone there wears them, because leeches wait in the marshy margins — and carry ash or salt for the ones that get through anyway.
- **A hammock with a bug net**, if there's any chance you won't be housed. The ground is root-mat and moss, springy, ankle-deep, and it swallows dropped tools within the hour. It will swallow you just as gently.
- **A ferro rod and tinder sealed in a tin.** You probably won't need it — hearths burn always, and fire will likely be shared with you — but a fire-starter that works wet is security and, frankly, a wonder to people for whom dry tinder is wealth. Which brings us to gifts.

**Bring to trade or give:**
- **Metal.** The town's few precious blades come up this same trail from the valley, paid for in feathers, resins, dried fruit, dyed cordage. Small blades, needles, awls, fishhooks — these are the closest thing to currency you'll have. Salt is a close second.
- **A truly watertight container or two.** In a place where everything is plumbed and nothing is dry, a box that genuinely seals is a small marvel.
- **Your labor.** This is the real price of admission. See below.

**Leave behind:**
- Paper books, leather goods, anything with glue, anything electronic you care about, anything synthetic you *don't* care about. The first group will rot; the last group won't, and there is no "away" in the bowl — the moss takes everything, and your junk becoming part of a thousand-year-old root-mat is a poor gift. Pack as though everything you carry in, you carry out or give deliberately.

## Health: the wet and everything in it

The dramatic dangers of the bowl are few and well-marked. The real hazard is slow.

- **Your feet are the expedition.** Dry them at a hearth every single night, without fail. Grease them. Treat every hot spot and wrinkle of chafe immediately. Wet maceration and its fungal sequelae are the trench foot of the cloud forest, and this place is better at wet than almost anywhere.
- **Small cuts heal slowly here and infect fast.** Keep them covered with wraps (adhesive bandages will not stick to your skin; nothing sticks to your skin), clean them at the hearth, watch them daily.
- **Leeches:** gaiters, ash, and an evening body check. Removal is trivial if you carry salt; the bleeding after is the messy part.
- **Drinking water:** the bowl is plumbed from a spring-seep under the rim and the channels run fast and cold — but those same channels pass through paddies stocked with fish and crabs before they reach the falls. Ask which flume your hosts drink from and drink from that one, upstream of gardens. When in doubt, hearth-boiled.
- **Biting flies are largely handled** — this is what the frogs are *for*, and why every house keeps its runnel loud — but dusk midges rise and the marshy margins are the margins. Smoke is the local repellent and it works; sit in it.
- **Food is hospitable**: paddy fish, tuber flour from the pestles you'll hear dropping all day like a heartbeat, dried fruit, smoked things from the lofts. Secure your own stores against the small arboreal raider everyone curses and half-tolerates — hang everything.

## Hazards that can actually hurt you

- **The lip of the falls.** Forbidden to the town's children, sworn off with ceremony, and the ceremony exists because everyone has seen the slick green rock at the edge and understood it instantly. You will understand it instantly too. Honor it.
- **The cloud.** When it pours over the rim late morning, distances collapse to meters. Do not attempt the rim, the trail, or even the bowl's outer paths in it. Wait. It lifts for a gold rinse before dusk, sometimes, and always by the next clear morning.
- **The giants.** Seven fig-like titans hold the town; an eighth came down a generation ago and the light-gap it tore is still a chest-high battlefield of saplings. In hard squalls, don't shelter beneath epiphyte-loaded limbs. Saturated wood drops without warning, which the locals know in their bones — watch where they don't linger.
- **Floods and squalls** are managed — the whole settlement is a tuned instrument of flumes, weirs, and terraces — but managed by *them*, not by you. Never touch a weir or gate unless a child (the weirs are children's work, morning and evening) has been told to show you. A blocked channel is a public wrong, answered publicly.

## Rules, etiquette, and how to be a good guest

The rules here are few and enforced by the logic of the place. Learn them on day one.

- **Never cut a bromeliad.** Each one cupped on a trunk is somebody's water and somebody's frogs. Generalize from this: *assume everything that looks wild is tended.* The epiphytes, the channels, the moss-lines worn across the rope bridges — all of it is maintained by someone you will eat beside tonight. Touch nothing that holds water. Disturb nothing that looks like it grew there on its own, because it didn't.
- **The water is commons.** Don't wash upstream of an intake, don't put soap or ash or anything into a channel, don't idle your feet in a flume. If your host's runnel goes quiet, say so — a quiet runnel means no frogs, and no frogs means flies, and that's a neighborly thing to catch.
- **Fire is wealth; act like it.** Don't light your own without invitation. Don't waste fuel. If you want to be welcomed like rain after drought, arrive with good dry tinder or contribute firewood labor. Don't hang your gear in a smoke loft unasked — those lofts are where food, seed, newborns, and the sick are kept warm.
- **The stones are named.** The town is a thousand years old and none of its wood is older than a decade; what persists is the footing stones, each relaid in the same place after every rebuilding, each carrying a family name. Don't move stones. Don't treat them as seats until you've seen others sit.
- **Two silences to honor.** When a hawk crosses the bowl, the entire town — people, birds, everything — goes quiet for the length of its shadow. Go quiet with them; it's read like weather. And when the cloud lifts and work pauses and everyone simply *looks* — down the falls' gap at the warm valley, across at the black rim — don't talk through it. It isn't quite a ritual. It is what anyone would do. Do it.
- **Expect to work.** Rot is the tax on everything — a post lasts six years, a plank three, a roof two — and the town persists like a body, by constant replacement. A guest who eats and does not pick up an adze, a lash, a thatch bundle is a guest tolerated once. You don't need skill; you need willingness. They will teach you, and the teaching is the friendship.

## The daily wheel, and your place in it

Plan your days by the bowl's rhythm, not your own habits:

- **Dawn:** a wall of sound that ignites rather than builds — pigeons, parakeets, the heavy fruit-bird gliding between the giants like something thrown. Cold, silver, the falls smoking. Everyone outside at once. You will be too.
- **Late morning:** the cloud pours in. Midday market under the long boat-hull shelter, loud as a rookery — this is where your metal and salt become whatever the bowl has that you want.
- **Afternoon:** rain as event, drumming the vast leaves until speech drowns. Everyone under roof doing indoor work — cordage, weaving, carving, mending. You will be handed something to mend. Accept.
- **Dusk:** bats from the eaves, moths like leaves at the lamps, and then the frogs begin and do not stop — layered past counting into a single chord the size of the valley, with the falls underneath like a held breath. Sleep comes easier than you'd think inside a sound that continuous. By the third night it will be the *silences* that wake you, and correctly.

Bring earplugs if you must, but use them sparingly. The chorus is information — it is how everyone there knows the world is right — and after a few days you will not want to be deaf to it.

## What to make sure you do

- Stand in the meeting house between the root-buttresses of the largest giant, on river stones worn by a millennium of feet, under lashings renewed every year since before the songs run.
- Walk the light-gap on the west side where the eighth giant fell — the loudest acre of wings in a loud town.
- Ask an elder about the **gutter-frog**: small, dark, bronze-striped, living only in the town's own channels, found nowhere up the ridge and nowhere down the valley — a creature that exists because the town exists, as domestic as the smoke lofts. Then ask how long the settlement has stood in the bowl, and receive the answer the stones give.
- If you're lucky: a fruiting tree drawing its three-day screaming riot of birds, or a night the winged termites rise in their millions and every bird, bat, and child is out gorging. Go out with the children.
- Follow the foxfire log's green line at dark, at whatever distance the children dare.
- Listen for the water-pestles — the heartbeat you stop hearing until it stops.

## Leaving

Descend in a clear morning, on rested legs, with your pack re-balanced and nothing in your hands. Do a final leech check at the valley floor. Keep one set of clothes hearth-dry until the last morning so you leave warm. And know this: somewhere in your kit, in a seam, in a fold, the damp will travel home with you, and weeks later you'll open a bag and smell woodsmoke and wet moss and the sweet dark smell of rot, and to anyone raised in the bowl that smell means only one thing. You'll understand them then.

The stones don't move. The town will be the age it always is. Go while you can climb.
