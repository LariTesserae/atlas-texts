# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Bath

## Understand What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, fix three facts in your mind, because everything else follows from them:

1. **It belongs to someone.** This is not a ruin. It is a weeks-old building whose masons stepped out to fetch a sluice-key and have not yet returned. You are a guest in an unfinished story, possibly a pre-dedication sacred space. Everything you do should assume the owners come back tomorrow.
2. **You are seeing a moment, not a place.** The water has not yet learned mosquitoes. The bronze spout is still pink-gold. The stalactite is a grain of rice; the scum-ring is a pencil line; the tile floor is still readable from the surface. Within a season the green will thicken, the insects will arrive, the crust will band. Arrive now or accept that you are visiting a different room.
3. **There is exactly one dry way in, and it is a vertical clay throat.** The door is drowned. You will enter and leave through the wellhead chimney, down a shaft narrower than your shoulders, onto water, roughly a body-length of air between surface and vault. This single fact dictates most of your preparation.

## When to Go

- **Time of day:** Descend late morning. Be in the water at local noon, when the equatorial sun stands over the wellhead and the pool becomes a lamp — the light column, the steam visible along its whole length, the caustic nets crawling the vault. This is the reason you came.
- **Time of season:** It is storm season. Afternoon storms arrive most days. This sets your cardinal rule: **out of the water and off the mound by early afternoon.** A wall of rain drops straight down that shaft. You do not want to be under it, and you especially do not want to be in the shaft when it comes.
- Morning has its own reward — after the cool night, the vault rains indoors, a hundred drips at a hundred periods, and the highest resonator jars are awake with it. If you can only make one visit, choose noon. If you can make two, take a morning for the drip-rain.

## Topside Reconnaissance

From above, there is almost nothing to find, so look properly. Lie down on the mound — be the person who does — and feel the warmth through the turf and the hum through the hill. Put your ear to the chimney: the roar arrives through bone before air.

Then survey, quietly:

- **The spring cistern upslope** and the clay inflow pipe running down. Admire. Do not touch, shift, or shade it. That pipe is the room's heartbeat; a kink changes the level and the chord.
- **The outflow rill** below the mound, where the overflow discharges. Confirm it runs free. If it is backing up, the room's one regulation system is compromised — postpone your swim.
- **The work camp:** spoil heaps, formwork offcuts, ash, cart tracks. Learn whether the builders left in order or in haste. Watch for fresh tracks. If someone has been back recently, expect company.
- **Weather over the slope.** If clouds are stacking early, cut the visit short.
- Leave word of your whereabouts at your camp or port of entry. The hollow conceals the mound beautifully — which means no one will ever find you there by accident.

## What to Bring

- **A rope ladder, not just a rope.** This is the single most important item. Humid air, steam-slick clay, wet hands, and warm-water-fatigued arms make a hand-over-hand climb genuinely dangerous at the end of a visit. Fifteen meters of line plus a ladder whose lowest rung kisses the water surface. Rig it so you can find a rung with your foot without looking.
- **Stakes and a plank, or poles for a tripod.** There is nothing topside to anchor to but turf. Do not anchor to the chimney — it is clay, it is the room's throat, and it breathes.
- **A plumb line** (any cord with a weight) to sound your drop zone.
- **A float** — an inflated bladder, a dry bag sealed with air. There is almost nowhere to rest in that water: benches, walkways, and coping are all submerged. You will tread water or float the entire visit.
- **A wax tablet and stylus.** Paper hates 100% humidity; wax doesn't care, it's period-plausible if anyone arrives, and you will want to draw that tile floor.
- **Your own water**, drink topside before descending. Undyed, simple clothing to swim in — no fresh indigo, no leather (tannins stain), no oils or perfume. You are about to enter someone's unfinished sanctuary and the clearest water you will ever meet; nothing that bleeds or films comes with you.
- **Trade goods left topside** — salt, bronze wire, a good knife — in case you meet the builders.
- Notably absent: no fire kit (you will light nothing down there), no soap, nothing you're not prepared to carry back out wet.

## The Descent

Lower your gear first on a line. Then the shaft itself: it is no wider than a wellhead, steam rises through it, and the clay will be slick. If your shoulders are broad, check the fit before you commit. Go down chimney-style, back to wall. Note the fern rooted on the ledge — pretty, and treacherous; don't trust any foothold that is green.

From the shaft's lower lip the water lies about a body-length below. Before you drop:

1. Sound the spot with the plumb line. The floor is two and a half body-lengths down — plenty for a feet-first sitting drop.
2. Confirm you are **clear of the south wall**, where four lotus pots sit sunk to their rims in the floor — kicking one would be clumsy, and the single rising stem is irreplaceable.
3. Confirm you are clear of the drowned stair and its one emergent step, and well short of the spout's plunge zone on the east wall.
4. **Never dive headfirst.** Ever. The submerged benches and coping are exactly skull-height for a diver.

Then let go. You will land in wrist-warm, mineral-clear water in a room of light and roaring sound, and it will be worth every minute of the rigging.

## Rules of the Water

- **You must be able to swim — really swim.** Comfortable treading water for twenty minutes, comfortable in deep water you cannot stand up in. If that's not you, do not descend; the visit from the wellhead alone (see below) is worth the journey.
- **Watch the offering bowl first.** It circuits slowly from spout to weir and back, and it is the room's current-meter and clock. Give it one full circuit. It will teach you the surface drift better than any explanation. If its circuit ever quickens noticeably, inflow is up — rain at the spring, weather coming. Get out.
- **Keep your distance from the weir slot** on the west wall — a slow pull, but you are a soft body near the room's only drain, and during storms it drinks hard. Stay an arm's length clear, minimum. You are not the bowl; do not follow its route in.
- **The spout is a small waterfall.** Beautiful to approach, but its strike point drives a downwelling — it will push you under — and directly beneath it the drumming is total. Visit, feel it drum in your chest from a body-length away, then drift off.
- **Time yourself.** Warm water deceives: you will not chill — you will overcook slowly and not notice, because sweat is invisible in water. Forty minutes of swimming, then rest on the emergent step. Light-headedness on climbing out is real; climb slowly, holding the line.
- **Do not dive the doorway.** A competent swimmer may take one breath-hold look at the eleven steps descending into their own reflection and the dim passage beyond with its patient stack of firewood — in daylight, with a companion watching. Do not enter the passage. Do not touch the sluice mechanism. The jammed gate is the builders' work to finish, not yours. A freed sluice means surging currents and a room changing state with you inside it.
- **Do not put your fingers in the resonator jars.** Their mouths are flush with the vault within reach of a raised hand. You will want to. Don't.
- Drink nothing down there, or if you must, a sip from right at the spout where the water is newest. And it should not need saying: nothing leaves your body into this water. Nothing.

## The Senses

It is loud in a way the silent mound does not prepare you for — a bright, crowded, multiplying roar, with a tuned chord humming under it. Agree on clock-times with any companion, not shouts; no voice carries out of that water. If two of you swim, stay within sight of each other at all times.

Do these specific things:

- **Float on your back with your ears just at the surface**, away from the spout's drift. The clattering bright room lifts away and the second room arrives through bone — the spout's bass note filling your skull, the jars' chord beneath everything. This is the signature experience of the place. Give it five full minutes.
- **Look down at noon.** Read the tile floor: the interlocking wave border, the sun disc whose rays shiver with every disturbance you make. You are making the sun wobble. Know that.
- **Watch the light when a cloud crosses** — the fall into silver dusk, then the blaze returning. Glare off the soaped plaster is like wet bone; squint and don't stare.
- Vertigo is possible while floating and staring up the steam-filled column at the tiny disc of sky. It's pleasant. Roll over if it isn't.

## The Departure

Everything about leaving is harder than entering. Stage yourself at the emergent step — you should have located it in your first five minutes — rest until your breathing is calm, then climb the ladder slowly. The climb is the most athletic moment of the day; never attempt it tired.

Leave nothing: not a hair tie, not a candle stub, not a footprint's worth of soot. The room's inventory is known to the last petal — one moth, pale as a pressed flower, near the coping; one bowl on its endless circuit; one lamp, unlit, wick still white; one lotus leaf unrolling; one grain of rice becoming stone. Do not light the lamp. Do not take the moth, a tile chip, a sprig of moss. The chisel marks are sharp enough to catch a fingernail, and the record is sharp enough to catch you. You are reading page one of a book that will take centuries to write; do not smudge it.

## If the Builders Return

They may arrive while you're inside. You'll likely not hear them over the roar — but your ladder at the wellhead has already confessed you, so own it immediately. Call up a greeting before they look down. Climb out slowly, hands visible, and say it plainly: a traveler, alone, unarmed, who found a wonder and could not keep from entering.

Then compliment the tuning of the resonator jars by name — the chord, the way the deepest answers the inflow's drum. You could not say anything better to the people who built a bell for water to ring. Offer salt, labor, and patience. Do not offer to fix the sluice. Whether or not they meant it, they have made one of the great rooms of any world, and its completion is theirs to conduct. If they judge that you profaned an undedicated place, accept the judgment and make amends as directed. You did, a little, and you knew the risk.

## If You Should Not Descend

Be honest with yourself about swimming, shoulders, and nerve. If any fail, do not force it — instead, take the wellhead visit, which is honestly half the wonder: ear to the clay at dawn, feeling the hill hum through your skull; an eye over the lip at noon while the steam rises through the beam and the roar comes up the shaft like breath; the drip-tick of the one emergent step somewhere below. Some travelers will find this the better visit. No shame attaches.

## What You Carry Out

Nothing but this: the memory of a room playing itself like an instrument whose musicians have stepped out; sketches on wax of a sun disc with shivering rays; the two-rooms sound, bright above and dark below; the sight of one leaf opening in bright drowned air, ahead of everyone; and perhaps — if you judge it harmless, and I think it is — one flask of water taken from the spout's arc, warm as a wrist, faintly sweet with lime.

Go before the mosquitoes learn the way. There will never be a better morning in that room's entire long life.
