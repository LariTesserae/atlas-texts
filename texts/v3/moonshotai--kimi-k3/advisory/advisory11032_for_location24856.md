## Understand First What You Are Visiting

You are not visiting a ruin. A ruin was built and then abandoned; this was never built, and it is not abandoned — it is dying, at a tempo too slow to watch, while continuing to speak. Prepare for three expeditions at once: a high-altitude mountaineering traverse, a retreat in a cathedral that is also a hospice, and fieldwork in a place where the laws of physics are maintained infrastructure with a shrinking maintenance crew. Every decision you make should serve all three.

Before anything else, obtain the last visitor's log. Maps decay; the last traveler's measurements, patience-census, hum-pitch readings, and aliveness boundary are worth more than any chart. And plan to leave yours behind for the next one. The City's sentences take a century; no single human will ever hear a whole word. A shared log, kept across generations of travelers, is the only ear long enough. Contribute to it.

## The Body: Air, Cold, Water

**Air.** The lowlands are already thin; the City is higher, and the climb takes days. Prepare as you would for the high Himalaya, then go further. Acclimatize in the grit plains before ascending. Stage the climb. Know the symptoms of altitude sickness and treat any of them as an immediate descent order — there is no rescue, none, and the region's entire population fits in a paragraph. Bring supplemental oxygen or, better, a closed-loop rebreather, for a second reason beyond the first:

**Water.** This is the defining logistical fact of the journey. It has never rained. The rivers are dust; the cisterns hold dust with tide-lines. Every drop you will drink, you carry. Calculate for approach, stay, and descent, then add half again — because the descent may not retrace the ascent, and your margin is what absorbs that. You will lose more moisture to breathing than to sweat; the air strips it from every exhale and freezes it onto the nearest stone. A moisture-reclaiming breath loop is not a luxury; it is the difference between a three-week visit and a six-day one. Seal everything. Check seals daily. A leaking bag will be found within hours — crystal will flower around it in concentric rings — and the garden will be beautiful, and it will be your water, and it will be dead by dusk.

Understand this emotionally before you go: your breath is the only rain the world has ever had. You are a walking water-event. That will matter later.

**Cold.** Constant, thin-aired, altitude-cold, with no season of warmth. The stone has had ten thousand years to become cold all the way through, and it will take your heat from below all night. Insulate from the ground first; your pad matters more than your bag. The exception is living stone, which runs faintly warm, like a wrist. Camp with your back to a living wall and you will gain a few degrees, a steady hum in the sternum, and — this is not sentimental — firmer local physics. The safest bed in the City is a living plaza against a warm wall.

**Time.** The light never changes. The stars are always out. Nothing in the sky will ever tell you when to eat or sleep, so bring two mechanical watches — electronics deserve backup near the films' static — and run a strict, arbitrary, unbreakable schedule. Eat by the clock. Sleep by the clock. Travelers who don't drift into a grey, meal-less, sleepless haze within days, and in this place, judgment errors are topology errors.

Note also: the City's rules keep their own schedule even when the sky won't. In the quiet districts, a street is reliably longer at noon than at dusk. On arrival, calibrate: pace a measured street at different hours, find its short phase, call that dusk, and schedule any necessary quiet-district crossings for it.

## The Kit

- **Rebreather with moisture reclaim**, plus masks and goggles. In drifter season the spires are hung with torn glass fiber; in storm season the air itself is abrasive. Protect your lungs as if they are the only pair you have.
- **Full-coverage dust armor**, sealed cases, spare tape and seals. Grit gets into everything, and near the dead spires it rains *upward*.
- **One glove you can bare the palm of.** Your palm is your single most important instrument. The warmth test and the hum test require skin on stone.
- **A contact microphone or geophone.** The hum lives below hearing, in the teeth and sternum and the flat of the hand. Its strength is the integrity of local reality. Log its pitch, always dated — it changes over years, and your reading joins the long record.
- **An infrared thermometer.** Living stone reads wrist-warm. Sweep the walls and you are mapping life itself — which is to say, mapping where the world's rules still hold. An aliveness map is worth more than any street map.
- **A pocket of glass marbles and a plumb bob.** Drop a marble at every new plaza and watch it fall. A dropped stone should drop. If one pauses on the way down, as if reconsidering — leave. That is the field test. There is no better one.
- **Measuring cord, pencil, paper.** Pens freeze. Survey as you go; date everything. When a room measures differently from inside and outside, record both numbers. They are both right. Do not argue with the territory; annotate it.
- **Rope, tethers, removable anchors.** Never bolt anything into living stone — a bolt in a living wall is a splinter in a sentence.
- **Ribbons on short stakes**, for breadcrumbs in quiet districts — with the caveat that a moved or missing ribbon is not a malfunction. It is data. Stop and re-survey.
- **Lamps, redundant.** Where the two shadows cross there is a knot of true dark, and the underlight does not reach interiors.
- **All your food** (nothing requiring water), salt, a real medical kit, and wag bags. You will pack out everything. Your waste is the only organic chemistry in a silicate world; leaving it is not a gift, it is a contamination.
- **No weapons.** Nothing here hunts on your timescale except one thing, and you cannot fight it. You can only not be under it.

## Reading the Stone

The prime directive of the whole visit: **the hum is load-bearing.** The builders' conversation is what holds the world's rules taut, and where they are dead, the weave loosens. Every navigational decision reduces to one question — *how alive is the ground beneath me?*

Signs of living country: walls warm to the palm; a strong hum in the chest; mineral films present in their acres-apart patches; avenues polished clean by grazing skaters — a polished avenue is a grazer trail, and the grazers know something, so prefer their routes. And the decisive test: **routes commute.** Walk a street both ways and time it. In firm country the way there and the way back are the same length, and maps drawn decades ago are still true.

Signs of quiet country: cold stone, faint hum, films absent, dust behaving oddly — streamers that curve around nothing, grit falling upward in slow ribbons — and your watch disagreeing with your feet. On arrival, survey your district: thermometer, palm, microphone. Draw the aliveness boundary on your map. Redraw it every visit. It moves street by street, and it will have moved since the last traveler.

## Protocols of Movement in the Quiet Districts

- **Test before you commit.** Marble drop. Paced street, both directions. A look at both ends of every bridge.
- **Threshold discipline.** Some rooms are larger than the towers that contain them. Step in, step back out, confirm the exit still commutes, and only then enter with your pack.
- **Bridge protocol.** In the dead districts there is a bridge that meets a span it did not grow toward. Before crossing anything in quiet country, confirm the far side matches your map. Cross unladen first, return, then cross with your load. After crossing, look back. If the way home now shows you something else, recross immediately, while recrossing still means something.
- **The one hard rule.** In healthy physics, you see the dust plume first and hear the thud after. If you ever hear a thing before you see it — any effect arriving ahead of its cause — turn back that hour. Not after lunch. That hour.
- **Turn-back triggers, committed to memory:** a pausing marble; breath-frost that no film finds (absent films mean deep dead ground); grit streamers bending around empty air; and identical stars — if the sky above you matches a sky you should not be able to see, you are already somewhere you must not be.

## The Dead Quarter

There is one rule, and it is not "with caution." It is *do not enter.*

Detect it from outside: the dust-rivers curve away from it in avoiding streamers, no films grow there, and even the grit refuses to drift in. If your map says you are far from it and the grit disagrees, believe the grit — it has better instincts than your cartography.

From its highest point you can see the City — the City you are standing in — spread below you across a chasm that does not exist on any approach, under the same stars in the same positions. Some travelers will want that view. Want it all you like; do not collect it. It is not a vista; it is a symptom. Nothing there will harm you — harm requires conviction, and the Dead Quarter has none — but you may simply be let go of, the way a rumor is let go of. There is no rescue from it, because there is no reliably *there* to be rescued from. Plan every route so that no single error — a wrong bridge, a renegotiated stair — can deliver you to its edge unawares.

## Heights, Chasms, and the Inversion

The chasms have no floors. They have light, and it rises as the Ember goes down — whatever Ember-set means in a sky that never changes, you will learn to feel it — until every object in the City wears two faint shadows, one down, one up, and looking into the depths is looking at a dawn. The higher you go, the stronger the sensation that the world is hung from its own sky, and you with it. This is not vertigo and will not respond to vertigo's remedies. It is an accurate perception of an unusual situation.

Tether early, not when frightened: at every chasm edge, on every dead-district bridge, and always on the high spires, where gravity is about half-convinced and the grit streams upward past your shoulders in slow ribbons. Go to the highest spire — you should; it is why you came — but go roped, go briefly, and go at dusk. Expect to weep without quite knowing why. This is normal and correct.

## Seasons, Sounds, and Neighbors

**Choose your season deliberately.** Drifter season brings the glass balloons snagging on the spires by the thousand and the occasional single chime as a strand tears — the only music of the quiet weeks — plus airborne glass fiber, so mask always and touch nothing. The loud weeks bring the grit-storms: the hiss rising to something almost like weather, dust-rivers rerouting through streets, and the aerodynamic old ruins waking as infrasonic flutes. That music acts directly on mood; expect awe and unaccountable dread, and know in advance that the dread is the flute's, not yours, so you do not mistake it for judgment. Never shelter under spans — somewhere, an arch that has been thinning for ten thousand years is always about to fail — and never camp in a dust channel. A dust-river through your camp is a flash flood that happens to be dry. Read the ripples; read the tide-lines; camp above the flow.

**Sound discipline.** Sound dies within arm's reach. Companions stay in sight or on line; whistles are nearly useless; agree on lamp signals and line-tugs. Learn the silence's inventory: the hiss (grit moving), the dusk ticking (stone adjusting — a house settling the size of a city), the rare far thud, the chime, and, if fortune likes you, the faint scrape of a skater feeding.

**The skaters:** a few hundred, a plaza a day, harmless, polishing the avenues as they graze. Never block a grazer trail. If one approaches your camp, yield — it will not alter course, not from malice but from tempo. Sharing a plaza with one is the best luck the place offers; take it quietly.

**The eleven long patiences:** the only predation, and barely that. They hang folded in tower-shadow for a season and fall once, silently, and what they take is uncertain — a skater, a drifter, perhaps heat, perhaps hum. You are warm, wet, and loud in frequencies this world almost never gets. You are interesting. So: census them. Eleven is a number a person can hold. Learn the folded silhouette; check every tower-shadow before you walk under it; never camp beneath overhangs; and note the mercy in their biology — a patience falls *once*, so a tower whose patience has spent its fall is the safest shelter in the City for a season, while a tower that still holds one is loaded. Map the eleven near your route. If you cannot account for one, treat all deep shadow as occupied.

**The builders:** you will never meet one as you meet a person. Press your palm flat to a warm wall and wait — that is the handshake, and it is enough. Chip nothing, mark nothing, sample nothing living; you would be cutting a word in half. You are visiting inside a sentence older than your species. Conduct yourself as you would inside someone's utterance.

## Courtesy

Take nothing: no black glass, no film crystal, no drifter strand. Your permitted harvest is photographs, measurements, and one other thing.

Your breath is the only weather of grace this world receives. Where it touches cold stone, frost forms, and within hours a frost garden blooms around it — concentric rings of crystal, the only flower there is, dead by the next dusk. Some travelers, before leaving, choose one dead wall at the boundary of the quiet country and breathe on it deliberately: one garden, given on purpose, as thanks. Grow one. Not ten.

Do not try to help. You cannot re-tauten the weave; you cannot answer the hum; there is no fix a human can carry in. Witness is the help. It is the only kind available, and it is genuinely wanted by no one, and it matters anyway.

## The Descent, and After

Start down with margin — water, oxygen, days — because the stairs you descend will not be the stairs you climbed, and both of these things will be true, and arguing about it costs hours you budgeted for walking. Trust your lowland caches, not anything you left in the City; the plains are plain, and the City, politely, at its own pace, is letting small differences matter less and less.

Expect the return to thickness — of air, of sound, of rules — to feel strange. Rain may make you cry. A room whose inside matches its outside may look like a small miracle, because it is one.

And prepare for what you will carry, because no gear addresses it: you will have stood inside a conversation that began before your kind existed, felt a syllable end under your palm, and understood not one word of it — while watching, street by street, the voices go quiet and the wind win its long negotiation with the dream. Decide before you go whether you are the sort of person who can be given a gift you cannot keep and still call it a gift.

Then go. Go anyway.
