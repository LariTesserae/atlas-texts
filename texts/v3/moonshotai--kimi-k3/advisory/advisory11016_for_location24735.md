## Preparing for the Room Beneath the Plain

### First, decide what kind of visitor you will be

No preparation can make this visit harmless, so prepare to make it honest. Understand before you go: nothing in that room erases. Your footprints will be the first marks ever made there, and they will remain for as long as the room does — which, given that nothing flows, blows, rots, or hunts there, means effectively forever. You are not visiting a place with a history. You are becoming its history, the first event since the water left.

So the real preparation happens before you pack a single bag. Sit with the question honestly: *why am I going?* If the answer involves collecting — a flake, a gypsum petal, a crystal, a trophy photograph to display — do not go. The room has never been stolen from, and you should not be its first thief. If the answer is witness — to be the one pair of eyes that ever sees the columns take their colors — that is enough. That is, arguably, the only sufficient reason.

Know also that there is an honorable partial visit: descend to the second bend, stand in the last grey the sky sends, see the pale garden that has never been grazed, feel the dark begin like a material at the next turn — and go home. That is a complete experience of the place, and it leaves the room untouched. Many travelers, having understood what the room is, choose exactly this. Consider whether you are one of them. There is no shame in it. There is, if anything, a particular distinction in being the person who found the door and declined to open it.

If you go in, go alone or with exactly one companion who understands restraint, and rehearse the visit like a spacewalk — because in a place where nothing can be undone, improvisation is permanent.

### The plain

The country above is a hot, flat, featureless slab. Treat it as serious desert:

- **Water is everything.** Nothing flows and nothing drains, which means there are no sources. Carry a minimum of six liters per person for the day, plus a cached reserve at your arrival point. The room below sits within a degree of your blood temperature and the air is thirsty — it will borrow moisture from your lungs and return nothing — so you will lose water even underground.
- **Cross at dawn.** By midday the plain shimmers, and there is no shade worth the name; the scrub keeps its distance from itself and from you.
- **Navigation is a genuine problem.** The one landmark in all that levelness is a shallow dimple, and the slot at its bottom is, as you know, the least dramatic opening in the world. You will walk past it. Use a compass and dead reckoning from your arrival point, and when you find the dimple, mark it — a tall wand or small cairn — so you can find your way back out of the desert. Remove the marker when you leave the region for good. The room's entire defense across ten thousand years has been its inconspicuousness. Do not fix that.
- **Handle all bodily business on the surface**, well away from the dimple. Nothing may be spilled, dropped, or left below. Eat before you descend. Pack out everything.

One more thing: somewhere above the room stands an unremarkable scrub tree whose roots have found their way down through a hairline crack. If you notice it on the surface, let it be ordinary. It does not know it is touching the dark. Keep its secret along with the rest.

### The descent

Wear a helmet — the talus is ancient and every stone is at rest, but your weight is a new force in a place that has forgotten what events are, and the ceiling of the room below is low enough to brush your hair. Test every foothold; three points of contact on the slope between the slabs. Carry three independent light sources and spare batteries, but do not light them yet if you can help it.

The grey zone asks for etiquette. The tufts of thread-fine grass stand a hand apart with bare dust between — step in the bare places, deliberately, one clump at a time. Do not brush the algae-filmed wall. Pass through by the place's own diluted grey if your eyes can manage it; those plants live on the day's average light, and a headlamp at full power is more photons than they have ever received at once. Beyond the second bend, the dark simply begins. Stop there. This is your last chance to turn back without consequence.

Then light your lamp — on its lowest adequate setting — and accept what you are doing: you cannot see the room without giving it color. No light exists that does not change this place. When the columns take their colors, let it happen slowly. Do not sweep the beam around. The room's first experience of light might as well be calm.

### Inside the room

Move as though the floor were paper that remembers. Some specifics:

- **Walk one line.** The dust is flour-fine and deep, a snowfall never walked in. Choose your path — a few steps in, a place to stand — and take it once, returning on your own boot prints. Do not wander. Every step is a word in the first sentence ever written there; keep the sentence short.
- **Manage your sweat.** This sounds strange and is not optional. You will be hot, and the dead-still air offers no cooling except evaporation. Wear a sweatband. The drip that built the tallest column stopped ten thousand years ago; do not let a drop from your forehead resume the dripping. In this room, one falling drop of water is a geological process restarting.
- **Touch nothing.** The gypsum petals at the pan's edge would powder at a touch and have never been touched, and those are the same fact — do not make them different facts. The salt polygons would take the shape of your hand; keep your hands off. The chert flakes in the alcove have edges that could open your hand, and no one struck them — do not pick one up, do not sit at the waist-high block your mind will insist is a workbench. It is a knapping floor with no knapper; let the mind refuse the scene and refuse its refusal, and leave it at that.
- **Do not take the crawl.** The low passage to the closet-sized cell is a solo trap with no rescue, and another consecrated space besides. One room of firsts is enough for any traveler.
- **The roots are not exhibits.** Do not touch them, including the one holding its tip a finger's width above the salt. Breathe the faint resin — the room's only perfume — and let that be all.
- **Speak if you need to**, but know that the dust and soft crust drink sound; your voice will come back dead, without echo. Clap and the room files it away. This is normal. If somewhere in the crust you hear a small tick — a crystal letting go — do not flatter yourself that the room noticed you. It does that once a decade, to no one.

Set a timer before you enter and obey it. Forty minutes in the room is a long visit. The air sits at blood temperature, and after an hour you will not be able to find the boundary of your skin by temperature; combined with the absolute dark behind your lamplight and a silence so massive it rings, this unmooring is the place's one real hazard to a healthy adult. Practice beforehand if you can — a float tank, a dark room at night — so the sensation is strange rather than frightening. Your watch is your anchor. Nothing in that place has hours; even the grey outside delivers only the day's average.

If you photograph, decide beforehand, and keep count low. Each flash is a lightning strike in a place that has never had weather. Records and memory are the only exports permitted.

### What to carry

- Six-plus liters of water, electrolytes, and a cached reserve on the surface
- Sun protection for the crossing: hat, pale long clothing, sunscreen
- Compass and route stakes; a removable marker for the dimple
- Helmet, primary headlamp, two backup lights, spare batteries
- Gloves for the talus (remove them before the room)
- A dust mask or light respirator — your footsteps will raise the flour-fine dust, and it will not forgive your lungs
- Freshly laundered clothes or a clean oversuit for the room, and clean soft shoes carried down in a sealed bag — you will shed fibers and skin cells regardless, but minimize what you leave
- A sweatband; hair tied back under a cap
- Watch or timer, notebook and pencil
- First aid, including wound closure — the flakes are scalpel-sharp
- Whistle and signal mirror for the surface, where sound and light actually travel

Arrange your return transit from the surface, not from underground — stone is indifferent to whatever carries you between worlds, and you should not bet your life that it works from below. Leave a trip plan and a hard deadline with someone at your home base, with the plain's coordinates, under seal of confidence. If you twist an ankle at the talus, no one in that universe knows you exist. Rescue is something you build yourself, in advance, from another world.

### Leaving, and after

Leave on your own boot line. Do not smooth the dust behind you, do not try to erase — erasure would be a second, larger disturbance, and a dishonest one. Your footprints say only: *someone came, and looked, and left.* Let them say it. Sign nothing. The room keeps everything without opinion, and an anonymous track kept forever beside nothing else is the most honest signature available.

Within an hour of your departure your warmth will have equalized into the general warmth, and the room will be exactly as it was, plus one sentence. On the surface, retrieve your cache and your marker. Cross the plain before dark.

Then expect the after-effects, because there will be some. Ordinary rooms will feel loud and bright and provisional for a while. You will carry the only record of the columns' colors — colors that occurred exactly once, for you, and exist now only in your memory. Write it down while it is fresh. And then decide, if you have not already decided, to tell no one where it is. Telling creates a second visitor, and the second visit doubles the disturbance forever. The room survived ten thousand years on anonymity. You can give it that again for free.

One last comfort for the superstitious: nothing will follow you out. Nothing lives there to follow. The room is not waiting, not asleep, not watching, and it does not miss you — it cannot; missing implies a mind, and it has none. It will simply continue, at the temperature of a held hand, in the middle of its finished work, keeping your footprints the way it keeps the tick of a letting-go crystal: completely, and without comment. Go gently, or do not go. Those have always been the only two preparations that matter.
