# Preparing for the Draft

A field guide for the water-carrying mammal

Everything below follows from three facts. Hold all three at once and the packing list writes itself.

1. **There is no water.** Not scarce water — *no* water, in the whole range, in all of its memory. No mud, no ice, no weather. You will carry every drop you drink, and the one item you cross off the standard expedition checklist is the filter: there is nothing to purify.
2. **The bowl is electrical, and it is tuned.** The distances between the blooms are load-bearing. You — a warm bag of salt water — will be the best conductor in the valley and the only moisture. Both of those facts come with rules.
3. **Your perception is part of the terrain.** The path in resists being found; the rim resists being looked at; the whole place resists being believed afterward. You must pack for your memory as carefully as for your body.

## The Mountain

The approach is an ordinary mountain in every way except the ones that kill you. It is very high — high enough that the sky stops being blue and becomes enamel — and it is a desert with altitude's thin air on top of it. Acclimatize for several days at the highest comfortable staging point below. Climb in the early morning; by midday the scree is a griddle and your own breathing will be the loudest thing you own.

Water: plan on a minimum of a liter per two hours of exertion in dry heat at altitude, plus a full extra day in reserve. For a three-day round trip that means 15 liters or more — 15 kilograms — so stage it. The lower mountain is honest scree and takes cairns and caches normally; bury or shade two-thirds of your water at a marked lower camp and carry 6–8 liters up for the in-bowl portion. Cold food only (see below), so none of your water goes to cooking.

The scree itself has been falling since before falling had rules: angular, unweathered, unbound by any soil. Wear a helmet. Move one at a time through steep sections. Gaiters, stiff boots, leather gloves — every edge in this place is a birth edge, and the mountain outside the bowl is no gentler. There is no weather to shelter from — it has never rained here — but there is wind, and the night may be bitter; bring a bivy and a shade tarp rather than a tent. Nothing lives on the mountain, because nothing can drink on it. Your food needs no hanging. The silence will need some getting used to.

## The Fold

Near the top, the talus folds: a gyre in the scree, a winding that goes nowhere twice and then, if you keep faith with it, once more. Treat this as technique, not metaphor.

- Walk it single file. Do not mark it; marks slide off it the way eyes do.
- When the winding dead-ends, continue. When it dead-ends a second time, continue. The path completes on the third nowhere. Counting them helps; it gives the faith a shape.
- Do not search for the fold the way you'd search for a trail. It does not respond to scrutiny. It responds to persistence. Walk it; don't look at it.
- If it genuinely will not open on a given day, go back down to your cache and come back another morning. It is not refusal. It is just not Tuesday.

## The Lip: Threshold Protocol

The crossing is like entering a room, and you should treat it with a room's etiquette. At the lip, stop. This is the last ordinary ground.

**The metal check.** The iron trees are built in laminations — layers thin as pastry leaves — for the same reason transformer cores are: to keep the eddies out. The bowl's field induces currents in solid conductors, and your belongings are not laminated. Before you cross:

- **Remove every closed loop of metal.** A ring is a one-turn induction coil fitted snugly to a finger. Earrings, metal watchbands, underwire bras — all of it comes off and goes into the lip cache. Zippers and small fittings are tolerable. Fillings you cannot remove; they will tick, and it is harmless.
- **Leave electronics at the lip.** Phones, GPS, digital watches, power banks, drones — induced currents will fry or confuse them, and none of it works right inside anyway. Bring a mechanical film camera if you want photographs (the light inside is honest, properly exposed noon; the film will be fine). Bring a paper notebook and a pencil.
- **Powered implants are an absolute contraindication.** Pacemaker, ICD, insulin pump, cochlear implant: do not cross. The test is free and built in — you will feel the hum in your teeth at the lip. If your fillings are ticking, a powered device is already inside the field. Turn back at the teeth.

Leave the cache just outside your crossing point, shaded, marked with bright cloth. Add a liter of water to it — this is your fold-delay insurance for the exit. Take the wide photograph now, from the lip: the bowl is the size of a village square, entire at a glance, like a pocket watch opened on a palm. It is the only vantage from which it fits in one frame. Inside, you are within the mechanism.

Then cross. Your ears may pop; the wind stops at the boundary as if it had been noise from another part of the building. Your shadow will hesitate once on the threshold. Pause and let it catch up with you. After that it behaves — mostly.

Before descending, read the floor from above. Locate the blooms (porcelain-skirted, on dark stalks) and the corridors between them. Check the dry channels seaming the floor: if they are dark, it is the dry century and you may walk freely; if they are glowing sodium-orange, you have arrived in the wet season — which here is a temperature, not a weather — and the floor carries rivers of molten light. Reroute around them and do not cross. And note the star holding steady at the zenith: your one fixed point in a place that will otherwise decline to be mapped.

## Inside the Bowl

**The blooms.** The emptiness between them is load-bearing, and you are a conductor. Never stand between a bloom and its neighbor; if your route crosses a corridor, cross it promptly and keep moving. A bloom opens over thirty years and discharges perhaps once a decade, but you cannot read the schedule — give any bloom that looks near-open a wide berth. If you smell ozone with no event seen, or your fillings tick repeatedly without cause, shorten your visit: the tuning may be approaching threshold, and you are the most persuadable gap in the bowl.

**The trees.** Put your ear to a trunk. It is required. The hum you have felt in your teeth since the lip becomes, against the iron, a low held organ note — the sound of a line on shift. The trunks are warm and faintly oily with their own freshness. On hot afternoons the sap beads and runs, quick and mirror-bright, the way water flees a hot pan. It is gallium, or something gallium remembers being: do not touch it barehanded (nitrile gloves, or wipe fastidiously after), and under no circumstances let it touch anything aluminum you own — gallium destroys aluminum on contact, and whatever this is will not be gentler. Do not drink it. There is nothing in this valley your body can drink. The water table here is a voltage; a well would fill, slowly, with light.

**The felt.** Walk on it. The wire-grass gives and springs back, holds a faint light for a breath where you press it, and forgets your footprints by dark — it is the fastest thing in the bowl and the only forgiveness on offer. Soft camp shoes are kinder to it than lugged boots, and quieter, which you will want: your footfalls are the loudest thing that has happened here in some time.

**The shadows.** There are more of them than there are things — the floor carries the shadows of a denser garden, cast backward from some fuller shift. At noon, stand in one. It is the only amenity the Draft offers: shade from a tree that has not been placed yet, and it is genuinely cooler. Expect, while you stand there, to feel read — like a proof being checked over someone's shoulder. It passes when you move. Most people move.

**The margins.** Stay off the newest ground near the rim. This is a work site with no workers: forms half-sunk in fresh rock turning slowly as hour hands, extruding crystal; a boulder specified on one face and a low gray suggestion on the other; a fault line that stops mid-word. Your eye will slide off these things and you will forget them while looking at them — that is normal, and it is also the warning. Do not touch the turning forms. Do not lean on the half-specified boulder. Take your photographs anyway, expecting the margins to come back blank or unfindable; write your notes immediately, expecting them to be the first thing you stop believing.

**The small things.** The dry vents breathe heat without steam — test the air above them with a held hand, never your face. The sulfur flowers blooming on the black glass are the foundry's bouquets; do not pick them. If a seed lands near you — a wound coil the size of a fist, arriving with a tick — let the felt forgive it before you approach, and do not catch one mid-drift. Do not pick anything up at all, in fact; see below. And count the trees. Record the number. Count again. Record that number too. Both are correct. That is what *draft* means.

## The Body

You will not feel yourself sweat — the held, dry heat evaporates it instantly — so drink on a schedule, not on thirst, and take salt with every meal. The honest gauge is urine color, which brings up the second rule of moisture: **pack out all of it.** Every drop, in a bottle, out over the lip. This is not ordinary Leave No Trace. You are carrying the only water this world has ever contained. Nothing here has ever rusted, because rust requires water — do not be its first rain.

And a related temptation, because you will feel it: the newest stone has gutters cut into it, channels with the profiles of rain-thinking, installed by whatever specifies such things, never once used. You will want to pour a little of your water into one, as a gift — a first rain. Don't. The rumor of water is not yours to deliver, and the water is yours to drink.

Eat cold, dense, salty food on a schedule; heat lies about appetite. No stove — a canister stove is solid metal in an induction field, there is nothing here that burns, and the heat is being held for you anyway. High-SPF sunscreen, lip balm, saline nasal spray (the air cracks mucosa; expect nosebleeds without it), serious sunglasses for the mica glitter and the enamel sky, a bandana or respirator against scree dust and mica flake.

Time: your clocks will not survive the visit. Quartz glitches; a mechanical watch magnetizes. Eat and drink by schedule agreed with your companion, judge the hour by the sun, and do not rely on the zenith star — it holds steady all afternoon, a rivet, fastening the sky rather than marking it.

## Time and Company

Go with one other person. Not three — the bowl is sparse and a crowd would feel wrong — but two: one to carry water if the other is sick, and one to believe with. The bowl resists being believed once; two witnesses are much harder to erode.

Stay long enough to include a noon — the shadows are the reason — and no longer than two nights. The limit is not danger. The limit is acclimation. After an hour the categories leak — machine, plant, mineral — and after a day your warmth reads as fuel and your pulse as a small unreliable clock brought in from outside. Keep human rituals: meals at set times, notes read aloud, names said. If you stay the night, the dusk brings a single tick of cooling metal — a shift note, not an alarm — and the felt will write your steps in brief light and erase them. Sleep is reported to be shallow and full of diagrams. Earplugs will not help; the hum is in your teeth and bone. Accept it the way you accept a ship's engine.

Nothing will happen for you. A bloom opens in thirty years; the rim advances a hand's width in a century; the felt, the hasty one, forgets you by dark. Your whole visit is a single tick of cooling metal at dusk. Do not wait for anything. Arrive having already decided when you will leave.

## Leaving

Allow hours for it. The fold in the scree resists being seen twice, so the way out hides — not physically; perceptually. The technique is the same as the way in, inverted: do not search for the fold; walk it. Start down, let the winding happen, endure the two nowheres, take the third. If it will not resolve, bivouac at your lip cache (this is why the extra liter lives there) and try again at a different hour. Never force a descent of the raw scree at night; the talus at night is worse than the fold's stubbornness.

At the lip the wind resumes like noise from another room. Retrieve your cache; your rings will feel like relics. Then descend, and — this is counsel, not rule — do not turn around to verify the bowl. It hides. The glance back is where belief starts leaking, and you will need your belief intact for what comes next.

## Afterward

The same evening, before you sleep, both of you write full accounts — independently, without discussing them first. Then compare. Photograph the pages. Mail a copy to yourself; the postmark is an anchor the Draft cannot argue with. Do all of this not for posterity but for yourself, next month, when the whole memory begins to feel like something you read once. Expect the film to show blanks at the margins. Expect your tree counts to disagree with your companion's, and with your own.

Keep the magnetized watch. It is the honest souvenir: a small unreliable clock, which is all you ever were there. Take nothing else — no mica flake, no fallen seed, no stone. Objects from the Draft do not travel well; the finished ground there completes itself quietly and then, without seam or ceremony, stops being here, and a pocketed stone may do the same, or may sit on your shelf resisting being seen until you forget you own it. Photographs, notes, and the watch are enough.

The lasting effect is the one you cannot pack for. You climbed to the bowl across plains and scree, and after you have stood in it, all of it — every step you have ever taken, anywhere — carries the unmistakable feel of product. Of output. The pavement at home will feel finished in the manufactured sense, tenderly archived, stood upon by its makers. Most travelers report that this never entirely fades, and most report that they stopped wanting it to. Treat it as integration, not symptoms: walk barefoot for a while. Notice the ground. Be gentle with things; they are artifacts now.

If you try to return — and you will try to return — know that the fold resists a second finding, and most second attempts end on ordinary scree, walking a gyre that goes nowhere twice and then nowhere again. If you go anyway: wait years. Bring new faith, not the old map. If you get in, nothing will be where you left it and nothing will have moved. A bloom may be open that was closed. The rim will be a hair wider. Your footprints were forgiven before your first evening ended.

## Pack Lists

**Carry over the lip:** all your water in plastic (no steel bottles — they warm); cold salty food and electrolytes; stiff boots, gaiters, gloves, soft camp shoes; sun hat, sunglasses, sunscreen, bandana; bivy and warm layer; paper notebook and pencils; mechanical film camera; first aid (cuts, blisters, altitude medication, analgesics); nitrile gloves and wipes; headlamp you are prepared to lose; wide-mouth bottle, for the rule of water.

**Leave at the lip, cached and marked:** rings, earrings, watchbands, underwire; phone, GPS, power bank, drone; steel thermos; spare batteries; one liter of water and a day's food, against the fold's refusal.

**Leave at home:** powered implants (or do not come); the water filter; the stove; anything lithium you would mourn; the need for anything to happen; and any intention to prove the place, improve it, water it, or bring a piece of it back.

One last thing, and it is not equipment. *No hand has touched this place, because hands are what it is for.* When you stand in the bowl and feel the categories leak, remember which side of the manufacture you are on: not the worker, not the tool — the product, back on a site visit. Conduct yourself the way finished work should conduct itself in the shop that made it.

Go quietly. Drink on schedule. Keep faith through the second nowhere.
