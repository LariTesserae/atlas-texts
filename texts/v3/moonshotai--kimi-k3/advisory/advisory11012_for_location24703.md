# Briefing: The Young Mountain

## Understand where you are going

This is not a reef. It is not a wreck. It is a construction site, a concert hall, and a chapel occupying the same bowl of fresh basalt at the bottom of the twilight. The rim sits where the last thin blue dies — figure roughly a thousand meters down, a hundred atmospheres, in water as warm as a held hand. Everything about your preparation follows from those four facts: the pressure, the warmth, the dark, and the newness.

You are an ordinary human. Nothing about this place will accommodate that. Prepare accordingly.

## Pressure and breathing

There is no unprotected version of this visit. A thousand meters is beyond any scuba, beyond any saturation table, beyond any record a human body has honestly set. You need a one-atmosphere solution: a crewed submersible with good manipulators, or a hard atmospheric diving suit rated well past the depth. If your travels have given you access to exotic kit — pressure-equalizing membranes, liquid breathing — leave it stowed anyway. The anomalies here act directly on whatever is in the water, and you want a hull between you and the mountain's habits.

Arrive high, above the rim, in open water. Never translate directly into the crater, and never, under any circumstances, into the lake.

## Warmth

Deep water is supposed to be cold. This water is not. Held-hand warm at depth, blood-warm at the lake, hotter at the seeps and chimney throats. You will not get hypothermia; you will slowly cook inside your own suit. Bring active cooling — a circulating garment, more than you think you need — and treat sweating, headache, and irritability as instrument readings, not moods. Test every rock with a probe before a hand. The cracks with the dull orange seam-glow are described as holding *remembered* heat. Let the probe find out how good the rock's memory is.

## Light

Bring your own sun and keep it on a leash. Below the rim there is no light but the seam-glow and, occasionally, the jelly. Carry dimmable floods for the bowl and a tight spot for detail, and default to low. Your lamps will be the brightest event in this crater's short life. There are five tussocks of pale grass near the rim feeding half on the last thin blue — do not park your hull's shadow over them, and do not blast them with light to admire them. Time your arrival for local noon on the surface above; the rim will be as lit as it ever gets, and you can watch the blue give out as you descend past it.

## Sound: the three rules

There is no quiet version of this place, and water does not muffle sound — it delivers it whole to your skeleton. You will hear the carillon of cooling through your teeth and the long bones of your arms. You will feel the lake's bass note everywhere at once. Earplugs are nearly useless against bone conduction; a damped helmet helps some. Accept that you will not hear the mountain so much as be included in it.

Three rules:

1. **No active sonar. No pinging. No loud tools.** The mountain answers questions it has not yet been asked. Do not ask it anything with a transducer. Passive hydrophones only — and bring them, because the carillon is the correct souvenir.

2. **A crack you hear is a crack that is coming.** Sound arrives before its cause here. If a crack rings out close by, move before the rock obliges it. If it rings across the bowl, note the direction and continue. This is not a haunting; it is an early-warning system. Use it gratefully and do not test it.

3. **When the silence falls, hover and hold.** The lake turns over on no schedule, and when it does, all sound stops at once — the only silence here, and total. That silence is when the chimneys grow: a hand's width of new mineral in a held breath. If you are touching a chimney, let go. If your line crosses the chimney field, it may be pinned there forever — route it so it doesn't. Lift off the bottom, keep station, and wait. The beads on the shore will chime when the lake finishes its roll. The chime is your all-clear. Do not resume until you hear it.

## The lake: standing orders

The pearl-green water in the crater's heart is denser than the sea holding it, has a true surface, a salt-crusted shore, weather of its own — and gravity that points at it from every side. A stone released beside it falls sideways, and the stone is correct. This means:

- **Tether before you approach.** Anchor to the rim, reel slack as you descend, and trust the line over your inner ear, which will lie to you near the lake. Keep a standoff distance until you know how the pull behaves at your mass.
- **Never breach the surface with any part of yourself.** From above, you do not see the lake bed; you see the crater from beneath, as if the lake were the sky of a smaller, hotter world. Sometimes your own face arrives on that surface a breath after you do. Inspect with a camera on a pole. Do not look down at it with your bare face, and do not make a habit of looking at all. Some mirrors here are slow.
- **Do not be on the shore when the surface tilts.** If the lake sinks at one shore and rises at the other, the roll is beginning and the shoreline will be elsewhere when it ends. Leave nothing on the salt crust you want back. Watch the slow dimples on its face — nobody knows the schedule, but a stirring lake is a lake to back away from.
- **Mind your wake.** The water keeps shapes the way warm wax keeps them; your wake will stand behind you for minutes, a glass sculpture of you having passed. Move slowly. Do not back into it. And brief your buddy: in the dark, in lamplight, a standing wake shaped like a diver looks like a diver. Confirm each other by light signal, never by silhouette.

## Rock, glass, and the furnished room

Every edge here is a first edge. The basalt is glassy with newness and will take skin through a glove seam. Full coverage, no bare anything, edge guards on every line, no kneeling, no bracing a palm without looking. The obsidian beads on the shore are knuckle-sized and sharper than any scalpel you own — and they are the lake's chime, part of the instrument. Leave them.

What the crater holds is countable, and you should count it, every visit, the way a pilot reads instruments: a half-circle colonnade, eleven stair-ledges, three chimneys standing, two fallen, five tussocks, one eel. The colonnade rings when the current worries loose stones through it — learn its baseline chord, because a new note means the current has moved something, which means conditions have changed. Keep your hands out from between the columns; the stones that play it are in motion. The stair is level as laid flagstone and is your best resting place and instrument platform. Count the ledges as you descend; they are your depth cues in the dark.

Outside the rim, down is the only direction and it is total. The slope lets go of everything. Cache nothing there, drop nothing there, and if you find yourself drifting past the rim, trim positive and come back inside, where down is negotiated.

## The neighbors

Life is sparse and mostly waits. White eyeless crabs farm bacteria in the fur of their claws, some of them upside down on the underside of the lake's sky — do not disturb the veils they work. The eel in the colonnade is the only creature here that has ever been seen twice; it is a resident in a way you are not, so give it the right of way and do not follow it. When the great bell jelly comes over the rim, slow as weather, carrying stolen light in its tissues — dim your lamps and let it be the lamp. It arrives like a light carried into a chapel. Behave as you would in a chapel.

## Time

Give the place an hour and it will spend it — but plan sorties of two to four hours, enough to see a full cycle: the carillon, the lake's breathing, the doming inhale, the turnover, the silence, the growing chimneys, the chime, the resumption. Do not leave before you have watched one silence; it is the only time you can watch the mountain build. Several short visits beat one long one: infrasound doses accumulate, heat soaks in, and this place rewards return — it is not done deciding what it is, and no single hour will show you the same crater twice.

## Mind and company

Do not go alone. Not because anything here hunts — nothing does — but because you will hear cracks before they happen, see your face arrive late, and watch your own wake stand in the water like a monument, and you will want a second witness for your own sake. Keep a spoken log. Count the furnishings when you feel unmoored. Expect the bass note to press on your mood; expect the silence to be the most affecting thing you have ever not heard. Decide in advance what you will do in it. Watching the chimneys grow is recommended.

## What to take home

Recordings. The carillon, the bass, the chime of the beads, the silence itself — which will be the only true silence on your tape. Nothing physical. This is a room, furnished, and the nothing in it is well-kept. You are a small warm thing with a lamp, visiting a bell that has not finished being cast. It rings while it cools. Listen, keep your hands to yourself, wait for the chime, and come back while it is still deciding.
