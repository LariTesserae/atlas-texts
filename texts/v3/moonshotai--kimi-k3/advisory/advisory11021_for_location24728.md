# Preparing for the Lowdark

Most of your preparation happens before you ever reach the iron-colored slopes, and most of it is subtraction. You are a seeing animal. The Lowdark has no use for seeing animals — it has one use for light, and that use is a mouth. So the first thing to understand, fully, in your bones and not just your plans, is this: you are not going to *see* the Lowdark. Nothing ever has. You are going to hear it — and it is going to hear you. To be is to be heard, down there. The moment you step onto the floor you join the ledger of sound: your breath, your heartbeat, your footfalls, the small cloth noises of your clothing. You are not visiting a place. You are joining a choir that has been singing since before anything remembers, and the choir will notice the new voice.

Prepare accordingly.

## 1. Become a listening animal before you leave

Give yourself months, not weeks.

**Live in darkness.** Rent, build, or find a lightless space and spend lengthening stretches in it — hours, then a day, then several. Not sleeping: *functioning*. Cooking, packing, walking, navigating. You are training your brain to stop waiting for photons and start building the world out of echoes and textures.

**Learn to echolocate.** Humans can do this; it is a trainable skill, not a gift. Tongue-clicks, soft taps of a staff. Start in rooms, then corridors, then wooded ground at night with eyes closed. By the time you descend, you should be able to walk toward a wall and stop before you touch it, using only the return of your own sound. In the Lowdark this is not a party trick. It is your eyes.

**Invert your threat response.** This is the hardest drill and the most important. Every instinct you own says: *noise means danger, silence means safety.* In the Lowdark it is exactly backwards. The full wall of voice — the hum, the clicks, the screams, the wing-thunder — is the all-clear. It means the basin is feeding, singing, busy, indifferent to you. Silence is the only warning the place gives. When the world stops, something is moving that makes no sound at all. You must practice until the reflex is autonomic: sudden silence → freeze → slow the breath → wait. Not crouch-and-look. Not run. Freeze. The Hush lasts seconds or minutes, and the traveler who makes the only sound in the basin during it has introduced themselves to the Hushmaker. Drill this the way soldiers drill incoming fire: a partner, a training space, a sound cut without warning, again and again, until your body does it before your mind finishes the sentence.

**Inoculate yourself against phantom light.** After long enough in true darkness, the human visual system begins to invent things — drifting sparks, pale blooms, a candle that isn't there. This happens to everyone. In any other world it is a curiosity. In the Lowdark it is a lethal ambiguity, because the only real light in the basin is bait, and you will not be able to tell a lure from your own neurons misfiring. So the rule must be absolute and pre-committed, decided now, in the light, while you can still think: *you never move toward light. Ever. Even if you are certain it is your companion's lamp. Especially then.* Say it out loud before you go. Write it on your gear. Any light you see down there is either a mouth or your own mind, and both are answered the same way: stillness, and then slow withdrawal in another direction.

**Train your ears like a musician's.** You will need to parse layered sound the way a conductor parses an orchestra — to lift the hum out of the screams, the screams out of the wing-weather, and to notice the one thing that has changed. Practice with dense recordings: orchestras, crowds, rainforests at night. Learn to estimate direction and distance. Learn to tell "many small things" from "one large thing" by ear alone.

**Train stillness and silence.** Sit motionless for two hours. Then four. Learn to breathe slowly under stress. And break yourself of every nervous sound: no humming, no whistling, no tongue-clicking absentmindedly, no drumming fingers. Your voice, down there, is the rarest sound in the world. Novelty draws investigation. You do not want to be investigated.

## 2. The laws you cannot break

Carry these the way you carry your water.

- **No light. None.** Not a headlamp, not a chemlight, not a glowing watch dial, not a fire — there is no fuel anyway, only the living and the damp. Everything in the basin that kept its eyes kept them for one purpose: to be drawn. Light your lamp and you have rung the dinner bell for the most specialized predators in the country. Carry one shielded emergency light, wrapped deep in your pack, for the single scenario in which dying in the dark is otherwise certain — and know that using it changes you from visitor to event.
- **The Hush means freeze.** Already covered. It cannot be covered enough.
- **Do not trust the second sound.** Echo-wells return sound late and wrong, and hunters station themselves at the wells to let the lying echoes do their work. If your footfalls come back to you with extra steps in them, leave. If a sound arrives twice, believe the first arrival and disbelieve the copy. Never call out to test an echo. Never navigate toward a sound you cannot corroborate with touch and memory.
- **Do not ring the stones.** The resonant towers and blades and cliff-faces are not scenery; they are speech. Creatures drum them to declare territory, and at the breeding pulse whole cliffs sing with claims. Strike one accidentally — a knocked stone, a careless staff-blow — and you have said something in a language you do not speak, possibly a challenge, possibly to something the size of a house. Pad your gear. Mind your elbows.
- **Never be still on the open floor.** The floor is not the floor; it is a crowd that moves in one grazing direction, and the gathered — those colony-walkers that flow over carcasses like poured things — read the motionless as either food or material. Rest on ledges, on shell-dunes, in a hammock slung between formations. Never lying on the ground. Never sleeping on the ground.
- **Water is a hunting ground.** Everything drinks at the seeps and the condensation threads, which means everything that eats drinkers waits there too. Approach water during loud weather, drink fast, leave.

## 3. What to carry

**For your ears — the most important items you own.** Adaptive electronic hearing protection: the kind that compresses sudden loud sounds while passing quiet ones through. This is not optional. The concussion-clicks of hunting bursts arrive as physical blows, pressure behind the eyes, a thump in the sternum — and chronic exposure to the basin's full voice will erode your hearing, which is to say, erode your only working sense. Carry spares. Treat your ears the way a diver treats their regulator. Never fully plug them; a blocked ear is a blinded eye.

**For your lungs.** A soft, baffled respirator with particulate and acid-gas filtration, and spare cartridges. The air carries ammonia, sulfur, wet iron, the smother's falling ash, molt-snow, and the motes the velvet casts. It is breathable — but you will be breathing it for days, hard, on the climb out. The exhalation valve must be quiet. Test it.

**Clothing: the wardrobe of silence.** Soft, brushed, rustle-free layers — wool above all. No stiff synthetics that swish. **No velcro** — velcro is a scream in that world. Tape every zipper pull and metal toggle. Sealed boots with soft, quiet soles, gaiters sealed at the top against the slug-tide and the films. Thin gloves you can feel through, and spares. A hooded veil for ash-fall. Everything you bring will become wet and stay wet; pack for total saturation.

**Sleep.** A hammock with fabric straps (silent), strung between formations, off the floor, out of the grazing tide and the gathered's path. A mesh bivvy rather than a sealed tent — you cannot afford walls; sound is your warning system and you must sleep inside it, not behind a barrier. An insulated pad. Chemical warmers for the cold troughs. Do not be tempted by the huddle-mounds — predator and prey stacked in steaming armistice is a miracle to witness and a terrible place to be when the mound dissolves back into appetite.

**Water and food.** A condensation sheet — a cool metal plate or tarp for the cloud-lid's sweat to bead on — plus filter and purifier for the seep-springs, whose mineral load is harsh. Bring all your food; the local life runs on sulfur-and-iron chemistries that have never negotiated with a human gut, and the grazers concentrate whatever the seeps sweat out. Repackage every ration into soft, silent, odor-sealed containers before arrival — no crinkling wrappers, ever. Carry double what the descent requires. The way in is down. The way out is up, on deadened legs, in the cold damp, and gravity is not your friend in that direction.

**Tools.** A sounding staff with two ends — a soft leather cap for probing the floor quietly, a hard striker for when you need an echo — which is also your last-resort weapon. Cordage. A knife (a knife is quiet). A non-luminous, vibrating timekeeper. A med kit heavy on coagulants and antisepsis: assume that bleeding draws attention and that every scrape inoculates you with the velvet's chemistry. Sealed bags for your waste — you will pack out everything, because everything you leave is an anomaly, and anomalies get investigated.

**Leave behind:** firearms (one shot would deafen you, ring every stone for a mile, and paint a target the size of the basin on your chest), anything luminous, anything that beeps, anything you cannot afford to lose in the dark.

## 4. How to move

Move during loud weather. Wing-thunder overhead, a ring-stone chorus, the full screaming swell of the floor — these are your travel windows, the masking noise under which your small sounds vanish. In lulls, you freeze or creep. Learn to read the basin's voice the way a sailor reads the sky.

When you cross a slug-tide — and you will; in the rich hollows they lie knee-deep, a slow wet tide of them — treat it like fording a river of flesh. Angle with the grazing direction, not against it. Accept what your soles are telling you: you are walking on bodies, and every step is a killing. Make your peace with that before you descend, or do not descend. The Lowdark does not offer guiltless ground.

When you feel a concussion-click — the thump in the sternum, the pressure behind the eyes — drop to stillness at once and let the hunt pass. Then, when the brief unraveling sounds begin, move: a feeding hunter is an occupied hunter, and grim as it is, that is your window.

Note the huddle-mounds and give them wide, respectful berth; a mound means a cold trough, so layer up before you reach one, and never disturb it — a broken mound is a panic of everything at once. Note the gathered's paths and never camp in them. Count the slow, huge, widely-spaced wing-beats of the high fliers when they cross beneath the lid, and let them remind you of the scale of what shares the dark with you.

Learn the chord of the country around your entry point. The hum has geography — the seeps breathe gas through hollow stone, and the resonance shifts from region to region — and the ring-stones are landmarks that sing. They are the lighthouses of the Lowdark. Fix their voices in memory. And remember, always, which way is up: the basin slopes toward its heart, so down is in and up is out. If you are ever truly lost, ascend.

## 5. How to live there

There are no days. There are pulses — rain, breeding, molt-tide, lean — and your circadian rhythm, deprived of light, will drift into chaos within a day or two. Fight it with your timekeeper: fixed sleep periods, fixed movement periods, fixed rituals. Ritual is portable sanity. The off-watch inventory, the whispered check-in, the meal at the appointed hour — these small ceremonies are load-bearing.

Go with one or two companions; never alone, never a crowd. Every added person doubles your noise signature. Agree on a tactile code — pulls on a soft link-rope, taps on a shoulder: *stop, freeze, regroup, withdraw* — and drill it until it is faster than speech. Speech is for emergencies only. Decide in advance what you will do if separated, because you cannot shout for a lost companion and you cannot light a beacon: a rendezvous formation, a click pattern, a time. And agree that anyone who sees a light says so immediately, and no one, under any circumstances, goes to look.

Sleep to the basin's full voice. This will feel wrong the first time and natural by the third: the wall of sound becomes the condition of the air, a roar that resolves into a kind of silence, and it is precisely what you want to hear as you drift off — because it means everything is normal. What should wake you, what must wake you, is the absence. If you wake and the world has stopped: do not sit up. Do not whisper. Breathe slowly and wait for the ragged, swelling return of the voice.

Expect the psychological weight. The screams whose reasons you would rather not know. The floor moving under you in its patient grazing direction. The darkness that is not an event but a medium, hour upon hour, until your eyes give up and start manufacturing ghost-lights. Expect the moment — it comes to everyone — when the noise stops registering as noise and starts registering as weather, and you realize you have begun to think in it. That moment is not breakdown. It is arrival. But it is also your cue to start counting the days remaining, because a traveler who stops wanting to leave has stopped preparing to leave.

## 6. When to go, and when to go home

Time your arrival for the tail of a rain-pulse: the films blooming, the floor loud with grazers, the hunters fat and vocal — maximum masking noise, minimum desperation. Avoid the lean times entirely; a basin where the hunters have begun taking each other is a basin with no slack in it. The breeding pulse is the basin's great music — entire cliff-faces struck a thousand times a minute, chords rolling for miles — and it is worth the risk of territorial fury if, and only if, you have already survived a quieter visit.

Stage in the high bare country above the cloud-lid. Cache supplies at the rim. Descend in stages — rim, slopes, floor's edge, deep floor — and let your ears acclimate before the full wall of voice hits you; a human who walks from the gray dusk-country straight into the deep basin will panic, and panic is noise, and noise is attention.

Stay days, not weeks. Set your exit criteria before you enter — a date, a supply level, any measurable hearing change, any injury — and honor them without negotiation. The basin's cycles are ancient and indifferent; they will not tell you when to leave. The most dangerous traveler in the Lowdark is the one who says *one more pulse*. Leave while you are still strong, because the way out is up, and up is work.

## 7. What you owe the place

Take nothing. Every shell is architecture-in-waiting; the dead are the ground; the accretion and the wearing are in a balance older than memory, and your souvenir is not in the budget. Leave nothing — no markers, no trash, no food, no blood if you can help it. Drum no stones, answer no echoes, join no mounds, follow no lights. If you must keep something, keep sound: a recorder is the one instrument the Lowdark does not object to, since sound is the only thing it gives away freely, endlessly, to anything willing to hear. It has never once been seen. It does not ask to be. It asks — if it asks anything — to be heard, and to be left exactly as unwitnessed as you found it.

You will come back up into the wind-scoured iron country changed in small permanent ways. You will hear silence as an event for the rest of your life. You will flinch at sudden quiet and relax into crowds. You will close your eyes to listen to things, and people will find it strange, and you will not entirely be able to explain that you once stood in a country-sized crowd of life in the dark — complete, in balance, deafening, glorious — and that for a few days, you were heard too.
