# Preparing for the Coldworks

The Coldworks asks three things of a traveler: warm blood, dry socks, and manners. You are an ordinary human, and nothing downstairs requires more than that — but it will require *all* of it. Prepare as you would for a working stay inside a young machine the size of a town, staffed by a handful, in a climate like a North Sea winter: wet cold, not dry, with a schedule older than the building that keeps it.

## Before You Descend

**Check the weather — the right weather.** The Works takes its weather by mail, two days late. Rain on the surface becomes the Lag underground forty-eight hours on: ceilings weeping harder, an indoor rain, faithful and delayed. So the forecast you need is not tomorrow's but the day before yesterday's. If it stormed topside two days ago, plan your canal walks and lower-avenue errands for later in the week. The oilskins on the hooks labeled RAIN GEAR are not a joke; the crews stopped finding it funny, and so will you.

**Choose companions if you can.** The cage is rated for six and usually carries one, and being alone downstairs is normal — not, in itself, an omen. But the Works is easier on pairs: someone to lean an ear toward in the pump hall, someone to stand beside at the Tide. If you go alone, you will be in the local majority.

**Fix your return date before you arrive, and write it down where it can be found.** The noticeboard will accept it; its rosters already name more people than anyone has ever met, so one more name is no burden. Whether signing protects you or enlists you is a question the crews leave alone. Sign anyway. Politeness is ballast down there.

**Duration.** Three to five days is a visit. Past two weeks you will catch yourself setting your day by the horn, and that is the first symptom of the condition the whole Works has — everything down there is learning the timetable, and the timetable is patient.

**Moor your way home at the top.** If your traveler's kit includes an anchor, beacon, or return door of any kind, set it at the surface or at Head Station. Do not attempt departure from the deep rooms. The Reservoir returns sound late; the Cold Store gets colder against the gradient; the Quiet Bay swallows the Hum whole. These are thin places, each thin along its own axis, and a traveler who steps out from a thin place may arrive the way the echo does — later than the distance allows. Use the cage. It is the intended path, and the intended path is the one the place respects.

## The Kit

**Wear:** wool or synthetic base layers, two sets, and no cotton anywhere on your body — the air is four degrees and saturated, and wet cotton at four degrees is a slow argument you will lose. Active insulation you can work in. A warm hat and a balaclava for the deep levels. Waterproof, insulated boots with real tread: gratings, wet concrete, lime film, rime. Gloves in three weights — liner, work (the arrises of that concrete could cut fruit; believe it), and waterproof shell. Zip pulls you can operate gloved.

**Carry:** a headlamp, a handheld light, and spare batteries kept in an inside pocket — the cold takes batteries first. A watch that glows. A thermos; it will be the second-warmest thing in the Works, and you will be third. More food than seems reasonable. Chalk. A notebook and pencil — ink freezes, pencil is immortal. A closed-cell sit pad, because every seat downstairs is a heat sink. Dry bags. Chemical warmers. A small first-aid kit.

**Borrow on site:** earmuffs from the pegboard at Head Station — a hundred pairs hung out is an invitation, not a display. Oilskins from the stairhead hooks. A helmet at the brine doors, if you're going deep. Return all of it. The Works lends; it does not give.

**Leave behind:** umbrellas (the indoor rain will tempt you; resist; the crews have earned sole rights to that joke), houseplants (the threshold garden already lost that argument), pets (the Works has one dog and he is sufficient), and anything you cannot carry back up smelling of lime.

**A note on optics:** coming up from the Cold Store into the damp halls, every lens you own will fog or weep. Bag cameras before the transition so the condensation forms on the bag. Carry a handkerchief. Accept that some views are for the notebook.

## The Descent

Nine minutes. Use them. Swallow and yawn from the first meter — your ears will pop twice and should not be allowed to fall behind. If you are congested, do not descend; ride back up and come tomorrow; the Works will keep. Add a layer at minute three. Watch the form-tie holes stitch past and the shaft water glitter; it is the last moving water you will see that isn't on a schedule. Around minute four your breath appears — that is the place reading you back. Then the Hum comes up through your boot soles before it reaches your ears. Do not brace against it. You will spend your whole visit leaning on it like a wind. Start practicing standing in it.

## First Hour

Set your watch by the station clock. Take earmuffs. Sign the roster. Greet Valve, asleep in his pool of sodium light — the warmest thing in the Works and aware of it. A scratch behind the ears if he offers an ear; do not ask him to move; he is, among other things, equipment.

Then walk the first three islands of lamplight out Long Street and back, so your eyes learn the dark's distances before your errands do. Claim any made-up bed in the dormitory — the nine slept-in ones are taken, however empty the street of rooms looks. Set your things at the canteen table's occupied end, and eat there. Sparseness here is not emptiness; it is occupancy at low pressure, and the pressure equalizes fastest at the table.

## The Weather Underground

**The Lag.** When the ceilings begin to weep harder, that is the surface's rain arriving two days late. Oilskins on. Shift your bed if it sits under a joint. Plan around it as you would any weather — because it is weather, simply delivered by a slower postal service.

**The Tide.** Posted hours. A horn — one blast, then two — and the canals stand up a foot and breathe out. Learn the times before you walk the lower avenues. A foot of rising black water is no danger if you're on the walkway and expecting it; the walkways are built for it. But don't be on the gratings at the waterline, and don't be startled by what rides through: insulation fiber, a single glove, things from bays nobody visits. About the eel: it is seen at the Tide, and it is seen by people who are alone. Decide which way you want that. If you hope to see it, go alone and watch the water. If you'd rather not, bring your companion and talk. Both choices are respected. And leave the loaches alone — they washed in from the aquifer, they are proof of something, and proof should not be fished for.

**The Chord.** Nine pumps of twelve is the normal voicing; you will learn it your first day whether you mean to or not. Wear plugs under the muffs for any long stretch in the hall — the whine lives in your teeth and the bass in your swallow, and both are cumulative. Speech is ear-to-ear; accept the intimacy or take it to the gallery. If the chord changes, note it. A missing tooth is only a pump in service. Anything else, mention at the canteen in a level voice. You cannot read the plant by ear the way the old hands do, but you can notice the way a dog notices.

**The Thaw.** Once a day, in the Cold Store, the rime lets go all at once and it sleets indoors for twenty minutes. Helmet on, or door closed; do not be in the aisles. The crane creeps its rows on its own schedule, unattended and unbothered, and it has right of way the way weather has right of way. Watch one Thaw from the doorway before you decide how deep your business in the racks really goes.

## Getting Around

Long Street runs four kilometers; walk it or bring a bicycle — the crews do; the single tire track crossing a dry hall's dust is the local signature. Travel is island-hopping: orange pool, dark span, orange pool. A third of the lamps are dead, and the dark between is not absence — it is terrain, and it has distances you will learn. Keep your headlamp off in the lit pools. Your eyes need the practice, and the lamp gardens need their dark edges.

Chalk your turns at junctions; chalk persists down here — the builders' signatures in Bay 40 are the proof. Do not sign Bay 40. Sign elsewhere, small, dated.

The junction maps are honest as far as they go. They omit halls, and the hall numbers skip. Let them. The omitted halls are omitted from the maps and should be omitted from your itinerary. That is the whole of the arrangement.

## The Tram

It comes out of the dark with its lamps lit and no driver, frost on the insides of its windows, on a route no map carries. Step back from the rails when you hear the bell. If it passes, note the time — it is always on time. If it stops for you, and one day it may stop for you: let it pass. Look at your map. Tie your boot. It has somewhere to be, and you do not. This is the one rule with no footnotes. Everyone who has ever kept it is why it remains a rule and not a story.

## Manners of the Rooms

**The Pipe Organ.** Go first to the visitors' gallery. The chairs are filmed with dust because there have never been visitors; you may be the first. Sit down. It is what the room is for, and being a tourist there is, uniquely in the multiverse, a historic act. Then the floor — muffs on, stays short.

**The Lamp Gardens.** Under every live lamp on a wet wall: a green rectangle, a moss fringe, the pale slow moths the crews call lampwives. Look as long as you like. Do not harvest, do not touch, and do not shine your handheld at them — the fixed lamp is the sun of that country, and your light is weather they didn't plan for. Where a lamp has died, its garden browns into a ghost. Read the walls like tree rings; annotate only in your notebook. Keep your food sealed — the rats keep to the warm motor rooms, and you should keep to the reverse arrangement.

**The Reservoir.** Through the lock doors: drinking-still, two degrees, no visible far side. Do not swim — water that cold takes the breath first and the rest within minutes. Do not lower the skiff; the davits are not an invitation. You may shout once, as the crews do, and count the seconds; the echo is an instrument, and a short one means a full house. If your echo comes back long — later than the map allows — write the number in your notebook and do not ask what it means. Nobody says, so nobody will answer, and asking twice marks you as new in a way that asking once does not.

**The Cold Store.** Colder as you descend, against the physics of holes. That is the whole point of the place, and it is not your mystery to solve. Rime furs the railings; do not touch it barehanded — skin sticks. The cylinders are numbered in the thousands and their contents are known by code: do not read the codes aloud, do not tally the rows, do not warm anything. The inspection port on the lowest level is sealed, its bolts undisturbed, frost on the *inside* of the glass. You may look once, from a standing step back. Do not bring your light to it. The frost is on the inside; whatever it is on the inside of, it does not need illuminating. If you ever find the bolts disturbed, ascend, and mention it at the canteen in a level voice.

**The Quiet Bay.** The absorptive tile stops the Hum dead, and your heart becomes the loudest machine in the Works. Go — but not on your first day, and not when you are already lonely, and chalk your name and the time outside the door. Stay until your pulse stops sounding like a warning. If you find yourself reluctant to leave, that is the sign to leave. The Bay is a decompression chamber for a pressure you didn't know you were carrying. Use it in doses, the way you'd use oxygen.

## Body and Mind

Eat more than you think you need; cold, damp, and vibration spend you quietly. Drink on a schedule — cold suppresses thirst. Sleep in the dormitory and nowhere else: not the gallery chairs, not the Quiet Bay, certainly not the racks. The showers run cold by design; budget accordingly. You may work a full shift and see one flashlight crossing a far junction like a ship at sea. Wave or don't; both are correct. If you find tea steaming on a bench and no one there, do not wait for the owner and do not worry for them. Low pressure. Occupancy. It is fine.

And know the exit symptom: when the timetable starts feeling like *yours* — when you know the Tide without checking, when the Thaw becomes your appointment — check the date you wrote down. Leave on it. You can come back. The Works is young and going nowhere; it is the rest of the multiverse that moves.

## The Rules

1. If the tram stops for you, let it pass.
2. Frost on the inside of the glass stays on the inside of the glass.
3. One shout at the Reservoir. Count. Accept the number.
4. Helmet under the Thaw. The crane has right of way. Nothing in the racks is yours to warm.
5. Rain gear is for rain — especially four kilometers underground.
6. The nine slept-in beds are taken. So is the forklift. So are the keys.
7. Return the earmuffs.
8. Leave Bay 40 to the builders. They are coming back after lunch.
9. If Valve goes to the cage, go to the cage. Nobody has written this one down either. Follow the dog.
10. Leave on the date you wrote down.

## Coming Up

Ride the cage in daylight, if the surface has any. The cold lets go a degree a minute; shed layers as you rise, because arriving topside sweat-soaked is how colds start. Your ears will pop twice. The Hum will leave your boot soles last, and for a while afterward you will keep reaching for it with your feet. The sun will feel unearned. Weather that happens the moment it happens will seem showy and rude. Somewhere, weeks on, you will hear a building's ventilation and think: *a tooth missing.* You will set your watch by nothing and miss the horn.

And in your cuffs, months later, you will find lime dust — the smell of newness itself. That is the Works keeping its appointment with you, by mail, on its own delay.

Let it. It is always on time.
