# Preparing for the Rehearsal: A Field Briefing

First, understand what kind of expedition this is. You are not visiting a hostile world. Hostile implies intent. You are visiting a *busy* one — a world mid-construction, indifferent to you the way a foundry is indifferent to a moth. Nothing there wants you dead. Nothing there will notice if you die. This is, perversely, both the good news and the entire problem.

Your enemies, in order of likelihood: thirst, sharpness, gas, altitude, gravity, and your own patience. The terrain's patience is geological. Yours is not. That asymmetry is the whole game.

---

## Before You Cross Over

**Acclimatize.** The high country is genuinely high — thin air, short breath, a resting heartbeat you can hear. If your crossing method allows it, stage somewhere elevated for a week beforehand. If it doesn't, plan your first two days in-world as near-total rest: walk an hour, sit an hour. Learn the symptoms of altitude sickness and treat headache-plus-nausea as a descent order, not an inconvenience. Consider acetazolamide if your physician agrees.

**Train for dark navigation.** There is no sun, no scheduled dawn, no stars you can trust — the red points on the horizon are mountains, and mountains move. Practice dead reckoning, pace counting, and route-finding by faint landmark in total darkness before you go. If you've never navigated without a sky, this world will teach you, and the tuition is steep.

**Get your dental work done first.** Not for the usual reasons — because the hum of a lifting ridge arrives through your skeleton, and travelers report that old fillings sing. It's harmless. It's also the kind of sensation you want to be *expecting* at hour thirty of a solo trek, rather than discovering fresh.

**Learn volcanic terrain.** Walk lava fields, obsidian flows, columnar basalt. Learn what fresh fracture looks like and how it behaves under load. Everything in this world is fresh fracture. Nothing has been tested by weather. Edges that look load-bearing may be Tuesday.

**Fix your exit.** Whatever brings you home — anchor, beacon, charm, door — goes *on your body*, never in a cache. A cached way home is a way home that a ridge can lift, a seam can cook, or first-dust can bury. Redundancy too: two if you can manage it.

## Water: The Whole Arithmetic

There is no water. Not scarce water, not hidden water — *none*, as a category, except as a structural idea inside certain crystals, which you cannot lick. The wettest thing for a hundred miles is you, and the dry air knows it: sweat vanishes before it beads, which feels efficient and is actually theft, because you never notice how much you're losing.

- Budget **six liters per person per day** for moderate exertion in warm, dry, thin air. Carry a thirty percent margin on top.
- This math is brutal and defines your trip shape. A week on foot means 40+ kilograms of water. Realistic options: short excursions of three to four days; staged caches (see below, with caveats); or reclamation gear — a stillsuit-style system or urine-recycling unit, which converts this world from "brief visit" to "expedition possible."
- Sip on schedule, not on thirst. Set a timer. In air that dry, thirst is a lagging indicator.
- Bring electrolytes and use them. Salt rims your lips within hours there; that's your body negotiating with a world that finds you implausible.
- Check urine color twice daily. Dark means you're already behind.
- The molten sulfur river with its blue flame is not water, is not near water, and offers you nothing but a landmark and a struck-match smell. Admire from upwind.

## Food and Fuel

Nothing is edible. Nothing has ever been edible; the concept hasn't been invented there. Carry everything: 2,500–3,000 kcal per day, favoring salty, fatty, low-moisture foods. There's no fuel for fires either, so bring a stove — or do what experienced travelers do and set your sealed rations on a warm slab for twenty minutes. The ground is a slow cooker that never asks anything of you. Just test slab temperature first; "warm as a held breath" grades into "baker's door" without warning near the crevasses.

## The Kit

**On your feet:** Stiff, cut-resistant boots with the thickest soles you can hike in — the ground is glass, literally, and it will find the soft part of your sole. Gaiters, because the first dust is angular, electrostatic, and climbs. Carry a resole kit and accept that your boots will come home looking chewed.

**On your hands, always:** Cut-resistant gloves. Treat every rock in this world as a knife that has never been blunted, because that is precisely what it is. The rule is simple: *no bare hand touches stone.* The one time you break it, bracing a stumble, is the one time the world collects a fingertip's worth of tuition.

**On your head:** A climbing helmet, worn whenever you're within falling distance of anything vertical — which is often, because spurs shear off new slopes without ceremony and fresh cliffs shed. Eye protection against spalling flakes: cooling rock *ticks*, and ticking rock occasionally flings a shard, knapped by nothing but thermal stress. A respirator or good dust mask; the dust is young silica, sharp at the microscopic scale too, and your lungs deserve old age.

**Light:** Your eyes will adapt to the deep red under-glow better than you'd expect — seam-light is enough to walk by once you've settled in. Carry a headlamp with a red mode to preserve that adaptation, plus two backup lights. Light discipline matters in reverse here: your lamp is the only artificial light in the world, and it flattens the subtle red gradients you're navigating by. Use it sparingly.

**Navigation and time:** Two watches, minimum, and a strict logged schedule — there is no dawn to anchor you, and "after you sleep" is not a time system. A compass may or may not behave; a world full of lightning and warm iron-rich stone is a world where you verify rather than trust. Better: an inertial tracker or diligent dead reckoning, a surveyor's notebook, and nightly azimuth checks on the red horizon-glows — recorded in pencil, because you'll be revising. Critical rule: **your own tracks cannot be trusted.** Footprints fill with first-dust or get carried off by uplift within the month, sometimes the week. Navigate every leg fresh, out and back.

**Instruments:** A small multi-gas meter (CO₂, SO₂, H₂S) is not optional if you intend to go anywhere near low ground — see below. A pocket thermometer or IR gun for checking ground heat before you sit, camp, or cook. A camera, because you are the only witness this world has ever had, and because in six months everything you saw will be unrecognizable and no one will believe you.

**Sleep system:** No insulation needed underneath — the ground is a warm wall, and sleeping on it is one of the great strange pleasures of the place, like being held by a kiln with good manners. What you need instead is a *puncture-proof* pad, thick, over a groundsheet you shake out for shards. A light tent keeps off dust and the occasional spall. Anchor everything; tremors are the local weather.

**Medical:** Weight your kit toward trauma — sutures, steri-strips, clotting gauze, a tourniquet, splint material — plus a burn kit for ground contact and sulfur-flame curiosity. Saline doubles as wound rinse. There's no infection risk anyone's documented — nothing lives there to infect you — but dust in a wound is its own problem, so irrigate generously.

## Rules of the Ground

These are learned from the world itself, and it grades on a curve of one.

**Light precedes terrain.** If you see red glowing *through* the rock along your route — a seam brightening beneath the surface — you are standing in a future mountain's blueprints. Seams crawl at walking pace, and uplift follows days behind. You can outwalk a seam easily; you cannot out-sleep one. Never camp on or downslope of a glow, and give the footprint a wide margin: the mountain is always bigger than its light.

**Watch the red not-stars.** If one has been getting closer for days, a seam is walking toward you. The correct response is unhurried and immediate: pack, move perpendicular to its approach, and feel free to admire the inevitability of it all from a safer postcode. Consider moving. Do not stay to be the first thing the mountain lifts — it will lift you gently, a handspan an hour, and then drop your camp off a fresh cliff it hasn't finished making.

**Ticking means fresh, fresh means shedding.** The whole range ticks like a cooling kiln, and that's ambient. What you're listening for is *change*: a quickening tick rate, a new pinging voice nearby. That's stress rearranging itself. Leave. And never linger at the base of any cliff — that little apron of first scree at the bottom is the mountain's opening bid, not its final offer.

**Feel, don't listen.** The air is too thin to carry sound honestly; booms arrive twenty seconds late and strangely gentle, like doors closing in another room. Your early-warning system is your boot soles, your sternum, a gloved palm flat on stone. Pause often. Put a hand down. The world is always talking; it's just using the floor.

**Low ground is a trap.** The deep crevasses concentrate heat toward baker's-door levels and pool whatever heavy gases the world is exhaling. CO₂ is odorless and the thin air won't help you notice. Never descend into any low place without the gas meter reading first, and if you catch the struck-match smell of burning sulfur, you are downwind of the river — move crosswind, then away.

**Respect the static.** Out on the charge-swept flats, the lightning is silent and the warning signs are small: dust behaving strangely, hair lifting, the glass needles that stand on their points taking their small correcting steps. If the needles are walking, *you* should be walking — elsewhere. Fulgurite fields, those hollow tubes scattered like a careful bird's dropped straws, mark recent strikes. Photograph from a distance; they're still warm, still ticking, and the storm that made them may not be done.

**Move camp every night or two.** Two reasons. First, the ground revises: what was flat when you slept may be a slope by your second sleep. Second — and travelers argue about this in low voices — the crystals nearest your bedroll lean toward where your body warmed the stone, by a degree you can barely prove, and the towers molt and regrow a body-length away, and nobody has ever established whether a growing structure would route *around* a sleeper. It probably would. Probably. Move anyway.

## The Structures

You will find them days apart, across long black glass plains: the Stair climbing into nothing with a new step accruing every few days; the spiral colonnade whose innermost columns almost arch; the waist-high wall that grew its doorway first and is building outward from the opening, as if the gap were the point.

No one built them. There is no one to have built them. Treat them the way you'd treat a cathedral mid-construction in a country whose language you don't speak: look, photograph, circle slowly, touch nothing, climb nothing, chip nothing. Not because anything will punish you — nothing will; nothing has ever needed to — but because you are the first soft thing to walk through a rehearsal that has been going on without an audience, and the only courtesy available to you is to not smudge the stage.

If you camp near one — and you will want to, the doorway-wall draws campers the way hearths draw hands — keep a respectful distance and keep your schedule. The mineral world responds to warmth slowly, at the pace of crystal habit, and you are the warmest idea it has ever had.

## The Daily Rhythm

With no dawn, you are the clock, and you must take the job seriously. Wake at your watch's hour, not your body's whim. Log everything: water consumed, seam positions, tick rates, azimuths to the horizon glows, ground temperatures at camp, sleep quality, mood. The log is not bureaucracy — it's how you notice that the red not-star has been getting closer, that your urine has been dark two days running, that your judgment has been quietly fraying since sleep cycle four.

Eat hot when you can. Wash with a cloth and fifty milliliters, no more. Talk out loud to yourself without embarrassment — in a world where every sound is the first of its kind, your voice gets to be the first of many of them, and the thin air makes you your own best company anyway.

Expect the psychological weather to be strange. The silence has texture. The darkness is warm, which your animal brain will read as *safe* in a way that is both wrong and wonderful. You will catch yourself anthropomorphizing the leaning crystals, the stepping needles, the wall with its doorway. Everyone does. It's not madness; it's pattern-hunger in a place with no patterns older than Tuesday. Let it comfort you. Don't let it make decisions.

---

## Turnaround Discipline

Set your abort criteria before you cross, write them on the inside of your logbook cover, and obey them without negotiation:

- Water at half with no cache in reach: turn back.
- Any gas reading you don't like: leave the area, then decide.
- A seam's light under your intended route or your exit corridor: reroute immediately, shorten the trip.
- Laceration deeper than a fingernail, any burn larger than a coin, altitude symptoms that don't clear with rest: go home. There is no rescue. There has never been anyone to perform one.
- Hair standing on end, needles walking, tick rate doubling: move first, analyze later.

---

## What It's Actually Like

For all the warnings, understand that this is one of the gentler hard places a traveler can choose. No cold, no storms worth the name, no predators, no infection, no malice — a warm dark quiet broken only by the world's soft, late, unhurried noises of making. You will sleep on warm stone like a cat on a windowsill. You will watch a ridge lift a body's height between meals, in near-silence, lit from within. You will be the first sound of many kinds. You will stand in a doorway that no one built, that was grown as an opening first, and you will feel — everyone reports this — that you have arrived very early to something.

You have. The whole world is a rehearsal, open to a dark sky, and you are a walk-on in work boots with a water budget. Move like a stagehand: quietly, attentively, never under the rigging, always aware of what's coming down from above and rising from below. Drink on schedule. Touch nothing bare-handed. Keep your way home on your body. And when the red light on the horizon starts keeping appointments with your skyline, consider moving —

or don't, and learn exactly how a mountain makes its entrance. But you can only take notes on that once.
