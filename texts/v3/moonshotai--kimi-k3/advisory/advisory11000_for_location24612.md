# Preparing for the Condensary

First, understand what you're packing for. The Condensary is three places wearing one coat: a ship, a bathhouse, and a high-altitude building site. Pack for all three simultaneously and you'll be comfortable. Pack for any one of them and you'll spend your visit miserable in an interesting way.

Nothing there will try to kill you on purpose. It's a workplace, not a gauntlet — inhabited, habitable, run by a crew who laugh under scheduled rain. But it is a *workplace*, small enough to walk around in ten minutes, perched above the weather, in permanent wet, and it has the workplace's calm indifference to visitors who don't watch their feet.

Three rules cover most of it:

1. **Everything is wet.** It never dries. Not the ground, not the rail, not you.
2. **Everything is measured.** The weirs are numbered, the gauges are glass, the records are pencil. The place is a sentence being written, and you will be a word in it while you're there.
3. **Everything you touch is someone's morning's work.** There is no wilderness and no idle scenery. The nearest wild thing is the cloud, and the cloud is cargo.

## What to pack

**Wear:** Quick-dry everything. Wool or synthetic against the skin — cotton will stay wet for your entire stay and take your mood with it. One warm layer for the lift ride up through the cloud (the cage is cold and the cloud is colder) that you'll shed at the landing into air like a held breath. A brimmed hat, not a hood — rain at the Condensary comes from above (condenser drip), below (weep channels), sideways (the fog tide), and on schedule (the Cold Main), and a brim handles all four better than fabric. Footwear is your most important decision: soft rubber soles, the kind made for wet river rock or boat decks. Much of the works is glazed, and wet glazing under a fingernail-thick sheet of running water is honest ice. The crew will be wearing the local answer; copy them at the first opportunity.

**Carry:** Roll-top dry bags, plural. One sealed, *never-opened-outdoors* set of dry clothes and dry socks for sleeping. Microfiber towels, two. A headlamp for the fog tide. A whistle on a cord. Sunglasses — the daylight goes bright, grey, bright again as clouds pass, and the bright, at that height, is closer to the sun than you're used to. Sunscreen for the same reason; you can burn through a gap in the grey in twenty minutes. Salts — electrolyte tablets or a pouch of good mineral salt. This is not optional, and here's why: the water is distilled-clean, straight out of the cloud, and it tastes like the ghost of water. You can drink your fill all day and still cramp, because it carries nothing. The crew will offer you water constantly; accept it always; take your salts quietly after.

**Gifts:** Dry things. This cannot be overstated. In a place that never dries, dryness is luxury. A genuinely dry towel is close to a religious experience. Good pencils and grease pencils are near-currency — the whole works is annotated in pencil because pencil writes on damp, and arriving with a fistful of quality pencils makes you instantly useful and instantly liked. Salt, dried fruit, waxed hard cheese, tea and coffee in tins. Fresh bread is a legend there. Songs and stories travel better than books; paper swells.

**Leave behind:** Leather (it will mildew before you descend). Umbrellas (wind at the rail, rain from below, pointless). Anything rust-prone that isn't oiled. Any expectation of horizon — your horizon is a ten-minute walk around a rail. And scrub your boots before you board the lift. The algae found the distilled water within a day of its first flowing, all by itself, arriving on wind or cloud. You are a weather system too, and it costs nothing not to seed the works with whatever's living in your tread.

## Arrival

The cage ride is your transition: cold, wet, rattling up through cloud, and then — the last of it thinning — warm, full air and a bell. Ring it once. It's how they count who's come up. Sign whatever slate or log they keep at the landing; they'll enter you the way they enter a gauge reading, and you should let them. It's how the place knows what's in it.

Take the first hour slowly, at the rail, and let your eyes do the strange thing. The whole works fits in a single turn of the head — and it behaves like a watershed. Your mind will want to read it as a model, something miniature and safe. Correct that thought firmly. Models don't have edges that kill. Below the rail the poured apron falls away, below the apron is air, and below the air, most days, is the top of the weather. Some travelers read that as a view and some read it as a summons. Know which one you are before you lean.

Walk the perimeter in the morning. By early afternoon the fog tide climbs the slope and the works goes inside its own weather, and the outer terraces are not where you want to be learning the layout in a whiteout.

## How the day runs

The place keeps its own schedule, and you should run on it.

**Morning** is for the outside: the perimeter, the condenser rows on the upper terraces — tall cold alloy fins, beaded and streaming, beautiful, and cold enough to take skin off a bare palm if you grip one in the warm air and forget what they are. Cold burns in warm fog are the Condensary's signature injury; touch things briefly until you know them.

**Early afternoon**, the tide comes in. Not water — cloud, climbing the slope like something that owns it, and then the world is forty feet of glowing grey. This is when you learn the interior navigation: the fog harps hum when the wind is right and are the closest thing the place has to lighthouses — lighthouses you hear. The Cold Main runs the length of the works at shoulder height and doubles as a handrail that rains. Count weirs. Stay off the perimeter rail in fog unless you're walking it hand-over-hand.

**Six o'clock** is the heart of it. The Cold Main sweats so hard in the warm air that it rains beneath it on schedule, all day, but six is when the crew walks under it bareheaded, laughing, in the middle of a clear sky. If you're invited — and you will be, if you've been even minimally decent company — go. Take nothing. Don't wear the hat. It is the only scheduled weather you will ever stand in, and it is how the place says welcome, and you will tell people about it for the rest of your life and they will not quite believe you.

## The water

It does not respect gravity, and you must. Pumps send it uphill as often as down; the same stream will pass you at ankle height in a glazed runnel and again overhead in a flume going the other way. Step over runnels, never in them — the algae finds everything within a day and a green-skinned channel is twice as slick as a clean one. Mind the grates under the walkways and the sheets of water on the terrace walls. Assume every surface is lubricated, because it is.

And tie down your belongings. Anything dropped goes into the circulation, and since the circulation goes uphill as readily as down, your lost pen may be returned to you three days later from a flume above your bunk, by a crew member who finds this funnier than you do. Retrieval is a favor. Lanyards are cheaper than favors.

Drink from the weirs they point you to — the young water, today's, before the green finds it. The gauge glass and pencil mark at each weir aren't decoration; they're the works keeping its diary, and the crew can read freshness off them the way you'd read a date on milk. Never lean on a gauge glass. Never erase, adjust, or joke about adjusting a pencil mark. If you think a reading's wrong, say so and hand someone a pencil. Being trusted with a pencil here is a small promotion; accept it solemnly.

## The ground

Your briefing on this place ends mid-sentence — *the mountain is under construction, and the*—. Treat that as accurate reporting rather than a copying error. The ground is the unfinished part, and it stays unfinished.

Some of it was mixed and cast, some riveted, some glazed, and some of it was *grown* — accreted, farmed, knitted in the wet — and all of it is younger than the peak it buried, much of it younger than you, some of it younger than your breakfast. Fresh pours are flagged. Respect the flags absolutely: a new pour will take a footprint and keep it, and the crew will pencil the date beside it, and some visitors consider that an honor, and some crews consider it a repair. Ask before you find out which you've got. Don't chip souvenirs from the grown sections; that's not stone, that's years. On pour days there will be dust, vibration, and shouting — stay out from under scaffolding, and expect the cage to be shared with aggregate, harp wire, and whatever else the mountain needs next.

Stand on the newest apron once, deliberately, and think about it: rock younger than your boots, above the weather, with nothing native showing anywhere in the turn of your head. It does something to you. Let it.

## The crew

Small crew, everything shared, everything measured — behave as you would as a guest aboard a working boat. Water is free and freely given; never refuse it, never waste it theatrically (they've seen every joke), and never block a weep channel with your pack, your chair, or your self. Offer labor before being asked — wiping gauge glasses, hauling for the pour, taking a turn at the algae. An hour of competent help is the highest currency in the place, ahead of salt, ahead of pencils. Learn the jargon fast: *young water*, *green water*, *the tide*, *pour day*, *six o'clock*. Ask before photographing people and gauges; accept cheerfully that the lens will fog anyway, and that the best picture is the one you'll take at the landing in your first minute, and the second-best is the one you'll take under the Cold Main at six with no camera at all.

## If you stay the night

Stay at least one. Two is better: one fog tide to understand the place, one six o'clock to belong to it. Ask after the dry room — there is always one, the paper room, the archive, the one sealed space where the log slates live — and ask nicely, because it's also their only refuge. Sleep off the ground; the ground may be newer than your bedroll. Earplugs, if the pump thrum and the harp hum and the endless braided dripping aren't a lullaby to you yet. They will be by the second night, and you will miss them by the fourth day after you leave.

If fog holds the cage, you're fogged in, and you're a guest, and this has happened before, and there's a shelf for you. Do not fret at the bell. The works runs on weather's timetable, and so, now, do you.

## Last things

Watch your feet more than the view, and the view more than your feet, and accept the contradiction as the local condition. Re-dry your feet whenever you can; warm constant wet softens skin the way cold never warns you about. Drink the water, take the salt. Wear the dry layer at the windy rail in the one cold hour. Ring the bell once when you leave.

And know this going in: there is no smell of soil. Rain here smells of cold alloy, wet cast stone, and faint green, and somewhere around the third day you may feel a thin, unnameable homesickness that is simply the absence of earth-smell — a thing you never knew you were breathing everywhere else. It passes. What replaces it is better.

*The mountain is under construction, and the* rain is on time, and at six o'clock the crew is under the Cold Main in a clear sky, bareheaded, laughing — and there is room for one more.
