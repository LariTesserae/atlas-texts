# Preparing for the Stack: A Traveler's Briefing

The first thing to understand is that you are going somewhere your eyes do not work — not because of any failure of the place, but by law, custom, and ecology. Everything else follows from that. Pack and train accordingly.

## Train Before You Go

Give yourself weeks, not days.

**Learn to click.** Ordinary humans can learn rudimentary echolocation — tongue-clicks off walls, the difference between an open doorway and a dead end, a ledge and a canal edge. Practice blindfolded in your own home, then in unfamiliar buildings, then outdoors at night. You will not approach the fluency of a Stack child, who learned echo before speech, but you need enough to not be a burden on your guide. Speaking of which:

**Practice being guided.** Take a sighted partner's elbow for whole days. Learn to walk a half-step behind, to trust the pull, to not flinch at curbs. The guide's elbow is the basic unit of hospitality in the Stack, and squirming against it is rude in a way you can't afford.

**Train your memory for melody.** Maps are songs. If you can't carry a tune, you can't carry a map — so start now: memorize long sequences of intervals and returns, hum them back days later. When you arrive, the first thing you'll be taught is the song of your own street. If you learn nothing else, learn that one. Hum it constantly.

**Condition for the ice.** The approach is days across the Glacier-dark — high frozen plains, total silence, cold that pings like struck stone. You need genuine expedition fitness and genuine expedition kit: parka, insulated boots, mitts, goggles, face coverage, a bag rated for deep cold, stove, fuel, and more food than the distance suggests. The ice gives nothing. There is nothing to forage between warm islands.

**Get your click-phonemes and your low register ready.** The local tongue freighted with clicks that double as sonar, and thin cold air carries bass. Learn greetings, numbers, the courtesies, and — non-negotiable — the distress motif. Emergencies in the Stack are acoustic. You call for help with sound or not at all.

## The Approach

You will not navigate there by sky. There is no sun that matters; the Lid hides even that, and the plains outside are featureless dark. Your realistic options, in order of wisdom:

1. **Arrange to be met.** Word travels between warm islands by stone-speech — drum-pulse through the basalt, arriving hours late. If you can get a message to the Stack before you set out, do. A guide walking you in from the breathline converts the hardest part of the journey from lethal to merely arduous.
2. **Travel with a caravan** between warm islands, if your entry point is one of them and caravans run.
3. **Dead reckoning** with inertial tracking and a marked return line. Possible. Lonely. Bring twice the supplies and leave a message trail.

You'll know you're close in stages: a tremor underfoot like the memory of sound; then a roar stitched with crackle, like surf through a wall; then the sting-fog finds you. Slow down at the rim. The outer rings are where water performs its slow tricks — channels frozen into dams, floods caught mid-cascade, terraces of icefalls still finishing falls that began years ago. It's beautiful, treacherous footing, and it's the last cold you'll feel for a while, so don't break an ankle in it.

## Crossing the Breathline

This is the rite of passage. Do it deliberately.

The sting-fog is supercooled — liquid only because nothing has dared it to freeze — and it will freeze on you gladly: lashes, nose-hairs, every exposed thread. Goggles and face coverage for this stretch. A hundred paces later the fog is merely wet, then drizzle, and **the rime falls off you in slabs and you are instantly soaked**. Wear a waterproof layer under your arctic insulation, and have a plan for the shed gear — a cache, a porter, a guide's sled going back out. Inside the breathline, expedition down is a liability; the inner rings are tepid-to-steaming.

Expect to be met. You cannot sneak into a city that hears. Your crunch on the crossings, your breath, your wrong-clicking accent announce you hundreds of meters out. Have your lights already off or already compliant. First impressions in the Stack are acoustic, and you only get one.

At entry, handle the paperwork of the place: declare every lamp, obtain licenses, register for the Salt Tax, accept the clack-stones and the guide's elbow. And then do the single hardest and kindest thing you'll be told to do: **stop trying to look.**

## The Dark Law

This is law, not custom, and the wardens are not forgiving, because the stakes are not abstract. The graze navigate partly by light; an unshielded lamp on the water draws a boiling, a frenzy, a living eclipse — and the frenzy draws the rest of the food web, the way blood does. Clothwings will batter themselves against your brightness. People have presumably died of a tourist's flashlight. Do not be that story.

- **All your light must be ember-red, low-lumen, shuttered, and sparing.** Indoors. Pointed down.
- **Tape every status LED on every device.** A charging diode is a violation. No glowing watch faces — carry a tactile or audio timepiece with a vibrating alarm.
- **No screens that glow.** Take notes with an embossing slate and stylus, or a voice recorder at murmur volume. You will be writing in the dark anyway; learn to emboss.
- **Passive thermal or night-vision gear is a gray area.** It emits nothing the graze can read — but a traveler who can see others in a city built on mutual blindness violates the spirit of the place. Privacy in the Stack rests on the assumption of darkness. Ask the lamp-wardens directly; expect restrictions; accept them. If you want machine assistance, choose an audio-output echo device — a clicker that renders the world as sound through bone conduction. That's culturally congruent. Note that it may require a *sound* license. Everything pings at a price under the Lid.
- **Learn to read the mender dots.** The feral construction swarms carry dim ember status-lights no graze cares about — the only commonplace lights in the city. A dotted path is maintained. A dark stretch is reef: abandoned, film-choked, colonized. Don't sleep there. Don't linger.

If a frenzy starts near you — someone's contraband lamp, or the decennial Kindle — step back from the water's edge, go still, go dark, and let the wardens work.

## Sound Discipline

Under the Lid, the city is a sounding board, and your voice is a shared resource. Speak low. Never shout across a canal. Ask before drumming, singing, whistling a tune in public, playing any instrument — sound is licensed, and busking is bureaucracy.

Learn the tick — the district bells that mark shifts. They're your clock and, once you know the districts, your compass: you can triangulate by bell. Learn the difference between the city's voices: the hearth's fundamental hum in your teeth and soles; the graze-choir, when the swarms phase-lock and whole districts ring like struck bells (the Answerers will go up the towers and sing it back out of tune — it's glorious, it will wake you, it is not an emergency); and the drum-pulse of stone-speech arriving hours late through the floor, which is mail, not earthquake. The young plateau does genuinely quake; locals know the difference; ask until you do.

About the hum: expect headaches, unease, and strange dreams for the first ticks. It's in the bones of your inner ear and there is no earplug for bone conduction. It passes. Later, absurdly, you'll miss it. **But if the hum ever changes, stop what you're doing and watch the locals.** Do what they do.

## Water, Food, and the Intimacy of the Food Web

**Filter every mouthful.** Buy local filters immediately — they're tuned to the brine, the film, the dissolved stone, the ever-present graze — and keep your own as backup. Drink what the rime-harvesters sell. Do not drink what condenses on your gear.

Eat where locals eat. The menu is canal protein — graze preparations, sump-kine, prized bell-eel — and film-farm produce, all of it in a closed loop where nothing is wasted, including, eventually, you. Make peace with that before your first meal. Introduce local food slowly: these are extreme-environment proteins with sulfur chemistry behind them, and ordinary travelers react unpredictably. Carry your own rations for the first ticks and taper.

**Never swim.** The canals are full of graze, of electrosensitive eels that are prized precisely because they're dangerous, and of kine traffic that lows like a foghorn and cannot stop for you. The ledge-walks are narrow and slick with living film. A fall into black water in a black city is the archetypal bad end. Walk with a staff, keep a hand for the wall, and if you do go in — make noise. Splash, shout, sing if you must. In this city, sound is how you're found, and survival outranks any license.

The film is on every warm wet surface, and it will colonize your gear within days and your cuts within hours. Wipe equipment down daily. Treat small wounds as serious: waterproof dressings, antiseptic, no exceptions. The warm humid dark is a fungal paradise — rotate socks, dry your feet religiously (nothing dries passively under the Lid; pay for heated drying in the inner rings), carry antifungal powder, wear quick-dry fabrics. No cotton. Brackish sulfur air corrodes metal and rots leather stitching; bring corrosion-resistant everything and expect patina anyway.

For long stays: vitamin D (there is no sun that matters), and fierce circadian discipline. Keep your own day by the tick, sleep behind your silt-screens on a schedule, enforce it with a vibrating alarm. A lightless city will happily dissolve your sense of day, and ordinary humans break on that faster than they expect — faster than on the cold, in my experience.

## Dress for a World in Twelve Kilometers

The Thaw is zoned in rings, and you may cross all of them on foot in a single day: steaming core, tepid canal ring under perpetual drizzle, chill rime-fur ring, the spiteful sting-fog, the breathline, the Glacier-dark. Layer for arctic-to-tropical-cave and carry both extremes in dry bags. Tall waterproof boots with wet-grip soles for the ledge-walks. Gloves — the film can irritate, and the rime-fur's mineral feathers can cut. A hat against the endless drizzle. A mask for sulfur pockets, guano-scraping zones, and the film farms.

## Law, Money, and the Menders

**The Salt Tax is the one tax nobody evades.** Join them in this virtue. Carry salt or the local tender, pay visibly and cheerfully, and keep your salt obligations to the menders scrupulously. The knee-high swarms will re-point your rented cell or wall it up entirely depending on how you keep faith, and the locals have politely ignored the animal/machine question for centuries — you should too. Never block a mender's path. Never take anything from a mender. Keep your belongings sealed and hung; anything left still on a wet surface becomes substrate, and half the unplanned architecture of the Stack is mender-work. Your unattended pack is, to a mender, an interesting beginning.

Warmth is rationed by ring and inherited like land. You'll be assigned a visitor's allowance; don't waste heat, and understand that hospitality here is literal substance — an offered warm room is a gift of wealth. Reciprocate.

Beyond salt, currency is labor, craft, and **news**. The drum network stitches the Thaw to other warm islands scattered across the frozen night; some stations have been silent for centuries, and three turns ago one spoke again in a dialect nobody recognizes. The city has been composing its answer ever since. If you know anything of the wider night, that knowledge is treasure — offer it to drum-keepers or breath-watchers, not in the market. And decide early how honest you'll be about where you're from. In a city that hears everything, lies are a bad instrument; they have the patience to notice inconsistencies across decades. "Far away" is fine. They'll draw their own conclusions, politely, at their own pace.

## The Etiquette of the Unseen

- **Announce yourself.** A soft clack at doorways, a hum approaching from behind. In a blind city, an unannounced hand is a shock.
- **Choose a click-signature for your name** — one you can reproduce reliably. That's how you'll be known, along with your footstep and your accent. Walk with a steady rhythm so people can read you.
- **When your hosts switch to sign-cant or whisper-hooks, avert your attention** the way you'd avert your eyes elsewhere. Privacy is made of hands and jawbones here. Don't record audio indiscriminately; a recorder is a kind of lamp.
- **Cold Galleries:** go only with a special license and escort, treat it as someone's family home — because it is. The frozen are great-grandparents caught mid-errand in clear ice. Slow, quiet, no jokes. The ember light you're given there is a trust.
- **The Firsthearth:** if you're permitted down the shaft, follow Keeper procedure to the letter, touch nothing, keep it short. The field-works are unparsed and the Kept Warmth has no polite name in physics. Be courteous to the mystery and don't loiter in it.
- **The Growth Front:** horn-choirs by arrangement only, and stand exactly where you're told. Lattice-song is shaped resonance; a column rising a meter a decade looks slow and is not something to stand inside of.

## Hazards of the Calendar

- **An Opening** — once a generation, the Lid parts. Stars mean the blanket is off: the temperature falls out of the sky and frost fur crawls at a walking pace. If it happens, move inward without panic — you can outwalk it — go silent with everyone else, and wait for the Lid to knit. It is endured, not celebrated. Do not remark on the beauty of the stars.
- **Ice-dam bursts** in the outer rings: years of pressure, then a crack, a roar, a kilometer of flood that freezes mid-cascade. If you hear it, get off the channel floors and upslope.
- **The Kindle** — once a decade, one great lamp lit above the core canal, and the whole city turning out to watch the swarm storm rise and devour it. If your visit coincides, attend. Stand where the wardens put you. Do not try to photograph it. You can't. Be there.

## Mindset

The first ticks will be hard: wet, lost, hum-sick, aching for photons, touched in the dark by things you cannot see — graze brushing your boots, a clothwing flitting past your face, menders ticking across your floor at night. Schedule nothing. Let the elbow teach you. Around the time you stop reaching for a light that isn't there, the city begins to render: footsteps as texture, bells as compass, the hum as a hearth you carry in your sternum, the market's thunder pressed lovingly down onto your skin by the Lid.

You will never see the Stack. But it will hear you — your crunch, your clicks, your improving accent — and if you stay long enough it will remember you: in accretion, in a mender's re-pointing, perhaps in a verse added to a street-song. Time does not pass here; it accumulates. Travelers who fight that leave tired. Travelers who accept it leave changed, and usually leave late.

The kindest instruction you will receive is also the whole art of the visit: *stop trying to look.*
