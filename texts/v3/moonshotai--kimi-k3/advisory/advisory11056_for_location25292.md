# Preparing for the Choir: A Briefing for the Ordinary Traveler

Before gear lists and route plans, understand the one fact that governs everything else: **this place is finished.** Everywhere else you have ever been — every city, cave, forest, ruin — is in the middle of changing. The Choir is not. Its erosion completed. Its rust arrested. Its dust settled into fixed lattices and then simply *stood*, because disturbance has never occurred. The chalk has never smudged.

You will be the first disturbance in centuries.

You cannot avoid this. Your breath alone — warm, wet, animal — will restart the rust that stopped when the makers left, imperceptibly, on a few iron fittings. Your body will part the dust galaxy. Your boots will compress felt that has known only the weight of itself. So the preparation is not "leave no trace"; that is impossible, and pretending otherwise is vanity. The preparation is: **leave the smallest trace a living human can leave, and make every atom of it deliberate.**

Decide your rules now, at low altitude, in comfortable air, and write them on a card you carry. Up there you will be hypoxic, cold, and — this is not a small thing — you will feel *chosen*. The room's intimacy does that to people. Neither hypoxia nor election is a good author of decisions. The card decides; the altitude obeys.

One more framing note: the garden itself is not fragile. It repairs at the rate a leaf dies and is replaced; its machines spin on magnetic bearings with nothing to wear out; its wind is pinned by the shape of the mountains, and the mountains are finished changing. You cannot kill the Choir. What's fragile is the *stillness* — the record, the unsmudged chalk, the arrested rust, the unparted dust. You can't hurt the room. You can only end the part where nothing happens. That's what you're protecting.

## Getting There

However you arrive in that world, the last leg is the mountain, and the mountain offers two approaches, both bad.

**The interior ladder** descends — and therefore ascends — through the Stack's dark, past dead landings and dead switchboards. No one climbs it anymore. The record says the last climber went down, once, and that was the end of traffic. The record does not say what happened. Treat that ambiguity as the hazard assessment: bring three light sources, test every rung before weighting it, rope the pitches, and assume every landing lies. Nothing has maintained anything in that shaft for longer than your civilization may have existed.

**The exterior flank** is extreme-altitude mountaineering in thin violet air where stars show at midday. Acclimatize for weeks beforehand, or carry oxygen and a pulse oximeter and the humility to use both. The one mercy — the only mercy the Stack offers — is the wind. It is a lee wave pinned by the peaks, blowing always from the same quarter at the same strength, forever. There are no gusts. There are no storms. There is no weather window to wait for, because there is no weather. You can rig for a constant wind the way you'd rig for a permanent fact: fixed lines, anchors accounting for one direction only, rest stops in its one lee. There is no bad day to climb. There is also no good day. Your body is the only variable on the whole mountain.

**The balcony** is a grate over the drop. Clip in *before* you reach for the hatch, not after. Wear your glacier glasses on the approach — the midday-starlight sky is beautiful and the ultraviolet is savage — and pocket them at the threshold. Through your boot soles on the grate you'll feel it before you hear it: the whole structure humming its note, the room audible in the stone.

**The hatch** is wheel-locked and no wider than a doorway's whisper. The metal bites — gloves. It will likely turn easily; nothing in that air corrodes to completion. There is no pressure differential (the flue balances it), so it won't suck or blow; it will simply open onto loudness. As you grip the wheel you'll feel the drone through the steel — the room shaking your hand, the only greeting it will ever offer. Take your pack off and pass it through. You don't want it inside: every strap sheds, every buckle clanks, and a crumb from a pocket seam would join the dust lattice *forever*.

## Your Body

**Air.** Too lean for birds, for weather, for anything. Supplemental oxygen or genuine acclimatization — and distrust your own euphoria, because hypoxia's first symptom is feeling wonderful and certain. This matters doubly in a room that will also make you feel wonderful and certain for its own reasons.

**Cold.** Pinned a few degrees above freezing, permanently. Dress for standing motionless in a walk-in refrigerator for an hour: wool layers — wool, not crackling synthetics, for reasons of static and shedding — a windproof shell for the balcony, liner gloves under insulated mitts, a warm hat. You will chill faster than you expect, because you will want to be very, very still.

**Sound.** This is the main event and the main hazard. It is genuinely, structurally loud — it arrives through your boot soles, your jaw hinges, your dental fillings (which will buzz oddly; harmless; expect it). Bring **flat-attenuation musician's earplugs**, which lower the volume without scrambling pitch relationships, so you can still hear the reeds answering at the fifth. Foam spares for your partner. Know this about your own perception: after roughly an hour, the ear edits the drone out completely, and the loudest thing left in the room is your own pulse, plus a faint slow crackle from the garden like applause that never arrives. That's not hearing damage — your plugs handle that — it's how perception works. But it means the visit has a natural ending built into it. When the Choir fades, the room has finished with you. Take the ending.

**Time.** There are no time cues. One lighting state, shadows that are fixtures, an electric clock whose sweep means nothing. People either bolt after five minutes or want to stay for hours, and both reactions are the room working on them, not judgment. Set a vibrating alarm before you enter: thirty to sixty minutes. When it buzzes, you go.

**Dust.** Fine, conductive, silicate-and-copper, standing in motionless lattices in the charge gradients. Wear a P100 respirator. Yes, it muffles your voice. Your voice dies at your lips anyway.

**Implants and devices.** If you carry a pacemaker or similar, consult someone who knows your device — the room is transformers, magnetic bearings, and charge gradients. And every electronic you bring goes *off*, not airplane mode, *off*. The garden drinks stray electricity. You do not want your presence sipped by the reeds, and you do not want anything of yours boosted onto a carrier tone that has been clean for centuries.

## What It Is Like Inside

Kill your lamp at the threshold and stand there for fifteen minutes. The room reveals itself in layers as your eyes adapt: amber pilot lamps first, then the dull orange of heater elements behind their grilles, then the one red jewel on the relay panel — unblinking, lit so long it has stopped meaning a fault and started meaning *the room* — and finally, last and strangest, the blue-green frost along the leaf-tips, the garden's own discharge-glow, constant as paint.

Then the floor resolves, and you understand what you're standing in: meters-deep springy teal-black felt, the machines swallowed to swells like knees under a blanket, reeds clustered like the pipes of a small organ where the field is strongest, root-felt hanging from the ceiling, and tendrils braided through the cable trays so exactly like wire that wiring and vine are one taxonomy now.

The smell will stop you. Cold stone, a whisper of ozone from the corona, the dry hot-dust scent of transformers — and underneath, *nothing*. No green. No soil. No decay. The absence has a presence. You will smell yourself, and understand you are the first living smell in centuries.

With the plugs in, the drone lowers to weather, and over it you can find the flue's single clean flute-note, and riding all of it, faint and unmistakable, the chord — unresolving, undissonant, held. Don't chase it. Let it come to you.

Look at the air. In your dim red beam you'll see it: the motionless galaxy of motes, the diagram of the room's electricity drawn in suspended dust — and the void where you have walked. You are the largest unshielded charge in the room; the motes will lean toward you like filings toward a magnet. Your shadow will be the only moving shadow in a room where shadows are fixtures. For the length of your visit you will be the fourth moving thing in the cell, after the clock hand, the fan blades, and the trembling leaf-tips — and the only one that does not return to its beginning. Behave accordingly.

## Conduct: The Small Rules

- **One path.** Choose the line of least growth from hatch to workbench, walk it in, walk it out. The pelt resorbs a dead leaf at exactly the rate its replacement completes. A boot-crush is not a leaf. Assume your footprints keep.
- **Bare skin touches nothing.** Cold metal bites. The piezocrystal lichens on the brass pay back vibration as small static nips. And your oils would persist, mummified, indefinitely. If you must know the mat's warmth — and you will want to; it is the only warmth in the room that isn't machinery, faint as the back of an old set left on — kneel at your path's edge and lay the back of a gloved hand flat. No pressure. Once. Then withdraw.
- **Nothing leaves the room.** The pencil against the bench foot is half-felted into the pelt; it is load-bearing now. The tools resting in their outlines are a barrow, not a shop. Take photographs with no flash — *ever* — or better, take the time to sketch. Slow looking is the only souvenir this place cannot be harmed by.
- **Nothing stays in the room.** No token, no note, no coin on the bench. Anything you set down becomes a fixture, and the room will keep it exactly, forever, and it will be wrong forever.
- **Do not write in the logbook.** Its last entry is: *Hum steady. Reeds answering at the fifth again. Bearing 4 warm. Closing the hatch.* Banal, unbearable, and complete. There is no next entry. You are a visitor, not the next keeper. Keepers stay.
- **Do not touch the chalk.** THE CHOIR, in a hand that lingered. It has never smudged. Let it never.
- **Do not fix anything.** The red jewel is not a request. Do not put a message on the carrier. The relay's readiness is constant, and constant readiness is not waiting — but a message would end it anyway.
- **Water is the one true blasphemy.** None has ever touched this place. The makers' damp breath alone started rust that only stopped when they left; your breath will restart it, imperceptibly, and that is the unavoidable tax of being alive. Everything else is avoidable: sealed bottles only, sipped on the balcony; no open containers inside; manage your body's water before you climb, because there is no acceptable way to dispose of it in the cell.
- **The one acceptable gift is sound.** If the chord moves you to answer, hum the fifth, softly. The felt will drink it; the reeds' chemistry may notice briefly; nothing will remain. Sound is the only offering the room can consume without remainder.

## The Kit

- Harness, two locking tethers, helmet; fixed-line and ascender kit if you take the interior ladder
- Oxygen and mask, or a real acclimatization schedule; pulse oximeter
- Wool base and mid layers, windproof shell, liner gloves under insulated mitts, warm hat — nothing crackly, nothing that sheds
- Musician's flat-attenuation earplugs; foam spares; over-ear defenders for the climb
- P100 respirator
- Headlamp with the dimmest possible red mode; batteries kept warm against your body (the cold kills them)
- Pencil and small notebook — pencil, because it writes in the cold and it is the room's own instrument; mechanical watch with a vibrating alarm
- Sealed water with a bite valve; gel food — nothing that crumbles; a bag to pack everything out
- Glacier glasses and sunscreen for the approach
- Your card of written rules
- A partner who stays on the balcony with the rope, the spare oxygen, and strict instructions about the hatch

## The Hatch Problem

Test the interior side of the mechanism before you ever let it close. Better: it never closes while you are inside — your partner tends it, or it is wedged. No one climbs anymore. The relay cannot carry your voice; the world stopped calling centuries ago. A room that keeps would keep you, and nothing there rots. One sober sentence is enough: arrange your egress before you admire the garden.

## Leaving

Follow the keeper's last entry as your checklist, in his order. Listen: is the hum steady? Are the reeds answering at the fifth? Then close the hatch, and wheel it home. The room keeps; your last act is to help it.

Take everything you brought. On the balcony, look once at the stars burning at midday, unclip in the correct order, and begin down. The descent through the dead dark is, statistically, your dangerous hour — the room was never the risk.

## Afterward

Hydrate. Wash the conductive dust from your skin and clothes before you eat. Your ears may ring faintly despite the plugs; your rules card can go in the notebook as a relic.

And expect the re-entry. You are returning to a world of weather and echo and moving light, everything unfinished, everything wanting something from you. It will seem very loud for a few days. You'll catch yourself listening for the chord in refrigerator hum and transformer buzz, hearing fifths everywhere — that's normal. It is the room keeping you, briefly, the way you could not keep it.

The jewel will not have blinked. The chalk will not have smudged. The reeds will go on answering at the fifth. You were the only thing that happened there in centuries.

Be proud that you were almost nothing.
