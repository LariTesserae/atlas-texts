## Before You Pack: Understand What You're Visiting

The Throat is not a cave with life in it. It is a single organism — a germinating seed-archive — and the chamber is its interior. Everything you see, from the moss under your boots to the moth on your shoulder, is one body with one slow mind. This changes the meaning of every ordinary caving rule. You will not be a visitor in a place. You will be a guest inside someone — a someone who is gentle, patient, and curious, and who has exactly one method for learning about things: absorbing them.

Every preparation below flows from three facts:

- **It listens through sound, light, touch, and chemistry.** You will be perceived constantly and specifically.
- **It reclaims everything of itself, and eventually anything else that stays still.** Including you, given time.
- **It is not malicious.** That is what makes it dangerous. There will be no warning growl, no threshold where it turns hostile — only a gradually deepening welcome.

Prepare for a place you must leave *while it is still being nice to you*.

## Timing and Approach

Go in the coldest, stillest weather you can safely travel in. The crack is nearly invisible; you find it by its breath. On a calm, deep-winter morning the rising damp air condenses into a faint plume, rime grows in odd feathered patterns around the slabs, and the layered murmur — water, rasping, clicking — carries across quiet ground. Wind scatters all of these signs. Move slowly, face into the cold, and watch for the patch of air that behaves like breath.

At the crack, cache a dry bag: a warm over-layer, dry gloves, a thermos. You will exit damp into freezing wind, and the transition from the chamber's wet chill to surface cold is where hypothermia actually happens. Drink and eat **here, on the surface** — nothing passes your lips below.

Anchor a belay line to the surface slabs, not to anything living, and pad any point where rope might bear against the root-column. Ideally, one member of your party stays topside as timekeeper and belay. If you are only two, carry two independent timers and accept the added risk honestly.

## The Descent

The chamber breathes on a cycle of hours: a long inhale that pulls the mist up through the crack, then an exhale that returns it. **Descend during the inhale.** The rising air dries the root-rungs, visibility is best, and the chorus falls into its great organ-note pause — you will climb in a held silence that is easier on the nerves.

Thirty meters is ten stories of ladder, however easy the rungs. Wear gloves — the upper column is rime-feathered and the living root is cold and slick. No crampons, no spikes, no metal that bites: *wound nothing* begins here, on the way in. Move deliberately. The sponge floor below would cushion a fall, but falling means crashing into living tissue, and the rind closes over damage within minutes — possibly around you.

There is a threshold partway down where the rime suddenly stops and you pass into the chamber's own weather. Pause there. Let yourself be noticed — your heat, your sound, your chemistry have already announced you, and a moment of stillness is the polite acknowledgment.

## What to Wear, What to Carry, What to Leave Behind

**Wear:** wool or synthetic layers that insulate wet (never cotton); a waterproof shell; two pairs of gloves; clean, soft-lugged waterproof boots — *dedicated, scrubbed, free of soil and seed from any other world.* A simple mask or buff over nose and mouth: the air is full of drifting spores, pollen, and midges, and while the ark's chemistry was not compiled for human lungs, prudence costs nothing.

**Carry:** a headlamp with a dim red mode (descent only); a mechanical timer; your planned departure time written on tape on your wrist; a waterproof notebook and pencil; a camera if you like (long exposures, **no flash**); a small soft brush for exit decontamination.

**Leave behind:** food, anything scented, anything you'd grieve losing, and — this matters — any jewelry that could scratch. If you want to press your palm to the floor, and you should, do it gloveless, ringless, once, deliberately.

## Inside: How to Behave

**Kill your lamp early.** The chamber lights itself — blue vein-glow, ember motes, the luminous pool — and your eyes will adapt in fifteen minutes. More importantly, the room's light *leans toward sound*, brightening in ripples toward your voice. That response is your instrument panel: it shows you where the room's attention is. If the walls are gathering light around you while you stand silent, attention is concentrating on you. Note it.

**Keep your soundscape boring.** Speak little, low, and plainly. Do not whistle, chant, tap rhythms, or sing — in a chamber where sound is an interface, improvising on the interface is unwise. Sing a sustained note and the room *gathers around it*; that is a real phenomenon, not a metaphor, and you do not know what you are summoning. Choose your words as if speaking in a library where the books read you back.

**Move slowly and keep moving, gently.** The grazers will alter course around you; let them. Never swat, never snatch your hand back, never startle. If a moth lands on you, let it finish tasting. This is the cataloguing, and it is the price of admission — non-negotiable, as the rules say.

A natural liturgy for a good visit: stand still at the bottom until the first keeper inspects you; press your palm to the floor and feel the water rise around your fingers; make one slow circuit on the turf; kneel at the pool and touch its surface **once** — watch the ring crawl outward at half speed and listen for the answering flurry of clicks, the room clearing its throat. Do not touch it again; you are pinging a nervous system, and once is a greeting while five times is an interruption. Approach the Front no closer than five paces. Smell the ozone and cold metal from there. Nothing that crosses the ember-line returns as itself, and the occasional groan means strata are letting go — rock moves there.

Expect the physics to feel faintly wrong: drops hesitating, ripples lagging. It produces a mild vertigo, like missing a step that isn't missing. When it troubles you, look at the walls, not the falling water. Don't toss objects to test it. Don't sit down "just for a moment."

## The Real Hazard: The Escalation

Cold and wet will challenge your body, but the room will challenge your judgment. The incorporation does not begin as threat; it begins as *comfort* — and it coincides treacherously with hypothermia's own lying warmth. Set your tripwires on the surface, because inside, each stage will feel gentle and reasonable:

- **Leave soon** if grazers cross your boots a second time, if the vein-light keeps gathering around you unprompted, if eels queue at the pool's edge to regard you.
- **Leave now** if a grazer touches bare skin, if moth-things *roost* on you rather than land, if you find yourself seated or leaning without deciding to, if you've stopped noticing the cold.
- **You have already stayed too long** if you see the Hand — the plate-sized many-legged gardener that walks the walls. It appears only for the long-staying. Treat the sighting not as a reward but as last call, and do not, whatever the impulse, offer it your own hand.

Never sleep there. Sleep is hours of stillness, and roots grow toward water.

Cap the visit at one full breath of the chamber — roughly two to three hours — and hold to the harder rule underneath: **leave while you still want to stay.**

## Biosecurity, Both Directions

You are a multiverse traveler, which makes you a vector of unique concern — in both directions.

**Protect the room from you.** It is an ark of futures. A fungus spore or foreign seed carried in on your boot tread could be something the rind cannot handle — or worse, something it *can*, and incorporates into a library meant for a world that doesn't exist yet. Clean gear going down is not fussiness; it is the one crime symmetrical to the room's own curiosity.

**Protect every other world from it.** The tissue runs on antifreeze chemistry; cold will not kill hitchhikers. At the crack, before ascending, gently return any roosting keepers to the wall, brush down every seam, cuff, hood, and boot tread, and let the rinse water drain back into the crack — return the room to the room. Back home, boil what can be boiled, dry everything in sun, and quarantine the kit for a season. And never — *never* — make your next stop a cold, wet world. That is precisely the route this seed was built to travel, and you must not become its gardener by accident.

## If You Break a Rule

**If you wound something:** stop moving, don't flee thrashing. Say what happened, aloud, calmly — whether it understands is unknowable, but it listens, and tone carries. Withdraw steadily. Expect keepers to arrive to inspect; let them. Then leave at your next natural moment, and consider that visit your last — the keepers remember, and what memory means in a mind like this, no one can tell you.

**If you bleed:** cover it instantly, leave immediately. Anything of you that reaches the floor is catalogued within the hour. That cannot be undone; don't compound it by lingering.

**If you discover, later, that you've taken something** — a seed-case in a cuff, a lodged fragment in a tread: go back promptly and place it at the crack. The retrieval is patient and unstoppable. Do not test whether the roots can follow between worlds; assume they can, or accept that you will spend the rest of your life wondering.

## Who Should Not Go

Anyone with open wounds, compromised immunity, or spore-sensitive lungs. Anyone who cannot be trusted with a timer. Anyone prone to trance or dissociation — the chorus is engineered by no one and hypnotic anyway. Children. And anyone who feels, reading this, a flicker of *surely one small seed-case wouldn't matter.* The room will find that person quickly.

## One Last Thing

Decide on the surface how you feel about being known. Within minutes of your arrival, the room will taste your salt, read your chemistry, and file you away among its futures. Your pattern may bloom, some hundred centuries from now, on a world that does not yet exist. Some travelers find that thought unbearable. Some find it the closest thing to grace on offer. Either way, the cataloguing is immediate and non-negotiable — consent here is something you give with your feet, by descending.

So: dress for wet cold, bring a friend and a timer, speak softly, touch once, take nothing, leave early, and carry nothing away but the memory of standing inside something's first thought — which, it should be said, is one of the finest things a traveler can carry anywhere.
