# Preparing for the Bench

The bench is the kindest dangerous place you will ever visit, and the danger and the kindness are the same thing. Understand that before you pack a single sock. Everything else in this guide is a footnote to it.

There is one rule above all rules, older than anyone's explanation for it: **no one stays past the seventh morning, and no one says why.** Treat this the way sailors treat a tide table compiled from drowned men. The reason is not recorded. The rule does not care. You are not going to be the traveler who finally tests it, because testing it is indistinguishable from losing to it.

---

## I. What you are actually preparing for

Most journeys reward observation. This one punishes it. Note the trap built into the place: a traveler who stays a week *begins* to suspect the day repeats; a traveler who stays two stops suspecting. Which means that by the time you have verified the nature of the bench with your own eyes, you have already overstayed by a week. **You must arrive believing.** Accept the testimony of everyone who came before you — the custom of the meeting-house, the full jars, the seventh morning — the way you accept a chart of reefs you cannot see. Empiricism is for other mountains.

The terms of the place, as best they are known:

- **The day repeats.** Same mist in the same fold at dawn, same sun through the same notch, same quarter-hour of rain at dusk. Footprints heal. Bent grass rises. Cut turf knits. Moved things return.
- **What you take is returned.** Souvenirs are impossible. Don't attempt them; futility is the best-case interpretation of what happens.
- **What you leave remains.** This is the seam, the one exception, and it governs your whole ethics of the visit. A tin cup rusting on a windowsill. A scarf faded to a ghost. The bench cannot digest what it did not make. Everything you abandon there is permanent — it will outlast you by centuries, lying exactly where your carelessness left it.
- **The sky keeps real time.** The moon waxes; the stars wheel. The sun is local furniture — it will clear the same notch every morning and tells you nothing. The moon is the only clock on the mountain. Bring a printed ephemeris.
- **Your body is a guest.** Small wounds close overnight. Old travelers say softer and stranger things than that, and the valley people below say only that *it keeps what it finishes*. Sit with that phrase until you understand it as both promise and warning.

## II. The approach

**Timing below, timing above.** The bench itself has no seasons — it is always the same fine first morning, lupines out, frogs active. But the valleys and the climb are in your world, with your world's weather. Ask below when the cloud belt is most stable and the thickets least sodden, and go then.

**Acclimatize.** The bench stands above the cloud sea; your breath comes short the moment you break the surface. Spend three to five days at the highest tolerable altitude in the foothills before you attempt the climb. Learn your altitude symptoms honestly — headache, nausea, dizziness, foul mood — and hold yourself to this: if they arrive, you descend. This mountain makes turning back easier than any other, because you cannot miss anything. It will be identical when you return. It keeps.

**The climb** takes the better part of a day, most of it inside the cloud — wet, gray, featureless, with dripping thickets that shrink as you rise. This is the objectively dangerous part of the journey, more dangerous than anything ontological:

- Rope up in the cloud section if you are not alone; visibility can drop to arm's length. Carry wands or flags and mark the route; remove them on the way down.
- Compass, altimeter, paper map — instruments that do not require the cooperation of any world. Assume nothing electronic is guaranteed anywhere you travel.
- The ridges are blade-thin and the scree is loose and loud. Keep off the crests; keep to the slope; use poles.
- **Time your arrival for early afternoon at the latest.** Each evening the cloud sea rises like a tide and tops the rim. To be anywhere near the rim when the tide comes in is to be standing in a whiteout beside a very long fall. If the day runs late, camp below the cloud's ceiling and top out tomorrow. Again: it will wait. It is the one destination that cannot be missed, only entered.

**The valley people** will not speak of the bench much, and what they do say will come as a proverb, not a briefing. Do not press. Do not ask twice. Their reticence is not rudeness; it is the residue of old griefs and long respect, and it is also — understand this — the mountain's protection. Accept a guide if one is offered for the lower slopes, and do not be surprised or offended when no one will go above the cloud line with you.

**Leave word below.** Tell someone your plan and your hard deadline: *down by the seventh day, without exception.* And write this part plainly in your letter: if you do not come down, no one is to come up after you. There is no rescue on the bench. A rescue party is just more people for it to keep.

## III. What to carry

The beautiful economy of the bench is that it provides water (the cisterns never fall below their dark line), food (the sealed jars, refilling), shelter, and fire. You are packing for the climb and for your own decency, not for survival — which means you can, and must, **pack out everything you pack in.** Every gram. Plan your load around the day when you will be tired, at altitude, and possibly heartbroken, because that is the day you carry it all back down.

**Wear:** Real boots with ankle support for scree. Wool layers you can swap in seconds — the sun has some warmth and the shade has none, and you will be stepping between the two all day like crossing a border. A warm hat and gloves for the nightly frost. A waterproof shell for the dusk rain and the climb. Sunglasses and serious sun protection: thin air, pale stone, and an eternal clear day will cook an unprepared traveler by the second afternoon.

**Sleep:** A bag rated well below freezing and an insulated pad. Frost feathers the stones every single night; the meeting-house hearth takes the edge off but does not heat the room. Earplugs only if the sound of dripping cisterns and shifting hooves on roofs will unnerve you — but consider doing without. The quiet is the exhibit.

**Kitchen:** One pot, one bowl, one spoon, a cup you are attached to — literally attached; tie it to your belt. (The rusting tin cup on the windowsill belonged to someone who wasn't.) Salt and whatever seasonings you love, because the grain is sweet but plain. No soap, no toothpaste in the runnels, nothing disposable. A week's unwashedness harms no one; a century's residue of your convenience would be obscene. Carry wag bags and use them. The bench stays clean because nothing imported is allowed to accumulate. **Do not be the accumulation.**

**Light:** A headlamp with a red mode. The runnels are knee-deep, lidded only in stretches, and they run full at night; the mist stands in the folds until dawn; you do not want to navigate by starlight however glorious it is. Red light preserves the sky — and the reservoir holding the stars at midnight is why you came.

**The time kit** — this is your life, treat it as such:

- A mechanical or battery watch with a date function. Imported things keep honest time there; the world's own time is a closed loop. Trust the watch over the feel of the day, every time, because the feel of the day will be lying to you by design.
- A paper journal and pencil. **Write in it every single evening without exception**: the date by your reckoning, the count of mornings, the phase of the moon against your ephemeris. Your memory cannot be trusted here — every dawn is identical, and identical days do not stack in memory; they blur. The journal is your external memory, and imported marks persist. It is the one archive the bench cannot tidy.
- A tally worn on the body: a cord you knot each dawn, notches cut into your own staff. Something you cannot misplace and cannot rationalize away.
- The printed ephemeris. When the moon disagrees with your sense of how long you've been there, believe the moon, and feel the floor tilt, and keep walking.

**Health kit:** Full first aid for the climb — the bench heals small wounds, but the thickets and the scree are in your world and play by your rules. Blister care, analgesics, any personal medication in double supply. Do not rely on the mountain's mercy for anything that matters.

**Do not bring:** Pets, ever — the bench keeps what it finishes, and you do not want to learn what it makes of an imported animal. Drones or anything that can crash and become permanent litter with a battery in it. Weapons — the herds neither grow nor shrink, and you are not going to be the exception to that accounting. Anything you would not want exhibited, rusting honestly, for the next three hundred years.

## IV. The count

This is the discipline the whole visit hangs on.

1. **Decide your stay before you arrive.** Three mornings, five at most — well inside the window, with margin for a lost day. Write the departure date in your journal on the first evening, in ink, in your own hand.
2. **Reconcile every evening at the fire.** Journal, tally cord, watch date, moon phase. All four should agree.
3. **Travel with a companion if you possibly can,** and keep independent tallies. Make the pact before the climb: either of you can call the departure, at any hour, for any reason or none, and the other complies without argument. No debate at altitude. No "one more morning."
4. **If the counts disagree, the higher number governs.** If your records are lost, damaged, or you wake one dawn and cannot make them agree with each other: you leave at the *next* dawn, and you do not stop to pack neatly.
5. **Obey the person who arrived.** Write yourself a letter on the first night — why you came, what you have below, when you leave — and read it each morning. The traveler you are on day one is your commanding officer. The traveler the bench is slowly making of you is not.

Know the warning signs, and know they will not feel like warnings. They will feel like health: You stop bothering with the journal because every entry is the same. The rule about seven mornings begins to seem charming, superstitious, meant for other people. Your blister is gone; your knee hasn't felt this good in years; you sleep like the dead and wake before the frost ticks. The word *finished* occurs to you about yourself, quietly, approvingly. **That is the moment to go.** Not the next morning. That morning.

If a companion refuses to leave — this is the hardest paragraph in this guide — you cannot carry them, and you must not stay to argue past your own window. Go down. Tell the valleys. They will nod, and say nothing, and you will have to live with that. It is still better than the alternative, which is the two of you, kept.

## V. Conduct on the bench

**Sleep in the meeting-house**, as custom dictates — it is many-benched, smells faintly of cold smoke, and the fire in its hearth burns low and blue-hearted and has never been fed. Do not feed it. Dry your socks near it, not in it. Take its warmth as a guest takes bread.

**Never open a closed door.** In every third house, something is sleeping. You will be tempted to determine which houses; resist this; do not count, do not listen at shutters, and if you think you hear breathing, you do, and you move along. The sleepers are none of your business, and praying they remain none of your business is a reasonable posture.

**Eat from the jars** — one at a time, in your own bowl, with gratitude and without experimentation. In the morning the jar stands sealed and full again. Travelers report this as the most frightening thing about the bench until they decide it is the kindest. Make that decision early and deliberately; it costs you nothing and it colors everything after.

**Water:** Drink deeply from the cisterns; dip with your cup, touch nothing, and note the dark wet line on the wall that is never higher and never lower. Do not wash in cisterns, runnels, the reservoir, or the cup-and-bath pools along the rock — each of those has its own moss, its own thumbnail frog, and none of them is yours. The herds drink at the stepped end of the reservoir. Give them the courtesy you'd give anyone at their own table.

**The animals have inherited everything,** and you are visiting their estate. Mottlebacks on the rooftops at dawn are the hoof-scuff you hear overhead — don't be alarmed, and don't feed them. Duck under the lintels; every third doorway has a wren's nest. Step around the foxes on the meeting-house steps as you would step around any doorkeepers. If you see the wide, smoke-colored cat at dusk — you probably won't — count it as luck and do not follow. Do not climb the bell tower; the condor has the shoulder of it, and the bell does its own work: at the evening wind-shift it gives one note, sometimes two. **Take that note as a harbor gun.** When the bell speaks, you should already be walking in from the rim.

**The quarry** is where the place tells on itself — the bite fresh, the chisels bright in their hide roll, the ropes sound, the next course of stones laid out in the mason's own legible order. Go and look; you should; it is the honest heart of the mystery. But do not sit on the sledge. Do not test a chisel's edge. Do not straighten anything, tidy anything, or complete anything. The work stopped one morning and the morning never ended, and it is not your morning to finish.

**Sound:** The altitude edits everything down to water, wings, hoof-scuff, frost ticking. Speak low. Do not shout at the cirque to hear the echo come back from impossibly far away — travelers try it once, and not twice, and no one has ever written down a good reason for the second attempt being a mistake. Accept the accumulated wisdom. Once is data; twice is provocation.

**The name in the lintel.** A dozen visitors across the centuries have scratched their names, the oldest in alphabets no one below can read. It is the one permanent mark custom permits — the guestbook of the bench. Decide before you arrive whether you will add yours, so that you aren't deciding in a mist of feeling on your last evening. If you do: small, in the meeting-house, with a tool you carry home. If you don't: you lose nothing. The place does not need your name. You may find, though, that you need to have said *I was here, and I left.* Both impulses are honorable.

## VI. Body and mind

The altitude, the cold, and the sun are ordinary problems with ordinary solutions — hydrate, layer, descend if sick. The extraordinary problem is this: **the bench heals.** Small wounds close overnight, and old travelers say softer and stranger things, and you will feel, by the third morning, better than you have in years. That is precisely why some people should not go.

Do not visit the bench seeking a cure. A cure that requires you to stay is not a cure; it is an adoption. Do not visit freshly bereaved unless you are very sure of your anchors below. Do not visit at all if you have nothing to return to — because the bench offers eternity as a first morning, jars full, fire lit, every doorway open, and your life below must be able to outbid that offer, or you should not put it to the test. It keeps what it finishes. Go unfinished, and stay that way.

## VII. Going down, and after

Leave at dawn on your appointed morning, as the cloud sinks and the day comes up clear. The descent re-enters the cloud — rope up again, follow your wands, take the thickets slowly. Look back from below the cloud line. You will see nothing: gray wool, and the guess of a rim. That is correct. That is how the place survives.

Expect the aftermath. You will measure mornings against it for the rest of your life. You will dream of runnels filling in the dark and wake grieving in ordinary rain. Old travelers say softer and stranger things, and now you are on your way to being one of them. Two cautions for the years after:

**Be careful what you tell, and whom.** The bench is findable but seldom found, half-believed and half-hidden behind the valleys' silence, and that is its armor. A published map is a kind of violence against a place that cannot change to absorb what comes. Tell it as a story. Leave it deniable.

**And you may go back.** It will be the same — exactly the same, the same notch-dawn, the same frog in the same basin, the same mason's plan legible on the ground — and you will not be the same, and standing in that difference is the whole education the place offers. Travelers who understand the bench best describe it as the one appointment you cannot be late for and must not keep too long. A held breath, scaffolded, half-made, jars full, fire lit, every doorway open.

Visit. Eat the sweet grain. Watch the condor resettle against the evening bell. Count your mornings like a miser.

Then go home, and leave nothing behind you but — if you must — one small name, honestly carved, in a world where that is the only honest decay there is.
