# Preparing for the Youngs: A Traveler's Briefing

Most places you will ever visit are finished. Their mountains have stopped arguing, their rivers have signed their treaties, their stone has forgotten what it heard. The Youngs are not like that. You are visiting a rough draft — a world still being written, at the pace of geology, by an author in no hurry. Nothing there wants to eat you. The hazards are patience-shaped: damp, dark, slow, and quietly absolute. Prepare accordingly.

## The First Thing to Pack Is Time

Haste is the local heresy, and the place will enforce it. The tram-cages move at a walking pace. The great beam engine in the lower gorge completes one stroke a minute and has outlasted every machine built to replace it. Distances are given in hours, and the local rule — *don't trust a distance you haven't walked this year* — is meant literally. Whatever itinerary you bring, pad it until it stops being an itinerary. If you are the kind of traveler who counts days, the Youngs will be a long lesson. If you can stop counting, they may be the best place you ever go.

## Kit

- **Wool layers and a waxed, waterproof outer.** You will live in the condensation band, where the whole world sweats — walls, cables, windowsills, you.
- **Broken-in boots with aggressive tread, plus gaiters.** The scree is young, sharp-edged, and eager. Nothing underfoot has settled.
- **Gloves.** The iron is cold and the rails hum; you'll feel the Note through your palms otherwise.
- **Three independent light sources, minimum.** The darkness between lamp-strings belongs to the herds; you are a guest in it. The rule *never kill a lamp-line at night* applies to your own lamps too.
- **Pencils and rag-paper notebooks.** Ink smears in the damp. This is a pencil culture — everything worth knowing is penciled onto something. Join in.
- **Dry sacks for sleep clothes.** Interiors sweat; young walls weep as they cure.
- **Earplugs** for the crushers, though nothing blocks the Note itself — it comes up through the floor, the bedframe, your teeth.
- **Multiple water bottles.** Despite the mist, there is no casual surface water. You will buy and refill at cistern-houses.
- **Trade goods:** good paper, graphite, lamp consumables, salt, quality fasteners, fine tools, preserved foods. A ledger culture also runs on recorded obligations — if you are extended credit, honor it exactly.

**Do not bring anything alive.** No seeds, no plants, no animals, no untreated timber, no soil crusted in your boot treads. The entire civilization is a Catalogue of what lives here and when it arrived. Introducing an unscheduled organism is the one truly unforgivable thing a traveler could do. Clean your gear before you come; declare stowaways.

## First Days: The Note, the Light, the Warmth

Expect poor sleep and a vague, sourceless unease for the first few nights. That's the Note — a standing hum you feel more than hear — and it does this to newcomers. It isn't yours, and it fades. The light needs adjusting to as well: a permanent pewter twilight, an hour of dawn, an hour of dusk, almost no shadows. Flat light hides edges; move slower than your eyes suggest, and trust your feet. Keep your own clock and learn the long-interval bells, because the day will not structure itself for you.

Seek lodging near a dynamo hall — a row of workers' cottages around warmth and light, with the Catalogue of Arrivals kept inside where it's warm. Introduce yourself there early. In cold weather, lean against a young building with your eyes closed: walls stay faintly warm for their first twenty years, and this is an accepted local pleasure, not an oddity.

## Getting Around

The Spread is not one place but a constellation — an hour of empty moor can separate one lamp-string from the next. You travel by the String: passenger-cages sagging across gorges at a walker's pace, which is wonderful if you surrender to it and maddening if you don't. If heights trouble you, walk the moor paths instead — with your three lights, calmly. Herds will share the darkness with you; they are unbothered, so be likewise. If every light you carry fails: **stop, sit, and wait for the long dawn.** Do not grope forward. The edges are raw and the gorges do not forgive.

On damp nights you'll see wire-glow — blue corona halos on every line, drips hissing off the cables. Your hair may lift; your fillings may tingle. It's treated as a nuisance, not a danger, but don't touch the lines, and keep your instruments dry.

## Water, Food, and the First Soil

**Water is property.** The fog-veils on the ridges, the roof gutters, the drips off every cable, the beads on a windowsill — all of it belongs to someone under the fog-rights. Drink what you're sold or given. Never help yourself to a veil's catch; that is the oldest theft here.

Tarns: look, don't touch. They sit in undecided basins and can vanish overnight — or receive water suddenly — and besides, people are watching them with a patience you'd do well to respect. No swimming, no skipping stones, no camping at the waterline.

Buy your food; do not forage. Every green thing is pasture, calendar, or treasury. Soil is built by hand in trays, pampered and inherited, and the hand-sized moths drifted under the arc lamps each night are tomorrow's soil — if you camp, expect to be gently asked for your morning moth-sweepings, and give them gladly. It's a compliment. And never step in a bed, a tray, a moss-garden, or across clock-lichen: a single bootprint erases a year.

## The Stone Is Listening

Green stone — fresh-cut, still curing — records sound and plays it back across years. This has two practical consequences. First, **volume**: a shout near a ripe slope can bring young scree down, so keep your voice low near fresh faces and gorges, no whistling for echoes. Second, **content**: assume anything you say near green stone will be audible to strangers for a decade. Workers there have learned to speak so they'd be proud of the playback. Do the same — and if you like, leave something deliberate: a kind sentence, a song from home, offered to a fresh-cut wall with permission. It will outlast your visit by years.

If a foundation suddenly repeats a dropped tool's clatter, or a quarry face murmurs last year's thunder on a still day — that's not haunting. That's the material. Deep listeners can read a face like a record; if one offers to show you, accept, and be quiet while they work.

Mind the thresholds. Every doorway is a diary of thin wedge-stones, penciled with dates. Don't kick them loose. If you lodge anywhere long, you may be handed a wedge and a pencil and asked to help re-level a sill. That is an honor. Accept it.

## The Neighbors

The **herds** are the true center of the city and they know it. Broad, shagged, pale-eyed, muzzles faintly luminous from grazing the clock-lichen — at night they drift through the dark with glowing faces, lowing in long chords. Give way, don't startle them, and enjoy it. **Wirebirds** pour off the pylons at dawn like smoke — startling once, harmless always.

**Arrivals:** you may encounter something that matches nothing you know — soft-shelled, pale, improbable. The rule is absolute: **record it, report it to the Catalogue, harm nothing.** Even underfoot. Even if it looks like vermin. The beetle-thing was harmless; the fish hasn't come yet; the judgment is the Catalogue's, not yours. And expect, as a multiverse traveler, to be asked for your own account — you are, after all, an arrival yourself. Give it graciously. Bring a fact or two about your home world's natural history. They'll keep it in the warm, and so will you.

**The inspector.** On winter nights it follows the herds through the streets: low, long, pony-sized, silent, leaving tracks like two hands pressed together in the frost. **Step off the path. Do not meet its eyes.** Don't photograph it, don't follow it, don't ask questions while it passes. No one will explain this rule, and you must not be the traveler who tests it. Afterward: warm drink, dynamo hall, mention what you saw. They'll nod.

## The Unsettled Line

Above everything, marked with cairns and iron poles, resurveyed each year at the Reckoning, creeping upslope as the range grows: the contour above which the world is not finished. Up there, distances drift, the sky darkens at the wrong hours, tools come back aged decades or impossibly un-aged, and sleepers wake with their memories misfiled — or carrying small memories of things that haven't happened yet.

The traveler's rule is simple: **do not go above the Line.** If some once-in-a-lifetime reason takes you up with a High Seam crew: go in the morning, come down well before dark, never sleep there, carry nothing you can't afford to lose to strange aging, and read your own journal each evening. If your entries feel like someone else's memories written in your hand, descend and stay down. Travelers describe the feeling up there as *being unremembered by the world*. If you feel it beginning, believe it, and leave.

## Weather and Terrain Emergencies

Rain comes a few times a season, violent and brief, and sends young streams roaring down courses they haven't finished carving. If rain starts: **get out of gorges and dry watercourses immediately** — there are no old floodplains to warn you. The drainage of the whole range is undecided; streams change their minds; before any gorge route, ask the watershed clerks where the water currently is. In mist, which is often, navigate by the lamp-strings and the String itself, and if the lights vanish, stop and shelter rather than wander onto raw edges.

## Customs and the Calendar

Slowness is competence; impatience marks you as a fool within an hour. Sign the ledgers you're asked to sign. Log your wedges. Don't mock the beam engine — sit through one full minute-long stroke instead; people who do tend to leave quieter than they arrived. If you can time your visit for the **Reckoning**, do: the Line is resurveyed, the Ledger of Distances reissued, and the whole society of clerks, tenders, and guild-folk is out and visible. Work is the language of welcome — sweep moths at dawn, haul veil-catch, re-level a sill — and a traveler who works will be fed, warmed, and remembered kindly.

## Worth Doing

Ride the String end to end in daylight. Visit the Slow Hall in the lower Spread and put your hand on walls that took sixty years to bear their roof. Ask, humbly, to see the Catalogue. Walk out at dusk to a height and watch the Spread become a sparse constellation in the mist — lamp-strings trembling with wire-glow, the herds moving through the dark intervals with their lit faces, chord after slow chord rising out of the gorges. Lean on a young wall in the cold. Listen to a deep listener read a quarry face. Stand still long enough to hear the layers: crushers, cable-song, water underground, bells, the herds, and under everything the Note — a semitone lower than your grandfather would have heard it.

## Leaving

Brush your boots, empty your pockets of crumbs and seeds — what goes *out* matters as much as what came in, in both directions. Say your goodbyes near old, cured stone, unless you want them replayed to strangers for a decade. And be warned: the silence of home will feel wrong for a while, a semitone-shaped absence where the world used to hum. That's the souvenir. You came to a place that is not finished, and for a little while you lived at the pace of its revision. If you did it right, it will have found you acceptable — and you will spend the rest of your travels quietly measuring every other mountain against the Youngs, and finding them all a little too settled.
