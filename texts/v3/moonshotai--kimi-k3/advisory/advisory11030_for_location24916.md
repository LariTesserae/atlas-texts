# A Field Guide to the Mould

## First, understand what you are preparing *for*

Every expedition you've ever packed for assumed six things: ground, sky, sun, sound that carries, seasons, and stone. The Mould has none of them. You are not preparing for a harder version of somewhere you know. You are preparing for the absence of the floor of the world, the ceiling of the world, and the clock of the world — all at once.

Also understand: it is not a mountain. It is a reef. You do not summit a reef. There is no summit to have, no view to earn — from inside, there is no whole to see. If you need an achievement, go elsewhere. The Mould is finished, and it will not give you a victory. It will give you something stranger, if you let it.

## Who should go

Screen yourself honestly. The people who do well are cavers, polar winterers, ocean rowers, long-silent meditators — people whose interior weather is stable and slow. The people who do badly are the fast, the loud, the goal-hungry, and anyone who needs a horizon. In the Interior you will see perhaps forty meters, and the far will not be smaller, only paler. Some travelers experience this as peace. Some experience it as being buried in green. You should find out which one you are *before* you are three weeks from the rim.

Go with three or four total. Not fewer (there is no rescue, anywhere, by anyone — there are no lowland peoples, no rangers, no satellites that can see through two kilometers of leaf), and not many more (you are loud and strange-smelling, and the whole province will know you).

## The approach: three journeys stacked

Getting in is the hard part. The Interior is gentle. The doors are not.

**The wall.** From the lowlands, the rim is a weeping green cliff face draped in soft growth, wrapped in its own manufactured cloud. Count it in days, not meters. Every hold is alive, wet, and questionable. Ice tools are useless; bring wide slings to throw around buttresses of root, and treat every anchor as borrowed from a citizen. You will climb in mist, in condensate, in your own sweat, with no views and no sense of progress. This is normal.

**The Roof.** At the top you emerge into glare, wind, and storm — a heaving sea of leaf-tips under white burning haze. The air up there is thin. You will need storm shell, eye protection, and altitude medication for the crossing, and you will need to move: the Roof is where the weather lives, and you are a visitor in its house. Do not camp on the Roof. Cross to your descent point and get below.

**The descent.** Then comes the strangest mountaineering of your life: you climb *down into the dark*, a kilometer — sometimes two — of stacked and interwoven growth, descending through the tax brackets of light, each layer dimmer, greener, warmer, wetter. Two to four days of rope work. Hang your camps in the scaffolding. Watch the world's brightness be spent around you, layer by layer, until you reach the pearly gloaming at the bottom and unrope onto a floor that gives like flesh.

Cache your storm kit at the canopy crown, sealed and hung high. When you return for it, the forest will have had opinions about it. Accept this.

Before you go in, scout for the alternative: an animal run that crosses the rim. Where walkers have passed for a hundred centuries, the forest has fossilized their traffic into causeways and bridges. A run turns a nightmare climb into a weeks-long walk. It is worth a season of looking.

## Air, and learning to breathe it

The Interior air is warm, saturated past satiety, sweet, and faintly narcotic. Breath comes short until your blood learns it — budget three to seven days of deliberate slowness in the upper canopy twilight before you descend fully.

Wear a real filter mask at first. The spore load is beyond anything your lungs have negotiated. Wean off over a week or two, watching yourself for cough, fever, headache, and strange moods. The sweetness of the air is not benign; it is simply not hostile. There is a difference.

Carry one instrument above all others: an oxygen/CO₂ meter. Decomposition sinks. Hollows beneath the felt, deep vault floors, and still basins can pool heavy, bad air, and the narcotic sweetness of the good air will mask the approach of the bad. Meter every hollow before you enter it. This is the single most likely way to die stupidly in a place that does not want you dead.

And note: warm, wet, oxygen-rich air and accumulated old fiber mean you do not light fires. Ever. Bring a stove. Flame belongs to the Roof.

## Light, and learning to see without it

There is no noon. There is a pearly less-dark and a total dark. Your eyes will need two to three weeks to fully dark-adapt, and they will adapt — the gloaming becomes readable, then rich, then ordinary. Trust the process.

Bring lamps, but practice light discipline. In a world where light is rationed, a lantern is an event, and a flash is a brief private Brightening that everything in the grove will notice. Use the dimmest usable setting. Read by it, cook by it, and then turn it off and let the green dark be what it is.

Bring two clocks. The local cycle of less-dark and dark will not match your body's, and nothing else will keep your medicine schedule and your rendezvous schedule honest. Sleep with the dark. Wake with the less-dark. Let the grove's drip-tempo set your walking pace — you cannot beat it anyway, and the travelers who try are the ones who step through soft places.

## The floor, and walking on a living thing

There is no ground. There is a felt, meters thick, warm as blood, metabolizing beneath your boots, tented here and there over voids with water moving patiently below them. Walk it like a crevassed glacier: probe with your staff before you commit, rope up in sagging country, and consider wide felt-pads strapped underfoot to spread your weight. Learn to listen before you step — hollow sounds, water close below, the wrong kind of give.

Five to eight kilometers a day is strong travel. Budget accordingly, and understand that the felt eats your effort and your sound alike. Your footfalls announce nothing. Nothing's footfalls announce anything to you, either.

Your staff is your most-used tool: probe, third leg, tester of vines that spiral too regularly, scepter of slowness. Choose it well, because — remember — nothing mineral is exposed up there. You cannot replace a single broken hard thing. Every nail, needle, buckle, and blade you will ever have, you carry in.

## Water

You will never carry more than a liter. Water is everywhere; it accumulates rather than falls, and every leaf is handing it downward, bead by bead.

The tea-dark seep water is your staple — filter it (your prefilter will clog fast in the tannins; bring spares) and treat it chemically as backup. The milk-pale water near weeping root-wounds is sap-cut; avoid it. The black spring water rising from the Underroot is ancient and mineral and something else — the animals gather at these memory-wells, drink slowly, and stay long, and no one can tell you why. Field practice: test it, and if you drink, drink the way the locals do. Slowly, and for a while. Approach the wells the way you would approach a shrine in a religion you don't follow — quietly, after watching what everyone else does. Some wells are the only places you will meet the large citizens at close range. Wait your turn.

## Food, and the patience of rot

Assume nothing is edible. The abundance around you is abundance for the citizens, not for you — alien biochemistry is a locked pantry. Carry roughly a kilogram a day of dense food, which is the arithmetic that actually limits your trip: a first visit is forty days, door to door, no more. Staged caches (sealed barrels, hung high, checked each clock-month) are how longer visits get built, one expedition at a time. If you eventually attempt local calories — the pale soft filterers of the seeps are the obvious candidate — run a cautious micro-dose protocol on yourself over days, and write it down.

Now, the real adversary: the world's first law is decomposition, and it cannot tell that you and your gear are not dead. Leather rots. Thread rots. Cotton rots. Metal corrodes in the warm wet breath of the place; electronics drown in condensate; zippers clog with spores. Bring synthetics, titanium, ceramics, roll-tops, lashings. Bring waxed thread and an awl, because you will re-stitch your entire kit at least once. Hang *everything* at night, including your pack and especially your boots — the felt takes back what touches it, within a season or, for small tempting items, faster. A wound here is not an injury; it is an invitation. Close cuts immediately, keep them clean, watch them like a hawk. Your medical kit should be, in this order: antifungals (topical and systemic), wound closure, antibiotics, antihistamines, a dental kit, electrolytes. The air is blood-warm and still, and sweat does not evaporate — heat illness stalks anyone who hurries. The slow air enforces the pace. Let it.

## Campcraft

It never rains, so you do not need a roof — you need a *drip-tarp*, rigged taut and steep, because a slack tarp drums all night under the drip and you will need your hearing. Sleep in a hammock inside fine mesh, slung between the pillars of a vault if you are lucky: the colonnades are the natural inns of the Interior — still, moss-floored, sheltered. Check three things before you move into one: the air at the floor (meter it), the residents (fauna use the vaults the way water uses a ditch), and the floor itself.

Because here is the rule with no exceptions: in some vaults you will find the stone eggs — small concretions, the only true stones the surface makes — arranged in rings, lines, spirals around a spray of bones. Something does this. Something patient and quiet that has never once been seen doing it. You will not touch the arrangements. You will not pocket a single egg, though your hands will itch. A traveler who steals from the spiral has stolen from a mind, and has also announced, to whatever notices such things, exactly what kind of creature just arrived.

Do not camp on the breathing mound-spires of the great decomposers (they are ventilated, inhabited, and working). Do not camp *in* a gap of light (everything is racing toward it; camp beside it and visit). Do not camp on any slope that respires.

## Movement, navigation, staying found

You cannot see far, GPS sees nothing, and sound travels meters. Accept this and navigate by **sequence, not geometry**. You will not make a map. You will make a litany: *third black spring; vault with the apse; the humming mound; left where two drip-tempos meet.* Write it down on synthetic paper, in pencil — ink blooms in the wet — and read it back aloud at every node.

The runs are the roads. Follow them. Generations of walkers have made the route-finding decisions for you, and the forest has thickened their choices into causeways and bridges. Where a run forks, the deeper-trodden fork is the older decision. Off the runs, you are a rumor blundering through someone's house.

Party discipline, non-negotiable: rope pairs in sag country; nobody solo beyond voice range (which is short); whistle codes for stop, come, and *up*; and a separation protocol — return to the last named node, wait one full dark-cycle, leave markers behind you at every node you pass, knowing the forest will digest them in a season and forgiving it in advance.

## The neighbors

Almost nothing is loud, and almost everything is harmless, and the exceptions are patient.

The ox-sized moss-backed browsers are walking meadows and moving terrain; step aside and let weather pass. The gliders steer by smell — everything in the grove knows you are coming before you arrive; you are the loudest-*smelling* thing in the province, and there is no fix for this, so be slow enough that your scent is the only alarming thing about you. Do not wade bare into the warm ponds of eyeless many-legged things. Fish the seeps with your eyes, not your hands, until you know better.

The dangerous ones are the ones that read as scenery: ambush things on the flora-fauna borderline, snare-vines with animal accomplices. Learn the tells — a spiral too regular, a bloom out of rhythm with the grove's drip-tempo, felt at the wrong warmth. Test with the staff before the foot. And if the small animals of a grove seem to be *herding* you toward something: disengage. Politely, gradually, now.

Then there are the Sown, whom you will not see. You will see a corridor of crushed growth a kilometer wide, healing at the edges — fine to cross; it is history. A fresh corridor is not history: leave the watershed. A lake sitting below its old rings means someone has been drinking the drainage. And if a moss-backed, tree-bearing slope breathes beneath your feet — if the *terrain* respires — back away on your own line, reroute, and do not announce yourself. The largest citizens have become indistinguishable from the country, and their passage is measured in watersheds, not footfalls.

## When something happens

Events are rare and enormous, and you should know the two protocols.

**Crown-fall.** You will feel it first: a deep pressure, the whole forest drawing one breath, minutes before a long dampened boom arrives *through the ground*. If you feel the breath — move off the line of emergents, out from under the canopy giants, and count the minutes. Afterward, a gap of slow gold-green light stands open, and it will stand for decades. Mark its bearing. It is a landmark, a solar farm (the only place your panels will ever wake), a pilgrimage site for your own light-starved eyes — and a congregation point for everything racing to fill it, at a pace measured in centuries. Visit it. Don't live in it.

**The Brightening.** Every few hundred years the haze thins and the light swells to silver-green, and everything flowers at once, and the pollinator nations wake, and the Mould becomes actually loud with wings. It will almost certainly not happen during your forty days. If it does: you are blessed, and you are in danger. Get into a vault, seal your skin, put your filters back on, and watch the world's single festival from shelter. Do not travel in it. It is not for you; it is for them; and it is the most beautiful thing you will never adequately describe.

## The mind

This is the section that decides whether you finish the trip.

The dim presses. Around week two, some travelers develop a hunger for light that is close to thirst — ration lamp-lit reading as morale, and if a gap exists within reach, pilgrimage to it. The silence presses too: the felt eats distance, the loudest standing thing in the world hums below hearing, and your own tinnitus becomes a companion. Then there is the drip — each grove's own polyrhythm, older than your species. You will either synchronize with it or be slowly unspooled by it. Probably both, in alternation, and that is normal.

The scale does its own work: rivers that run a lifetime without a sea, species turning over across a thousand walking-days, a calendar kept in centuries. You are brief. Some travelers find this holy. Some erode. Watch each other — buddy-check judgment itself, because the sweet air blurs it — and set abort criteria *before* you go in, in writing, because nobody makes good decisions at the memory-wells on day thirty.

And decide in advance how you will carry the feeling of being *known*. The forest metabolizes information about you: your scent rides on the browsers, your camp's scar heals behind you, the eggs are rearranged by something with preferences that you will never meet. There is no evidence either way about what notices you, and you will not resolve it. Practice not-knowing as a hygiene.

One somber note: if one of you dies, the felt takes back its dead within a season. Agree, beforehand and in writing, what your party will do.

## Conduct, and the border

Take nothing. No eggs, no cuttings, no specimens that aren't shed and dead. Leave nothing that cannot rot — carry out every battery, every polymer, every hard foreign gram, because the world has no stone to bury your trash under and the decomposers have no mandate for it. Speak under the drip, and if you must make music, make it smaller than the grove's.

And the quarantine, both directions, is sacred duty: scrub your gear and your body going in — the Mould is a *finished* equilibrium, every conflict composted, and one foreign seed or gut microbe from your world could be the first new event in centuries. Do not be the new event. Coming out, bake, freeze, and sterilize everything porous, and if you develop a cough afterward, tell your doctors where you have been, knowing they will not know the half of it.

## The pack, briefly

Filter mask and spares; O₂/CO₂ meter (the non-negotiable); barometer (it logs the forest's breath — you can time crown-falls with it); compass (test it, bless it if it works); two clocks; 300+ meters of static line, party rope, wide trunk-slings; staff; felt-pads; hammock, mesh, drip-tarp; stove and fuel; headlamps with strict discipline; synthetic paper and pencils; a sound recorder — the drip polyrhythms and the mound-hum are the true souvenirs, the only ones the place offers freely; antifungals, wound kit, antibiotics, dental kit, electrolytes; food at a kilo a day; water treatment; waxed thread and awl; storm shell, goggles, and altitude medication for the Roof alone; spares of every hard thing you own.

Leave behind: firearms, drones, fire, pets, speakers, hurry, and ambition.

## The shape of a first visit

Forty days, door to door. Five up the wall and across the Roof. Ten descending and acclimatizing. Twelve to sixteen in the Interior — one watershed, one run, out and back on your own line, one vault-district, one memory-well, a gap if the forest gifts you one. Then out the way you came, slowly, with your kit rotting on your back and your litany in your pocket.

Do not try to see it. You will never see it whole; there is no whole to see. Stand in it instead. Let a full dark-cycle pass doing nothing. Learn one grove's drip-tempo until you can hum it. You are not exploring the Mould; you are being digested by it very slightly and very politely, and your entire discipline is to remain undigested without ever once fighting back loudly.

Go quiet, go slow, go briefly. The place is done — let it be done around you, and leave it done behind you. Somewhere far off, the forest will draw its breath, and the ground will deliver its soft boom, and the moss will tremble, and the drips will resume.

They were never really interrupted. Make sure the same can be said of you.
