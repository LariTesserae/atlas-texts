# Preparing for the Room That Has No Door

## First, understand what you are proposing

Everything else in this guide follows from one fact: the room has no door, and has never had one. You will not be visiting it the way you visit a cave or a ruin. You will be doing something that has never happened in nine thousand years of sealed, self-contained history. You are not a guest. You are an event — and you should prepare the way weather prepares, not the way a tourist does.

Understand also that you do not fit. The room is five paces tall and three across, but it is not empty space: it is a braid of interlocking quartz spears threaded with crawl-spaces sized for an animal the length of your thumb. Every hollow already holds a body. There is nowhere to put yourself that is unoccupied, no surface you can touch that isn't grazed, lived on, or made of the dead. Entry at human scale is not impossible, but it is never free. The honest preparation begins with accepting that you will arrive with a budget of damage, and that your job is to spend as little of it as possible.

There are three honest modes of visiting: sending your body, sending a probe, and sending only your attention. This guide assumes the body, because you asked. But read the last section before you pack.

## Choose your era — and listen first

You are a multiverse traveler; presumably time is one of the axes available to you. Use it. The room is terminal — it has been closing since the day it opened, and in forty thousand years it will be solid. Go now and you get the fullest version: twenty thousand millers, crawl-spaces still threaded, the note low. Go late and you get a seam the width of a hand and the last generation living out their three centuries in the dark. If you go once, go now. If you are the kind of traveler who can keep appointments across millennia, consider a pilgrimage instead: three visits — now, in ten thousand years, near the end — from the same perch, listening to the world's voice climb its semitone. No one else can hear that arc. You could.

Before any entry, spend time outside. The room leaks exactly one thing: vibration. The note transmits through ninety meters of rock, and the stroke comes up through the crater floor every seven minutes like a heartbeat through a wall. A season of seismic listening from the surface will tell you the chamber's geometry — the standing waves encode the shape of the bottle that makes them. The millers read the tone as a map. With enough patience, so can you, before you ever break the seal. Map the hollows. Choose your perch — ideally a meter below the crown, where it's coolest, where the brimstone rime grows, where a body-sized void might exist that you can occupy by displacing gas rather than crystal.

## Train the traveler

The closest earthly disciplines, in order of importance:

- **Cave diving.** Overhead environment, no visibility, no direct exit, finite consumables, absolute dependence on procedure. If you have not spent long hours in black water with rock on all sides, do not make this room your first.
- **Sensory deprivation and isolation.** Total dark, a low eternal drone, infrasound in your bones — this combination reliably produces the *feeling of a presence* in humans. Know in advance that the awe you will feel is partly physiological, or it will run you.
- **Stillness.** Nothing in the room hurries. Predation there happens in months. A human moving at human speed is a landslide. Practice holding one position for an hour. Practice moving at the speed of the sulfur rain.
- **Dark adaptation.** Thirty to forty minutes of true darkness before entry, minimum, and more is better. The stroke's few seconds of cherry-red will be, briefly, enough to see by — but only if your eyes are ready for it.
- **Exit rehearsal.** Drill your departure until it is a reflex. Panic burns coolant and snaps crystal.

## The crossing

However your multiverse transit works — portal, phase, insertion — the gas discipline is the same.

Do not open a threshold from a warm, wet, oxygenated world into that chamber. Your air is water vapor and oxygen; the room has been dry and reducing for nine thousand years. Sulfur vapor and hot surfaces meeting a sudden oxygen inrush can flash. Even without fire, a breath of terrestrial humidity becomes acid in that chemistry, instantly and permanently. And remember the pressure: the room outranks you by an atmosphere and a half. It will exhale into your threshold the moment you open — sulfur dioxide, chloride fume, molten-sulfur aerosol at a hundred and forty degrees. Let it exhale into a space built to hold it.

So: pre-fill your threshold or airlock with dry inert gas at two and a half atmospheres. Equalize before you open. Open slowly. And once you are through, accept that you are unreachable — ninety meters of rock is absolute silence for any signal you know how to send. No one can come in after you. Nothing enters this room quickly, including help. Your exit must be something you can fall through, not a procedure you perform.

## The suit and the supplies

Borrow from the sea, not from space. The pressure is trivial — two and a half atmospheres is a fifteen-meter dive — but the heat is an autoclave's, and the fume is acid. What you want is a one-atmosphere armored hardsuit, acid-proofed, smooth-skinned with nothing dangling to snag the crystal, and **actively cooled by a phase-change thermal battery** — in practice, ice in a steel core.

Your stay is measured in ice, and ice converts to strokes. Between your metabolism, your electronics, and the heat conducting in through the shell (dense hot gas couples far better than air), expect to absorb something on the order of four to five hundred watts. A kilogram of ice buys you roughly a quarter of an hour. Five kilograms buys you perhaps ten strokes. You will not use them all. Rule of thirds, as in the caves: a third for being there, a third for leaving, a third for the room's surprises.

Breathing: fully closed loop, scrubbed, nothing vented. This is not only for your survival. A single exhaled breath is more water than the room has encountered in its entire existence — there has *never* been water there — and water in that atmosphere becomes acid. You are sixty percent the room's most exotic substance. Behave like a hazmat shipment of it. You will carry the room's first water in with you, frozen, sealed in steel, and you will carry every gram of it home as warm water. It will be the only water the room never touches.

Kit, kept short:

- One-atmosphere hardsuit, actively cooled, corrosion-proofed, smooth profile
- Closed-loop breathing, sized for double your planned stay
- Five kg ice battery, ruled in thirds
- Passive thermal viewer (emits nothing; reads the room's own heat)
- Contact microphone and bone-conduction headset
- A stroke counter — or just count
- A damage ledger, decided in advance, with the number at which you leave

## Seeing, hearing, and the discipline of emission

The room spent its entire light budget on a single thread of ember. A one-watt headlamp, in its first second, delivers more light than the room has produced in nine thousand years. If you switch on a light in there, you become the brightest event in its history, and you should decide whether that is who you want to be before you arrive, not after.

So perceive in the room's own currencies: heat and sound. The thermal viewer emits nothing and shows you the room's actual economy — the seam cooling and blooming, the warm rain falling, your own dangerous footprint of heat on every surface you near. The contact mic and bone conduction bring you the note, the rasping rain, the rifle-shot cracks. Emit nothing yourself. **Do not ping.** Sonar in that chamber is screaming: twenty thousand animals steer by the standing wave, and your pulses would redraw their map in noise. Speak not at all, or only inside the helmet.

And know this before you go: for nine thousand years that chamber has absorbed nothing. Its note stands because nothing soft has ever entered it. You are soft. The moment you arrive, the room's voice will shift — a fraction retuned, a shade less eternal — the bells will answer slightly differently, and the millers will turn along a map that has, for the first time, a shadow in it. You will hear the room notice you. It may be the most extraordinary sound available to a human being anywhere, and it is also a cost. Hear it once. Then make yourself small.

## Moving inside the braid

You will not stand; you will thread. Move at the speed of the rain. Every contact you make with the crystal will announce itself — snapping whiskers flare blue-green, the room lighting up exactly where it breaks. Those sparks are your damage ledger written in light. Keep the entries few.

Do not put weight on the floor. The floor is the dead — half a meter of ancestral shell, the room's archive and its ore body, with the living lying two deep in its hollows. Wedge against the great interlocked spears, which are structure, and keep off the shingle, which is memory. Stay well clear of the throat: it is the room's mouth, and every seven minutes something comes up it. Be gentle near the bells — thin metal cones that ring only when the stroke swells the note — and if you see the flat star in the shingle, give it its space. It is no danger to you; even the room's predator works in months. Nothing in there can hurt you on the timescale of a visit. The hazards are heat, fume, pressure, dark, and your own hurry. Respect geology, not jaws.

The rain will find you. Molten sulfur at a hundred and twenty degrees sticks to the shell like wax; brush it from your joints before you articulate them. And understand that wherever you touch, something was already there — the tarnish films every surface, the millers are three centuries old apiece, and there is no small casualty in this room. You cannot enter without killing. Set the budget before you arrive. When you reach the number, leave.

## The stroke protocol

The stroke is the clock, the weather, and the tide. Your first stroke inside the room is orientation: the pressure-sigh through the shell, the seam below going from rust to cherry, a few seconds of red light on faceted walls — and then the sound of twenty thousand rasps stopping at once, the loudest silence you will ever be inside, before the redoubling wave sweeps the chamber and the bells ring faint at the harmonic. Do nothing during your first stroke but count and listen.

Thereafter, plan in strokes. Move on the pause, when the room has already stopped to listen. Be nowhere near the floor seam when the sigh comes. If you allow yourself one glimpse of added light — deep red, dim, a few seconds — spend it during the bloom, when the room is lit anyway, so your photons hide in its own. A first visit should last three strokes. Perhaps twenty-one minutes. You will want more. Wanting more is what the second visit is for, a thousand years later.

## Exit, accounting, and the one souvenir

Go out through your threshold and seal it behind you; leave no passage you would not have wanted to find. Then bake your suit. Everything on its exterior is room-stuff — sulfur dew, metal film, living tarnish, the first exports in the room's history — and it burns off as brimstone smoke in an airlock sited where that smoke harms nothing. Quarantine until the bake is done; the tarnish almost certainly cannot live in your world, but "almost certainly" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Take no samples. The room's law is *nothing leaves*, and you have already broken *nothing enters*; do not break both. The sanctioned souvenir is sound. The room leaks its voice into the mountain every seven minutes — what the mountain hears without understanding, you may carry away and understand. A recording is something the room has already let go of.

Keep the ledger honestly: crystals snapped, millers crushed, calories of heat dumped into a world that runs on a hair a year. You will need it for the next traveler, and for yourself, when you decide whether there should be one.

## The last preparation

Train for the possibility that, standing at the threshold with everything charged, equalized, and cooled, you choose not to open it. A year with a seismometer on the crater floor is also a visit. You can lie on the raw scoria at four and a half thousand meters, where the stars come close, with your ear to the ground through a season of seven-minute heartbeats, and learn the room's rhythm without costing it a single crystal. The best-prepared traveler is the one free to make that choice.

But if you go — go once, go gently, go to the crown where it's coolest, for three strokes, with your emissions at zero and your senses wide open. Be the warm anomaly that came in carrying the first water and took every drop home. Then the stroke comes, the seam blooms cherry, the rasps rise like rain in the dark, the bells answer at the harmonic — and for twenty-one minutes, one of the bodies in the hollows was you.
