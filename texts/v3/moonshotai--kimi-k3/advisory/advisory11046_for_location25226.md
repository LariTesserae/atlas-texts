# Preparing for the Cistern World

The essential facts are two, and everything else follows from them.

First: this is a deep ocean under permanent ice, lightless, cold enough that the water at its ceiling sits a half-degree from freezing, and deep enough that the bottom layer hasn't touched the roof in four hundred years. You will need to survive it the way you'd survive any abyss — pressure hull, heat, breath, power — except there is no rescue. Whatever you bring is the entire supply chain.

Second: the pace. The fastest thing this world permits is ice, falling as a brinicle over the course of days. Nothing swims. Nothing hunts. Nothing eats. And then you arrive — a warm, lit, loud, quick thing, moving body-lengths per second through a world whose precipitation moves body-lengths per day. You will be the second-fastest object in the ocean, the brightest event in any province you enter, and the loudest sound since the last vault fell a century ago. Almost all of your preparation is about reconciling those two facts: surviving the first, and being worthy of the second.

## The Body

**Pressure.** Assume abyssal depth until proven otherwise. A hard atmospheric suit at minimum; better, a small one-person submersible with a lockout suit for excursions, because distances here are continental and your endurance is not. Full redundancy on everything life-critical. You are beyond any reach of help, and the place will not notice an emergency.

**Cold.** The coldest ordinary water in that world waits at the Roof, and the brine in the deep basins is colder than freezing, held liquid only by salt. Active heating with generous margin, and one hard rule: near the Roof, keep moving. The film of water on your suit's skin wants to freeze. The killer here is never sudden — it is cold-soak over hours, patience turned against you. The world's weapon is the world's temperament.

**Breath.** Closed-circuit rebreather, and not merely for duration. Your exhaled bubbles would be the only rising gas in the ocean — a grotesque signature, noise and light and disturbance climbing to the Roof to accomplish nothing. And consider what else you carry: you are a sealed vessel of eaters — a microbiome of billions of hungry things — in the one world without hunters. Nothing there has any defense against what lives on you. Keep the seal. Vent nothing. Pack out all waste; the Snow buries things on the timescale of decades, and there is no chemistry there obliged to forgive a dump.

**Supplies.** Everything. All food, all water, all power. The ocean is undrinkable — mineral-heavy, metal-bearing, "a heavier water now" from a million years of leached masonry. The finished water in the clean hollows tastes of nothing and is not yours besides.

**Maintenance.** The Snow is the world's only erosion, and it will work your seals, intakes, and joints exactly as it works the seams of the masonry. Filters on every intake, spare gaskets, and a daily cleaning ritual you do not skip. Fine sediment is the medium of that world; plan to live inside a slow blizzard that never stops.

## Time

The world has one clock, and you should learn it before anything else. The Long Groan comes on its cycle of days — a tide in the ice shell itself, arriving as pressure through every cubic meter at once, felt in your hull and your bones rather than heard. Log it. Sync to it. It is oddly companionable — proof, delivered regularly, that there is an Above and that it still moves.

Then recalibrate your expectations about *events*, because you will almost certainly witness none. Spore blooms come decades apart and last a week. Overturnings come every few centuries. A vault collapses somewhere once in a hundred years. The blue shivers — the hills answering a far-off pressure wave in sequence, sound made visible hours late — come years apart in any given place. What you will see is: Snow falling, forests leaning, one Groan after another, and your own light. A recoverable hydrophone array widens your luck — if a vault lets go within earshot, you may have hours to position yourself near the gardens for the answering light — but hope is not a plan here. Hope is the *only* plan. Arrange your soul accordingly, or arrange a longer stay.

And choose a parish, not a planet. Swim a year along one seam and you will not reach its end; the works are continental and you are not. Pick one basin complex, one brine shore, one stair system, and know it thoroughly. Depth over breadth is the only scale at which a human visit means anything.

## Light, Sound, and Speed Discipline

Adopt the discipline of weather: occasional, gentle, never deliberately harmful.

- **Lamps:** necessary, but treat every switch-on as what it is locally — the brightest moment that province has had in years. Dim, shielded, brief. Never shine light to wake the glow in the fronds; that light is theirs, spent from decades of gathering, and it is not yours to perform.
- **Sound:** passive hydrophones are your real eyes. Active sonar is shouting in a world where sound arrives one thing at a time; use it at the lowest duty cycle you can, and never inside the hollows. Keep an upward-looking watch for brinicles — this is the one noise worth making.
- **Propulsion:** no thrusters anywhere near the floor. Their wash does a decade of damage to felt in seconds. Trim and ballast, slow and deliberate, mid-water as your default. Touch bottom only where you mean to.
- **Arrival ritual:** arrive in the open column, mid-water, lights low — never inside a hollow, never at a shore. Then hold still and keep one full Groan before you go anywhere. Let the world's rate set yours. This is not sentiment; it is calibration.

## Navigation

The dark is total, but the world is not featureless — it hands you two compasses. High in the column, the Roof's underside is fluted in long parallel hollows running with the great circulation; the grain of the ice points along the Gyre. Low, the frond-forests all lean a few degrees off vertical, every one, everywhere, pointing downwind like a held breath. Between them, the water itself is filed: each stratum differs by a half-degree, a faintness of minerals, a century. Your CTD profiler and chemistry sonde are your tongue — learn your parish's profile until the numbers taste like places, because to descend is to move backward through the ocean's memory and you will navigate by memory too.

The Gyre itself you will never feel. You will know it by a new taste in the water and a patient sideways push over days. Correct for it. And beware false recognition: the works repeat their dialects — hexagonal basins here, long flumes there — and one basin looks like another in lamplight. Drop recoverable acoustic breadcrumbs. In the hollows, run guidelines and obey full cave-diving discipline, because that is what this is: the ultimate overhead environment, in a vault older than your species.

Silt is your constant enemy and the world's clean conscience. Snow-sediment settles at body-lengths a day; a cloud you raise can blind a room for days. And the clean hollows are clean *because* stillness has kept them so for millennia — your wake introduces their first sediment since before your kind existed. Perfect trim. No kicks. Enter few, and enter like a rumor.

One more practical matter: the stratification will surprise your buoyancy. Trim for one half-degree layer and sink through the next; approach the brine and float on it. Re-trim constantly, move gently, and never trust the water to be the water you trimmed for.

## Hazards

There are few, and none of them hunt you. The deadliness here is thermal, structural, and yours.

- **Brinicles.** The one fast thing. A faint hiss, a sheath of ice growing down through the dark for days, and at the floor a white ring in which everything dies. The rules are simple: keep your upward watch; if you hear the hiss, relocate laterally — you have days of warning, and you are the only thing in the ocean quick enough to simply step aside. Never be under one, never be in the ring, and if a strike happens nearby, witness it from the side: it is the local weather, the world's one permitted spectacle. Do not touch the white.
- **Brine shores.** True underwater lakes, colder than freezing, with shimmering surfaces and frost-rimmed shores. Do not breach the interface with your suit — the film of common water on your shell becomes ice, and your seals and joints will ice-lock at the worst possible place. Observe from above; if you must instrument the brine, send a probe on a line. The mineral shorelines took centuries to grow; touch nothing. And the lush belt at the mixing edge — the thickest life in the world, the farmlands of the dark — is also the most fragile. Approach like a held breath.
- **Veils.** Filament curtains strung across doorways, the only traps in a world without hunters. They will entangle you like net, and freeing yourself destroys decades of growth. See a veil, stop. Choose another door, or no door.
- **Ticking stone.** Stone tick is normal — weak thermal gradients, the world settling. But inside a hollow, if the ticking quickens or changes character, leave. Not from fear: the world permits itself one collapse a century and does not consult you about the schedule, and you are simply in the way of something it needs to do.
- **Copper-green crusts.** The builders' pins, gone to concretions that dissolve at a touch. A puff of metal salts — toxic to you, and an artifact destroyed in the same instant. Gloves on, hands off.

That is all. No teeth, no hunger, nothing in the water that wants you. In most worlds the danger is the place; here the danger is you, in both directions.

## Conduct in a Finished World

You are visiting something that is nearly complete — a cistern the size of a planet, built by legged, handed, patient hands, still doing its job with no one left to do it for. Treat it the way you would treat something very old and sleeping: with care, with quiet, and without expecting your comfort to be the point.

- Take nothing but recordings, and a little open-column Snow — the Snow is news from above, and reading it is polite. The finished water in the clean hollows is not a sample. It is a completed work.
- Touch deliberately, once: a handhold in a stairwell, your palm in the smooth hollow that other hands wore at the height of a hand, underwater, for a very long time. That is the communion the place offers. Your hand will understand before your head does. Otherwise, keep your hands to yourself.
- If you brush a frond by accident and the blue light travels away from you neighbor to neighbor — receive it, note it, move gently on. Do not do it twice.
- The wells in the Roof are sealed shafts rising into darkness, and no one has sounded how far they go. Decide before you arrive, and my counsel is: leave them sealed and unsounded. The world closed those doors, or time did, and either way the seal is the message. If the builders left upward, the answer lies past ice you have no right to open.
- Recover everything — beacons, lines, batteries, waste. The felt will have all things eventually; do not feed it your century.

## What to Go For

Drift dark in the mid-column through a Groan, and feel the entire ocean take one breath at once. Descend through the filed centuries and read them off your instruments as you go. Follow a flume to its basin and stand at a brine shore; watch a single mote of Snow cross the black surface and tremble it, the tremor spreading and fading — and let that be headline news, because it is. Climb a stair built for legs and match your stride to its rise; read chisel ranks under a lintel by dim light, and let the two irreconcilable facts — *hands, stone, simple metal, patience* and *planetary extent* — do their slow work on you. Watch the forests lean downwind in a current too slow to feel: the whole world's held breath.

If luck grants you a shiver — dark hills answering in sequence, light chasing a sound across a continent — you will have seen that world's aurora and its weather report at once. If luck grants you a spore week, the column reverses and the snow falls upward. You cannot schedule either. Become the kind of person who is fine with that, or budget the decades.

## The Mind

Train before you go: cold-water overhead diving, rebreather hours, cave silt discipline, long tolerance of darkness. Study stone — go read tool marks in old quarries — and study waterworks, weirs and settling basins and cisterns, because the plan of that place is legible and you will want it legible *to you*. Learn passive acoustic listening; your ears, augmented, will do most of your seeing.

Then prepare for the two failures of the inner life. The first is grief — or rest; they arrive together and both are correct. The builders left no images, no bones — the felt has had their substance either way — only ergonomics and tallies worn to dimples. You will invent faces for them. Let yourself; a hand that fit where yours fits is real companionship of a kind, and it is the only kind on offer. The loneliness there is specific: not hostility, not emptiness — presence without witness, a completed thing that does not consult you.

The second failure is subtler. Boredom, the fast mind starving — bring work, survey and catalog and listen, give your attention a job at the local pay rate of a finger's width a decade. And its opposite, which is the real danger: the pull. The whole grammar of that world is *hold, slow, keep*. It kept the water. It kept the works. It kept the builders. Slow places exert a gravity on certain travelers — the wish to stop being an event and become a condition, to let the Snow have your tracks and the felt have the rest. Set your departure date before you arrive. Keep your clocks even while you honor the Groan. When the date comes, go — the world will not know the difference between the water, the builders, and you, and that is precisely why you must.

## Leaving

Recover every beacon and line. Clean the Snow from your gear. Ascend slowly through the filed layers and feel the centuries unscroll in reverse on your instruments. Hold at mid-column, lights off, and keep one last Groan.

Then go, and understand that the world will not mark your departure — the dark will receive it and close, exactly as it does for a mote crossing a brine lake. Practice being unmarked before you arrive. It is the local custom, and it is the last thing the place will teach you.
