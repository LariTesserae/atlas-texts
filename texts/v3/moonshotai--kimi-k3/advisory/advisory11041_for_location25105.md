# Visiting the Weir: A Briefing for the Ordinary Traveler

First, the good news: nothing about the Weir is trying to kill your body. The sun is ordinary, the days are the usual length, the air is breathable, gravity behaves (except where it very deliberately doesn't, and those places are marked and guilded). Ordinary humans are born there, live there, die there of old age. You are not preparing for an alien environment. You are preparing for a human environment taken to an intensity you have never experienced.

The honest summary is this: the Weir is easy on the physiology and hard on the nervous system, the ears, and the law. Your preparation breaks down accordingly — roughly one part gear, one part legal literacy, and three parts getting your senses and your sleep ready for a place that has never once been quiet, empty, green, or still.

---

## Before You Leave Home

**Get your hearing tested.** Not as a formality — as a baseline. You will be exposed, for every waking and sleeping hour, to sound that ranges from a crowded market's roar up to the literal crack of rifle fire (that's the mason curing; you'll learn not to flinch). The old of the Weir are mostly deaf, and they are your warning. Know what your ears are worth before you spend them.

**Learn sign-cant before you arrive.** You will not be able to shout over the city; nobody can. The locals speak with their hands as readily as their mouths and read lips without thinking about it. You need, at minimum: numbers, water, food, help, where, how much, yes, no, slow down, and *I don't understand, please sign*. Practice conversing face-to-face without voice. In the Weir, speech happens close, with your face visible and lit — conversations you can't hear are still conversations.

**Audit your luggage for green.** Do this twice. Green does not occur in the Weir's chemistry — not in the bloom, not in the walls, not in the light — and the locals find it exotic, artificial, and faintly obscene: the color of jewels and foreign dyes. A green jacket will mark you as an outsider from across a canyon and will read to Weirfolk roughly the way wearing raw gemstones as clothing would read to you. Check shells, logos, straps, the lining of your pack, the elastic in your underwear. Dress instead in the palette of the place: amber, rust, pewter, bone, mother-of-pearl. You'll blend; they'll relax.

**Leave the plant fiber at home.** No cotton, no linen, no paper, no wooden anything you care about. Nothing in the Weir is a plant, and everything in the Weir is wet — the walls literally sweat by design. Plant material will mildew, swell, and rot in days, and it will mark you as foreign while it does it. Bring synthetics, cured materials, waxed or synthetic writing media. Oil your leather; leather exists there (the gliders are leather-winged) but the humidity is relentless.

**The gear that matters:**
- **Layered hearing protection** — deep-seal plugs plus over-cups, rated as high as you can get, with spares. You'll buy local ear-shells (the hinged cups of cured mason everyone carries at the belt) within your first day, but they take practice.
- **A sleep mask.** The middle depths never go dark — glow-films, lamplight, the burn of the eel-gardens below.
- **Waterproof everything.** Dry bags inside your dry bags. Spare seals. Desiccant for anything optical or mechanical.
- **Grip-soled, draining footwear.** Every surface is wet, worn into scoops by ten thousand years of shoulders and feet, and filmed with young bloom. Trench-foot is a real risk in blood-warm damp — bring antifungal powder and dry your feet completely every night. This is not optional.
- **A sieve-mask or respirator** for bloom-harvest districts and high-spore days.
- **Salts and electrolytes.** You will sweat constantly. There is no cold in the Weir — the sea is warm as blood, the rain is warm, the walls are warm to the touch, and the drinking water is the city's condensed breath, warm and flat and faintly metallic, faintly, strangely, of bread. If you cannot live without a cold drink, adjust that expectation now, or budget for whatever passes for refrigeration as a luxury.
- **A good lamp** for the lower levels, and a headband or clip that lets it light *your face* when you talk — people need to see your lips.

**Biosecurity, both directions.** The bloom is a single, tuned world-chemistry. Bring no seeds, no soil, no potted sentimentality. And when you leave, scrub and quarantine your gear before traveling onward — you will be carrying spores in every seam, and what is farmland in the Weir could be a catastrophe somewhere else.

**Trade goods.** The bazaars never close and foreigners are a known quantity (foreigners' dyes are proverbial, if faintly scandalous). Compact, high-value, non-plant goods travel best: metalwork, spices and salt (imagine an entire cuisine built on eel, mollusk, bloom, and vat-culture — novelty seasoning is precious), strong drink, songs and stories from worlds with weather. Do not bring mirrors as a casual trade item; light is law there, and you want no ambiguity around it.

## The Approach

Go by sea if you can — across the Shallows, over water rarely deeper than a ship's draft. It is the correct introduction and the gentlest one. You'll hear the city hours before you see it: first a pressure in the ears, then a texture in the air, then the unmistakable continuous occupied roar that sailors steer by. Then the line of it on the horizon like a storm-bank, and above it the city's own cloud — the column of breath it exhales and takes back as rain.

Book your first nights in a rim province, near the port, where there is open water and horizon. Do not, whatever your enthusiasm, plunge straight for the interior. There are provinces a thousand kilometers from open water where the packed volume of the city extends unbroken farther than weather, and you are not ready for that on day one.

**First-day protocol:** buy ear-shells and wear them on your belt like everyone else. Drink from a cistern — the water is safe; it's distilled, in effect, from the city's own breath. Stand outside in the Hour, the scheduled afternoon rain, at least once with your face upturned. It will be warm. It will taste of metal and of bread (that last is bloom-spore washed from the air, and it's harmless). The locals set their meals by this rain; you should too — it's the most reliable clock you'll have.

Then sleep early and schedule nothing important for seventy-two hours.

## Your Body in the Weir

**The first three days are the hard part.** Expect a headache that lives behind the eyes, tension in the jaw, possibly low nausea and an unfocused sense of dread. That's the Note — the city's infrasonic organ pedal, raised by the siphon-hearts and pump arrays, felt in the sternum and the teeth more than heard. Everything in the Weir is unconsciously tuned to it, and your body will spend its first days being tuned. Earplugs do nothing for it; it arrives through bone. It passes. What helps: routine, food at the Hour, hydration with salts, and not making any decisions about leaving until day four.

**Sound discipline is the discipline.** Wear protection in the canyons, near the freight tubes, and anywhere a district is growing (a growth front sounds, hour after hour, like a distant battle) or being demolished (which sounds like what it is). Give your ears a rest every single day — a hush-house if you can afford it, or lodgings in the old districts where the mason has cured dense as bone and damps the world down to a bearable roar. Budget silence the way you'd budget any scarce commodity, because that's exactly what it is: hush-houses sell it by the minute, in rooms lined with dead laminae cut at angles to drink sound, and the rich buy hours there the way the rich elsewhere buy waterfront. Buy ten minutes once, early, whatever it costs. The sudden absence of the Note after days inside it is the strangest sensation the city sells. Many visitors weep. Some panic. Both are normal.

**Sleep** comes through plugs, mask, and routine — the city never sleeps and never drops below a roar; work runs in shifts around the clock. Locals sleep like the dead through it, and you will learn to, but not by willpower: by protection, consistency, and exhaustion. Anchor your day to the Hour.

**The spores.** Expect what old travelers call bloom week: sneezing, a bread-smelling quality to the air at dawn and after the rain, maybe a day or two of feeling vaguely hay-fevered while your lungs decide the bloom is not an invader. Mask up in harvest districts and on heavy dawn-steam mornings. If symptoms persist past ten days or you develop a real cough, get up to the rim, into plain daylight and sea air, and reassess.

**Food and water.** Eat where it's busy and eat it cooked. The eel-gardens feed half the city for a reason; the mollusks are fine cooked through (they are filter feeders in warm water — do not eat them raw); vat-cultures and bloom harvests produce the rest of a cuisine that contains no plants whatsoever, so bring supplements for a long stay and expect a protein-heavy, mineral, unapologetically strange diet. Drink cistern water freely. Never drink from a canal, and never, under any circumstances, enter the water in the Drowned — the warm black lower levels are patrolled by pump-guilds and inhabited by eels, filter-mollusks, and pale blind things that have never seen the sky. The water is blood-warm, which is precisely the danger: it's comfortable while it takes things from you.

## The Law (Which Is Physics)

The Weir's serious laws are few and absolute, and you can break two of them *by accident, while standing still*. Learn this section before anything else.

**Never block a weep-path.** The walls sweat by design — the mason drinks the saturated air through one face and weeps it down the other into gutters, the gutters feed the cisterns, and that is everyone's drinking water. Capping a weeper is a crime against everyone's thirst, prosecuted like one. Practical rules: never lean furniture, bedding, or your pack against a sweating seam; never cover a trickle-line or gutter lip, even to keep your floor dry; when you take a room, find its weep-path and confirm it's clear. If you realize you've blocked one, unblock it immediately and apologize to anyone watching.

**Never shadow a registered shaft.** Light is carried, hand to hand, down mirror-towers and shafts; light-rights are property, inheritance, law. A registered shaft has the same status as a river you may not dam — and *your body casts a shadow*. Before you stop on any bridge, balcony, or catwalk, look up and down the canyon: is there a mirror, an aperture, a registered intake in the geometry your shadow falls across? At certain hours the angle makes a standing person's shadow very long. Keep moving on bridges; don't spread a stall, an awning, or a wide parasol without asking whose light lives beneath you; if someone shouts and points at the sun-line, **move first and apologize after**. This is the single easiest way for a visitor to commit a serious crime in total innocence.

**Never grow past the Envelope.** Mostly a builders' law — the silhouette of the city is the oldest protected thing there is — but it applies to you in small: attach nothing to the exterior, tie nothing off on growing edges, and never feed the mason. Spill feedstock on a wall and what grows will be gnawed off by the pruning crews' stock, along with whatever you attached to it.

**Pay the pruning tithe.** The city persists only because it is perpetually destroyed; left alone the mason would seal the canyons within a couple of centuries, so everyone pays, in labor or feedstock, to keep the shape. Short stays are usually folded into lodging fees — ask, and get it in writing. For a long stay, register properly and expect to work a shift: gnawer-handling, vat work, cutting crew support. It is not a scam and it is not optional; it is literally how the city doesn't drown in itself. Treat your tithe shift as the best cultural immersion you'll get. Bring gloves.

**The city eats loose objects.** Between the sweepers (feral maintenance-machines, generations deep, occupying their niches exactly like animals), the crab-like pickers that work every demolition face, and the recycling metabolism that sends all cuttings to the vats, anything unattended is feedstock. Never set down your pack in a work district. Never "save a spot" with belongings. What goes to the vats does not come back.

**The old works are liturgy.** The fall-wells and siphon-hearts run on inherited miracles the guilds can operate but no longer derive, maintained by doing what has always been done in the order it has always been done. Touch nothing that has not been touched before. Do not imitate guild chants or gestures. If you are offered a fall-well descent — loads drift down as if through oil and arrive weightless — follow the handlers' instructions to the letter, and understand that being offered a ride at all is a gesture of trust. Accept it as such.

## Getting Around

**Read the city vertically.** Altitude is light, and light is class: upper levels in plain daylight; middle levels in borrowed, silvered, mirror-routed light rationed by the angle of the hour; lower levels under glow-film trimmed along the ceilings like hedges; the Drowned in black water and eel-garden burn. You will always know how deep you are by the quality of the light. If you are lost: **go up.** Up reaches light, air, the canyon rims, and people who deal with strangers. Alternatively, follow a weep-path downward — the gutters lead to cisterns, and cisterns are attended commons.

**Read it by ear.** Each province has its own register, its own accent in the roar, and the locals navigate by it as much as by the stars they mostly can't see. Within days you'll start hearing the difference between your home province's voice and the next one's. The Note is the tonic underneath everything; demolition fronts and siphon-hearts are landmarks you feel before you see. Learn your neighborhood's sound-signature the way you'd learn a skyline.

**Hire guides** for anything below the middle levels, for the Drowned (pump-guild-sanctioned only), and for any journey beyond two provinces. This is not a walkable city in the way you understand walking — the interior is continental in scale, and travel by freight tube and rail, where passengers are permitted, should be planned like a sea voyage: provisions, an itinerary left with someone at the port, check-ins. The city is full of people; it is also very good at absorbing them.

**People and customs:** conversations happen close and face-to-face; a hand on your shoulder to get your attention is normal, not aggression, because shouting is pointless. Ear-shell etiquette is real — watch how and when locals cup theirs and copy it. The old are mostly deaf and entirely unbothered; don't raise your voice, raise your hands and sign. Meals come at the Hour. The bazaars never close but the people sleep in shifts, so a bazaar at any hour is fully alive — haggling is done in hand-signals as often as speech, and your numbers in sign-cant will save you money daily.

## Hazards, Briefly

The rifle-crack of curing mason means *growth*, not battle — the visitors who flinch into bridge traffic are the ones who get hurt. Demolition fronts drop cuttings and draw pickers and sweepers; watch from the galleries. Gnawers are domesticated stock bred to eat young membrane and are no threat to you. Gliders stoop on sieve-swarms in the canyon updrafts and are hunting spore-dust, not you, but don't wave food at height. Wall-grazers are pigeons with worse names — latch your windows. Keep your hands out of canal water (the eel-bells are charming from a distance). Corner no sweeper; they are hunted like animals and some have learned animal lessons. The crowds are total — nowhere is empty, anywhere, ever — so carry valuables accordingly, and if heights unsteady you, understand that the canyons are crossed by bridges upon bridges upon bridges and there is often no other way across.

And watch yourself for overload: irritability, weeping, insomnia that protection doesn't fix, flinching at your own heartbeat. That's the signal to buy silence, go up to the rim, look at open water, and breathe.

## Why It's Worth It

Stand in the Hour rain once, face up. Watch dawn steam fill the canyons from a high bridge. Watch a mirror-tower hand the sun down the shafts at the angle-change, hand to hand like buckets at a fire. Look up a canyon at midday and see an entire ecology hanging in the air — sieve-swarms straining the city's breath, uncountable leather wings stooping through the rising heat. Descend a fall-well. Take a guide and a lamp to the eel-gardens and watch the lights burn under black water while the bells sound. Stand in a demolition gallery and watch the fresh-cut laminae show like sea cliffs — ten thousand years recorded like rings, geology in fast motion — and understand that the form is ancient and the substance is days old, that the whole city is a standing wave in a river of matter, the same wave for ten thousand years and never the same water twice.

And at night, when the glow-films take over the middle depths and the canyons run with lamplight and the warm rain falls through the lights and the Note goes on in the bones of the city — then buy ten minutes in a hush-house, and feel the Weir leave your body, and understand what the silence costs there, and why.

## Pocket Checklist

- Hearing: tested before; layered protection always; silence budgeted daily
- No green. Anything. Check twice.
- No plant fiber. Waterproof everything. Dry your feet nightly.
- Sign-cant basics + numbers before arrival; face-to-face, face lit
- Salts, mask, sleep mask, lamp, gloves for tithe work
- Rim province first; nothing scheduled for 72 hours
- Never block a weeper. Never shadow a shaft. Never set down your pack. Never touch the old works.
- Lost? Go up, or follow water down to a cistern.
- Eat cooked, eat busy, drink cistern, never canal
- Itinerary left at the port for any interior travel
- Scrub your gear for spores before onward travel

One last thing: expect the after-effects. You will hear the Note in your pillow for weeks. Quiet rooms will feel wrong — too empty, too still, insufficiently occupied. And green, when you return to wherever has it, will look different to you for a while: exotic, artificial, faintly obscene. You'll have learned to see a little like the Weirfolk. That's the real souvenir, and you can't leave it behind.
