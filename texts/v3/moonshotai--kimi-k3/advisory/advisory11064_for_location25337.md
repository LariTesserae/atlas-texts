# Briefing for Travelers: The Drowned Cordillera

Read this twice. Once before you commit to the crossing, and once on the vessel, the night before your first descent.

## What you are actually preparing for

Understand the shape of the thing first, because everything else follows from it. This is a mountain range you visit from the top down, and you will never see all of it — not because it's large (though it is: several hundred kilometers, dozens of peaks) but because most of it is physically closed to an ordinary human body. The bright upper tiers, where nearly everything worth doing happens, lie in the shallow sunlit band. Below that, the rust tier begins at depths that demand technical gas and serious training. Below that — the petrified groves, the brine calderas, the bare black root of the range — you go in a machine or you do not go.

Make peace with this before you arrive. The traveler's mistake here is treating depth as a challenge. It isn't one. It's a curtain. An ordinary human visits the crown of this country and looks at the rest, and that is not a diminished version of the experience. It is the experience. The mountains are arranged so that you stand at the top of them and understand, all at once, how much is underneath you.

## The body problem

Altitude is inverted, but decompression is not. This is the central irony of the place and you will feel it in your joints: here, summiting is mandatory, and it happens at the end of every dive, and it is the one appointment in this entire patient country that keeps a strict schedule. You ascend slowly or you get hurt. Nothing else here will hurry you; only your own tissues insist.

So: arrive with your diving fundamentals genuinely solid, not certificate-solid but habit-solid. Buoyancy control to the level where you can hang motionless for minutes without a hand on anything. This is non-negotiable, because everything you might grab is a blade. Get checked out — ears, equalization, any history of decompression sickness — and confirm your charter carries a recompression chamber. It must. The nearest help beyond the vessel is a transit away, and a cut artery at forty meters in a place with no resident predators, no currents to speak of, and no witnesses is still a cut artery at forty meters.

Gas planning organizes itself naturally by tier, because the tiers organize themselves by light. Learn to think in bands:

- **The gold tier** sits just under the ceiling. Recreational depths. Warm, bright, surgy when the long swell runs.
- **The emerald thicket** is the body of the forest and also recreational to moderately deep — but treat it as an overhead environment regardless of what your computer says. More on that below.
- **The rust tier** is technical territory. If you are not trimix-certified before you cross, you will see the rust tier from an atmospheric suit or a submersible port, and honestly that may be the better way — it's a slow, dim, contemplative band, and it rewards a slow, dim, contemplative vehicle.
- **Everything below the light-line** is machine country. Book the submersible time early. There is never enough of it.

## The terrain problem

Nothing is rounded. Sit with that phrase until it stops sounding poetic and starts sounding like a load-bearing fact. Every ledge ends the way the fracture left it. Obsidian is the sharpest worked material your species has ever used — surgeons use it — and here it is unworked, tower-sized, and everywhere. Columnar joints like organ pipes, ridges thin as intent, shards the size of doors.

Dress as if the entire landscape were a broken bottle: full cut-resistant oversuit even in the warm shallows, gloves you can still clip a bolt snap in, a helmet. Yes, a helmet, in warm tropical water. You will feel foolish until the first time the swell leans the forest and the forest leans you into a wall.

Fin gently or not at all in the thicket. Consider a small scooter for open-water transits between ridges and accept that it's useless inside the emerald. Carry two cutting tools, reachable by either hand, and drill entanglement before you cross: the holdfast cables are taut with the buoyancy of what they anchor, and a frond under tension is a loaded thing. Never hold a frond you have just cut. It is going to the ceiling with or without you, and it will happily take you through the thicket at bladder-speed, which is faster than you want to go in a place made of knives.

After the range groans — you will feel it in your sternum before you hear it, if you're lucky enough to be there for an adjustment years in the making — get off the walls. Move to open water over a gap. Shard-fall is slow but it is patient, and overhangs are not to be trusted for days afterward. Then go check whether your favorite pinnacle still exists. Sometimes it doesn't. That's the place, working.

## Reading the country

Navigation here is not like navigation anywhere else, and it is easier than it looks, because the place is labeled by color. Gold means shallow. Emerald means thicket. Rust means dim and patient and below that the light gives out. You always know your depth band; the forest tells you. Trust your depth gauge more than your compass, and trust a physical line more than either. In the thicket, sightlines run a few meters on a good day and nothing in a spore month, and the density is a physical fact — you do not swim through the emerald so much as negotiate with it. Run a line from open water. Treat the thicket like a cave system that happens to be alive.

Do not bring a map as anything other than a historical document. The range grows a handspan in a good year; saddles become ridges within a lifetime; a new spire can split an old forest from below in the slowest violence there is. Any chart of this province is a rumor with a date on it. The better strategy is to choose one mountain — one saddle, one flank, one section from halo to ghost-grove — and learn it the way you'd learn a text. Descend it over days. Read the tiers going down: the living crown, then the petrified forests marking where the light-line used to be, pale grove below pale grove, each one a former surface of the world. You are not descending a mountain. You are reading its diary backwards, and the diary is written in the thing itself.

The ridges do one more thing for you: clean glass carries daylight down. Amber seams run into the dimness along the flanks, veins of captured noon. Use them as landmarks by day. And at least once — this is mandatory in the moral sense — do a dusk dive on a clean ridge and kill your lights. Give your eyes five minutes. The deep rock never goes fully dark where a good mass of glass connects it to the shoulders above, and sitting in that faint amber, after the open water has gone black, is the closest you will come to understanding the tempo of the place. The mountains let go of the light as slowly as they do everything else.

## Seasons, swell, and timing

Come for weeks, not days. Anything less and you are sampling, not visiting.

The spore months are the great spectacle and the great inconvenience. A green snow falls upward and downward and hangs; the thicket becomes the inside of a shaken globe settling at half speed. Visibility collapses, intakes foul, visors film over. Carry spares of everything that screens or filters, drill your spore-out procedures exactly as you would a silt-out — line, touch contact, patience — and then go anyway, because spore season is when the lens-grazers come through on their years-long circuits to sift the snow, and seeing one is the closest this country comes to showing you an animal.

Rain comes often and warm, and from below it is silent — a dimpling of the sky. After heavy rain, a skin of freshwater lies briefly on the denser sea and the light goes doubled and strange. Two practical notes: that freshwater skin is the only rinse your gear will get — the mineral water here sets on everything it touches, and regulators, zippers, and suit seams will crust within weeks if you neglect them — and the post-rain ceiling is one of the finest light shows in the province. Plan a shallow hang after every storm. Also: the tallest bladders of the gold tier push up through the ceiling into open air after rain and rest there like the backs of sleeping animals. You may lay a hand on one. It is waxy, warm, and indifferent to you.

Mind the long swell. It dimples the surfaces of brine lakes kilometers below, which tells you something about its reach. In the gold tier, when the swell leans through, the whole forest leans and frond rasps against frond, and you will lean with it. Do not brace. There is nothing to brace against that won't open you.

## The brine

The new rock weeps brine, and brine is heavier than the sea, and so the calderas hold true underwater lakes — with shorelines you can stand on, with surfaces, with a slow shiver when the swell passes far overhead. This is, by wide agreement among those who have crossed, the single strangest thing an ordinary human can do in this province: you can lie down on the surface of a lake at the bottom of the sea, on your back, on the halocline, on glass-that-is-not-glass, and look up at the hammered ceiling of the world.

Do it weighted for seawater, not for brine — you will float on the boundary like a drifting bladder, and that is correct. Do not push through it. Below the surface the lakes are nearly lifeless, dense, chemistry-heavy, and hard on any tissue not sealed away; nothing rooted survives down there, and the bladders that sink to the boundary crumple on it, which should tell you what the boundary does to membranes. Treat the lakes as a place you visit the way you visit a cathedral: briefly, quietly, mostly looking.

If your timing is blessed, a caldera will have overtopped and a fall will be running: a rope of heavy water spilling down the flank, bending around ridges, too slow and too coherent to be believed, utterly silent except for a faint granular hiss where the two waters shear. A fall can take a year to finish. Sit next to one for an hour. You will not be able to explain afterward why it took an hour.

## The silence and the emptiness

Prepare for this more seriously than you prepare for any hazard, because it is the thing that actually undoes travelers. A sea this crowded, and no flicker of fish, no clicks, no chorus, nothing darting at the edge of vision. Your whole evolutionary inheritance says that full water is loud water and that movement means life, and this place violates both, hour after hour, day after day. The quiet is nearly total: cooling glass ticking at geological tempo, frond rasp when the swell leans, rain static at the ceiling, and your own breath — which, on open circuit, will be the loudest sound in the province. If you can manage a rebreather, bring one. Not for the depth. For the decency of it, and because bubbles hammering up through the thicket and pooling under glass ledges feel, after a few days, like shouting in a library.

If the world bends — if the forest behind something lenses and warps and the light goes strange in a disc meters wide — stop. Do not fin. Do not reach. A lens-grazer is passing, the thicket is parting around it and closing behind it without a mark, and the correct protocol is stillness. You may not see another for years of local time.

And if you find something dead in the light that does not match the flora — something large, something that wandered up from the deep basins and died where nothing rooted ever expected it — you have found an event, and you are likely the only witness it will have. Photograph it. Touch nothing. The slow stone will take it in time, as it takes everything, and your record will be the whole of its memorial.

Bring a blank journal. Not a camera alone — a journal. The camera will come home full of green, and the green will not explain what happened to you.

## Gear, condensed

- Full cut-resistant exposure suit, gloves, helmet — warmth is secondary, armor is primary
- Two cutting tools, both-hand reachable
- Reels and line, more than you think, and the training to use them
- Redundant everything: gas, mask, lights, computers
- Small scooter for open-water transits between ridges
- Rebreather if qualified; otherwise accept that you are the loudest thing alive
- Trimix certification *before* crossing if the rust tier matters to you; otherwise book submersible and atmospheric-suit time early
- A tether for thicket buddy work, and an agreed protocol: a cut frond released to the ceiling out of season means *a diver below needs help* — the surface watch will learn to read the rising gold
- Spare filters and screens for the spore months
- No souvenirs. None. There is nothing here that is not either alive, an archive, or a knife.

## Conduct

The rules are few and the place enforces them with a straight face. What dies here does not rot; it sets. Silica and pale carbonate bloom over dead tissue, and within decades a fallen thing stands as a stone replica of itself, still anchored. This means the range will archive whatever you leave. Your mooring block, your lost reel, your litter — still standing in a human lifetime, catalogued in the diary, legible to whatever comes after. Carry everything out. Touch nothing petrified; a careless fin kick destroys decades of accretion and falsifies the record. Do not anchor to living gold. Do not chase the grazers. Do not break the surface of a brine lake for the thrill of it.

You are, as far as the entire province is concerned, a rumor with a date on it. Behave accordingly.

## A suggested fortnight

Days one and two: the summit halo and the saddle. Gold tier, shallow, bright — ridge after ridge running off in both directions, each crowned, the color grading gold to emerald to blue to a haze that is not mist but distance. This is where the scale opens and where you accept the strong sense of *more* past the farthest visible crown.

Days three through six: the emerald thicket, with line. Short penetrations at first. Learn its density in your body. If the spores are falling, stay in it anyway, an hour at a time.

Day seven: rust tier — by trimix or by suit — and the uppermost ghost-grove below it. Stand in the petrified forest with your lights low. You are standing in the light-line of an earlier world.

Days eight and nine: submersible. Down the section, grove below grove, to a brine caldera. Lie on the lake. If a fall is running, give it the hour.

Day ten: rest on the vessel. Let your tissues offgas; let your mind catch up. Watch the bladder-backs at the ceiling after the evening rain.

Days eleven through thirteen: one mountain, learned properly, top to light-line. Your mountain.

Last evening: the amber dive, lights off, on a clean ridge, as the seams let go of the day by degrees.

## What it will do to you

Nothing here is finished, and nothing hurries, and the place is contagious. By the second week you will find yourself moving at half your transit-world speed, speaking quietly on the vessel at night, checking the ceiling for rain out of a habit that has no function above water. You will catch yourself thinking in bands and tiers. You will stop expecting movement at the corner of your eye, and when a fish darts past you in some later, ordinary ocean, it will startle you like a shout.

This is the youngest country you will ever stand in, and it is still deciding what it will become, and it will make no accommodation for you whatsoever — and that, in the end, is the courtesy it offers. It does not perform. It simply continues, at geological tempo, saturated and silent and nearly empty and completely full, and for a few weeks you were allowed to keep its depth and keep your place in its light.

Ascend slowly. You must anyway.
