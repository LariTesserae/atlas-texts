# Preparing for the Warm Dark: A Briefing for Visitors

First, understand what kind of place this is, because it is easy to misread. It is not a cave, though it behaves like one. It is not a ruin, though nothing in it was finished. It is a sealed machine-room that has quietly become a garden, and it receives you on two contradictory terms: nothing in it can chase you, bite you, or wish you harm — and it is one of the more physiologically hostile rooms an unmodified human being can choose to stand in.

Almost everything you need to know reduces to four facts: the air is 40°C and saturated; the only way in or out is 350 meters of ladder; light exists in three islands that do not touch; and the ecosystem is so closed, so predator-free and competitor-free, that the soles of your boots constitute an invasion.

Prepare accordingly.

## 1. The heat is the expedition

Everything else — the darkness, the depth, the loneliness — is secondary to this: **at 40°C and saturated humidity, your body cannot cool itself.** Sweat does not evaporate into air that is already full of water. The one cooling channel you have is closed, and from the moment you reach the bottom of the shaft your core temperature becomes a slow clock. A healthy, heat-acclimatized adult resting quietly might have a few hours. You will not be resting quietly — you have to climb out — and the clock runs faster under exertion.

So:

- **Acclimatize for 10–14 days before you go.** Daily sessions of 60–90 minutes of light exercise in heat (a hot gym, sauna sessions after workouts, layered clothing in summer). Plasma volume expands, sweating starts earlier, heart rate drops. The adaptation is real, cheap, and fades within two weeks — time it to peak during your visit.
- **Set a bottom-time limit and obey it absolutely.** First visit: 60 minutes. Experienced, with cooling aids: 90–120. Set two alarms — half-time and turnaround — and when the second one sounds, you leave. The rule is: *leave while you still want to stay.* This hall is a lullaby, and time dilates down there.
- **Pre-cool before descent.** Rest in shade, drink something cold, start the ladder already cool and fully hydrated.
- **Carry cold, because the place has none.** Every water source in the hall is bath-warm. Vacuum flasks of cold water are not comfort items; they are medicine. Reserve at least one flask exclusively for the ascent.
- **Hydration math:** expect to sweat 1–1.5 liters per hour. Carry a liter per person per hour underground, half of it with electrolytes, plus a sealed cache of reserve water at the ladder base.
- **Cooling aids:** a phase-change cooling vest or neck wraps, frozen at the surface. Wear them during bottom time; save at least some capacity for the climb out, which is where the heat will actually try to hurt you.
- **Know the abort signs, in yourself and your partners:** headache, nausea, cramps, dizziness, *chills or gooseflesh in the heat*, confusion, stumbling, or the cessation of sweating. Any one of these in any one person ends the visit immediately. No debate, no "five more minutes."
- **Screen yourself honestly.** No descent hungover, sleep-deprived, or ill. No alcohol for 48 hours prior. Check your medications — antihistamines, diuretics, and beta-blockers all impair thermoregulation. Heart conditions, pregnancy, and heat intolerance are reasons to read about this place rather than visit it.

## 2. The shaft: 350 meters of singular egress

The lift is dead. The sign pointing to it is perfectly legible and perfectly obsolete — do not follow infrastructure signage for egress, ever. Your way out is the ladder you came down, and nothing else.

350 vertical meters is roughly 1,100 ladder rungs. Descending takes 25–40 minutes with rest stops. Ascending is 60–120 minutes of real work in warm air at the end of your visit, when you are tired, dehydrated despite your efforts, and running a core-temperature deficit. **The ascent is the crux of the entire trip.** Plan around it.

- **Train for a month beforehand:** stairs with a pack, grip endurance, and practice resting while clipped in. If you cannot climb 350 meters of ladder with reserve to spare at the top, you are not ready.
- **Fall protection, continuously.** Best practice: have someone competent rig a fixed rope down the shaft and ascend with mechanical ascenders, ladder as backup. Minimum: a twin-lanyard system with an energy absorber, never unclipped, three points of contact. The galvanizing is bright and the rock is kind, but this hardware has gone thirty years without an inspection. Inspect anchors with your eyes as you pass them.
- **Haul your packs on a rope.** Climb light. Water for five hours weighs what it weighs; let the rope carry it.
- **Never go alone. Three is the right number** — one to stay with a casualty, one to climb for help.
- **Establish a surface anchor.** Radios and satellite beacons do not reach through 350 meters of rock. Your lifeline is a trusted party at the shaft head (or, if your arrival in this world permits no such thing, an automated check-in beacon tied to whatever rescue this world affords) holding a written timeline: descent time, expected ascent time, and the overdue protocol. Discipline is your communications plan.
- **Read the weather history, not just the forecast.** The seep wall answers surface rain on a 3–4 day delay. Avoid days 3–6 after heavy storms — heavier weep means an active breach face, and that is the one place in the hall with genuine rockfall energy. Big pressure swings also make the shaft breathe harder; you can feel the draft on the ladder.

## 3. Air, gas, and the microbial question

The hall breathes once a day through its single bent shaft — that is not much ventilation for a room with warm water, geothermal steel, and knee-deep compost.

- **Carry a gas meter:** oxygen (alarm at 19.5%), CO₂, CO, H₂S. Test at ankle height before kneeling or sitting — CO₂ is denser than air and the mirror-flat sumps and floor trenches are exactly where it pools. If you feel breathless without exertion, stand up and move toward the shaft. Treat sustained CO₂ above 1% as "leave the low spot" and above 3% as "leave the hall." Any sustained H₂S reading is a withdrawal — your nose fatigues within minutes and will lie to you; the meter won't.
- **Wear a respirator near the compost beds and the blooms.** The fallen leaves are never eaten, never carried off; they are digested by microbes alone into warm black beds. Those beds are incubators of thermophilic fungi and bacteria that no human lung has ever met. An N95 or P100 is cheap insurance against a very strange pneumonia. Keep sump water out of your nose, mouth, and any open cut; carry hand sanitizer; be current on tetanus.
- **Radon** is a cumulative concern for repeat visitors, not a single-visit one. Log a reading if you can, for the record.

Now the other direction, which matters more: **you are a contamination event.** This ecosystem has no animals, no competitors, no defenses. A fungal spore or a springtail egg riding your boot tread could be an asteroid strike on a world with no dinosaurs. Adopt the cavers' white-nose protocol as your model: dedicated clothing, freshly laundered; boots scrubbed, disinfected, and fully dried; *nothing* that has been underground anywhere else, in this world or any other. Pack out everything — including, and this needs saying plainly, your bodily waste. Bring a wag bag. The sump is not a toilet and the floor is not a field. You will shed skin cells and sweat regardless; minimize contact, and touch nothing living.

## 4. Darkness as terrain

Three lamps in a seventy-meter hall. Islands of amber with warm, total black between them. Treat darkness here as a physical feature, like water or height.

- **Three lights per person, minimum:** primary headlamp, backup headlamp, small handheld. Lithium batteries tolerate the heat better than alkalines; keep spares in an insulated pouch. Add chemical light sticks. Being caught lightless in absolute warm darkness, needing to find a ladder by touch, is the emergency your redundancy exists to make impossible.
- **Breadcrumb your crossings.** Place reflective markers or chem lights at the dark gaps between lamp islands, starting from the ladder — and retrieve every single one on your way out.
- **The sumps read as floor.** Perfect black mirrors in the dark; one of them holds a red ember that is only an LED's reflection, not a light. Mark sump edges, and never trust a reflection to tell you where the ground is.
- **Learn the other navigation.** Drip pitch locates the vault — high tick from the gantry, low note from the crown. The heat gradient on your skin points at the tap housing like a compass. Stand still and listen before you move; your eyes will, at intervals, simply give up, and that is normal here.
- **Psychology.** Warm darkness reads to the hindbrain as either womb or grave, and you will not know which until you're in it. Some travelers panic when their eyes quit; some weep; some find a peace they spend years trying to describe. Practice total darkness beforehand if you can. Keep voice contact with your party. If panic rises: light up, breathe, regroup under a lamp.
- **No earplugs, no music.** The near-silence is your early-warning system — the mains hum, the once-a-minute tick of steel, and the pump's relay are the hall's normal voice. New sounds are information. Cracking or popping from the rock is a leave-now signal.

## 5. Etiquette for a closed world

This is the section most visitors need and fewest expect.

- **Your footprints are nearly permanent.** There are no animals to disturb the dust; condensate beads on it like dew that never burns off. Every step you take writes itself into the record for years, perhaps decades. Choose one route, reuse it, keep to bare floor, and walk as though your tracks will outlast you — because they may.
- **Touch nothing living.** The waxy pale plates take fingerprints like bruises. The wrist-thick felt sleeving the pipework is simultaneously the room's insulation and its garden. The crowns of coin-shaped leaves are aligned to their lamps with a surveyor's precision, and one careless nudge undoes a season of patient growth.
- **Do not fix anything.** Not the pump deadheading into its capped line, not the panel with its one red eye, not the ore cart still loaded for a shift that ended thirty years ago. And — this will be the hardest — **not the lamps.** You will stand beneath an orphaned crown of leaves dying over a dead bulb and you will want, badly, to bring replacements. Resist it. The hall is not broken. It is becoming something — a blind garden, colorless leaves on warm steel, on a trajectory ten thousand years long. Swapping a bulb rewrites that for your comfort. You are a visitor, not a savior.
- **If a lamp dies while you are there** — six have failed in thirty years, so the odds are low but real — witness it. Note which crown, photograph it, record the time. You will have watched the place turn a page.
- **Measure the pilgrim lines.** The dried runners crossing the dark floor grew about a centimeter a week, which means every 52 centimeters of runner is a year since some lamp failed. The floor is an archive of the hall's darkening, legible to anyone with a tape measure. Read it; don't disturb it.
- **The flowers.** If the air thickens sweet — a ripe floral rot pooling in still air — a bloom is opening somewhere in the black. You may approach, gently, with dim red light. Do not touch it, do not pick it (it is seedless; there is nothing to take), do not hover over it breathing. If you hear the faint tearing sound of it coming open, understand what you are: one of the only animals ever to attend this flower, in a hall with no animals, opening for a pollinator that has never existed. Attend it. Then go.
- **Documentation is the kindest gift.** Count the burning lamps. Photograph the crowns. Measure the runners. Log the pump's cadence, the seep wall's state, gas readings, temperatures, scents. Leave the log with your surface anchor. The next traveler — in a decade, or from another world entirely — inherits your notes.

## 6. Kit list

**Wear:** helmet with light mounts; clean synthetic long clothing (full coverage against hot metal and crystal-sharp blocks — accept that you will be soaked within minutes); light gloves for the ladder; scrubbed boots with a defined heel; respirator at the ready; a watch with two alarms.

**Carry:** three lights plus spares; 2–3 L water (half with electrolytes); vacuum flask of cold water reserved for the ascent; cooling packs or vest; gas meter; notebook and pencil; camera **sealed in a bag** — cold gear fogs instantly in saturated warm air, so let it equilibrate for 30–45 minutes at the bottom before unsealing; small first-aid kit weighted toward heat illness and wound care; whistle; wag bag; hand sanitizer; chem lights and reflective crumbs; a small groundsheet for your base point at the ladder.

**Group:** fixed rope and ascenders (or lanyard sets); hauling line; two gas meters; barometer; thermometer; spare lantern; the cache box.

**Cache at the ladder base, sealed:** 2 L water, spare light and batteries, basic trauma kit, and a card reading ASCEND NOW with the heat-illness criteria written on it.

**Surface:** shade, cold water, dry warm clothes (you will chill fast in dry air after hours of wet heat), towels, a decontamination station for boots and gear — the quarantine runs both directions, and this hall's microbes belong nowhere else either — the logbook, and your rescue communications.

## 7. What it will be like

The warmth rises to meet you on the ladder like a held breath. By the twentieth rung you are damp; by the bottom you have accepted the first wrongness, that here warmth and darkness are the same medium. You will stand at the base while your eyes give up and come back, and you will notice the absence under the lamps before the presence: no moths, no gnats, no winged halo — the orbiting cloud around every night-light you've ever known is here made of leaves.

You will cross the dark gaps slowly, a chem light at your feet, navigating by drip-pitch and the gradient of heat on your skin. At the far end the tap housing crouches warm as a loaf, wearing its blind armor of tongue-thick leaves, and the felt on the pipes will show you — thickened wherever a gasket leaks warmth — that you are reading the plumbing by its garden. At the breach, helmet tight, you will not linger, but you will look: silver rebar, glittering blocks, and beneath them the first hair-fine soda-straw stalactites, two centimeters long — a geology restarting from zero.

If you are patient, the pump will perform its liturgy for you: a relay click, one deep swallow of water going nowhere, silence. If you are lucky, the barometer will swing and you will feel the hall breathe — a faint flex of the eardrums, a brief quickening of the drips — and you will understand that you are standing inside a single breath of something that breathes once a day, and that your whole visit is a fraction of one inhalation.

Then the alarm sounds, and you retrieve your crumbs, and you take your last look at the three amber islands, and you climb. The air cools as you rise, and it will feel like forgiveness.

## 8. Emergency doctrine

Be honest about the geometry: there is no fast extraction from −350 meters. A casualty who cannot climb is a multi-day technical rescue. Therefore the entire doctrine is prevention — conservative time limits, early aborts, margin reserved for the ascent, never solo. For heat casualties: clip them in, cold packs to neck and armpits, cold sips, ascend slowly at their pace. For lost light: stop, don't grope, deploy backups. For gas alarms or causeless breathlessness: stand, move to the shaft, ascend, trust the meter. For new rock sounds: leave. If the shaft itself is ever blocked, shelter at the base, conserve light and water, rest — the hall is survivable for many hours if you do nothing — and let the surface anchor's overdue protocol work.

## 9. Afterward

Cool down gradually. Drink. Change into dry clothes before the evaporative chill catches you. Decontaminate your gear before it touches any other underground place. And write the log while it is fresh — lamps burning, runners measured, flowers noted, pump cadence, seep state, your times.

Go as a witness. The place asks nothing; that is its whole character. The rarest event in its calendar is a visitor — warm-blooded, brief, irrelevant to its ten-thousand-year settling. It will not remember you. Prepare so that the only marks you leave are a single line of footprints slowly re-beading with dew, and a page in a logbook at the surface, waiting for whoever comes down next.
