## Preparing for the High Orchard

First, understand what you are visiting, because it changes the shape of every decision downstream.

This is not a wilderness. It is an estate: a managed sky, managed light, managed water, one landlord, no posted rates. The plateau is a high, cold, thin-aired desert at the end of its world's history, and everything in it that drinks, drinks from a single living organism that has been solvent for longer than your civilization has had plumbing. There are people there — herders — which means there is custom, contract, and etiquette. There are predators, weather made of shadow, and a soundscape that lives in your bones rather than your ears. The place is not hostile. It is *closed* — a loop with no slack in it — and you are an unbudgeted item.

Your three true preparations are your lungs, your water ledger, and your manners. Everything else is luggage.

One more thing before the practicalities: ask around the transit hubs. The locals keep almanacs, and almanacs travel. Any text that predicts the Veil's gap-drift, any herder's drum-phrase glossary, any sketch of the standards' crown silhouettes is worth more than another kilo of gear. And buy or borrow nothing you can't verify — but take what you can get, because the plateau erases marks, and a memory you carry in is the only map that survives.

### First, your body: air, cold, and the dark that never ends

**The air.** Stars at midday means the atmosphere up there is very thin indeed — treat the plateau as you would the high death-zone plateaus of your homeworld. Acclimatize before you ever see the salt: sleep in hypoxic conditions for weeks if you can, then stage your ascent through the mountain-stump passes, camping at each step. Bring bottled oxygen for sleeping if your means allow it, and know the signs of pulmonary and cerebral edema in yourself and your companions, because your evacuation route is long, flat, and entirely on foot. Thin air also means: water boils at a sad temperature (bring a pressure cooker — it will be the hardest-working item you own), fires burn badly (don't plan on found fuel anyway), ice sublimes rather than melts (any frozen stores shrink in your pack — budget for the loss), and you dehydrate through your breath at a rate that will astonish you. Which brings us to the central problem.

**The cold.** This is a mummifying climate — dry, cold, patient. The dead stand for tens of thousands of years and are carved into instruments. Your lips, your fingertips, and any uncovered skin will begin the same process on a shorter schedule. Layer for deep cold, manage sweat vapor ruthlessly, bring salves for cracking skin, and never leave flesh on salt or metal. The ground steals heat; your sleeping pad matters more than your bag. And note the one mercy: the root-ridges are warm, and the standards' heartwood is warm. The heat of this world lives in the tree, like everything else.

**The dark.** There is no night. There is a deepening of dusk. The stars are out at noon; the sun is a coal you can stare at. Your circadian rhythm will come apart within days unless you impose a schedule from outside: strict sleep hours, a bright artificial "morning" lamp, red task lighting otherwise, a sleep mask against the bronze twilight. This sounds like a comfort issue. It is a safety issue. Sleep-deprived people flinch, and on the plateau, flinching is a currency you don't want to spend.

Also understand what perpetual dusk does to your eyes: you will dark-adapt like a deep-sea fish. This makes the blaze-slots — below — genuinely dangerous to you in a way they aren't to the natives.

### Rule one: Water is owned; negotiate or steal

Do the arithmetic before you pack, because the arithmetic is the trip. At altitude, in dry cold, walking on salt crust, you need four to six liters a day. A fortnight's water weighs as much as a person. You cannot carry your visit on your back. Therefore the Orchard's water economy is not a local curiosity — it is your itinerary. You will plan your route well to well, standard to standard, and every evening you will settle accounts.

Your options, ranked by wisdom:

**Hire the professionals.** There are herders on the avenues, and herders hold water contracts older than your arrival. Water is money here — arrive water-rich and you arrive wealthy. Carry your water in many small sealed flasks, like coins; big skins are how banks walk, and you are not a bank. Pay for guidance, tithe-rate knowledge, and introductions. This is the single best use of your carried water.

**Take the flask-beast contract.** The beasts drink from the cistern-chambers in exchange for services: carrying seed, culling parasites, and eventually dying within reach of the roots. You have hands. Learn the local résumé — carry what wants carrying, cull what wants culling — and you may find cistern access extended to you as it is to them. The final clause of the contract is, for a short visit, negotiable.

**Match the moths.** The drill-moths bore the sapwood and are tolerated up to a quota; the wound seals the instant the tithe is exceeded. The moths, in other words, are the published rate schedule. Watch them. If you must tap, tap as they do: one small clean wound, under quota, then withdraw and let the tree seal itself. Greed gets you sealed out — or noticed.

**Glean the breaches.** When a standard breaches, antifreeze-sap floods out and hardens into sap-ice, the only free lunch on the plateau — and it draws everything within a day's walk, including sirens. A breach is a bank robbery at which the money feeds everyone, so decide which you are: witness, gleaner, or fool. If you glean, arrive late, crosswind, never between anything and the ice, and take fresh sheets from the margins. Old ice shrinks into scallops as it sublimes, concentrating whatever it carries; the scallops are weeks old and not for you.

**Test everything.** The sap is glycerol-thick and sweet, and "antifreeze" is a word that should make your hands slow. Carry an assay kit and a small still — your pressure cooker again — and never drink anything untreated, no matter how the natives relish it. Their biochemistry has had eons to negotiate terms. Yours has had none.

And the two absolutes: never drink the dawn brine that weeps from sun-facing salts — the plateau's only free water is poison, which is exactly why it's free — and never steal. Not because you'll be caught by police, but because you'll be caught by accounting. The loop closes. Everything returns to the root. You do not want to be the discrepancy a thinking landscape decides to reconcile.

### Rule two: Silence is armor; stillness is a bet

The plateau is loud, but its loudness lives in the ground. You will hear it through your boot soles before your ears: a standing hum, deep unhurried booms like doors closing in a country-sized house, a rolling cannonade of cracking trunks at dusk, and somewhere windward, the Choir of mummified dead standards singing through their hollow bones — chorded, dissonant, sometimes almost a voice.

Prepare for this physiologically. Infrasound cannot be earplugged away; it arrives through the skeleton. It causes unease, chest pressure, and a sense of presence even in people who know exactly what it is. Expect it. Name it when it happens. And expect the Choir to say your name some long dim afternoon. It does not know your name. Both of these things are true; hold both, and keep walking.

Now the survival craft, because the siren hunts by boom — mimicking the standards, then listening, patient as a loan, for the one small sound of something flinching.

**Practice the freeze at home.** In your kitchen. It will feel stupid; it will save you. When a boom sounds: stop, mid-stride if you can, slow your breathing, and stay stone longer than your fear insists is necessary. With companions, pre-arrange it: no one looks at anyone, no one signals, and the count resumes only when the most cautious of you resumes it.

**Never pair noise with sudden stillness.** Sound-then-freeze is the exact pattern the siren is listening for. If you knock something, drop something, crunch a frost-curled polygon edge — keep moving, smoothly, deliberately. Break the pairing. Then be quiet somewhere else.

**Carry a flinch you can throw.** Mechanical clickers, a pebble-line you can drag from stillness — anything that makes the one small sound ten meters away from you. In a world where the hunter listens for the flinch, a decoy flinch is worth more than any weapon.

**Walk soft.** Soft-soled overboots on the salt; avoid the crunchy curled polygon edges; never, ever walk on the root-ridges — you would be walking on the landlord's nerves, and everything with ears would learn your stride. Carry a staff, and learn to drum with it: the herds speak through the substrate, and a deliberate, simple, repeatable tap-code is both your voice and your passport. If you can learn even one herder phrase — *traveler, passing, trading* — drum it at plot borders.

**Get a geophone.** A contact microphone and headphones are the single best instruments you can bring. The plateau's real medium is ground sound: herd thunder, the cannonade rolling grove to grove, the slow pulse of the ridges at night. Learn the baseline rhythms of your campsite's ridge. An anomaly in that rhythm is information — possibly the only advance warning you will ever get of anything.

Finally: at dusk, be home. The cannonade of contracting trunks is the plateau's curfew bell. Everything freezes when it rolls through; everything hunts after it.

### Rule three: Salt is history; the sea is in the trees

Terrain craft, briefly but seriously:

The flats are crusted salt broken into frost-curled polygons — footing that crunches, shifts, and occasionally hides voids. Test before you trust. The gypsum dunes are beautiful and engulfing; they pile against anything that stands still, which includes your camp and your caches — anchor deep, clear drift every cycle, and never leave gear standing on open ground. The glass roads are excellent walking and better navigation, but note that they run past the last standard and stop at nothing: they outran their city, which means the Orchard once reached farther, or intends to. Either way, do not follow them far. Nowhere is a real destination here, and it is not provisioned.

The Last River canyon is the one safe canyon in any desert you'll ever visit — it has not flooded in longer than memory exists — and it makes a fine sheltered highway. Treat its wind-funnel reaches and its ambush cover with the usual respect.

The Drop is where you should arrive. Come in at the rim, camp there, and spend one full cycle reading the city before you enter it: the spiral mathematics of the plots, the lit avenues of root-ridges pulsing at night, the shadow-cells drifting. A night on the rim teaches you more than a week on the flats. Leave your reserve cache here too — buried deep in a pass, against both dunes and motes, with your return water in it. And do not descend to the deadlands. Nothing down there negotiates.

Navigation deserves its own warning: **the plateau erases.** Wind takes your tracks, salt creeps, and the maintenance motes — half of everything that drifts off the black vanes — polish every inscription away within the century. Your blaze-marks are temporary. So navigate by what persists: the phyllotactic pattern of the plots, the crown silhouettes of individual standards (sketch them — paper sealed in a bag outlasts stone here), the stars, which are conveniently out at all hours, and a dead-reckoning log kept with religious discipline. For caches, bury a slow mechanical clicker you can hear through the geophone from a kilometer off — a ground-beacon for a ground-sound world.

When you reach a standard, observe border protocol. The sterile circle of poisoned salt around each trunk is its address plate and its customs house. Stop at the edge. Drum your phrase. Wait, visibly, and let the motes inspect you — they will. Do not camp in the circle without leave, don't let its dust into your wounds, and if you are invited further — the chambers, the corridors, the stairs grown rather than built — then behave as what you are: a guest inside a body. No flames, no boring, nothing sealed, nothing cut. Sleep in the warm heartwood if permitted; it is the safest bed on the plateau, and you will feel it thinking through the wall behind your shoulder all night. Some travelers find this the deepest sleep of their lives. Some leave early. Both are normal.

Vaults: threshold only. The warm dark below is already occupied by roots, patient as water, and you do not want to be prised apart like a door.

One heuristic ties all of this together: **on the flats, slow is the signature of belonging.** Everything native moves at walking pace or root pace. Anything fast is weather, predator, or mistake. Don't be any of the three.

### Rule four: The light is rationed; take what moves

Learn to read the Veil the way sailors read clouds. Its gaps drift; where the gaps go, the light goes. Shadow-cells — patches of deeper dusk — are the plateau's fair weather, and the herds migrate inside them, grazing the dimness. Traveling with a shadow-cell means company, cover, and forage for the herds you may be shadowing. It also means traveling with everything that hunts herds. Choose knowingly.

Blaze-slots are the other thing the sky does: blades of raw unfiltered sunlight sliding across the salt like searchlights. For you — dark-adapted, thin-skinned, unacclimatized — treat every slot as unfiltered high-altitude noon on the brightest world you know. The protocol:

Watch the trees. When the black vanes ripple edge-on across a standard like a field turning its back, a slot is coming; the moths drop and the herds turn their plated flanks a breath later. You have perhaps minutes. Get your back to the light, hood up, goggles on — photochromic, with a fixed-filter backup — every centimeter of skin covered, crouched in your own shadow behind a salt curl or a trunk. Wait it out. It passes in minutes, and the dusk closes over it like water over a stone.

Then — and this is the part the natives can't teach you — *use it*. The light is rationed; take what moves. A blaze-slot is when your solar panels actually charge, when you can shoot a true sun position for navigation, when you can see the plateau in its raw colors. Deploy panels from shelter on a line, note the time in your slot log, and after a week you'll be forecasting them against the almanac like a local.

On charged nights, the standards discharge slow violet streamers crown-to-sky, and the Veil drinks the current. It is beautiful, and it is the reason you never climb a standard after dark and never camp against a trunk. Stay out from under the crowns; the trees are talking to the sky then, and you do not want to be the punctuation. Insulate your sleeping pad, shield your electronics, and watch the show from a vault mouth.

### Rule five: Everything returns to the root

This is the law under the laws, and it converts directly into conduct.

**Leave only what the loop can eat.** Pack out every synthetic, every battery, every scrap the motes can't metabolize — they will polish your plastic for a century without ever digesting it, a permanent irritation in a body you are visiting. But your organic leavings are not pollution here; they are rent. A closed loop running for eons prizes fixed nitrogen and trace minerals, and you arrive carrying both. Deliver them to the root-edge like a conscientious tenant. It is disposal, etiquette, and payment in one act.

**Take nothing.** Everything portable on the plateau is property, instrument, or ancestor — usually all three. The Choir is not dead wood; it is a played organ and a graveyard, and burning or breaking a husk is the one crime both herders and landlord will notice. Take photographs. Take rubbings. Take notes.

**Find the herders, and be legible doing it.** In a world where sneaking reads as predation, openness is courtesy. Walk the avenues at herd-pace, in the open, in shadow-cell company, drumming your phrase. They will hear you through the ground kilometers before you see them — you cannot surprise them, so don't try to. Display your water honestly; it is your wealth and your credentials. Trade for their almanac, their tithe-rates, their names for sirens, and their assessment of whether the Orchard has categorized you yet. Because it will categorize you — pest, guest, or pollinator — and your entire visit is an argument for the third.

**Do not try to talk to the Orchard directly.** The ridge-pulses are its thought, and injecting signals into a nervous system you cannot parse is not communication; it's interference. Observe, record, and let it think. No one is left who knows what it thinks about, and you will not fix that in a fortnight.

**Mind your mind.** This is the hazard no kit addresses: the hum that moves into your chest and redecorates; the dusk that erases your days; the Choir's almost-voice; the crushing patience of a finished world. Do not travel alone. Keep a logbook and read it aloud to each other. Enforce artificial mornings. Schedule your awe — rim sunsets over the deadlands, ridge-light from a vault mouth — because unscheduled awe at 3 a.m. is called something else. And set a hard return date before you arrive. The dose is cumulative: sleep degrades, the ledger thins, the dusk gets into you. The plateau is patient and will still be thinking when you return. Leave while leaving is easy.

And the clause no one wants: understand the terms before you sign. If you die on the plateau, the contract completes itself — you will be rendered, delivered, and spent well, and your gear will be polished for a century. There are worse endings than joining the oldest thought under a managed sky. But file your route with the rim cache and a friend at the hub, and prefer to settle your account on your feet.

### The kit

**On you:** layered vapor-managed cold kit; soft-soled quiet overboots; photochromic goggles plus fixed dark filter; full-coverage hood with a reflective emergency flip; dust mask or respirator for pollen-mote season; salt gloves; balaclava for breathing pre-warmed air.

**Camp:** four-season sealed tent with dust skirts and long guys for dune drift; high-R insulated pad; deep-cold bag; sleep mask and earplugs (for air-sound only — the ground will still talk); pressure cooker; fuel stove with windscreen; condensate capture; bright "morning" lamp and red task lights.

**Water and food:** all your food (there is nothing forageable that isn't theft or poison); water in many small sealed flasks — your wallet, in coins; assay kit (glycols, perchlorate); small still rig; wound sealant for yourself; one clean small auger, to be used only at the moths' rate.

**Signal and navigation:** geophone and headphones; tap-staff; flinch-clickers and a pebble-line; sketchbooks and pencils, sealed; logbook — your truest map; ground-beacon clickers for caches; your drafted star charts; oxygen if your means allow.

**Trade and tribute:** water, above all; small metal tools and needles; light sources; ointments; trace mineral salts — clean ones, not the plateau's perchlorate — which a closed loop may value more than you'd think.

### One day on the flats

Wake before full dim, sealed in your bag, the ridge-pulse still ticking through the pad — you'll have learned your campsite's rhythm by the third night, and you'll notice you're listening to it before you're conscious of listening. Artificial morning: ten minutes of bright lamp, logbook entry, water ledger reconciled. Check the east-facing slopes for the weep of brine and don't touch it. Drum your phrase at the circle's edge. Break camp while the world warms toward merely cold.

Move with a shadow-cell, soft soles on the avenue, herd-shapes parallel at half a kilometer. Mid-dim, the ripple: vanes folding edge-on across the nearest standard, black on bronze. The drill runs itself — back turned, hood, goggles, crouched behind a salt curl, panels out on their line. Six minutes of raw red light on the salt; six minutes of charging; then the dusk pours back. Walk on. By late dim, the next standard: moths observed, tithe estimated, one small wound weeping glycerol-sweet sap that you assay, still, and cut with melt-water from your fuel. The motes find you before you finish; you hold still and let them.

Be inside before dusk. The cannonade rolls through grove by grove, the flats carrying it like ice carrying the sound of a freezing lake, and you eat in the warm dark of a vault mouth while the root-ridges light up, pulse by deliberate pulse, junction to junction — the oldest city that exists, thinking. Charged night: violet streamers overhead, the Veil drinking. You sleep sealed, the hum in your ribs, your waste delivered to the root-edge, your tracks already gone behind you.

---

It will not miss you. That is the kindest thing a place that old can offer: you cannot harm it, you cannot help it, you can only visit correctly. So go quietly. Spend water like money, because it is. Take the light that moves. Give the root its due. And when the standard behind you booms once — like a door closing, in a house that has no intention of ever being empty — don't flinch.
