The first thing to understand is that you do not find the Bright. The Bright admits you. Everything else in your preparation follows from that.

### 1. Be honest about why you're going

Every visitor to the Bright reacts in one of two ways, and you should try to learn which one you are *before* you are thirty meters down an ice chimney with a covenant of silence above and below you.

So rehearse. In the months before you go, spend escalating stretches in silence and enclosure: an afternoon, then a full day, then three days without speaking, ideally somewhere small and dim. Sit with your own tinnitus, your own heartbeat, your own digestion. If the absence of input feels like suffocation — if stillness reads to you as a held breath that will never be released — that is not a failure, but it is information. The town has watched travelers climb out gasping into the wind with relief. There is no shame in being that traveler. There is only shame in discovering it late, loudly.

And examine your motives. This is not a place to collect, conquer, document, or fix. The town is finished — that is its nature and its achievement. If some part of you is already composing the improvements you'll suggest, stay home. An idea offered to the Bright is a small polite demand that forty generations were wrong.

### 2. You will not find it — arrange to be brought

The hollow is ringed by stones that read as no arrangement at all, on a plain with no hills, no landmarks, and light that flattens every shadow into nothing. Sound travels strangely far up there; landmarks do not travel at all. You could stand at the lip among the snow buntings and not know. This is not a challenge to your navigation skills. It is the town's immune system, and it has worked for centuries.

The way in runs through people: the sister-peoples of the coast, the traders trusted across years. Expect the trust to take time — possibly years. Expect to be vouched for, and expect the final stretch across the tundra to be guided in a way that leaves you unable to retrace it. Accept this gracefully. Your secrecy obligations begin before departure, not after: no maps, no logged coordinates, no records of the route in any medium, in any world, ever. Once, it is said, a lost surveyor found the place by accident — a person whose whole craft was making locations findable, arriving at the one location that must not be found. Do not hope to repeat his accident. Arrange to be found instead.

Go alone, or with one companion at most. In a town of three hundred, a party of five is not a visit, it's a demographic event.

### 3. Choose your season

**The bright season** gives you the daily Sunstroke — the column of unfiltered light standing in the floor-pool for the span of a held breath — and a descent lit from above, the Well freshly re-cut and polished by the Light-Keepers. The crossing is easier: continuous light, no whiteout dark. But the thaw makes the tundra wet and insect-haunted above, and the drip-rate below runs high — the Soft Rain, the only weather the Bright has.

**The dark months** give you the town transformed: four hundred oil lamps, small suns in blue ice, the Well pouring starlight, and — if you are fortunate — the aurora burning silently above the rim, the year's one spectacle. The crossing, though, is polar night travel in earnest cold, and you may wait at the coast for word that the Well is passable.

If you can only go once, go for the Sunstroke. If they invite you for the dark, accept without hesitation.

### 4. Train the body

**Kill your tics.** This is the hardest and most important preparation. The finger that drums idly on surfaces, the whistle while you work, the hum, the foot that keeps time — all of it must go. In the Bright, a drummed finger on ice is not a mannerism; it echoes the Old Death, the story that founded the covenant. Whether or not the falling fin ever actually fell, resonance in an ice chamber is real physics. Treat loud sound the way you'd treat open flame in a library: a structural hazard, not a social one.

**Practice silent living.** Walk without footfall. Eat without clatter. Handle every object you own and listen to it. Learn to cough and sneeze into layered wool. Learn to laugh without sound — you will need to, because joy exists down there, it just shakes instead of ringing. Learn to breathe through your nose. Sleep in the quiet and get used to hearing your own pulse in your ears, because for the first days below, your body will be the loudest thing in the chamber, and everyone will be kind about it, and you will be embarrassed anyway, and then it will pass.

**Get strong for the architecture.** The descent is rungs and rails. The streets are a roofscape — moss-upholstered roofs crossed by lanes two shoulders wide, pierced by ladder-holes, hung with nets. The oldest lanes require a grown person to stoop; time in the Bright is measured in stoop, and you will read it with your spine. Prepare knees, back, balance, and an honest reckoning with confined spaces. The side cells connect by crawl-tunnels. If crawling through blue ice toward lamplight sounds unbearable, know that now.

**Arrive healthy, or don't arrive.** Three hundred people in a sealed, balanced world: a traveler carrying even an ordinary cold is carrying a potential catastrophe. Spend time on the coast first. If anything stirs — a sniffle, a fever — postpone a season. A cough on the tundra is a cough. A cough in the Bright is an event with a name by evening.

**Go unscented.** Wash with plain soap for a week before. No perfume, no smoke-saturated clothing. Scent is rationed below — resin burned on rare occasions only — and your smell will arrive before you do, announced in a place where nothing else announces anything.

### 5. Pack like a rumor

The rule: if it makes sound, it doesn't go. If it can't be eaten, burned in the Hearth-Cell, or absorbed by the midden, it comes home with you, re-wrapped in silence.

**Bring:**
- Soft wool layers in the town's palette — blue-grey, moss-green, bone, dark brown. Modern synthetics *swish*; in that room a nylon shell sounds like a brush fire. Wool whispers at worst.
- Felt or soft-hide boots, silent-soled, well broken in, plus spares.
- Gloves: thin grippy pairs for the padded rungs (bare flesh sticks to bare ice), dry spares beyond those.
- Ties and wraps instead of buckles. Any unavoidable metal gets wrapped in hide and stitched shut. Tether everything. A dropped metal cup below is not an accident; it's a small desecration.
- A wooden or horn cup on a soft cord.
- A soft eye cover, if you go in the bright season — the Long Day glow does not go out.
- Gifts, **only as requested through your contact.** The town is finished; it does not need objects. The welcome gifts are the ones that vanish entirely: lamp oil (four hundred lamps burn all dark season), salt if they use it, pigment if they ask. Better still, gifts with no mass at all: stories, news told slowly, a weave pattern, a song taught to a wall. The worst gift imaginable is a drum. There is no second-worst; that category stands alone.

**Do not bring:**
- Instruments, bells, whistles, anything that ticks or beeps.
- Cameras, recorders, maps, positioning devices. Your memory is the only permitted archive. Calm is the town's one export, and it is carried, not photographed.
- Your own light. No lanterns, no beams. Their glow is theirs; your eyes will adapt faster than you expect.
- Red clothing. Red is spoken for — thread in the weaving, ochre on a cheekbone, the door-frame of a marriage house. Wearing it casually is like arriving in someone else's wedding clothes.
- Keys, coins, hard soles, velcro, swinging zipper pulls, plastic wrappers.
- A schedule.

### 6. The crossing

The tundra is the real cold — wind-driven, aggressive, an event rather than a condition. Full polar kit, and trust your guide absolutely; the plain offers nothing to navigate by, and the sea is past the edge of walking. If you are somehow separated: shelter, stay put, do not wander. You can die within a short walk of the lip and never know it was there.

When you reach the hollow, it will look like nothing. That is what it is for. Snow buntings nest at the lip; a fox dens ten paces off. Approach gently — you are arriving at a nursery. Do not disturb the fox. She is a resident of longer standing than you.

### 7. The descent

Two bodies wide, straight down through turf and frozen soil into old ice. Everything secured, everything muffled, three points of contact, slow. Communicate with your keeper by prearranged rope-tugs and signs — do not call down or up; the Well's geometry swallows sound anyway.

Mark the moment the cold changes character. Above, cold is an attack. Somewhere in the chimney it becomes a condition — steady, windless, a breath above and below freezing, held note-perfect by the buried glacier heart. Your breath shows. The wind ceases to exist — not fades, *ceases* — and that is a stranger sensation than you expect, the removal of something you've felt your whole life without noticing.

Then the glow rises to meet you: blue-white, sourceless, the whole ice body piping the low sun down and diffusing it, as if you were descending into the inside of a lantern. First-time travelers sometimes stop on the rungs to look. This is fine. The keeper has seen it before. Do not stop forever.

### 8. Arrival

You will be met, and guided up onto the roofscape — there are no streets at ground level; you walk on the town. Walk only the polished ways. Everything in the Bright has one of two fates: grown over, or worn smooth by feet. Stay on the smooth. Mind the ladder-holes — stepping through one puts you, uninvited, in someone's sleeping loft, which is the Bright's version of a traffic accident. Duck in the old lanes.

Your host will assign you a drip-basin and tell you its name. Learn the name. It may be older than your language. Each of the ceiling's eleven hundred weeping points is named, and the water-rights are inheritance as precise as genealogy — so being given a name of water is a legal act as much as a kindness, the closest thing the town has to stamping a passport. Drink from your basin. Touch no other.

Speak almost nothing the first day. Let your ears recalibrate to a room with no echo, where sound dies in three paces. If you must cough, adjust gear, or deal with a squeaking boot, do it in the Hearth-Cell, under the cover of the fire — its muffled crackle is the only acoustic privacy the Bright possesses. Treat the double doors as a sound-lock: one closed before the other opens.

Expect to be woken, when needed, by a touch on the shoulder. In a silent town, a hand is the only alarm clock. Practice not startling.

### 9. The law of sound

The physics will invert your instincts, so learn this cold: **open air is private; the walls are telephones.** A voice raised in the center of the chamber dies at arm's length — that is where people go for privacy, standing in the open, face to face, saying nothing the ice could carry. But press close and murmur along the curve, and your words slide a hundred paces to the exact ear intended.

So: never say anything against a wall that you do not want delivered, addressed, to someone specific across the room. The walls do not gossip; they deliver. And if a voice arrives out of the ice at your ear — your host speaking to you from a hundred paces off, intimate as a thought — do not flinch, and do not answer into the open air. Lean to the wall and answer along the same path.

If you are invited to sing a duet against the wall, with the ice itself as the instrument, that is an honor of a high order. Accept. Hum into the curve and let the room do the rest.

As for the proverb — *one cry to a lifetime* — plan to die unspent. If you are badly hurt, spend it; the covenant is old enough to contain mercy, and a town that sanctions a newborn's wail will not begrudge a broken bone. But know that you will have spent it.

### 10. The law of surfaces

Never step on growing moss; the lawns take decades to thicken a thumb's depth and are cropped at exactly the rate they grow. Don't lean on the hanging nets. Don't trail fingers through the melt-films — the black calligraphy threading them is ice worms, grazing, and the films are a pasture, not a texture. Bare skin sticks to bare ice; lean only where hides are hung.

The ceiling weeps, and one of its eleven hundred named drips will find the back of your neck. Do not resent it. Someone will tell you its name, which is the town's way of laughing. If the thaw above runs strong during your stay, the drip-rate rises and the basins brim — the Soft Rain, the only weather there is. Enjoy it the way they do: quietly, as abundance.

If a child places a woolly flightless moth in your palm and shows you how to fold it off the water-rim, you have been given your first job. Take it seriously. It is the same job they give their own children, which is to say the town is trusting you with its thirst.

### 11. The daily, and the visitings

The drip-basins keep the hours; drip-rates do not change. Your host's household rhythms will carry you. Abandon your clock at the lip.

**The Sunstroke:** once each bright-season day, for a held breath, the sun aligns with the Well. When three hundred people stop at once, you will feel it before you understand it. Stop. Watch the column stand in the floor-pool and the midges dance in it without a whine — a visible shaft of life inside the shaft of light. Hold nothing in your hands. Then the town resumes.

**The Hearth-Cell:** warmth, food, the loudest permitted sound arriving muffled, like weather in another country. Eat what is given. The tadpole shrimp from the basins are older in form than the ice itself; chew respectfully. Waste nothing — ask where things go, because everything has a where. The floor you walk on is forty generations of correctly answered wheres.

**The Lambency:** the darkest cell, where a cave-moss catches lamplight and returns it as a faint green-gold glimmer. If you are taken there — especially if by a child — practice astonishment in the local manner: quietly, with wide eyes and a soft breath.

**The Gallery:** go only when invited. The dead lie in niches wrapped in moss, and over decades the ice grows a clear rind over them, so they remain — blue, serene, visible. You may be introduced by name to someone's grandmother. Answer as if she can hear; the etiquette is not about what she can do, it is about who you are. Do not touch the rind. Note the mammoth in the wall — one shoulder, a curve of tusk, quarried for three hundred years and not finished. That is not inefficiency. That is the town explaining itself to you. Somewhere at an odd depth, wings spread, there is a bird. You are not the strangest thing in the ice. You may not be the strangest thing to arrive this century.

**The exhumed forest:** spruce and larch of a warmer age stepping out of the blue at the chamber's back. If needle-bundles are burned during your stay — it happens only on occasions that warrant scent — breathe it carefully. That is the smell of ten thousand vanished summers. It is also, faintly, the taste of the water, and you will understand why.

### 12. Among the Quiet

Learn the sign dialect's basics from your coastal contacts before you arrive. Let listening be your main social act. Expect to be watched — silence makes observers of everyone, and you are likely the most interesting thing to happen in some while. Be comfortable being looked at.

Work lightly and accept instruction from children; they are the authority on moths, wall-paths, and the Thousand Names of Water, which they recite as a game that is also a cadastre. Do not pretend you will learn all thousand.

Do not suggest improvements. Do not ask what the town is building toward, or where it is going. It is not going anywhere. Melt matches refreeze; births match deaths; the surplus of the young departs down the Well carrying ivory, worked flint, and calm. A guest is a drip: arrive on schedule, be clear, fall into the basin you're aimed at, don't overspread.

And do not ask to stay. There is no room for you in the arithmetic unless they make it, and making it costs someone something you cannot see. If *they* ask *you* — that is another matter, and no guide can help you with it.

### 13. Hazards

The ladder-holes. The slick polish of melt-film underfoot. Skin on bare ice. Damp cold that creeps — dry your layers, change your gloves. Above, the wind and the whiteout. Your own microbes — if illness stirs in you, tell your host immediately and take yourself to the Hearth-Cell's cover until it passes.

If panic comes instead — the walls pressing, the sameness, the held breath — say so with a gesture. They will take you to the Well. The town has watched both reactions since before your language existed, and keeps no score.

### 14. Leaving

Ascend slowly. The daylight at the lip will hit like a shout; the wind will feel like violence after weeks of windlessness; even the tundra's far-carried fox bark will seem loud as a city. Sit at the rim until the fox gets used to you. Let the buntings settle. Many travelers weep, or laugh, or sit very still. All are correct.

Afterward, you will be quiet for days. Your own world will seem unbearably loud — the clatter, the engines, the constant demand. You will catch yourself fixing a dripping tap and then stopping, listening to the interval, wondering what its name would be. You will stop in rooms when the light stands a certain way. You will speak less, and lower, and people will notice.

This is the export working. Calm is the one thing the Bright sends out, carried by everyone who leaves, and now you are carrying it. Guard the location as you guard your own pulse. Send your thanks back the way you came, across years, by the ones who are trusted.

And if, beside some basin in your memory, you watched a moth fold its wings and heard a single soft note of water falling into stone at the interval it has kept since before your language existed — and felt that you had finally arrived somewhere not waiting for anything, not becoming anything, not going anywhere —

then you understood the assignment. Somewhere that simply, luminously, is.
