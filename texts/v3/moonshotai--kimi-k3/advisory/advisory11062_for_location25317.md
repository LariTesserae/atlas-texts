# Preparing for the Still World

## First, understand what kind of trip this is

You are visiting a closed system at perfect equilibrium. That single fact dictates almost everything below. There is no food anywhere in the world — not a berry, not a fish, not a thing that can be eaten, because everything that looks alive is made of alloy and charge. There is no rescue, because there is no one. There is no weather forecast, because the weather is the same weather forever. There is no resupply, no firewood, no building material, no high ground, and — this is the part travelers underestimate — no narrative. The world will not present you with events. The rules are the only events. If you need a trip with a plot, go somewhere else. If you can bring your own plot, or need none, this place will change you.

Plan for full self-sufficiency from arrival to departure, plus margin, plus an exit you arrange entirely yourself. The world will accommodate you and it will not send you away. Those are both true, and the second one is the real hazard.

## Dress for permanent immersion

The defining condition is blood-warm water over machined plate, boot-top to knee, sometimes chest. You will be wet from the waist down for every walking hour of every day. Build your whole kit around that.

- **Footwear:** draining wading boots with a protective toe and firm ankle support. Not sandals — the seams between plates and the pristine, sharp bolt-heads will find bare toes, and the plates' machined tooth grips soles well but punishes skin. Bring a second pair so one is always drying, plus light camp footwear.
- **Clothing:** synthetic everything, quick-dry, long sleeves and long pants despite the warmth — the sun off the sheet doubles your exposure at noon. No cotton, no leather. A wide hat with a retention strap for storm gusts.
- **Polarized glasses.** Not optional. They serve as sun protection and as a depth-and-hazard instrument: with them you can read the floor — seams, grates, recessed hatch handles, the silver rising of an intake — through the still water. The reflections that make this world beautiful also make it unreadable without them.
- **Accept the wet.** Rain gear is beside the point; the rain is warm and you are already wetter than rain can make you. Carry one light shell only as wind protection near the walking storm columns.

Warm water means no hypothermia, which is a mercy. It also means your skin never fully recovers. Warm-immersion foot breakdown is the classic failure mode here: maceration, then cracking, then infection in a world whose only microbe is an alien gray monoculture you know nothing about. Bring antifungal powder, barrier cream, antiseptic, waterproof dressings, and the discipline to dry your feet completely at every camp, every day, without exception.

## Water and food

**Food is simple: bring all of it.** Every calorie, for every day, plus a reserve. Nothing here is edible and nothing here ever will be. Dense, no-cook rations are best; a small stove if you must, with all fuel carried in.

**Water is the elegant problem.** Do not drink the sheet-water untested — it tingles with a ghost of charge and runs opalescent near outfalls, which tells you it's a working fluid, not a river. But the world itself distills: condensate sheets down every radiator fin and drips from every pipe belly, endlessly. And here is the beautiful deduction — *nothing in this world corrodes*. Nothing leaches. The condensate dripping off inert, immortal alloy is likely the cleanest water you will ever collect. Test it with strips the first day, confirm, and then hang a bottle under a pipe run each night and let the world's dew fill it. Carry a mechanical purifier as backup.

## Shelter and sleep

There is no ground, so there is no camping as you know it. Your options, in order of preference:

1. **Plinth tops and walkway platforms** in gantry country and the transformer yards — dry, flat, elevated. The single best campsites in the world.
2. **A hammock strung between gantry uprights or pipe runs**, with a tarp rigged not against rain but against *drip* — everything overhead in this world is beaded with condensate and it will drip on you all night, arrhythmic and endless. Position matters more than coverage.
3. **A small inflatable raft**, which I recommend regardless: it ferries your dry bags across chest-deep stretches, serves as a floating dry platform, and can be tethered to a gantry leg for a night's sleep on the sheet. Drift is minimal — the water is always moving and never going anywhere — but tether anyway.

Bring a headlamp with a red mode. Nights are sparse amber and green marker light under a haze that hides the stars, and night wading among recessed hatches and grates is how ankles break.

## Travel craft

- **Carry a wading staff.** Probe ahead for hatch rims, weir steps, basin edges, and the breathing grates. The floor is honest but full of small traps, all of them sharp-edged and pristine.
- **Navigate by the spine.** The superstructure towers on the horizon — the ones with their own weather — are your fixed reference in every direction. Within that, count paces, note provinces (radiator forest, stack fields, gantry country, the reservoirs), and sketch your own map in a waterproof notebook with a pencil. Ink runs; paper dies; plan accordingly. You cannot blaze a trail here — the world closes over every trace — so your map and your pace count are the whole of it.
- **Fog protocol:** dawn fog is total. Stop. Wait. It burns off to white haze by mid-morning, every day, exactly as it did yesterday.
- **Storm protocol:** the convective rain columns are visible from far off, walking slowly, so routing around them is easy. What you must respect is lightning. In a storm you are the tallest object on an open, conductive, water-covered plain. Move perpendicular to a column's path, and — counterintuitively — get *out* of gantry country, away from the pylons, and low onto a plinth until it passes.
- **Intakes and outfalls:** give them a wide berth. The shoals are siphoned into intakes in kilometers-long streams; the water there has intentions. Outfall water is chemically different. Neither is worth it.
- **The tingle:** the faint charge on the skin is baseline and benign — the animals wade freely. But here is the traveler's master principle for this world: *in a place where nothing changes, any change is information.* If the tingle strengthens, get out of the water. If a needle on a gauge moves, note it. If the hum in your sternum shifts pitch, pay attention. Novelty is the only alarm system this world has, and it's a good one.

## The animals, and the etiquette of a balanced world

They are harmless in the ordinary sense — nothing here hunts, nothing here fears, nothing here dies. The waders will step over your feet. The kites will ignore you until their dusk homecoming, when thousands settle onto the pylons with a soft servo chorus that is the loudest sound of the day; use it as your curfew bell and be in camp before it ends. The plate-lickers carry waders on their backs as living islands, and a slow, tolerant licker may bear your weight the same way for a few minutes. Sit only if it plainly does not mind, and step off the moment it wants its circuit back — you could set a clock by those circuits, and you have no right to be the reason a perfect clock loses time.

The rules of conduct all follow from one fact: this world is an economy balanced to the last decimal, and you are a pure perturbation.

- **Do not draw power from the docking pedestals.** The animals queue for them with infinite patience. Your draw is a debt the world must absorb. Carry solar — the sun is ordinary and strong once the fog burns — and live within it.
- **Do not feed anything.** The gray whisper in the seams is the world's only crop and it is spoken for. Nothing you carry registers as food anyway.
- **Do not unscrew, pry, or take souvenirs.** There is nothing to take. A bolt here is not a keepsake; it is a load-bearing member of forever.
- **If an animal presents a port to you** — and one may, stepping out of its congregation to wait beneath your hands, patient as a pedestal — do not plug anything into it. You have nothing to offer its protocol. Stand still. Receive it as the honor it is. Let it step away on its own schedule.
- **At dawn, stop walking.** Every animal in the world turns to face the sunrise and holds still for a few breaths. Join them. It is the world's only ceremony, it costs you nothing, and travelers who skip it report, later, that they regret exactly one thing about the trip.
- **Keep your voice low.** The quiet here is engineered or it is law, and a shout carries across the sheet for a kilometer. Headphones, not speakers.

## Waste and traces

Pack out everything synthetic — every wrapper, every strip of tape, every dead battery. For organic waste: there is no soil to bury it in, and the world's metabolism will process what you leave, but make its work small and keep it far from pedestals, intakes, and the seams where the waders crop. Gear discipline matters beyond tidiness: anything you set down and abandon, the world will find and unmake. Tether your kit. Count your kit. If you want proof of the hypothesis, bring one coin you are willing to lose, leave it marked on a plate, log the location, and check it on your way out. That experiment is the only souvenir this world offers.

## Hazards, honestly assessed

The physical dangers are modest: lightning, intakes, sun, heat exhaustion while hauling a pack through warm water at midday (siesta under a pipe belly — shade exists only beneath structures), foot breakdown, and infected small wounds. Treat every cut with antiseptic immediately; the gray whisper is a monoculture microbe whose intentions toward human tissue no one has published.

Then there are the quiet ones. The hum you feel in your sternum is infrasonic, and some travelers develop headaches or a low, sourceless unease after days of it; if it gets to you, change provinces — gantry country hums differently than the radiator forests. And do not underestimate the sameness as a route-finding hazard: every horizon is identical, every plate is new, and inattention turns into lostness faster here than anywhere with trees.

## The mind — the preparation that actually matters

Here is what no kit list covers. This world has no past tense. Nothing accumulates, nothing erodes, nothing records your passing. An archaeologist would starve; so, slowly, will the part of you that expects days to *add up* to something. The first days are wonder. Then comes a stretch — different for everyone — where the perfection begins to feel like erasure: the water closes over your footprints, the whisper doesn't record your kneeling, the world registers you, accommodates you, lights the gantry ahead of you and dims it behind, and does not need you at all.

Some travelers come out the far side of that into something they describe, without embarrassment, as peace — the idle of the world becoming their own. Others start wanting to stay, which is the same thing said differently.

So: **bring a journal and write in it daily** — it will be the only accumulating thing in the world, and that makes it precious. **Give yourself a mission** — survey a reservoir, time a licker's circuit against your watch, catalog the bell-calls. And above all, **fix your departure before you arrive, with a trigger you will obey** — a beacon check-in, a prearranged rendezvous, a date you have promised someone. The world will never remind you to leave. It has no future tense either. Your exit is the one event it cannot schedule, and you must supply it.

## Kit, condensed

Draining wading boots ×2, camp footwear, synthetic sun clothing, strapped hat, polarized glasses, light shell, wading staff, roll-top dry bags for everything, small inflatable raft with tether, hammock and drip tarp, headlamp with red mode, full rations plus reserve, condensate collection bottle, purifier, test strips, solar panel and battery, waterproof notebook and pencils, compass (verify it against the sun on day one — fields here are unread), repair kit with tape and cordage, foot-care kit, antiseptic and wound closure, antifungal, antihistamine, personal meds doubled, extraction beacon, one coin to lose.

## One last thing

At some point — it happens to everyone — you will stop, shin-deep in blood-warm water, fog burning off around you, a rain column walking somewhere far off, and watch a thousand animals hold still for the sunrise of a day identical to every day before it, and the word that arrives will not be *factory* or *machine* but *held*. The world holds itself in both hands, warm and humming, at an idle indistinguishable from peace. Prepare your body for the water. Prepare your mind for the stillness. And prepare to leave exactly, exactly on time — while telling no one, not even yourself, whether you wanted to.
