# Preparing for the Throat (Nineflute)

## First, understand what you are visiting

This is not a ruin and it is not a natural wonder. It is a kept place — a working instrument, waterworks, farm, and animal city that happens to be shaped like a hole in the ground. Two keepers live in it at any time, rotated by the moon from the hamlets a day's walk back, and everything inside it — the water, the guano, the honey, even the sound — is someone's economy and someone's custom. You are not an explorer here. You are a guest in a house that is also a flock, a hive, a weather station, and a slow-motion cave. Prepare accordingly: half pilgrim, half farmhand, no part tourist.

## Before the plain: stop in the hamlets

Do not walk straight to the rim unannounced. Present yourself in the hamlets first. The keepers come from these villages, the harvests go to these villages, and a stranger appearing at the parapet without warning is bad manners at best.

- Say plainly that you are a traveler from very far away and wish to visit the Throat. This will not be the strangest thing they have heard; the place itself is stranger.
- Ask who is currently down, and when the moon-change is. Arriving near the rotation is ideal — you may be able to walk out with the incoming keeper, which solves your guide problem and your introduction problem at once.
- Learn the names. The hamlets say **the Throat**; the keepers sometimes use the older name, **Nineflute**. Use "the Throat" until a keeper uses Nineflute first, then follow their lead. Names here are rank and relationship, not trivia.
- Learn the sayings, because the sayings are the operating manual: *when the Throat sings low, tie the goats*; the first two echoes are yours and the third belongs to the Throat; never count the bats aloud. If you know these before you arrive, you will be read as someone who listens.
- Bring gifts that fit the place's metabolism: **lamp oil** above all (the Lamp Walk has thousands of cups), also wax, salt, rope, cloth, and news. Do not bring honey — they have it. Do not bring trinkets from other worlds that light up, beep, or gleam unnaturally; the Throat runs on flame, lime, and pitch, and anomalous objects will make you memorable in the wrong way.

## The walk across the plain

The plain gives you nothing to see and that is the point: you will navigate by ear. Get a goatherd to point you at the chord before you leave, and ask them to hum the two pitches — the ordinary chord and the low one, the rain note. Memorize both.

- Carry **all your own water**, there and back, plus a reserve. This is the single most important packing decision. The Throat's water is measured income, drip by drip, recorded on a notched staff. Arriving thirsty and drinking a family's wages is the one truly ugly thing a guest can do.
- Walk at dawn. The country is a griddle; there is no shade between the hamlets and the one half-dead thorn tree at the rim. Hat, long light clothing, dust scarf.
- Navigate toward the chord. It turns as the wind turns, swells and thins; trust it. The first visual confirmation will be the gray wheel of pigeons and swifts rising from nothing, and then the tree.
- **If the chord slides down toward the rain note while you walk**, take it seriously. Rain is rare, hard, and changes everything: the plain can sheet with water, and inside, the sluices are thrown and the whole place roars for a night. It is a privilege to witness and a bad time to be deep underground without a keeper's explicit instruction. When the Throat sings low, tie your plans down.

## What to carry, what to leave

Carry:
- Your water, a cup for accepting what is offered, simple food.
- Soft, grippy shoes. The stair is brick-on-edge worn into shallow dishes by centuries of feet and paws, and the deep floors carry flowstone slick.
- Washable clothes you don't love. You will be shat on in the Dove Ring. This is not a risk, it is a certainty; the droppings are also the dry-month harvest, so consider it a contribution.
- A warm layer for the rim at night — the plain cools after dark. The interior needs nothing: it holds a steady blood-warmth year-round, and you will sweat down there even in winter.
- A kerchief or mask if your lungs are touchy: lime dust, down, dried guano. The nine flutes keep the air moving, but the Dove Ring's air is thick and old.
- A small lamp or candle for the rim — with the understanding that it goes out at full dark like every other flame in the place.
- Your bee-sting remedy if you are allergic. The colony behind the wax-seamed brick on the Lamp Walk is older than anyone's memory, and the hamlets are a day away.
- A notebook. Sketching is slow and therefore welcome. Ask before measuring anything — measurement is the keepers' liturgy, and a stranger with a plumb line is making a claim.

Leave behind:
- **Your cat.** No cats below the rim; this is law, not preference.
- Any whistle, and anything that shrieks, chirps, or alarms. A whistle below the Lamp Walk is the alarm note and will empty three galleries in a panic of wings.
- Your hurry. Leave it on the plain; it will not fit down the stair.

And bring **a song**. One you can sustain, looped, for twenty minutes without embarrassment. It does not need to be good. It needs to be continuous. Humming is acceptable. This is your passport, your lantern, and your courtesy, all in one.

## The laws of the house

Learn these as law, because they are:

- **Call down before descending.** Wait for the answer.
- **Descend singing.** The birds shift around a known, announced presence, and the keepers always know where a singing person is. Silence below is not politeness; it is ambiguity.
- **No whistling below the Lamp Walk.**
- **Lamps out by full dark.** The moths' hour belongs to the geckos, and the Throat sleeps in its own voice, not yours. Plan your movements by the light blades; ask the keeper their schedule; be where you intend to be before dark, because the dark down there is total.
- **A shout returns three times.** The first two echoes are yours. The third belongs to the Throat, and should be answered politely — a word, a nod aloud. It will feel strange the first time and natural by the third.
- **Never count the bats aloud.** Watch the dusk exodus all you like; keep the numbers off your tongue.
- **The snake keeps its ledge.** At the Sump, stay off the smooth ledge above the waterline. The well-snake takes a nestling now and then and is tolerated — is even left offerings — because something has to keep the doves honest. It is not your enemy and it is not your spectacle. If you wish to leave the customary offering, ask the keeper how.
- **Take nothing.** No egg, no feather from a nest, no brick chip, no cup of water unoffered, no touch of the drip basins — each rings at its own pitch and the keepers know the notes the way shepherds know bells; a moved basin is a broken instrument and a falsified record. The three harvests go by rule and by turn, and you are neither.
- **If a brick clicks down near you, don't touch it.** Tell the keeper. A fallen brick is relaid before dark, in the same bond, by skilled hands — that is the oldest law, and the repair is not your work.
- **Stay on the scraped walkways.** The keepers clear the paths and leave the rest, because the building is eroding into a fossil of its own materials and decay and deposition have come to an agreement. Your boot is not part of that agreement.

## The descent, level by level

**The rim.** The parapet is whitewashed soft at the corners — do not lean on it. The rope grooves in the capping stones are centuries deep; loads and people have gone over that edge on rope for longer than the hamlets remember. Look down once, carefully: the square of daylight drops farther than you believe, bouncing off lime. The nine flutes stand around you, each a different voice; notice how the chord wobbles when the birds shift below. The building is listening to itself. You are standing in its ear.

**The stair.** Slow. Hand near the wall but gentle — the plaster is stratigraphy, layer on layer, and every layer is studded with palm prints. You are touching centuries of hands. You will find one that fits yours. This is normal. It is the place's way.

**The Dove Ring.** The density declares itself here: niches floor to ceiling, thousands, all occupied, nests generations deep. The cooing is continuous and *physical* — a pressure on the chest, not a sound you hear so much as a medium you wade in. Keep singing and the birds will part around you like water around a post, a rolling soft thunder of wings. Don't put your fingers in the niches. Don't touch eggs or squabs. Accept the droppings with grace.

**The Lamp Walk.** Thousands of blackened oil-lamp cups; the hum of the bees behind the wax-seamed brick; the air faintly tasting of honey. Walk past the seam calmly. At dusk the geckos come out onto the plaster, pale and deliberate, to wait by the lamp niches for moths; crickets hold the dark seams; spiders string the drip-lines. You are a guest at their shift change. Then lamps out.

**The bat gallery and the dusk column.** You likely won't enter the deepest gallery — it is the bats'. At dusk they leave in a single exodus up the light well while the swifts come screaming home down the same column, the two traffics threading each other, owls at the rim taking the late and the slow. **Do not stand in the column.** Watch from where the keeper stands. It is one of the great traffic interchanges of any world, and it is not for your body.

**The Sump.** Permanent dusk, jar-lined black water, pale fish whose origin story varies by hamlet. The smooth ledge above the waterline is the snake's — give it the room custom gives it. If the keeper drops the depth-stone and counts, be silent: that is the year being judged. And if you are permitted to be there for the one midday hour when a shaft of sun reaches all the way down to the Sump's rim — the only light the pale fish ever see — say nothing at all. Some things are for witnessing.

## The hours and the year

- **Dawn:** the Throat exhales — warm damp air rising, mist standing over the rim, swifts pouring out. Be on the rim for it.
- **Midday:** stillness; the blades of sun creep across the galleries at appointed hours. The keepers time their rounds by them — second blade on the pigeon stair means midday. Ask to be taught the blades; it is the only clock that works down there.
- **Dusk:** the inhale, the bats, the swifts, the owls, the geckos' shift beginning. Then lamps out, and the Throat sleeping in its own voice — the drips ticking their soft polyrhythm into the basins, never repeating, never silent.
- **The year:** honey after the thorn scrub blooms; guano in the dry month (busy — either avoid or come ready to carry); the roar of the rains a few times a decade, unplannable. If you luck into the roar, you will be told where to stand, and you will obey instantly and without negotiation.

Two days is the right visit. One day is a taste. Three, and the keepers may put you to work — carrying, scraping walkways — which is an honor, and you should accept it. Do not touch the masonry.

## Risks, plainly

The plain: heat, distance, no water, no landmarks but sound and one tree. Tell someone your plan. The stair: dished, slick, and a turned ankle is a day from help — slow feet, song, hand near the wall. The air: thick with dust and down; mask if sensitive, and don't stir anything. The dark after lamps-out: absolute; do not be mid-stair. Falling brick: rare, announced by a click; a hat is not a foolish idea. The snake, the bees, the bats: all governed by the rules above, all fine if you are fine. Vertigo at the rim and in the light well: the bouncing daylight can pull at you; sit rather than stand at edges.

## What it will do to you

You have presumably seen stranger places. This one will still recalibrate you. The sound will colonize your body — cooing, drips, the chord overhead, folded by the shaft into a single long thought — and for days afterward your pulse will keep the drip rhythm. The handprints will follow you: centuries of palms pressed into plaster, the galleries crowded with people no longer alive, and the strange warmth of being among them. And the *fit* of things will unsettle you before it comforts you — every hollow tenanted, every surface assigned, the flutes' pitches sitting exactly where the pigeons' coo reinforces them — until you feel yourself shrink to your correct size: one more niche-occupant, a little larger and a little more anxious than the doves.

That is the right size to feel. The Throat was made for water and the water left, and it did not die of it — it settled, became warm and loud and crowded and kept. It keeps its own time, and the time is slow, and it is not running out. The only real preparation is to arrive willing to move at that speed, to sing your way down, to answer the third echo politely, and to leave with nothing but the sound in your chest and, if you are very lucky, a keeper's nod at the rim.
