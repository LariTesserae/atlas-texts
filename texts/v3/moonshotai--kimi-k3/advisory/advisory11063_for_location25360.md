# Traveler's Preparation Guide: The Plateau (Vess and environs)

*Classification: settled world, late-stage; metaphysical hazard class — gentle. Lethality: near zero. Retention rate among visitors: poor. Read the whole document.*

---

## 1. What You Are Walking Into

Most dangerous places want to kill you. The Plateau wants nothing from you at all, and that is precisely the problem. Understand the hazard profile before anything else:

- **No predators, no pathogens, no parasites.** Nothing here has ever eaten anything. Your first-aid kit is for falls, burns, and altitude — not bites, not infection.
- **No weather violence.** The wind has no voice. Rain is rare and brief. You will not be stormed off this mountain.
- **The hazards are temporal, topological, cognitive, and metaphysical.** Roads that return you to your origin aged by the walk. Water that shows you the future. Sound that never dies, only travels. And a world-scale patience that will, without malice, without even urgency, invite you to stop being a visitor.

Nothing on the Plateau will hurry you. That is the threat. Everything else in this document is details.

## 2. Who Should Not Go

Do not visit the Plateau if you are between homes, freshly bereaved, recently heartbroken, or at loose ends in your own world. The Plateau specializes in the unfinished — it is gentle with them, it says so itself — and if your ties elsewhere are thin, you will experience the bench in the ninth court not as a curiosity but as an answer.

Come when your home life is strong enough to pull you back. This is not a metaphor. It is the single most important piece of safety equipment you will bring, and it cannot be packed.

## 3. Months Before: The Contract With Yourself

**The Rule of the Door.** Fix your exit before you arrive, and fix it *externally* — a scheduled recall, a beacon a friend holds the key to, a door that opens on a date whether or not you ask it to. Do not rely on deciding to leave. Decisions on the Plateau get slow. The world will not stop you from going; it hurries nothing, including your departure. It will simply make leaving feel like a young world's haste, and one season you will agree with it, and that will be that.

Set a dead-man's check-in if your transit tech allows one: if you do not signal every N days, the door comes and gets you.

**Choose your dates.** The Audible — the fortnight at the thaw, when the ice and stone release a thousand years of stored sound and the Greening takes a century's growth in weeks — is the great spectacle and the best first-visit window. Plan to arrive several weeks *before* the thaw, learn the city's habits while it is quiet, and decide in advance whether you depart as the fog tide rises or shelter through it inside Vess (the city is built for it; the city drinks fog). Both are defensible. Drifting into a third option is not.

**Choose your companion.** Pairs minimum; a trio is better, so that one of you can be fast while two are slow, rotating. Solo travel here is not forbidden. It is simply indistinguishable, eventually, from residency.

Before departure, make the pact: either of you may speak **the Word** — choose it now, an actual word, agreed and written down — and within three days you both leave, no debate, no appeal, one invocation each per trip. One caution: sound is conserved on the Plateau, and a thing spoken is in transit forever. Your safety word will outlive your species there. Choose a word you would not mind hanging in that air for ten thousand years. Some parties use "anchor." Some use "home." Choose kindly.

**Choose your sound-gift.** More on this below, but the decision belongs in the planning phase: you will want to speak one thing, once, deliberately, in a Syllable Court, as rent. Pick a poem, a song, a sentence that *deserves* to still be crossing a plaza when your grandchildren's grandchildren are dust. You are donating to the longest archive in the known multiverse. Do not bring a jingle.

**Get your lungs ready.** The air up there thins to a blade. Train at altitude if you can. And plan to take the climb slowly — see Section 5.

**Put your affairs in order.** Not because you will die — you almost certainly will not — but because the distribution of return dates is wide and includes "never, and happy about it." Write the letters. Tell people where you are going and what it means if you send word that you are staying one more season. (It means: invoke the Word on my behalf. Come get me.)

## 4. The Kit

**The Rule of the Sterile Sole.** Before anything goes in the bag: clean everything. Boot soles, tent seams, cuffs, pack linings. No seeds, no spores, no insect eggs, no soil. The Plateau has never had a flower. It has never had a second animal species. A hitchhiking annual in your boot mud would be the first courtship-colored thing in world history; an earthworm would be an epoch. Arrive as close to sterile as a living human can — which is not very, admittedly; you are a walking contamination event of fast microbes, and the world will absorb what you shed into its archive without complaint. But minimize. Planetary-protection standards. Treat it as the respect it is.

**Warmth.** The cold here is finished — it does not bite or snatch; it abides. This matters practically: it will not trigger your alarms. There is no windburn, no sting, no dramatic shivering fit to warn you. Hypothermia on the Plateau arrives the way everything arrives — politely — and its first symptom is *agreement*. You will simply, gradually, consent to the temperature. Pack expedition-grade insulation rated far below what the honest daytime sun suggests, and double your ground insulation: the stones gave up resisting the cold long ago and will patiently accept your body heat all night without ever warming or giving it back. Never sleep directly on stone. Chemistry is allowed on this world — lichen colors are chemistry, and chemistry passes — so chemical heat packs work and are, metaphysically speaking, legal tender. Bring many.

Watch each other for the Agreement: slowed speech, contentment with sitting, "I'm fine, just resting." Treat it as hypothermia until proven otherwise. And note, for later sections, that the Settling and hypothermia are the same gesture at two different scales. Both feel like rest.

**Food: the Rule of the Carried Calorie.** Bring every calorie. All of them, plus half again. There is no foraging — there is no food *web*; nothing has ever been eaten, and the moss banks and cushion-domes you will see are not vegetables, they are citizens, some of them ten thousand years old and hard as masonry besides. Do not be the first mouth in the history of a world. An act on the Plateau behaves like a sound: it does not end, it transits. You do not want "eating" to be the thing you introduced, replayed at some thaw a thousand years hence, faint as frost and unmistakable.

The margin matters more than you think, because itineraries mean little here (Section 9). For any walk longer than a day, fix your turnaround by *time*, not distance: "if not arrived by day four, we reverse, whatever we see." Distance lies on the Plateau. Your clock doesn't — probably — and in any case your clock is what you have.

**Water.** Carry a personal fog-comb: a grooved plate or fine mesh that condenses the mist, plus bottles. The world is damp rather than wet, and you can live off combed condensation exactly as the city does. Details in Section 7.

**Time.** Bring several clocks, mechanical and chemical, synchronized at arrival, plus one with an alarm you cannot sleep through and one that vibrates against the skin. These are not conveniences. They are your tether to fast time. Wind them religiously. The day you stop winding them is a data point, and not a good one.

**Fire.** Leave the white-gas roaring stove at home, or keep it holstered for true emergency. A loud fast fire on the Plateau is a shout in a library. Instead bring a small peat burner: peat is the sanctioned local fuel, cut from the deep moss banks, slow and smoky and smelling green. You will be burning ten-thousand-year growth — a library, combusted at library speed, with permission. Keep fires small and grateful.

**Light and eyes.** Glacier glasses — the sun is honest and the air is thin. Headlamps for dusk. But understand the curfew: the fog tide rises at dusk, and you should be under shelter when it does (Section 9).

**Recording.** A camera is fine — it is quiet, and someone at home will want proof of the Sleepers on the horizon taking the sun on their flanks. Know its limits: your camera captures the present, which is the least informative tense on the Plateau. The Syllable Courts keep the past; the tarns keep the future. Bring a journal regardless — paper, pencil, date-stamped entries, *daily, without exception*. The journal is your second tether. If your entries lengthen and your dates begin to blur, you are looking at the earliest symptom of the only serious disease this world offers.

**What not to bring.** No firearms (nothing to hunt, nothing to fear; a gunshot would be the loudest act of immodesty in recorded history, and *all* history here is recorded, in transit, forever). No drones — the sky has never had a voice, and you do not want to know what happens when it gets one. No pets, no plants, no musical instruments you are unwilling to donate to eternity. Radios and electronics: minimal, inside the tent, batteries kept warm.

## 5. The Approach

**The Rule of the Climb.** However your multiverse transit works, do not shortcut to the top. Take the climb, up out of the fog sea, over several days, arriving in the hour before dawn. The climb is the world's airlock: your lungs acclimate, and more importantly your *pace* acclimates. Days in the white slow fog strip the speed off you gently, so that when the climb levels and the Plateau receives you — without surprise, the way a mountain receives weather — you arrive already half-converted to local time. Travelers who portal directly into a plaza report a brutal first week: the silence ringing, the footsteps obscene, the heartbeat rude. The climb does that work in advance.

Navigation on the approach: keep rising ground to hand, carry an altimeter, and know the law. All downhill roads loop — walk one long enough and you will see your own starting point ahead of you, weathered by the years you spent. That is settled law. The breakdown is that lately some uphill roads loop too, which is the Unsettling reaching into the one reliable direction. On the climb this is still rare. Note your landmarks anyway.

**Absolute: never descend recreationally.** No one has returned from the fog sea to say what is below — whether there is a base, a lowland, an ocean, a bottom of any kind. The Plateau may be the whole world. Treat the sea as you would treat open ocean from a life raft: it is not scenery, it is the thing that does not give you back.

## 6. First Week: Campcraft on a Finished World

Find shelter in Vess. You will not be greeted at a gate; the Slow Ones tend the city but on timescales that make "hospitality" the wrong word — one of them has been raising an arm toward a comb-vane for what you would call a decade. Instead do this: walk the courts until you find a space that feels like it has been expecting you. It has. Goods on the Plateau await their final owners; shelter awaits its occupants; the markets are full and have never been busy. Take the rooms that fit you. Leave something that will wait well for the next occupant — a ceramic, a coil of good rope, nothing with moving parts.

Set up your camp with the fast things inside it: clocks, stove, food, journal. Keep the tent threshold clean. Establish, on day one, the two practices that will keep you free:

**The Daily Fast Hour.** One hour per day of deliberate speed: brisk calisthenics, quick conversation, a rapid card game, a brisk walk around the court. You are not exercising your body; you are exercising your native tempo, keeping it limber the way a diver keeps lungs. Skip it and you will not notice the difference for a while. That is exactly how the difference happens.

**The Winding.** Wind the clocks. Stamp the journal. Read yesterday's entry aloud *inside the tent, softly* — being read to in your own voice is a way of remembering who is fast here. (Softly, because even this will transit. A murmured date, arriving somewhere a millennium from now, faint as frost: there are worse legacies.)

Your footsteps will begin to sound immodest within the week. This is normal and is the first sign that you are hearing correctly. Let your gait soften. Walk as weather walks: pass through, water the moss a little, do not try to become climate.

## 7. Water, Food, Waste

**The Rule of Two Waters.** On the Plateau, water comes in two kinds: water in transit, and finished water. Drink the first. The condensate running down the comb-grooves, your own fog-comb's yield, meltwater on the move — this is water still going about its business, and it is clean beyond anything in your home world; there is nothing alive in it to hurt you.

Finished water — the black tarns cupped in the oldest stone, the still cistern deeps — is not beverage. It is *scripture*. Finished water remembers forward: it reflects the world not as it is but as it will have settled. The tarn above the city has been showing the towers leaning inward to meet in a dome for longer than your language has existed, and lately it shows other things. Drinking it is not known to be fatal. Assume, until shown otherwise, that it is a way of internalizing the world's memory of the future — that water which remembers forward will remember *you* forward — and treat every still black surface as a library you are not yet fluent enough to read. Section 13 covers looking. For drinking: moving water only.

**Food** is what you brought. Cook on the small peat fire. Cut peat from the deep banks, not the cushion-domes — the domes are single plants, citizens, and walking across them is permitted (they do not mind; minding is a fast emotion) but cutting them is not. Take peat sparingly and with something like thanks; you are burning strata.

**Waste: pack out everything.** Everything. Nothing rots here; rot is a young world's haste, and this world graduates its fallen things into stone that remembers grain. Your buried waste would not disappear — it would *enter the archive*, mineralized, permanent, the first dung in world history, studied by whomever the future sends. Wag bags. Sealed containers. It all goes home with you. You are the only source of refuse this world has ever had; do not let it be remembered for that.

## 8. Sound Etiquette

Sound moves slowly here and is never lost — only in transit. The full name of Vess was spoken once, at the founding, and is *still arriving*. Practical rules:

- **Speak little, and deliberately.** Every word you utter becomes a permanent public object, crossing the world for ages. Do not say anything here you would not want landing on a plaza, faint but intact, a thousand winters from now. Complaints, gossip, idle curses — leave them at home. You will find, within days, that this is less a restriction than a relief.
- **Never hum absentmindedly.** A tune you love, released carelessly, will outlive your species. If you make music — and at least once, you should — make it on purpose.
- **The One Gift.** Once, mid-visit, go to a Syllable Court — the amphitheaters shaped like storage jars, built to catch and hold what passes through — and speak the thing you brought. This is rent. The world conserves sound; give it a good one.
- **Listening.** The courts are the world's archive read aloud. Stand in the right one and you may hear the next syllable of a sentence that began before your language existed. Budget real time for this. It is the closest thing to conversation with the deep past that the multiverse offers, and it costs nothing but stillness — which, on the Plateau, is the only currency, and the one you are trying not to spend too much of. Alternate with your companion: one listens, one keeps the clock.

## 9. Moving Around

**Distance is a mood.** Heth, the sister city, is a year away at noon and a week away at dusk, and the builders' maps are not wrong so much as hopeful. Treat all maps as prayers. Practical consequences:

- Long journeys belong to dusk, when distance shortens — but dusk is also when the fog tide rises and the world becomes an archipelago of white silence and dark stone. Do not attempt dusk-road travel on a first visit except on the established builder roads, with your companion, with turnaround times fixed in advance. Experienced travelers depart at dusk deliberately and accept the fog. You are not yet experienced.
- **Fog tide curfew:** when the tide rises, be under shelter. If caught out: stop. Do not walk in fog on this world — walking blind here is how people discover the loops, and the loops charge in years. Shelter in place, tend the small fire, wait for the tide to fall. A whistle is worth carrying for companion signaling, but understand: your whistle's note may hang in transit for centuries, and the Slow Ones may hear it next year or next epoch. It is not a rescue device. Not getting lost is the rescue device.
- **Sun and shadow** are honest in settled country — the light here is ordinary, bread-normal, and you may navigate by it. But where stones are warm, shadows have been seen pointing at where the sun was *yesterday*. See Section 12: in unsettled ground, distrust your eyes and trust your turnaround time.

Do not attempt Heth on a first visit. A week at dusk is still seven fog tides.

## 10. The Citizens and the Guests

The census of the Plateau is short and very old, and most of it is not shaped like you.

**The stands** — groves of forty black-barked trunks — are each a single individual. One citizen, repeated forty times. Walk among them as you would walk through one person's many rooms.

**The cushion-domes**, house-sized, ten thousand years old, you may walk across; they do not mind. Tread as on a neighbor's roof.

**The Sleepers** — the smooth domes on the horizon and sometimes much closer, in herds and congregations — are each *someone*. The builders settled, and some settled into the land. Once an age the ground turns over in its sleep and a slope reshapes itself and a tarn is born where a shoulder pressed down, and it is not a disaster; it is a dream. Practical corollary: camp on stone, not on domes, and never cut peat or light a fire on a slope that might be somebody. The simplest rule that covers all of it: **treat the entire world as an inhabited house.** You are a guest inside someone's final form.

**The Slow Ones** are grey-green, lichened to the elbows, utterly unhurried, and the only visible tenders of the city. Do not expect conversation. If you want communion, budget it honestly: sit with one for a full season and it may *begin* to turn toward you, and by the time it faces you, you will have decided whether you are staying — which is to say, sitting with a Slow One is not an activity, it is a *decision*, and should be time-boxed like one. If a Slow One appears to be offering you something, understand that it may have begun the offering before you were born, and that accepting is measured in seasons. On a first visit: incline your head, slowly, and move on.

If you want to greet the *city* — and you will, it has a habit of itself and the habit is hospitable — do maintenance. Clear a comb-groove of rime. Carry peat to a cistern house. Oil a bronze gate-pivot. Work slowly and well for an afternoon. This is legible to Vess in a way words are not. But here is the warning that matters: **do not, on a first visit, take up any task that needs doing daily.** Daily tasks are how the city adopts people. The combs will condense without you; the habit ran for ages before you and will not fail for want of one traveler. Help as a guest helps — once, generously — and then stop.

## 11. The Audible

If you timed your visit for the thaw fortnight, here is what to expect: winter locks sound into ice and stone, and the thaw lets it out. Bell-notes struck a thousand years ago land soft as snow on the plazas. Chisel-taps arrive from a quarry finished ages ago. The combs drip, which counts here as percussion. A sentence in a Syllable Court advances by one syllable and the Slow Ones, who have been waiting since autumn, incline their heads.

The Audible overlaps the Greening — a century's growth in a few weeks, the moss and domes and stands humming with almosts — and it ends with the fog tide rising to stay, the world gone to white silence and dark islands.

Guidance: be out in it every day; it is one of the great things the multiverse offers. Keep the Fast Hour anyway — especially during the Audible, because the temptation to spend all day sitting and listening is the strongest you will have felt yet, and it does not feel like temptation. It feels like sanity. Keep the clock-keeper rotation strict. And when the fog tide begins its rise, execute the plan you made at home: depart, or button up inside Vess and watch the city become an island, the Sleepers taking the last sun on their flanks like a herd at rest. Both choices are good. Making neither, and drifting, is the only bad one.

## 12. The Unsettling: Field Guide

You will notice it eventually; better to be told. The Settling — the world-law that draws everything toward its final form — finished, once. The world reached its end-state. And then, quietly, the finishing began to come undone. Learn the signs:

- **Warm stone** in the cold. The first symptom ever noticed. If a boulder is warm, you are in young ground.
- **Un-cracking walls** — fissures closing, blocks going sharp-edged and young.
- **Young rock**, fresh-faced, set in the oldest pavement on the world. It will cut your boots and your feet; the rest of the Plateau is worn smoother than your gear expects, so in young zones reverse your assumptions.
- **Doorways remembering.** A wall solid for six thousand years may wake one foggy morning and recall it was a doorway. Do not sleep against interior walls of ancient sealed structures; do not stand in any opening that looks *newer than its building*.
- **Rain falling upward** near the young places. Pack gaiters; expect to get wet from below, which your instincts will insist is impossible.
- **Wrong shadows**, pointing at yesterday's sun. Navigation by light is void in these zones.

**The Rule of the Mid-Stroke Tool.** You will find artifacts left at the instant their owners settled — a mason's chisel mid-stroke on a half-dressed block, more patina than bronze and *still sharp*. Do not touch them. In a settled world, tools are retired; in an unsettling one, they re-enlist, and some tools seem to refuse rest because their final form is *use*. Treat every artifact as potentially loaded — not with malice, but with unfinished purpose. Finished goods in the markets may be taken if they plainly awaited you; anything mid-act, warm, sharp, or strangely patient stays where it lies.

Is the Unsettling dangerous? Not violently. Nothing here is violent. But young ground is *unpredictable* ground, the one thing the rest of the Plateau never is. Note warm zones on your hopeful map, report them by quiet note left in the market (writing stays where you put it, which on this world makes it the fast channel), and route around.

One more thing, and it is heresy, and you deserve to hear it from a document rather than from a black tarn at dusk: the tarns have begun showing the city's combs crowned in green fire, streets that do not exist yet, and — some Slow Ones say, though no one will say it loudly, because a thing said is in transit forever — *people*. The world was a sentence spoken so slowly it hardened into a place, and the sentence has noticed it is not finished, and the forward-memory of the world appears to include visitors. You are not a neutral observer here; you never were. The Plateau expected you the way a mountain expects weather: eventually, and without opinion. Do not flatter yourself that you are the cause — weather is not the cause of mountains — but do not pretend you are invisible, either.

## 13. The Tarns

Finished water remembers forward. You will want to look; looking is, arguably, part of why anyone comes. Follow the eclipse protocol:

- **Briefly, obliquely, never alone.** Your companion holds your sleeve and the clock. Ten breaths, then out.
- **Never at dusk.** Fog tide plus oracle plus failing light is how travelers go missing into their own reflections.
- **Never the tarn above the city on your final day.** It shows the towers meeting into the dome — the city's final form — and lately more. If it shows *you* among the unbuilt streets, invoke the Word and go. Being seen in the forward-memory means the future has already found a place for you, and from that moment your exit is no longer entirely your own.

Why not look too long? No one will confirm the mechanism; sound is conserved and the Slow Ones keep their ledgers across centuries. Travelers report two things: time slips — you surface from a still black pool missing an afternoon — and a subtler effect, a sense afterward that your own final form is a thing already decided and merely en route, like the rest of Vess's name. Both are consistent with the settled laws. Both are bad for visitors.

## 14. The Real Danger

Here is the section the whole document exists for.

Everything on the Plateau is drawn toward its final form, and you are not exempt — you are only *slow to notice*, at first, because your own world's haste is still ringing in you. It fades. Your footsteps quiet. The word "immodest" enters your vocabulary without irony. You begin planning in seasons. The quiet stops being an absence and becomes the medium, and your own heartbeat at night starts to sound rude, and some evening, watching the fog tide rise around the comb-towers, it will occur to you that the bench in the ninth court — worn smooth, facing the combs, waiting a long time and not particular — is *reasonable*.

That thought is the symptom. By the time it feels reasonable, you are well along.

Keep the practices: the Fast Hour, the Winding, the date-stamped journal, the clock-keeper rotation, the Word. Watch your companion for the Agreement and let them watch you. The symptoms of onset settling are: unwound clocks; lengthening journal entries with blurring dates; tasks taken up daily; sitting with the Slow Ones "just for a while"; referring to departure as "hasty"; finding the bench reasonable.

And about the bench: sit on it. Truly. Schedule a dusk for it near your visit's end — this is the capstone of the whole journey and one of the genuinely great experiences available to a traveler anywhere: the long level light on the moss, the cushion-domes glowing like green coals, the fog tide rising until the city is an island in a white sea, the Sleepers taking the sun on their flanks, and somewhere a bell-note landing, faint as frost, struck before your grandmother's grandmother. Set the vibrating alarm. Give your companion the standing-up job. Sit a while — and then stand up *when you planned to*, because the bench has been waiting a long time and is not particular, and what it is waiting for is whoever sits down.

The Plateau will not hurry you, and it will not trick you, and it will not take anything you do not give. It will simply wait beside you, patient as weather, until you choose your final form — and it will love, in its slow stone way, whatever you choose. That love is real, as far as anyone can tell. It is also not a reason.

## 15. A Sample First Season

- **Weeks 1–2:** The climb. Fog days. Arrive in the hour before dawn; watch the sun arrive normal as bread and let the ordinariness of the light teach you the first lesson — here, distrust the familiar-looking (a bench, a doorway, a still pool) and trust the slow.
- **Weeks 2–4:** Vess. Find your court. Establish the Fast Hour and the Winding. Learn the combs, the cisterns, the markets. Do one afternoon of maintenance. Speak your sound-gift in a Syllable Court on a still day. Walk the near stands; greet the Slow Ones with a slow nod and no expectations.
- **Weeks 5–7:** Short excursions — one day out, one day back, turnaround times fixed. The tarn above the city, once, with the eclipse protocol. A Sleepers' congregation viewed from bare stone. Map warm zones if you find them; leave notes in the market.
- **Weeks 8–9 (the thaw):** The Audible. Out every day; strict clock rotation; the Greening; the sentences advancing; the drip of the combs like percussion.
- **Week 10:** The bench, one scheduled dusk, alarm set, companion tasked. Then, as the fog tide rises: execute the exit you decided on at home.
- **Total elapsed:** under one season. Second visits may be longer. Anyone planning "a year, minimum" on a first trip should skip ahead to the next section and be honest about which trip they are actually taking.

## 16. If You Stay

Some do, and the guide's duty is to be honest: staying is not dying. Nothing here rots; things graduate. A human who settles on the Plateau would not molder — they would mineralize, slowly join the geology, become a stone that remembers grain, or a straightness in a wall, or, given time and the world's generosity with final forms, a dome on a horizon taking the sun. Whether a human can become a Sleeper is unknown; you would be among the first of your kind to try, though the tarns' new visions of *people* in the streets suggest you might not be the first, or not the last.

If you choose it — after your Fast Hours, after your companion has invoked the Word and you have overruled them, which is your right, once, with clear eyes — then choose *deliberately*. The builders settled into walls they loved, into slopes they loved. Find the wall you love. Find the bench, the court, the shoulder of ground. The choice of final form is yours, and the Plateau will love whatever you choose, so choose with your whole self, because your whole self is what is being chosen.

Send your companion home with your journals and your gear. Speak your farewell in a Syllable Court so it keeps arriving for a long time. And do not ask the document to bless the decision. The document only asks that it be a *decision*.

## 17. Leaving

Pack everything, including every gram of waste. Settle your rent — leave one gift in the market that will wait well for its final owner. Wind the clocks one last time and give one to the court that sheltered you; a ticking clock is a fast thing, but it is chemistry and mechanism, and chemistry is allowed, and some court-keeper in some future age may find comfort in a voice that ticks.

Depart at dawn. Do not look into the tarn on the way out — or look once, briefly, obliquely, with your companion holding your sleeve, and accept whatever it shows as the world's memory, not your orders.

At the threshold, where the climb drops into the fog, say thank you — aloud, once, plainly. Do not say a long goodbye; a farewell spoken here is in transit forever, which means a part of you will always be arriving on the Plateau. That is the correct way to leave this world: leave something arriving. The full name of Vess has been arriving since the founding; your thanks will be in good company, crossing the courts, one syllable per century, faint as frost and getting fainter, never lost.

Then go down into the fog by your own door, and do not descend the roads.

Go home, or stay — but choose. The one thing this world cannot do for you is hurry your choosing. That part, it insists gently, was always yours.
