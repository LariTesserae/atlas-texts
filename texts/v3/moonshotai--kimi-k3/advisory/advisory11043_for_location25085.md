# Preparing for the Rootplateau

Nothing on the Rootplateau will hunt you. There is nothing there that wants you dead, and that is precisely what makes it dangerous — because it means nothing will warn you, nothing will hesitate, and nothing will notice if you vanish. The dangers are six: the air, the water, the cold, the grey, the Revision, and the silence. And beneath all six runs a seventh thing, stranger than any of them: the plateau keeps what is left on it. Prepare for the six with gear and discipline. Prepare for the seventh with something more like a will.

This is what an ordinary human body and an ordinary human mind need to know.

## The Approach

Do not fly to the Lip if you can help it. The escarpment is not an obstacle; it is the preparation. The ascent is staged, and the stages are announced in sound, and you should camp at each announcement until your body stops complaining:

- **Camp where the insects go quiet.** Stay until your resting pulse, taken at the same hour each day, stops climbing.
- **Camp where the trickles stop.** The air is drying now; begin breathing through your reclamation mask at night so you stop paying water out into it.
- **Camp where the knock of your own gear stops returning to you.** This is the long stage. A week or more. Your lungs and blood are being renegotiated; let them finish.
- **Then the wind loses its voice.** It still comes, thin and fast and cold, but it slides past without remark. When you notice you can no longer hear it, you are days from the Lip.

Turn around if: headache that will not leave, breathlessness at rest, stumbling on flat ground, or a cough that sounds wet. The plateau will still be there next year. It has never once been in a hurry, and it will teach you not to be, either — the only question is whether you learn it on the way up or the hard way.

If you arrive by conveyance instead, use the moorings at the Lip. The fused-glass rings were sized for titanic tethers and have outlived their sea; they will hold whatever little ship you have. But you will still need to descend and climb back up slowly, or spend your first weeks on the plateau half-conscious. There is no cheating the air.

Be at the Lip at dawn at least once, and look down and west. The plateau lays its own shadow on the haze-sea — a dark slab the size of a continent — and it is the only time the place ever shows you its true shape. Everything after that is walking on the inside of it.

## The Body

**Air.** Thin enough that sound comes back flat, thick enough that you can learn to live in it. Carry bottled breath for your first weeks and a mask with a moisture-reclamation foil always. Two instruments you already own become essential: your pulse and your ears. Your pulse is the loudest thing you own up there, and it doubles as your altimeter and exertion gauge — learn your resting rate at each stage. Your ears will tell you nothing about the world, so repurpose them: they monitor *you*.

**Water.** There is no liquid phase on the plateau. This is the binding constraint of your entire journey — more than food, more than cold. Your plan has three legs:

1. **Carry ice, not water.** Nights are far below freezing; nothing melts. Ice is stable storage. To drink, warm small amounts in a black pouch laid on sunlit noon rock — the stone is warm to the hand even while the shade beside it freezes. The plateau will melt your water for you if you ask it by contact.
2. **Harvest the frost.** In certain basins — the frost gardens — hoarfrost condenses straight out of the thin air on certain dawns. Carry dark, thin radiative panels, thermally isolated from the ground, and set them out each night. Scrape at first grey light. You have hours, no more, because the bloom opens for the frost and drinks the year's only water by midmorning. Take your share from the edges. The bloom was keeping this calendar before your species had one, and everything alive up here depends on it. A guest does not drink a well dry.
3. **Follow the longwalkers at a distance.** They keep the frost calendar the way they keep everything — by hearing what you can only feel. A longwalker ambling with purpose at dusk is often aiming at a frost dawn. Do not chase it. Note its line, and be in that basin before first light.

Drink by schedule, never by thirst. Sweat flash-evaporates in that air; you will not feel yourself drying. Thirst lies. The fixed stars don't.

**Cold and heat.** Temperature on the plateau is local and tactile — thin air cannot hold heat, so the sun-warmed rock is warm under your palm while the shade beside it is freezing. Frostbite here is a *contact* injury: a bare hand on shaded stone, a knee rested on the wrong side of a spire. The ground is both your furnace and your freezer. Insulate from below, always — your sleep pad is more important than your tent. And learn the hour of kindness: for about an hour after dark, the stone gives the day back to you. Schedule your cooking, mending, and journal-keeping into that warm hour. Then the long night takes over.

At dusk the whole crust contracts and lets out one slow tick, felt through the soles of your feet, like a continent-sized house settling. That is your clock. When you feel the tick, the day is done. It is also your calibration: the tick is normal. A *hum* is not. Learn the difference in your bones the first week.

**Sun.** The light is the one ordinary thing there, but with almost no air to filter it and bone-gloss rock and salt pans returning it from below, it will burn you and blind you. Eye shades at all times in daylight; a veil; covered skin. And note the sky: so little air that a few steady stars hold at the zenith even at noon. Days that are night overhead. You will get used to it, and then you will miss it for the rest of your life.

**A warning about the pans.** The salts are pale oxidants, not sea-salt — there were never seas. Do not taste the flats. Do not let pan dust into food, eyes, or wounds, and do not make your bed in it. Carry your own salt. Nothing on the plateau is seasoning; it is chemistry.

## The Kit

Everything goes on your back, and everything you bring in, you plan to bring out. No pack animals — an off-world beast would suffer the air and the silence, and anything that dies up there gets *kept*. The list:

- **Boots with thin, insulated soles.** Your early-warning system arrives through your feet — the dusk tick, the deep slab-fractures, the hum that means walk. You must be able to feel the ground and not be frozen by it. This is the single most important item you own.
- **The resonator.** A tuning fork or slate you can press to your sternum. At your first Marker, put your palm flat on the warm black glass, feel the note it shares with every Marker on the plateau, and tune to it. Maps fail; the Markers don't. Whether the note can truly be followed across a month's emptiness or only confirms what the stars tell you — travelers argue. Learn it anyway. It is the plateau's one word, and you should know it.
- **A chart of the fixed zenith stars.** The one map that cannot be re-poured. Navigation and timekeeping both.
- **Frost panels, insulated ice flasks, black sun-pouch.** See above. Redundancy: two panels minimum. One cracked panel should never be able to kill you.
- **Low-profile shelter, no tall poles.** During the grey, you do not want to be the highest thing on a pan, and you do not want to be holding a lightning rod. Dust-sealed everything — the slow columns walk the flats for days, vast and translucent and silent, and the charged dust in them abrades seals, eyes, and lungs. You will see them coming from a day's walk away; step aside from their pilgrim path and let them pass.
- **Forty days of dense rations per Marker leg, plus reserve.** Do not eat the ashwort — it grows a fingernail's width per decade and is older than some of the civilizations whose cairns you'll pass; grazing it is vandalism against deep time. If you are blessed with a silk-fall, test the grey silk in small patience before you rely on it.
- **Paper journal, many pencils.** Not a recorder — you'll want to hear your own voice aloud, and batteries hate the cold, and anyway paper is the plateau's own medium. The only library up there is a house with two surviving pages. Copy them by hand.
- **A bell. Or a flute. Something.** This is medical equipment. See below.
- **A signal mirror and a length of cord.** Voices do not carry in that air; nothing returns them. If you travel with a companion, agree on visual signals and stay in sight. In the haze of a grey wall, cord yourselves together.
- **A letter from your lowland self.** Sealed. To be opened the first night you see lights on the horizon. More below.

## The Rules

The journal in the last house holds the only scripture the plateau has, and every line of it is operational. Here is each, translated into practice.

*Nothing echoes.* Sound comes back flat, local, apologetic — the air declines to be involved. Practically: no shouts for help, no voice navigation, no auditory warning of anything. You will also, within weeks, begin to hear things — music, your name, conversation. The human auditory system starved of input manufactures its own. Expect it. Name it when it happens: weather, not message. Mostly.

*Maps fail; the Markers don't.* The Markers stand a month's walk apart. That is the atomic unit of plateau travel: a forty-day leg between islands of absolute safety, across ground that a Revision can redraw overnight into something that looks *older* than before. Even change wears the mask of permanence here. Navigate by the fixed stars, the resonator, and the longwalker print-calendars. Treat every map, including your own, as a disposable hypothesis.

*Watch the thread for the grey.* The thread-lichen — black wire-filaments along the rock seams — rises slowly, like hair, as charge builds. That is your barometer, and it does not lie. When the thread lifts: get off the open pan if you can, drop your pack, sit on it (insulation between you and the ground), feet together, hood up, nothing tall above you, and wait. The walls move fast and the lightning inside them crawls and branches in deliberate angles, writing and erasing geometries in the murk — it seems occupied with its own inscription rather than with you, but do not offer it a reason to revise its priorities. Afterward the pans hold the charge for days and glow faintly at night; sleep on rock seams, not the flats. And watch the storm's wake for translucencies — the sails. A great storm may end in a silk-fall, and a silk-fall is the only abundance this world knows.

*Watch the longwalkers for the Revision.* This is the deepest rule on the list. The longwalkers hear the infrasound you can only feel, and they are simply never where a Revision is going to be. So read the pans like the calendars they are — prints a meter across persist for decades. Fresh prints mean the province is in use, which means it is safe by the best available intelligence. A pan with only ancient prints, or none, is a pan the longwalkers stopped crossing. Do not ask why with your body. Leave the way the prints leave. And walk *beside* the print-lines, never in them — you would be smudging a record the plateau itself is keeping.

*If the ground hums, walk.* The infrasound of a Revision builds for days — felt as objectless unease, as a hum in the teeth. (Travelers with old metal dental work report getting the news early, and have learned to be grateful.) Build a daily ritual: at every rest, palm flat to the stone, and at camp, one bare sole against the rock. Listen with your bones. If you feel the hum — do not run. You cannot run at that altitude, and you have days, not minutes. Break camp calmly and *walk*, every day, toward the nearest Marker, sleeping high, boots on. The Markers are never unmade. They stand untouched in their islands of swept ground while the world is re-poured around them. Get to one and you can watch the ground go fluent in perfect safety — spires subsiding, banding redrawn, matter persuaded without heat — and wake to a surface that looks older than the one you crossed. It will take you a while to decide how you feel about having watched that. Give it the time.

*The lights are not a city.* At night, a distant Revision shows on the horizon as a cold shimmer, exactly like city lights. After a month of silence and solitude, that shimmer will pull at you like grief, like rescue. Everyone walks toward one, once. So prepare in advance: write the letter to yourself before you ascend, and open it the first night you see the false cities. It should say, in your own hand, that the lights are not a city — and it should say honestly whether you are the kind of person who must go look anyway. If you are: go in daylight, with the resonator, tell your companion, and turn around at the first hum in your teeth. Watching a Revision from a safe ridge is one of the great sights of any world. Being inside one is not survivable, and the machine will not notice the difference between you and the mountains it is finishing.

*Leave nothing you do not want improved.* The machine keeps things, and it does not keep them as they were — it keeps them as they should have been. The Line, a visitor's groove, is re-cut after every Revision, straighter each time. Cairns left by the dead are copied — one becomes three becomes a row — each replica smoothed further toward some ideal. This is not a superstition; it is a documented behavior with millennia of evidence, and it dictates absolute discipline:

- **Cache nothing on the plateau.** If you must leave a reserve, leave it below the Lip, off the machine's grammar. A cache left up top may be improved — three caches where you left one, each edited toward the machine's idea of a cache. Do not build plans on what that means.
- **Carve nothing.** Your name will be iterated forever toward something that is no longer your name.
- **Build no cairns, leave no trash, bury nothing.** Pack everything out, down the escarpment. All of it.
- **Make the death pact before you ascend.** If you travel with others, agree explicitly: anyone who dies is carried below the Lip. Because the machine will keep a body, and improve it, and no one knows what a perfected person is — and the people who love you deserve to be the ones who decide how you are remembered. Discuss this on the lowland side of the escarpment, in the insect-noise, while everything is still easy. It will never be easy again.

And know the corollary: you cannot help leaving *something*. Your footprints go on the pans beside the longwalkers', and the plateau will keep them until they are perfect. The only choice you have is where you put them. Walk like someone leaving an inscription.

## The Mind

This is the preparation most travelers neglect, and the one the last house's first page is about. *We came to measure the silence. The silence is measuring back.*

Train in silence before you go — weeks of it, if you can, wilderness solo or anechoic retreat. The plateau's quiet has texture; it presses. Your pulse becomes the loudest thing you own, and then your blood, and then the manufactured sounds of a starving auditory system. Travelers who go unprepared spend their second month half-convinced the plateau is talking to them. Maybe it is. But you should know what *you* sound like first, so you can tell the difference.

Bring a companion if you possibly can. If you go alone, institute rituals of permitted sound: speak your log aloud at Marker camps, ring the bell at dusk after the tick, sing the day's walk while you walk it. The plateau permits few sounds; permit yourself those ones. This is not sentiment. It is hygiene.

Expect the feeling of being read. There is something hull-sized and slow that has spent a thousand years reading a half-absorbed lander, not dismantling it. The Markers all hum one note, one body asleep and dreaming storms. The crust is substrate — computation gone to stone — and you are walking on it, warm-blooded and brief and loud with pulse. You will feel noticed. Travelers split on whether this is the holiest thing they have ever felt or the most annihilating. Know which way you lean before you arrive, and hold the fact loosely. Guest, not surveyor. The last house came with instruments. Read their two pages, and note how many pages survived.

Set a return date and leave word below. The plateau's patience is contagious — each feature an event with a hundred kilometers of patience on either side — and travelers begin to linger, one more Marker, one more province, one more frost season. The machine finishes mountains, and finishing is infinite, and some part of you will want to stay and be finished too. Name that desire now, in the lowlands, so you recognize it when it arrives wearing the face of peace.

And prepare for the longwalker encounter, though you cannot schedule it. Four meters at the shoulder, hide like grey felt, feet as wide as shields, grazing ashwort so gently that a cushion outlives its grazer. They do not fear you. They will register you the way they register a Marker: furniture of the world. Do not touch, do not follow closely, do not perform. Just be there, being registered. Everyone says an encounter divides a life into before and after, and everyone is right, and no one can explain why. You will understand on the walk home, or you won't, and either way you will be quieter about it than you expect.

## The Shape of a Visit

**The short visit** — ascent, the Lip, the dawn shadow on the haze-sea, the moorings, descent. Two months. It is like reading one page of a book and putting it down forever. Legal, but strange.

**The true visit** — one full leg. Lip to Marker to Marker, or out and back: four months minimum. You will learn the note, sleep on swept ground beside warm black glass, feel the dusk tick through your soles until it becomes your pulse's twin, see the stars at noon, harvest frost at first light, read a pan's sixty years of crossings, and stand inside the plateau's actual life. This is the recommended minimum.

**The year** — keep the frost calendar with everything else alive. See the bloom open grey-green and faint gold for a few dawn hours. Ride out the grey with the thread-lichen as your oracle, and if you are blessed, walk a silk-fall: acres of grey silk dissolving in days, the bloom out of season, longwalkers who walked a month to be there — the only crowd this world has, a decade's eating in a week. Take from the edges. Yield the center. Witness it; it is the plateau's one festival. Make the pilgrimage to the last house: the pearly dome half-buried in a pan's edge, door open for millennia, the table, the bed of dust, the journal. Read the two pages. Copy the list into your own book by hand. Some travelers add one line of their own, meant for whoever comes next, knowing it may be improved. If you do, write something that deserves iteration.

Daily rhythm, wherever you are: wake before first light and check the panels. Walk the cold hours. Noon rest under the black day-sky; take your bearings from the zenith stars. Afternoon walk; camp before dusk. The tick. The hour of kindness for chores and journal. Palm to stone, listening. Sleep.

Etiquette, compressed: take nothing but copies. Leave nothing. Don't eat the ashwort. Don't drink a garden dry. Don't walk in the prints. Don't climb the spires — they are neither ruin nor growth, and they are not for you. Don't approach the reader at the lander; it has been at its work for a thousand years and has not once needed your help. Sleep near Markers when you can — the ground there is swept, warm, and safe — but not on the ashwort aureoles. And when you meet another traveler, which may happen once or never: share the note, share the news, share the fire. Out there, another human voice is a Marker too.

## Coming Home

Descend the way you came, slowly, and let sound return to you one thing at a time, the way it left. First the wind finds its voice again — you will stop walking and just stand in it. Then the knock of your own gear, astonishingly loud. Then a trickle of water, and this is where most travelers weep, sitting down right there on the escarpment, drinking with their faces wet. Then the insects, and the lowland haze, and the unbearable rich wet *noise* of the living world.

You will flinch at echoes for weeks — sound *returning*, from walls, from everything, the world shouting its shape back at itself constantly. You will wake on certain dawns before first light, thirsty, listening for a tick that isn't coming. You will speak more slowly. People will notice.

What you carry home: your journal, the copied list, and a life now divided into before and after. Nothing else. The silk won't keep, the stones were never yours, and the salt is poison.

What stays behind: your bootprints, somewhere on a pan, beside a longwalker's. The machine will keep them both. It will keep them until they are perfect — until they are what they should have been — which is never, which is the point. You went to the stillest place imaginable and it never once stopped moving, and now neither have you, and some small, glossed, improving version of your passing remains up there in the silence, being finished, forever.

Go as a guest. Walk quietly. And when you feel the note under your palm at the last Marker before the Lip — the one note all of them share, one body asleep, dreaming storms — take a moment before you start down. You're welcome back. The plateau is patient, and it already has your prints.
