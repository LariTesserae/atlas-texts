## First, understand what you are visiting

Before you pack anything, get the story straight, because your behavior depends on it. This is not a drowned village. Nobody died here. A landslip dammed the creek generations ago, the water rose over seasons — slowly enough to carry out the beams and the seed grain — and the people moved up the slope and then kept tending what they'd left. The lower village is not a ruin, not a grave, not a mistake. It is a garden, a workplace, and a calendar. The stone huts at the shore are called, without poetry, the landing. Learn that word and use it. Do not use the words *lost*, *sunken*, *drowned*, *haunted*, or — worst of all — *Atlantis*. Do not offer condolences. Nothing died here.

And understand the unit of time: not the hour, the breath. Everything you will be allowed to do is measured in how long you can hold yours.

## Choose your season

The year at the lake has four movements, and only one of them suits a visitor.

- **Melt and spate** (ice-out through early runoff): the ceiling breaks, the current runs through the chapel arch, and the great bronze bell swings and rings itself — a green note you feel in your breastbone before you hear it. This is the most magnificent time and the worst time to visit: the water is at its coldest and fastest, and the diving belongs entirely to the locals. If you are present for the first dive of the season, when the bell is rung by hand, you are a guest at a ceremony. Stand where you are put. Do not dive that morning unless asked.
- **The open season**, after the spate settles: this is when you go. Bright afternoons are best — the light turns the bowl green and readable, and the stonewort meadows pearl over with oxygen, beads rising in strings to slide along the ceiling like mercury. The gardens are open, the divers are working, and you can be useful.
- **Spawning season**: the mussel beds and meadows are closed, and at dusk the big charr gather over the meadows and drum — dozens of swim-bladders thrumming until the water seems like a drumhead being tuned. Worth hearing, but expect to do it from the shore or the surface. Closures are closures.
- **Winter**: do not go. The ceiling seals, the light goes gray-green, and the village belongs to the fish. There is no casual version of diving under ice.

Plan for a week: a day or two to walk in and acclimatize, several mornings of short work, and slack for weather.

## Train for the two hard things: cold and breath

You are an ordinary human. The water is snowmelt. These two facts must be reconciled before you arrive, not after.

**Cold.** Begin months ahead: cold showers, then outdoor swims in cold water, graduating your exposure. Learn your own warning signs — the "umbles" (fumble, mumble, stumble), slurred speech, the dangerous moment when shivering *stops*. Learn about afterdrop: your core temperature keeps falling after you exit, so you will come out, get horizontal, get wrapped in wool, and take a hot sweet drink before you feel like you need it. The locals grease their skin with fat and simply take the cold — and the cold, the text of this place is clear, narrows the mind to a point. They have trained into it since childhood. You have not.

**Breath.** Practice static breath-holds on dry land only, never alone in water. Learn a calm breathe-up of two or three full breaths — and then stop. Do not hyperventilate beyond that: it strips your body's warning system and is how visitors black out in three meters of water they could stand up in. Learn to equalize your ears; the bowl floor lies somewhere between four and nine meters down, shallow by diving standards, enough to hurt you if you can't. And take one rule as scripture: **never dive alone.** One person down, one person at the surface watching, every time, no exceptions for confidence.

Accept your real limit now: a working morning for a local diver is forty breaths — that finishes a panel of fish weir. Yours will be five or ten. That is not failure. That is the correct size of a guest.

## The walk in

The lake sits in a high green bowl, so treat the approach as an altitude hike: scree, wind-flattened grass, a few crouched pines at the tree line. Arrive a day early if you can, hydrate, go slowly on the rim, and get off the high ground before afternoon storms build.

Here is the thing to prepare for emotionally: from the rim, it looks like nothing. A bottle-glass tarn three hundred paces across, a white stream falling in, a rock lip where the water slides out, a heron standing in the shallows. You will be tempted to feel cheated. Do not judge it from the rim. The place does not exist at the surface. It exists under the ceiling, and nothing about the view from above will have told you the truth.

## Present yourself at the landing

Arrive dry. Walk down to the huts, find whoever is unhurried enough to receive you, and say plainly who you are and that you'd like to see the lower village and can work. Do not arrive and dive. The lower village is somebody's garden in the most literal sense — the mussel beds are rotated and thinned, the floats tethered a fathom down over the rows mark whose is whose, and the clipped pondweed hedges are property lines.

Bring gifts that are useful and humble: good knife steel, needles and awls, salt, fat, cordage, honey, tea. Do not bring a clock — the breath is the unit here, and a clock is almost a philosophical insult. Do not bring books or drawings *about* the place. Do not bring gadgets that hum. If you want to make images or notes, ask, and accept the answer.

Match their speech: plain, unpoetic, unhurried. Don't gush about beauty, don't perform grief over the houses, don't propose anything — no drainage, no dredging, no excavation, no heritage listing, no "you should." The tending is the culture, and it does not need your ideas or your audience. If you are offered work — reweaving a weir panel, carrying withies, thinning a mussel bed with your own hands shown what to take — that is your tuition and your visa. Do it exactly as shown, at half speed, and stop when told.

## Going under

**Kit:** a low-volume mask and a simple snorkel; no fins (fin wash damages hedges and stirs silt — you will move slowly, and slowly is correct); a wetsuit if you have one, worn without embarrassment — respect here is earned by care, not by shivering, and the locals will think none the worse of you for dressing like a seal among herons; a small blunt knife for the unlikely tangle in a crowfoot banner; wool and a windproof layer and a thermos for between dives. And two flat stones at your belt. Ideally, accept them from a hand at the landing rather than picking your own — being handed your stones is the closest thing this place has to an entry stamp.

**The code:** learn it cold, on land, before you ever wet your face. One knock: *here.* Two knocks: *look at this.* Three knocks: *up, now* — absolute, no questions, surface immediately and look to your buddy for why. Sound carries farther than sight in this water; the village talks to itself in knocks, and knocking casually is shouting in church. If, on your first dive, you are invited to knock once against the chapel wall — *here* — understand what you've been given: you have just been allowed to ring the doorbell of the lower village. Do it once.

**Discipline underwater:** decide your one task on the surface, while your mind is wide. Underwater the cold will narrow it to a point, and the point should already know what it's doing. Do not wander, do not improvise, do not renegotiate the plan at depth. Stay out of the old mill-leat, where the inflow pushes through the village as the one fast thing in this slow world — the current is real, the water-crowfoot grows in ten-foot banners there, and the delta silt nearby is soft and clouds at a touch. Don't hang on the garden floats; they are fences, not toys. Don't stir silt; the whole economy depends on water that stays readable.

**The chapel** is the deep end and the heart of it. The tower top rises to within a fathom of the ceiling — you can see it on an easy duck-dive. The arch and the bell hang deeper, and the apse holds the one absolute resident: a charr the length of a man's leg, uncounted years old, under the altar slab. Go only with a local, only when invited, and do not touch the bell rope — the bell is rung by the spate and by one diver once a year, and an accidental note from a stranger's hand would be something between a sacrilege and a false alarm.

## The rules that are not negotiable

- Three knocks means up. Now.
- The old fish is not taken, not touched, not reached toward. Ever.
- The otter's share: otters take from every weir and are not pursued. Yield gracefully; they were here before your sandwich.
- Closed means closed: meadows and mussel beds in spawning season.
- Take nothing out. Not a slate, not a shell, not a withy, not a photograph without leave. Everything down there is in use or scheduled.
- Nothing is fought here. Things are scheduled. Fit yourself into the schedule or stay at the surface.
- Check your skin when you exit. There are leeches. A dab of fat or salt and they let go; it is a small tax, not a problem.

## What it will feel like

The first moment is the cold — a cold that takes your mind down to a single bright point — and then you look up and the sky has become a ceiling of hammered silver, and the whole of your sense of *up* quietly inverts. It will be loud, which no one expects: the inflow falls into the lake with a roar the water keeps as a low hum you feel in your teeth, the crowfoot reaches hiss and bubble, and if you are lucky and the season is right, the charr drum at dusk like hands on a stretched hide. Dippers — small dark birds — come down through the ceiling walking, and fly from wall to wall underwater, and you will have to sit on your instinct to find this wrong. The heron you saw from the rim is, from below, a pair of stilts and then a lightning strike. Crayfish process across the chapel floor in the evening like a slow congregation. Fish go in and out of the doorways with the unhurried assurance of tenants — because they are the tenants, and you are the guest, and it will take you a dive or two to stop feeling like a trespasser and start feeling like what you actually are: a slow, clumsy, tolerated visitor in a place that was old in its arrangements before your grandparents were born.

You will be bad at this. Greased children will out-dive you without noticing they did. Let them. Five honest breaths, each one spent doing one small thing carefully, is a full visit.

## Leaving

Leave before the cold makes you stupid and pride makes you brave — the two travel together. Warm through completely before the walk out; the rim is no place for the umbles. Pay your debts in work or goods. Take nothing the lake didn't already give freely, which is to say: take nothing. And then keep the place unremarkable. From the rim it looks like a tarn with a heron, and that dullness is its armor — travelers who talk too well about it are how armored things get cracked. If anyone asks what you saw, the truest and kindest answer is the plain one: a lake in a high bowl. The hedges are clipped. The weirs hold. The old fish sleeps under the altar. And you knocked once, and were here.
