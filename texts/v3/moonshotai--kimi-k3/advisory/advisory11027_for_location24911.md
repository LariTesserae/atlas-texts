# Preparing for Gallery Two

**A field guide for the ordinary traveler**

## Orientation: what you are dealing with

Start with the good news. The physics is honest. Air is air — warm, dry, breathable, faintly ozoned. Gravity is ordinary. Time runs at par: your watch will keep true time down there, even when you can't. Nothing in the gallery is venomous, nothing is predatory, nothing will attack you, and nothing is cursed in any way that follows you home.

Now the actual news. Every hazard in Gallery Two is courteous, slow, and cumulative. The place has no malice and no mercy — only balance. You will be the only variable in the room, and the room has been absorbing variables for longer than anyone has been counting. It will not hurry you, scare you, or drive you out. It will simply, politely, begin to keep you. Your entire preparation should be organized against one possibility: that you will want to let it.

## Water: the one lethal thing

The salt takes water out of the air, out of your breath, out of your skin. Treat this as a serious desert compressed into a single room.

- **Carry 1 liter per hour of planned presence, plus 2 liters reserve — worn on your body, never cached.** Nothing set down in the gallery is truly set down; it is begun upon. A water bottle left on the floor comes back rime-glazed, and by then the floor has started a file on it.
- **Drink by schedule, never by thirst.** Thirst is a surface sense and down there it lies. Your sweat evaporates before it runs, so you will feel dry while losing water at full desert rates. Your gauge is the rime: when your forearms glitter, you are sweating hard. When they *stop* glittering and you feel odd — that is an emergency. Ascend.
- **Bring a heat-and-moisture exchange (HME) mask** if you can source one — the kind polar travelers and ventilator patients use. It recaptures the moisture of your own exhalation, which the gallery otherwise harvests from every breath. Failing that, any particulate respirator helps (the air carries oil aerosol and salt dust), plus saline nasal gel and preservative-free eye drops applied before you descend, not after symptoms start.
- **Occlusive lip protection — petroleum-based, not lanolin.** Read the chemistry of the place before you pack: the grazers eat films of wax and ester. To them, lanolin balm, leather dressing, and the oils of your own skin are forage. Do not dress yourself in their food group.
- **Eat dry food, over a wrapper, and pack out every crumb.** A crumb is an offering. The ledger will accept it. Don't start making offerings.
- **Never give the place water.** No spit, no poured-out bottle dregs, no waste of any kind. It will take anything you offer instantly and without comment, and you cannot afford the donation. Use the surface.

## Sound: protection and what protection can't fix

The drone sits around 55 hertz at loom-floor volume, plus the choir's stacked partials above it. Two separate problems here: your cochlea, and your sense of time.

- **For your ears:** foam plugs alone do little at 55 Hz. Best is active noise cancellation — a constant low tone is exactly what ANC eats — or level-dependent electronic earmuffs (shooters' muffs), which suppress the drone while passing speech, letting you talk with your companion at near-normal volume instead of shouting, which is what everyone there does without noticing.
- **But schedule listening windows.** You came to hear it. Choose two or three deliberate stretches — fifteen minutes at the gallery mouth, ten in the apse — with light protection, and actually listen. Then protect again. A visit to Gallery Two conducted entirely inside earmuffs is a postcard you mailed to yourself.
- **Stand in a mist curtain at least once.** The curtains hang at the standing-wave nodes. Crossing one, you will feel the pressure shift inside your ears — the chord has a geography and you can walk it. This is the cheapest wonder in the place.
- **For your clock:** the chord will, after some hours, become inaudible to you — not quieter, just *there*, the way silence is *there* — and your heartbeat will drift into step, and you will lose the ability to say whether an hour or a day has passed. This is the single most reliable effect in the gallery. Counter it mechanically:
  - A watch (any honest watch; keep electronics minimal and pouch them before approaching the bus bar).
  - A vibrating timer set to repeat every 20 minutes. When it fires: drink, write one line in a pocket notebook ("14:40, feel fine, partner fine"), look at your companion, look at the gallery mouth. Four actions. Do not skip the notebook — the written log is a spine your memory can climb down on.
  - Three exit alarms: a gentle one (begin your last circuit), a firm one (touch the ladder), and an absolute one.
- **The master rule:** plan to be climbing out *before* the chord turns to silence in your perception. If it happens anyway, go now. That quiet is not the place resting. That is you being tuned.

## The descent, and the lift

Statistically, the most dangerous object at Gallery Two is the ladder. Treat it as caving, not sightseeing: harness, two lanyards, clip the rail as you go, gloves, headlamp, test every rung. The timbers are salt-preserved and may be perfectly sound. "May" is doing work in that sentence.

**The lift:** it will pass you on the ladder — black iron, brass gate, empty, always empty. When you hear its bell, stop, hug the rungs, let it pass. Do not reach for it. Do not ride it. Its courtesy is not an invitation; hearses are courteous too. Its cable runs down into dark, its power comes up from below, and the shaft continues past the gallery with no marking on any wall to suggest anyone has gone to look. Read that absence of markings correctly: it is not a record of journeys. It is a record of returns. The question of what feeds Gallery Two is a real question, and it is not yours. Take the ladder.

## Conduct on the floor

- **Keep moving.** Rest standing. Never sit on the floor, never lie down, never sleep. The carriers — the tall, stilt-legged ones, the only things there that hurry — collect the dead and the broken, and their categories are functional, not biological. Do not test whether "resting" reads as "broken."
- **Stay off the track.** The grazers are blind, slow, and harmless, but their rasps strip wax and ester, and your boots, cuffs, and skin qualify. If one angles toward you, step aside. Do not pet them. Do not feed them. Do not name them.
- **Learn no individuals.** They are distinguishable by the accidents of their crusts, and if you stay long enough to learn the faces you will learn the thing the text warns about: the same one passes the same flagstone, always. That knowledge costs more than it pays. Leave it on the shelf.
- **The press and the grinder are unguarded industrial machinery that never stops.** Keep hands and straps clear. You may watch one birth at the press — a warm block, the molded seams cracking, the damp thing unfolding and walking to the track. One is enough; one is the entire doctrine of the place in five minutes. Do not watch a second.
- **The bus bar:** admire from two paces. Corona, spark-flies, ozone strongest here. The metal whisker-turf beneath it is conductive — don't kneel in it, don't let pack straps dangle, pouch your electronics before approaching. Do not bridge anything with anything. The flies that pop are replaced; you are not in the ledger's budget.
- **Touch nothing bare-handed.** The crystal is fresh-cut forever; the arrises will open a thumb, and salt in a cut hurts with great precision and heals reluctantly.
- **Sing, if you like — once.** Stand under the pipes, hum one sustained note, and feel the choir lean into it, harvesting the pressure of your breath like leaves leaning into light. It is the one audience in the multiverse that will never tire of you. That is exactly why you stop after one song.
- **Leave nothing.** This is Leave No Trace inverted: in most places your marks fade; here they don't. Chalk on salt, in still dry air, is permanent — permanence is the local pollution. No arrows, no initials, no crumbs, no trash, no offerings. Your footprints will erase themselves, grain by grain, over hours. Everything else you leave stays forever, and the gallery will keep it with the same fidelity it keeps the wrench.
- **Do not free the wrench.** The wall holds it exactly halfway, and has decided, in the way the gallery decides things, that halfway is where it lives. It is the only proof that anyone was ever clumsy here. It is held in trust. Not by you.
- **Take nothing that is counted.** No comb cells, no droplets from the flanges, no spark-flies, and above all **no blocks from the press output.** They are warm and loaf-sized and a traveler could pocket one, and two days later your luggage would unfold a pilgrim with no liturgy — a grazer in a world with no track, no bloom, no chord, rasping at your floorboards for a food that doesn't exist. Do not do this to a grazer. If you must carry something home, a vial of loose floor dust. The ledger will not miss it. That is what makes it permissible, and also what makes it sad.
- **Sweep yourself at the mouth.** Cuffs, pockets, pack seams. Nothing alive rides out. Nothing there can live off the chord — but that is their tragedy, not your permission.

## Time and the self

Recommended first visit: surface to surface in under six hours, with two to four in the gallery proper. Overnight stays are for a different kind of traveler than you.

Go with one companion — two at most — and agree on the protocol before the ladder: every 20 minutes, out loud, each of you answers three questions. Your name. The time. The exit time. Shouting is fine; everyone in that room is already shouting at no one.

Go for a stated reason. Write it down topside and carry the paper. Somewhere around the second or third hour, the gallery will present itself as the strongest argument you have ever personally encountered for permanence — and it will be right, that's the trouble, it *is* perfect, it *does* balance, the droplet *does* hang forever. Have your counter-argument prepared in advance, specific and mortal: people, work, weather, soup, whatever is genuinely yours. Abstractions lose down there.

Do not go freshly bereaved. If you must, bring the companion who will enforce the exit time against your will, and give them written permission to do it while you are still capable of signing things.

Know the exit triggers. Start for the ladder when any of these is true: the chord has gone silent in your perception; you cannot recall how long you've been down without checking the log; you feel, for the first time, *rested* — rested enough to sit; you catch yourself thinking of the procession as familiar, as yours, as something you would like to see again tomorrow. That last one is the bell at the end of the lift's journey. It means the interval is over.

## Who should think twice

Cardiac arrhythmia, respiratory disease, pregnancy, severe hyperacusis or tinnitus: reconsider, or shorten drastically. Low-frequency sound at these levels presses on the chest and the viscera; nausea, flutter, or headache means move away from the apse and toward the mouth, and if it persists, ascend. Anyone prone to existential vertigo should know that the gallery's final lesson — the one about the droplet and the fifty years — lands differently at different ages. The young find it soothing. Plan accordingly.

## If something goes wrong

If your companion goes down: get them upright and moving *immediately*. Do not test the carriers' discernment. For injuries, the ladder is your only exit — the lift does not serve you — so rig a rope at the headframe before anyone descends, leave a surface party or at minimum a filed trip plan with check-in times, and carry a compact first-aid kit including wound closure strips and a dedicated small medical water ration, because salt gets into every cut and you will want to rinse. If anyone stops glittering — sweat ceasing in that air — the visit is over that minute.

## Coming back

At the surface, sit down in the scrub before you drive. The silence will feel broken — a specific pressure absent from the world — and ordinary entropy will look sloppy to you for a day or two, like a sketch after a finished painting. This passes. Drink until your urine runs pale. Bag your clothes before they touch your car seats; everything you wore comes home filmed with oil and smelling of hot machinery and old paper, and some of that smell is permanent. Your boots, strange to say, will look newer than when you left — the gallery glazes what walks in it.

No quarantine is required beyond the stowaway sweep. The place exports nothing. Except this: 55 hertz is the note your own world's grid already hums — transformer windings, refrigerators, organ pipes all arrive at it. For the rest of your life, the modern world will ambush you with the chord, in parking garages and behind supermarkets. There is no avoiding this souvenir. Most travelers come to treat these moments as postcards. A few treat them as summons. Know which one you are before you hear the first one.

Write up your log while the rime is still on your cuffs. The memory won't fade, exactly — but your conviction that it happened might, because the mind edits standing waves out of a life. The notebook is your proof. The rime will wash off. The rest of it keeps.

## A note on the second visit

You can postpone this trip for decades and miss nothing; the gallery is the one destination that will not change before you arrive. That also means it will be there afterward, unchanged, holding the droplet you saw, running the same gray dome past the same flagstone. Many travelers go back — every ten years, say — to measure themselves against the changeless. First visits teach awe. Second visits teach envy, which is a heavier pack to climb out with.

If you return, go knowing that the hardest thing in Gallery Two was never the descent, the heat, the noise, or the thirst. It is the moment on the ladder, climbing toward the ordinary sky, when the chord fades below you and some part of you — honest, tired, and precisely located — asks why you are leaving a place where nothing is ever lost.

Have your answer ready before your feet touch the rungs.
