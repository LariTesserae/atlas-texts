# Preparing for Withymill

## First, understand what you're visiting

The Works is not a ruin, not a preserve, not a sight. It is a working steam plant, a managed wetland, and a village of forty-odd souls, all three braided as tightly as the roofs and willow crowns. Everything about your preparation follows from this: you are a guest inside a machine that is also an ecosystem that is also someone's home. Pack and behave accordingly.

It is also wet, loud, warm, green, and sinking. Hold all five facts at once.

## Timing your visit

**Stay at least one full week.** This is non-negotiable, and here is why: the Hearing. At dawn on the seventh day the engines stop, the silence arrives like weather, and the bowl begins, patiently, to fill while everyone drinks tea and watches the brass Telltales. If you leave before the Hearing, you have seen a mill. Only after it have you seen Withymill. Ask the office which day counts as the seventh — their calendar may not match yours — and build your stay around it.

Ask the barge master on the river when the coal barge is due; barge days are event days, and you may be able to ride in or out. Season matters less than you'd think — the climate is mild and the Works corrects what isn't — but summer brings the swifts in the dead flue and midges at dusk, while winter makes the steam visible and the dawn mist lie in strata like something painted. Any season. Go.

## What to pack

**Clothing:**
- Wool, everything wool — it stays warm wet, and everything will be wet. The air is warm and stays warm, but the damp is total.
- A waxed or oiled outer layer with a hood. Not for rain — for the drip. Condensation falls from every beam onto every roof, forever, indoors and out. A hood or a brimmed hat is genuine equipment here, not a fashion choice.
- Close-fitting sleeves, nothing dangling, nothing loose. Line-shafting and belts run unguarded by your world's standards — by any standards. Tie back long hair. Leave the scarf.
- Broken-in boots that can take perpetual damp, treated with grease or dubbin. Pack light: the causeway in is half a mile of bowed, mossed planks, and you'll want your hands free and your balance uncommitted.
- Gloves — old iron, wet grating, rust splinters.

**Kit:**
- Earplugs, for sleeping and for the whistle. But plan to keep your ears bare by day — more on this below, because it's a real tension.
- A hand-crank lamp. Yes, they have a dynamo and caged bulbs; no, you cannot plug anything in. Their current is theirs — nonstandard, probably direct, definitely not for you. Go analog.
- A notebook in a waxed wrap, with pencils. Ink runs in that humidity; paper mildews. They keep their own records in the office, but yours need protection.
- A small medical kit: burn dressings (it's a steam plant), antihistamine if midges trouble you, water purification as backup, and whatever your own world's standard immunizations require — tetanus first among them, in a place of soft brick and ancient iron.
- A personal mug. You'll understand by the second tea.
- **No alarms. No beeps. Nothing that chimes, buzzes, or alerts.** This deserves emphasis: the Works is read by ear. The night keeper diagnoses bearings through a brass rod pressed to his jawbone; the rule under all rules is *if it sounds wrong, it is wrong.* An electronic alarm is a false pathology shouted into a stethoscope. Disable everything before you reach the causeway.

**Gifts and payment:**
Money exists — fenboard goes out and money comes back — so small coins of honest metal will serve. But better: tea, sugar, salt, good needles, lamp oil, fine tool steel. All imports, all precious, all used. Tea above all; the place runs on it ritually. If you're bold, a mechanic's stethoscope for the night keeper is the one piece of foreign technology that speaks their language — listening to the machine's health — but offer it humbly. The brass rod has a century of seniority.

**What NOT to bring — the biosecurity briefing:**
This is the easiest way to fail as a traveler and the hardest to forgive. **No seeds, no cuttings, no soil, no pets.** Scrub your boots before arrival; shake out your luggage; check the seams of your pack. The Works is a closed, century-long negotiation with the fen, balanced at every point — reed to pulp to board to ash to bank to reed. One hitchhiking spore, one foreign seedling in the mortar, and you become the flood mark on some future wall. The fen's answer to drainage was willow and reed; its answer to your boot-mud is unknowable. Arrive clean.

## Skills to practice beforehand

- **Projecting your voice.** Everyone speaks in *mill voice*, a shout-register developed over the drone. Practice speaking from the diaphragm. You'll feel ridiculous. Everyone does, slightly, in the outside world — you'll carry it home too, and startle people for a week after.
- **Basic lip-reading and a few hand signs.** Children there read lips without being taught. You won't achieve fluency, but the effort is noticed, and faces are legible in the Works in a way you've likely forgotten faces can be.
- **Walking on springy, narrow, mossed planks.** Flat-footed, unhurried, eyes down at the margins. Which brings us to the cardinal danger—

## Hazards, honestly enumerated

**The green that isn't ground.** The drains are straight black channels sealed completely under duckweed and red water-fern. They look like lawn. They are not lawn. This is the thing most likely to hurt you: a confident step onto a green surface that is two inches of plants over four feet of black water. Walk only on plank, brick, or grating. Never trust a green surface you haven't been told is a roof. And remember the ground itself is notional — a footprint fills in thirty seconds and is a pond by evening.

**Machinery.** Beaters, presses, belts, line shafts, spinning armatures — with ferns growing three feet away, which tells you everything about the local guarding philosophy. Accept an escort in the mill sheds. Ask before entering. Touch nothing you haven't been invited to touch, and test hot surfaces with the back of a hand held *near*, never on.

**The whistle.** It is the one voice louder than the Works, and it is the clock of the place. Learn its times on day one; cover your ears when it blows; and ask the office how many voices it has — whether it can say more than "shift change," and what the other meanings are. If there is an emergency signal, you must know it. And never, under any circumstances, whistle casually yourself. Sound is meaning there. Don't speak falsehoods in the local language.

**The Hearing's flood.** During the weekly silence the drains rise a finger's breadth or two, and the lowest walks may go awash. Don't schedule your departure crossing for those hours. And learn the route up the Ring Bank on your first day — it is the high ground, the viewpoint, and the evacuation intuition all in one: if it ever goes wrong, you go inward and up, not outward. (It goes wrong slowly, when it goes at all — doorstep in three days, rehearsed weekly, calmly, with tea. This may be the safest doomed place in the multiverse.)

**Small harms.** Midges at dusk — ask for the local remedy; fen people always have one, and sweet gale or bog myrtle is the classic. Don't drink drain water; ask about the condensate, which is distilled by the engine itself and likely the cleanest water in the fen. The steep pits reek sweet-foul and steam on cold mornings — admire from the walk. The Sump has eels, which are lifted gently off the screens; do not be the reason one needs lifting.

## Etiquette and customs

- **The rain-burst rule.** No one attempts conversation through rain on corrugated iron. You stop talking and wait it out. This has the force of etiquette, which there has the force of law. When the rain starts mid-sentence, just stop. Smile. Wait.
- **The Hearing.** Whisper, if you speak at all. The silence is enormous and old, and people fall quiet out of something older than habit. Watch the Telltale wall with everyone else. Drink the tea. At dusk the pumps run back up and everyone exhales — you'll exhale too, and you'll understand something about the place that no amount of machinery-touring could teach you.
- **Don't pick, don't wipe, don't feed.** Every plant is inventory, infrastructure, or a neighbor. The ferns in the dynamo room are loved — nobody has the heart to wipe them, and a visitor who tidies them marks himself a barbarian forever. The willow in Shed Three is entered in the inventory as *Tree, one, in service* and inspected on rounds like the pumps. Greet it if you like. It's on the rounds; it's used to attention.
- **The cat is deaf from birth** and asleep on the warm dynamo casing, which is the best bed in the world and its by seniority. Do not startle it. Do not wake it. The heron on the outfall apron is deaf or philosophical and is the men's break companion — take your breaks there. That is where you will be told things.
- **Do not pitch improvements.** This is the single most important rule of conduct. You will, at some point, standing by the Post — the founder's column, flush with the turf once, now taller than a man — feel the urge to say *have you considered.* Swallow it. They know the bowl sinks an inch a year. They drive a nail at the doorframe annually and add a section to the rising main every generation. The Works cannot win; it can only continue, and continuing is the whole technology. They have heard every solution. They have chosen theirs. Your job is to witness, not to fix.
- **But: if something sounds wrong to you, say so.** The rule is democratic. Fresh ears are the one thing a traveler has that forty lifetimes of calibrated hearing don't. You may be wrong — you probably are — but the report itself is a contribution, and it will be taken seriously and checked calmly.
- **Offer to work.** Wiping brass is the universal visa. An hour with a rag teaches you more than a day of touring, and forty people with a century of patience will still notice who picks up a rag.
- **Sign the ledger.** The office keeps records meant to last a century. Write legibly. There is a real chance you will be entered in something like the inventory — *Traveler, one, in service* — and it is a genuine honor. Take it as one.

## How to spend the days

Dawn: mist lying in strata, sheds and chimneys standing in it like masts. Mid-morning: the mill floor, the beaters' thudding through the planks, the tram screeching in from the outer beds with reed and withies. Breaks by the heron. Rounds, if you're allowed — the pumps, the dynamo, the Telltales, the flood marks kept unpainted as instruction, the revolution counter in the office reading in the billions: the age of the Works, counted in heartbeats. Ask to see it. Ask about the flood year over tea, not on rounds.

Dusk: the louvered drying sheds glow from within like lanterns exhaling — and the midges come out, so choose your tradeoff and dress for it. Night: climb the Ring Bank and take the whole scale of it in one turn of the head, because it is small and bounded and complete. Outward, the grey marsh to the world's edge and a barge lantern moving on the lode like a slow star. Inward, the lit green knot, steaming, beating. Stand at the Spout and watch the bowl being emptied uphill forever. If you want birdsong, face outward — birdsong is an outside thing there. If you want the drone, face in. The bank is the dial between two worlds, and you are the needle.

If you see a kingfisher, report it truthfully and precisely. They are spoken of by year, like floods. You may hand them a year-name. Do not lie about it — they read faces better than you do.

## Sleeping, eating, washing

Lodging will likely be over the stores, if the office grants it. The first two nights the drone will keep you awake; by the third it will be a lullaby, and on the seventh morning the silence will wake you more violently than any noise. Expect plain, good food — eel, fowl, bread, root vegetables, endless tea — and bring some rations to contribute. Washing is by the washhouse or condensate; ask about the Engine's Garden, the steaming lagoon behind the boiler house where the condenser loop discharges under gunnera leaves like tabletops, with a fig against the boiler wall. If there is a bathing custom there, it is the best bath in the fen. If a fig is offered, accept. That is communion.

## Leaving

Go out by barge down the lode if you can arrange it — slow, and the lantern at night is the slow star you watched from the bank. Settle everything at the office. Say goodbye to the tree.

Then know what happens to you afterward. Your wool will carry the smell — tannin, peat water, coal smoke, fermentation, moss — for weeks; air your kit and consider it the souvenir that keeps. You will speak too loudly for a while. You will wake at dawn on the seventh day for a while. And you will find yourself listening to your own home — the refrigerator, the pipes, the furnace — reading its health through the floorboards, because the Works teaches you that sound is health and the drone is safety, and once learned it does not unlearn.

One warning, traveler to traveler: the place runs on long time, and long time is contagious. Rotations in years, apprenticeships in sevens, a counter in the billions. Set your return date before you go, tell someone at home, and keep it — or decide, deliberately and with both eyes open, not to. People have stood by the Post watching the nail marks climb the doorframe and felt the arithmetic of their own lives rearrange itself. The Works cannot win. It can only continue. You will come home with that sentence, and you will find, to your surprise, that it applies everywhere — and that this is not the despairing thing it sounds like, but the most hopeful thing you own.
