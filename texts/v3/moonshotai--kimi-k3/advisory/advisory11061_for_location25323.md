## What you're walking into

Calibrate first: this is one of the gentle worlds, physically. Ordinary sun, ordinary day length, breathable air, potable water that falls out of the air indoors, nothing venomous, nothing that hunts you. Your watch works, fire works, your knees work. Every danger here is knowable in advance, and most of them are printed on the landscape — the sharp ground, the open cellars, the machines that hum. The demanding part is not survival. It is behavior. This is a place that runs on accounting — of water, of sound, of names — and a traveler who arrives loud, helpful, and thirsty in the wrong way will do real damage without ever meaning to.

So prepare less like an explorer and more like a guest apprentice: someone coming to learn a schedule, a set of names, and one unwritten rule.

## When to go, and how to arrive

Go in late spring or summer. Late spring gives you the basin's signature paradox — frost lying on the silt at dawn while the rim above is already soft — and the frog population at full argumentative strength. Summer gives you dragonflies over the gardens and swallows screaming through the indoor rain. Plan on three days to a week: long enough to feel one full cycle of the weekly sump tide, to catch at least one scheduled Still, and to stop being a stranger to the sound.

Arrive on foot, over the rim, in the morning. This matters more than it seems. The basin is cupped, and its noise is cupped with it — you hear the hum before you see a single house, a low continuous note with a heartbeat inside it, rising out of a place that looks from above like it should be silent. That reveal is the front door of the place. Don't rob yourself of it by arriving fast or by night. Stop at the bathtub ring — the bleached snags, the mineral-white silt, the matted reeds — and listen for a full minute before you descend. You are crossing from the world into the Bed, and the temperature will drop as you go, and the crunch underfoot will change, and both of those are the place introducing itself.

Walk straight to the Engine House. The hum will guide you; you could do it blind. Introduce yourself to the tender on shift, say plainly what you are — a traveler, passing through, willing to work — and ask three questions: where you may draw water, where you may sleep, and what the week's schedule is. That third question is the one that will mark you as someone worth keeping.

## What to pack

- **Stiff-soled boots, already broken in.** The bed is young land and it hasn't softened. The silt lies in polygonal plates cracked and curled like pottery sherds, still crisp, still sharp enough to flake — and to cut. The gravel bars are angular and ankle-turning. Thin soles will be shredded in a day.
- **A warm layer and a hat, even in high summer.** The bed is a frost hollow. Cold air drains down at night and pools, and summer mornings come with your breath visible and dew heavy on every surface. The rim's season is not the Bed's season.
- **An oilskin or waxed waterproof.** Not for rain — rain is rare here, an occasional cool drizzle that's gone in a day. Your waterproof is for *manufactured* weather: the dew, the weeping flanges, the sweating standpipes, and above all the Engine House, where on still days it rains indoors, slow fat drops condensing off the cold roof trusses and ringing on the floor plates. The tenders walk their rounds in oilskins under a roof that does not leak. You will understand, once, why.
- **Leather work gloves.** For the plates, the robbed stone, the cold brass.
- **A headlamp, plus a spare light.** The pole lamps along the pipe-track are widely spaced — islands of yellow, each with its own moth halo — and between them is real dark, and in the dark there are toads on the track, slow-worms on the plates, doorsteps at ankle height, and cellars open like dry wells.
- **A watch.** They tell time by the plant, not the sky, but the plant runs to a schedule, and the tenders check their watches. Carrying one lets you anticipate the Still, catch the frog surge, and feel briefly like a citizen.
- **A flask.** For the new water. You'll see.
- **Compact food and gifts** — tea, sugar, oats, dried fruit, salt; and for giving: good lamp oil, candles, brass polish, machine oil, needles, strong thread, soap. Nine houses and a coal shed cannot feed a idle mouth without noticing it.
- **Earplugs** — with a caveat I'll explain below.
- **A notebook and pencil.** More valuable here than any instrument.
- **Do not bring:** seeds, plants, animals, or anything alive — this is pioneer land, barely colonized, and it does not need your world's ambitions. No fishing gear; the Sike is accounted, and the kingfisher has seniority. Nothing loud. The tenders diagnose the machines by ear; your noise is their static.

## The rules of water

Learn the accounting before you drink anything: **first water to the canal, second water to the gardens, new water to the kitchen.** The sumps, the seeps, the Sike itself — spoken for, measured, owed uphill to the canal on the far ridge. You are an unaccounted mouth when you arrive; get accounted. Drink only what is offered, from where you're told.

Never touch a valve, a gland, a weeping joint — and this is the hard one for a certain kind of traveler — **never fix anything.** Your instinct to be helpful is the single greatest social hazard you carry into this basin. The leaks are not failures. They are the settlement. Each of the nine houses tends a named weep — the Lych Gate Weep, Kettle Gland, the Long Cry — and each weep is somebody's garden, somebody's watercress, somebody's mint, listed by name in the leak-book at the Engine House. No leak is ever fixed without another being opened; they speak of *tuning* the system, not repairing it. If you see a dripping gland, the correct action is to ask its name.

The drinking water is the strange lovely part. Troughs set beneath the coldest pipe runs catch the condensate — new water, they call it, water that has been nowhere and touched nothing. Every kitchen has its drip-barrel, everyone will tell you it is the sweetest water in the district, and they will be right, and everyone will omit that it is the sweat of the machine they serve. **Join the omission.** It is not hypocrisy; it is sacrament. Drink, say it's the sweetest water you've ever tasted — easy, because it will be — and keep your analysis off your tongue. Fill your flask when offered.

And take nothing out of the drowned village. The hamlet robs its lintels and walling stone legitimately, wears the old village like an inherited coat; a visitor pocketing a sett or a door-handle is just a thief. Notes and sketches are the only loot permitted.

## Sound literacy — the real preparation

This sparse little hamlet is *loud*, and its loudness is a language you must learn, because here, noise is the all-clear and silence is the alarm.

The hum never stops — a low note you feel in the breastbone, with a slow heartbeat inside it. That's health. Occasionally a valve seats somewhere and a water-hammer goes down the rising main like a struck bell, once, rolling across the bed. That's also health — startling, harmless, the system clearing its throat. Learn the difference in your first evening, or you'll spend nights bolting upright at bells.

Then there is the Still: the scheduled pause, when the pumps rest. It has a name and a feeling all its own — a held-breath strangeness where even the frogs seem to listen and everyone lowers their voice without deciding to. Do not fill it with chatter. Stand in it. In the first seconds of it, the frog chorus surges up into the silence as if released, and the tenders check their watches and say the second shift has clocked in. It is the hamlet's standing joke and its quiet ceremony, and your first Still is your initiation.

And then there is the other silence — the unscheduled one, the wrong one. The rule is absolute and includes you: **when the hum stops wrong, come running.** From anywhere in the basin, from the furthest garden, run for the Engine House. You will feel which silence it is in your spine; trust it. This is why the earplugs come with a caveat — use them the first night if you must, but wean off them. You want the wrong silence to wake you. It's the best alarm system you've ever slept inside.

Prepare for the drill like a grown-up: on your first day, walk your route from wherever you sleep to the Engine House, in daylight, noting every doorstep and open cellar; then walk it once after dark with your lamp. Keep boots and light by the bed. When you arrive at a real alarm, report to a tender, take instruction, and obey it even if the instruction is *stand here and hold this*.

## The shape of a stay

Dawn: deer stepping over the ring, frost on the plates, your breath in the air, tea made with new water. Morning: work, if you're wise — ask to help in your host's garden, thin the cress, harvest the mint, learn how the lift schedule plants and waters everything. Midday on the dry plates: walk out to the old boat ramp that ends in midair six feet above the bed it once served, read the board-marks in the unweathered concrete, listen to the drowned snags click in the wind. Afternoon: the drowned lanes, with a local if you can get one — the setts underfoot, the cellars dripping in the dark, the doorstep that wasn't visible last decade. Peer into cellars; descend none alone; and test silt with a stick before you trust it. Dusk: be at a permitted sump, or the intake pool, for the frog wall — not a chorus but a percussive, deafening braid of croak and trill that you feel in your teeth, the whole frog nation of a dead reservoir compressed into a dozen ponds. Watch the herons stand custodian, one per sump. Watch for the kingfisher on the very pipe that is stealing its river. Walk back by lamplight watching for toads. Ask, once, if you may walk a night round with a tender — the hum in its night register, the ticking switchboard, the indoor rain, the swallows in the rafters, the moss and fern garden growing in the gloom at the feet of the machines.

If the annual storm comes through while you're there, stay off the bars while the Sike re-cuts its channel, and the morning after, if you're invited on the survey walk, go — walk behind the tenders, point, touch nothing. You are watching the village come back a few centimeters more. They measure their careers in such events: two doorstep years, the year the church showed its corner. Being present for one is an honor you cannot schedule.

## The physical hazards, briefly

Cuts from the plates; twisted ankles on angular gravel; the six-foot drop off the ramp's end; ankle-high doorsteps placed by archaeology precisely where a stride lands; cellar stairs that drip and go down past the light. Cold and damp at night even in summer — if you sleep rough without a tarp the dew will soak you as thoroughly as rain. Biting insects at dusk near the sumps, minor. Nothing to swim in; don't dabble in the sumps (someone's garden, cold as a cellar) and keep clear of the intake grill. Keep your hands off the machines. Keep your fire in a hearth.

## The one place you don't go

There is a deep cellar by the intake, whose stairs go down further than the light does, where the Sike slides into the grill and the cold comes up the steps like something breathing. There is no written rule about it. No one goes down. Lintels are robbed from all over the old village but never from its threshold.

Traveler's axiom: an unwritten rule that everyone keeps is load-bearing. You may stand at the top of the steps and feel the cold breathe up at you — that is the whole of the experience, and it is honestly plenty. Don't descend, don't shine your light down it, don't toss a stone to hear the depth, don't press the tenders for an explanation they don't have in words. This is a world of accounted water and honest gauges, and it has exactly one door it doesn't open, and your only job is to not be the story they tell the next traveler.

## Being good company

Learn the weep names as geography — they are the map and the addresses and the family history. Trade labor: garden work, carrying on rounds, a rag and a tin of brass polish for the gauges will buy you more intimacy with that machine than any passport. Expect evenings of questions about the worlds you've seen, and answer them precisely — this is a culture of measure, of first water and second, and it can smell an inflated account. Match their emotional register, which is practical tenderness: they pity a failed weep the way other places pity a failed harvest, they do not perform grief over the drowned village, and you shouldn't either. Keep your voice down at night — the basin holds its noise, and yours too.

## Leaving

Go in the morning, back up over the ring, and stop at the rim for the reverse of your arrival — from there you can count every light the basin has on your fingers, see the whole of it cupped small and young and working, and hear it anyway. Take nothing but your notes, your flask of new water, and — if the tenders will trust you with it — a message carried over the ridge to the canal side, where all that lifted water goes. It's a fitting errand: the basin's water completes its journey in your hands.

Expect after-effects. Silence will sound wrong for a while — thin, unwatched, vaguely alarming. You'll catch yourself listening for the heartbeat inside refrigerators and traffic. When a room goes quiet you'll glance at your watch. That's the place staying tuned in you. Let it.
