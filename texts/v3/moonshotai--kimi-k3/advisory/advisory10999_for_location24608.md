# Field Briefing: The Finished Plain

## What you are walking into

Understand this first: nothing here is hunting you, and that is precisely the danger. This is not a dramatic world. There is no apex horror, no hostile civilization, no exotic physics. The plain kills the way all very old, very patient places kill — through heat, water, distance, disorientation, and the small infections you didn't notice until day four. It is a finished landscape, and finished things do not hurry. Your emergencies will arrive slowly too. That means discipline and checklists will save you, not heroics.

The second thing to understand: you will be wet for the entire trip. Not rained-on wet — *ambiently* wet. The air is nearly water, the ground is nearly water, and at dawn the world wrings out its overnight surplus onto every surface, including you and the inside of your shelter. Do not fight this. The war you can win is not over dryness — it is over **one sealed, sacred dry kit**: sleeping clothes, sleep system, and foot-care supplies, double-bagged, never opened outside the tent. Everything else, surrender to the wet.

## When to go

The year has one heartbeat — the flood pulse — and everything schedules around it.

- **Late dry season** is the walker's window. Ridges are firm, pans have shrunk (concentrating wildlife to an almost theatrical density), and camping is easiest. The cost: heat, glare, and the fact that shrinking waterholes become tense places — everything drinks there, and the great reptiles know it.
- **Early wet season** is the boater's window and the naturalist's. The frog orchestras reach full strength, and if your timing is lucky you may wake to the single greatest event this world offers: the morning the sky simply fills with arriving birds, the empty flyways suddenly grammatical. The cost: sheet-flow a hand deep across the whole country, erased trails, and lightning.
- Avoid the peak of the rains unless you are an expedition-grade paddler. When the country becomes a river a horizon wide, the correct response is to become a stone: camp on high ground and wait.

Whatever season you choose, add a 50% time margin to every leg. Distances here lie. With no scale references and a midday horizon that dissolves into a gentle boil of evaporation, "a day's walk" to the inselberg may be two — or three.

## How to move

**The fossil rivers are your roads.** This is the single most important piece of practical knowledge in this briefing. The plain's oldest riverbeds — cemented gravels now riding the ridgetops as low, sinuous ridges — are the only reliably dry, firm, elevated walking in the country. They are your highways, your campsites, your sightlines, and your lightning-calculated refuge (more on that below). Learn to spot them from a distance: a slightly darker, slightly raised line of vegetation snaking across the flat. When in doubt, gain a ridge and follow it. They were rivers; they go somewhere.

**Bring a packraft.** A three-to-four-kilogram inflatable boat converts this world from an obstacle into a network. The clear living channels are navigable and lovely; the black still waters are navigable and eerie. In the wet season the raft becomes essential; in the dry, it's a luxury you'll bless every time a channel bars your route. Portage it across the gravel ridges. Paddle the mornings; the midday shimmer makes distance judgment on open water nearly worthless.

**On foot:** the sedge tussocks are ankle traps — each clump an island with dark water between. Step *on* tussocks or *between* them deliberately, never assume flat ground. Carry a staff and probe ahead in black water, which hides snags, holes, and logs that blink. Shuffle your feet in sandy shallows rather than stepping. Accept a pace of perhaps half your normal speed and plan rations accordingly.

**The grazer trails:** the barrel-bodied amphibious grazers maintain packed trails between pans, used so long they now guide the sheet-flow itself. These are the best walking on the plain — and they are someone else's highway. Walk them freely at midday. Be off them by dusk, when the frogs begin and the grazers move. Never camp on one, never block the line between a grazer and its water, and if you meet one on the trail, yield early and widely, on the landward side. Treat this animal with the full respect you'd give a hippopotamus, because that is the body plan and likely the temperament.

## Navigation and communication

The flat gives you almost nothing, so build your own reference system:

- **The inselberg is your lodestar.** Take a bearing on it every dawn, when the air is clear and the mist has drawn you a map of the drainage in white ribbons. By midday it rides on nothing, displaced and shimmering — midday sightings of it *cannot be trusted*; mirage will move it. Do your navigation at first light.
- **The termite mounds are your compass.** They align north–south, all of them, across the whole plain, as if the land were magnetized. Verify once against the sun, then use them as a thousand redundant compass needles. This trick alone is worth the trip.
- **The mist is your survey.** At dawn, fog lies in every hollow and old channel — you can literally read the drainage. Note it before it burns off.
- **Keep a dead-reckoning notebook.** Channels migrate, maps are fictions here, and satellite fixes may or may not function depending on your multiverse's infrastructure. Paces, bearings, times, sketches. Write it down daily.

Now the sobering part: **sound dies here.** The soaked air swallows everything; there is no echo; a shout carries perhaps thirty meters and is finished. This makes party separation the single most likely way to lose a companion. Establish visual-contact intervals. Carry whistles — they pierce where voices drown — plus a signal mirror for the abundant daylight and a bright panel for camp marking. Agree in advance: if separated, everyone makes for the nearest gravel ridge and stays visible. If you portaled in, anchor your extraction point on a ridge crest within sight of the inselberg, record it inertially, and do not trust your memory to find it again. Every hundred meters of this plain looks like every other hundred meters.

## Water and food

There are three waters, and they are not interchangeable:

- **Clear water** sliding over sand in the living channels — tea-tinted, cold — is your drinking source. Treat it anyway.
- **Black water** standing under trees is tannin-stained and, like most blackwater in such worlds, surprisingly hostile to microbes. Fine for washing; drinkable treated; never swim in it (see below).
- **Milk-water** — the pale post-rain sheet flow — is a filter-killer. The suspended clay will blind a cartridge in liters. Let it settle overnight in a collapsible bucket; if you carry a pinch of alum or a flocculant, it clears dramatically. Better trick: dig a seep hole in the sand a few meters from a channel and let the ground filter for you — the water table is at the skin, and the seep fills with far cleaner water than the sheet carries.

Carry a gravity filter with a cleanable element, spare elements, and chemical backup. Carry electrolytes in quantity: the midday heat and saturated air will drain you faster than any desert — sweat does not evaporate here, it simply accumulates.

Bring essentially all your food. Yes, the water is thick with fish — gulping, rolling, occasionally walking past you on stiff fins — and fishing would be easy. But standard first-contact food protocol applies: unknown proteins, unknown toxins, unknown parasites. If you must experiment, follow the full graduated exposure protocol and treat it as an experiment, not a meal. Do not eat the clay at the licks, however healthy the parrots make it look.

## Campcraft

**Camp on the gravel ridges** — off the exact crest (lightning), on the sandy apron just below it. You gain drainage, a breeze that thins the insects, sightlines, and distance from the water's edge, where the night shift works. The ancient cemented gravel can be sharp; a groundsheet earns its weight.

**Shelter:** a free-standing tent with a full mesh inner is the reliable choice — the trees stand too far apart to promise hammock spacing, and you want sealed mesh against the insect weather. If you are a committed hanger, bring long straps and a fallback plan. Manage condensation ruthlessly; at dawn everything weeps, including the inside of single-wall shelters. A pack towel will become your most-used item.

**Fire:** carry a stove, full stop. Every stick is waterlogged, and there is a subtler reason to distrust combustion here — once in a generation the peat ignites and burns *underground for years, slow as thought*. Before pitching or walking across any suspicious bare patch, probe: ground that is oddly warm, wisps of smoke at dawn, a line of dead trees, a smell of sulfur or ash means a fire floor that can collapse under you and take your leg off at the boot line. Rare. Memorable. Respect it.

**Waste:** you cannot dig — a heel-print fills with water, let alone a cathole. Pack out everything. Nothing leaves this plain quickly; the sea is weeks downstream and owns the place, and your trash would simply *stay*, forever, on the oldest ground in the world. Act accordingly.

## Health and hazards

- **Heat is your primary enemy.** There is no shade; the wide-crowned trees stand alone like eccentrics, each in its own room. Carry your shade with you — a tarp, and honestly, an umbrella, which in a treeless warm-rain world functions as a personal tree. Enforce a midday stop. The insect drone that builds to physical pressure at noon is the plain itself telling you to sit down.
- **Feet:** maceration and fungal bloom are near-certainties without discipline. Wet footwear all day; every evening, wash, dry thoroughly, powder, antifungal, dry socks from the sacred kit.
- **Wounds:** warm standing water is a Petri dish. Seal every break in the skin immediately with hydrocolloid dressings; carry a course of broad-spectrum antibiotics and take any spreading redness seriously. Assume the standing waters carry parasites — minimize wading in still black water, towel off fast, check skin nightly (leeches and worse).
- **Insects:** permethrin-treated clothing, repellent, head net for camp evenings, full mesh at night. Treat this as a vector-risk zone and take your multiverse-standard prophylaxis. The thousands of dragonflies are allies, and watching them work is free entertainment older than the mountains were.
- **Lightning:** you are often the tallest object on a world-sized plate. When storms build, get off the ridge crest, stay away from the lone trees (the classic fatal mistake), crouch on your pad, feet together, and wait it out.
- **The great reptiles:** the rule is simple and absolute — *treat every log as a reptile until it proves otherwise, and remember that logs don't blink.* Never swim in black water. Never camp at the water's edge. Never retrieve a dropped item from the water without a pole. Never establish a routine at the same bank spot at the same hour — these animals learn patterns. In the dry season, give shrinking pans a wide margin; everything is concentrated, thirsty, and tense.
- **Clay licks at dusk:** the kaolin exposures draw parrot-like birds in shrieking hundreds and grazers in the gloaming. It is the one mineral wealth this leached country pays out, and it is their pharmacy, not your spectacle. Observe from well off, or not at all. Predators also know the address.

## The living world — etiquette

This biosphere is ancient, finely tuned, and — because it changes at the speed of seasons and centuries — likely has no resilience against novelty. Decontaminate your boots, boat, and gear before arrival; carry out everything; feed nothing; touch the carnivorous plants only with your eyes (the bladderwort's snap you feel more than hear; you don't need to trigger it). A single spore from another world could be the first new thing to happen here in a million years, and not in a good way. You are visiting a completed work. Move like a guest in a museum that is also a wilderness.

## Mind and senses

Prepare yourself psychologically as carefully as physically. The soundscape runs on a schedule — the dawn rush of birds, the midday insect drone that moves across the plain like weather, the lunatic frog orchestra at dusk (ratchets, bells, dropped coins, two stones knocked together underwater), the night splashes of something large changing its mind. Bring earplugs for sleep; keep one ear available. More subtly: the echolessness unsettles people. Your voice goes out and is swallowed, finished, returned as nothing. Some travelers report a gentle feeling of being erased. At midday, when the horizon dissolves and the far pans float, scale itself abandons you — and in certain light the aligned termite mounds rise out of the mist exactly like the rooftops and steeples of a drowned city, and you will *swear* someone was here. No one was. The plain will also fill your silences with meaning; that is what very old, very quiet places do. Keep a journal. It anchors you in time in a landscape where change comes at the speed of seasons and you are perceiving a still frame.

Do not mistake any of this for hostility, or for sadness. This is not a dying landscape; it is a finished one. The mountains are underfoot as sugar-sand; the rivers of deep time ride the ridgetops as stone; everything patient has already happened. What is left for you is the dusk — mist returning to the channels, the inselberg going violet at the horizon, and ten thousand brief bright voices rising off the oldest ground in the world like steam off warm water. Arrange to be sitting quietly on a gravel ridge, camp made, dry kit sealed, when it happens. That hour is the entire reason to come.

## Condensed kit list

- Packraft + collapsible paddle; dry bags (many); one double-sealed "sacred dry" kit
- Free-standing mesh-inner tent + tarp; groundsheet; pack towel; camp umbrella
- Gravity filter + cleanable spare elements + chemical backup + settling bucket + electrolytes
- Full food supply + fishing kit (protocol use only); fuel stove (no fire reliance)
- Quick-dry layers ×2, long sleeves, wide-brim hat, sunglasses, strapped sandals + camp footwear
- Foot-care kit (antifungal, powder, moleskin); wound-sealing dressings; antibiotics; prophylaxis; repellent + permethrin + head net
- Compass (×2), dead-reckoning notebook, whistle (per person), signal mirror, bright panel, inertial/recorder beacon, rescue beacon, headlamp with red mode, solar + battery bank (double-bagged with desiccant)
- Binoculars and a weather-sealed camera — the dawn mist and the bird lines are the souvenirs
- Probing staff. Waste kits. Biosecurity: cleaned gear in, everything out

**Final note:** plan to stay longer than feels necessary. This world's treasures are scheduled, not stocked — dawn chorus, dusk frogs, the bird arrival, mist drawn like a map at first light. A two-day visit is a photograph of a symphony. Give the plain at least a week of your time. It has given everything else twenty million years.
