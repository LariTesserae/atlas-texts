# Preparing for the Fontanelle

## First, decide whether you should go

This is not a difficult place to reach so much as a difficult place to *be*. An ordinary human body can make the climb — there's no climbing worse than steep scree and one raw ridge — but the bowl itself imposes costs that begin the moment you arrive and compound daily: hearing, sleep, and something harder to name that the keepers watch for in each other. If you are already hard of hearing, prone to obsession, the kind of mind that *must* find the pattern in things — think carefully. The chord recruits pattern-hunger. That is its danger, more than the cold water or the knife-rock.

If you go, go in high summer, and plan a short stay. The keepers' second rule is *no one stays past a season*. Set your own limit well inside that — a week, two at most — and **write the departure date down before you arrive**, somewhere you'll see it daily. The psychology of the place works against urgent leaving. Everything there is slow, growing, patient; you will start to feel patient too. Don't trust that feeling. Keep the date.

Before you leave the lowlands, learn the hand-cant. Someone below knows it — the keepers trade down there, and the cant travels with them. You need perhaps forty signs: *hello, thank you, water, food, sleep, cold, danger, leave, how long, may I, where*. Speech is useless at the rim — not rude, not discouraged, simply *useless*, physics — and pantomime will only get you so far. Also bring your own slate and chalk. For anything complicated, the keepers write; you should be able to as well.

## The approach

You will hear it a full day out — first through your boot soles, then in the air. Use it. The chord is your compass; you navigate toward the hum when cloud closes the ridges. But understand the trade: from that day onward, you will hear *nothing else*. No rockfall warning. No storm building behind the ridge. No companion calling. Your situational awareness becomes entirely visual. Look around more often than feels necessary. Watch the sky, because thunder will arrive mute.

The rock above the treeline is unweathered and will cut you. It shreds hide; the keepers know this and dress for it. Bring heavy gloves — two pairs — thick-soled boots plus a spare pair, and the means to repair leather. Budget extra time; scree that hums underfoot is tiring in a way that's hard to explain until you've walked a day inside a bell.

Bank your sleep the last night below the treeline. You will not sleep well your first nights at the rim no matter what the keepers give you.

And when you finally come through the notch: **stop and watch before you descend.** The basin breathes on a seven-second cycle, and the outflow gushes through that same notch on each breath. Count it. Feel it. Enter on the draw-back, move briskly, and don't stand in the channel admiring the view. Knee-deep in four-degree water is a bad first minute, and wet boots at that altitude are a bad first day.

## What to carry

- **Hearing protection**, the best your world makes — wax plugs at minimum, muffs over them if you have them. Understand their limit: the drone arrives through your sternum and bones. Earplugs take the edge off the roar; they do not make the place quiet. Nothing makes the place quiet except a hush-cell.
- **Slate and chalk**, as above, plus a personal notebook. You'll want to record things, and you'll want the record later when the experience has finished reverberating.
- **All your food, all your fuel, and a water plan.** Nothing edible lives in that bowl — sparse moss, sedge, lichen, limpets, two birds, none of it dinner. There is no firewood above the treeline; the keepers haul or trade for everything. As for water: **do not drink from the font, and do not drink from the young stream below it.** That water is liquid stone, supersaturated, actively precipitating. Ask the keeper (in cant, on a slate) where they drink — they drink something; find out what. Otherwise carry water up, which is heavy, or melt snow on the lower slopes before it gives out.
- **A sacrificial outer layer** and spare everything that might get splashed. This is the discipline of the place: water that meets air lays down stone, and agitation hurries it. A dipped rope is stiff within the hour. A cup left out overnight grows a lid. Spray on your coat will set like plaster in the wind. Brush off splash immediately, dry what you can, accept that some gear simply won't come home.
- **Goggles or dark glasses.** The glare off white churn and pale new stone is real, and there's a subtler reason: you do not want this water in your eyes. Bring a small reserve of clean water expressly for rinsing.
- **Trade goods.** The ejecta — the nodules, the black pellets, the occasional impossible agate sphere — are valuable everywhere below, and the keepers carry the finest away. That's their economy. What they need is everything that must be hauled up: food, salt, fat, fiber, good tools, news. Barter fairly. Do not expect to buy the best pieces cheap; the keepers know exactly what a stone from the inside of the world is worth.
- **Nothing you can't afford to lose, and nothing you wouldn't want preserved forever.** Hold that thought; I'll come back to it.

## Rules of the water

Treat the bowl as a beautiful machine that will kill you without noticing.

**Do not go in. Ever.** The water is four degrees, violently churned, breathing on seven seconds, with a standing hump the height of a man that wanders the bowl and collapses where it pleases — and beneath it all, a shaft no sounding line has ever bottomed. There is no swimming out of that. And understand the particular horror this place adds to drowning: nothing here erodes; everything deposits. Ice trapped against a growing wall is entombed within the week. What the water gets, the stone keeps. You would not be recovered. You would be *incorporated*.

Keep to the grey, hardened stone that rings under the knuckle. The fresh growth — cream-colored, faintly blue-veined, soft as hard cheese — may not bear weight. And learn this trap by sight: the calm margins grow a floating mineral film, pale as fat on broth, that looks exactly like a solid ledge. It is a scum. It will take your foot, give way, and put you in the churn. Any flat pale surface near calm water is a lie.

Watch the water's breathing before you approach any edge, and watch the hump. Everything at the rim runs on that seven-second clock; make it your metronome.

If you want a souvenir of the water itself, here's the one the place will make for you: leave a cheap cup on the rim overnight. By morning it will have grown a stone lid, sealed itself like a canning jar. That's the font's party trick, and a genuinely remarkable artifact to carry home — water from the shaft, sealed in stone by its own chemistry.

## Rules of the sound

This is the real preparation, and the part most travelers get wrong.

The roar is total and it does things to minds, on a schedule the keepers know well. Newcomers dream in noise for about a month. After a season, the dreams change — the chord begins to *resolve*, to take on the shape of meaning, the way a face nearly appears in clouds. Long-stayers begin answering it, under their breath, in the gaps between pulses.

So:

**Sleep in a hush-cell. Non-negotiable, from the first night, whether or not you feel affected.** There are three cells lined with the black sound-drinking stone from the north vein. Ask for one on arrival — this is the keepers' own first rule, scratched on the first slate, and they will not think less of you for asking; they'll think more. Inside, the roar drops to a rumor in the bones and you hear your own heart for the first time in days. Be ready: after days of the chord, that silence is itself startling, and your head will keep producing the roar for a while, the way the sea keeps moving in your legs after a voyage. That's normal. It fades.

**Do not listen for the pattern.** This is the keepers' one great prohibition and you should treat it exactly as you'd treat a warning about thin ice — as physics, not superstition. Don't meditate on the chord. Don't try to find the meaning in it. Keep your mind on tasks: the path, the cooking, your notebook. And if you catch yourself *answering* — humming in the gaps, completing the pulse — that's not a quirk. That's your departure bell. Leave the next morning, whatever your written date says.

On a short, disciplined visit — a week, cell-sleeping, no pattern-listening — an ordinary mind comes through fine. You'll dream in noise for some weeks afterward anyway. Consider it the last of the place leaving your system.

## Living with the keepers

There may be one keeper at the rim, or none. If none: the huts and cells stand open in the way of remote shelters everywhere; use a hush-cell, leave things better than you found them, and be fully self-sufficient.

If a keeper is there, the etiquette is simple. Don't shout — it marks you as a fool and accomplishes nothing. Sign what you can, slate the rest. Pay your way in labor: the whole camp is perpetual maintenance, because nothing there *stays* built. The fitted-stone path is forever being re-laid as the ground grows under it; hut doorways narrow as the walls thicken and must be re-cut every few years; fresh stone is workable with flint and copper, and an hour of re-cutting a lintel is worth more than most gifts. You'll notice abandoned huts half-fused into the rim, slumped like loaves — don't shelter in those; they're becoming landscape.

Two social cautions. Don't ask what the chord *means* — they refuse to listen for it, and pressing the point is somewhere between rude and dangerous by their lights. And don't try to settle the question they won't settle themselves — whether they're there to prevent the closing or to witness it. If you ask, you'll get the open-palmed answer they give everything: *we are listening it up.* Take it as complete. Attend, as they attend. That posture — witness without interference — is the correct one for a traveler too, and it extends to the shaft: throw nothing in, lower nothing down. Many lines have gone down that dark and none has bottomed it, and yours would only crust and join the rim. The aperture is already narrowing year on year, the feedback running one way. Don't feed the machine.

Don't disturb the records. The notched obelisk at the notch, buried to its second ring at nine years old, and the generations of dated slates — that's a centuries-long instrument log, the pitch of the drone kept keeper to keeper, rising a semitone a century as the font tunes itself shut. If you can learn the scratch-script, copy those records. That dataset is one of the great treasures of the place, and it weighs nothing in your pack.

If you have any choice in timing, come after a strong fountaining week. That's when the stonegrazers come up from below — translucent, blind limpets in their slow thousands, writing their tight cursive loops across the new crust. The keepers read the script half in earnest: where it's dense, the rim will hold. Watch them graze; ask a keeper to read you the writing. And watch the two black red-billed birds riding the updraft over the overflow plume, opening their beaks against the roar with nothing coming out — apparently mute, whether to each other or only to you, safe behind their fortress of noise. On most days they are the only moving thing that is not water or stone. You'll find them strangely good company.

Also, walk the rim's outer edge where the silver lichen lives — the pioneer that grows only on stone less than a year old, forever migrating onto the newest growth, exiled by the maturing of its own ground. It exists nowhere else in the range, because nowhere else is there anything young enough. While you're there, you can use it as the keepers do: the lichen is a clock. It tells you what's new.

## Pack out everything

This deserves its own section because the usual leave-no-trace ethic has teeth here. Nothing erodes. Nothing decays. Anything you abandon — a broken buckle, a charcoal stick, food waste — doesn't wash away; it gets *entombed*, sealed in next month's stone, a false fossil for some future keeper to puzzle over. The mountain is still deciding what shape to be, and it is suggestible: those spiral stones, those shell-shapes, those vertebrae that never lived. Don't hand it your garbage as raw material. What arrives with you leaves with you.

## Leaving

Expect the after-effects. Your hearing may be dulled for days even with protection — get it checked when you can; if you skipped the plugs, the loss may be permanent. The chord will persist in your head and your dreams for weeks. And you'll notice something stranger: ordinary streams will look *wrong* for a while — erosive, decaying, water tearing its bed apart. It will take a little time to remember that this, not the font, is how the world normally works. The bowl recalibrates you. Be patient with the recalibration.

Before you go, do one last thing: record the pitch of the drone as best you can, and check it against the oldest slates if you've learned to read them. The font is closing — a semitone a century, the rim creeping up and in, and in a hundred years or five hundred (the keepers sign about it in the evenings and disagree) the soft spot will knit, the roar will stop, and the river will be born quietly from a seam no one finds. The pitch is the clock. It tells you which chapter you visited, and how much of the story is left.

And if your travels ever let you choose your century: go sooner rather than later. This is a place with a timer on it — one of the few sites in any world where you can stand at the rim and watch the world still being decided, loud and white and cold, breathing every seven seconds. Witness it while it is still there to witness. That's all the keepers are doing, in the end. Listen it up, carry the record outward, and leave the stone to its long knitting.
