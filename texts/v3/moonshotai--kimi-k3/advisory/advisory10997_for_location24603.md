First, recalibrate. Whatever worlds you've come from — ones with teeth, ones with weather, ones with hosts who want something from you — set those expectations down at the bottom of the ridge. Header Tank № 7 is not dangerous the way frontiers are dangerous. Nothing in it will harm you on purpose; nothing in it does anything on purpose at all except the plant, and the plant's only purpose is to continue. The hazards here are the quiet ones: heat, warm water, your own haste, and the ease with which an unconsidered traveler can bruise a thing that took five years to grow a single hand-width. You prepare for № 7 the way you'd prepare for a monastery that is also a lung.

The first rule is literal. There is no shore entry, no ground-level valve you may open, no ladder at the waterline. The only way into the water is up the stair, through the hatch, across the air gap, and down. *You climb to go under.* Take it as instruction and as temperament: everything about this visit is earned slowly and entered from above.

And go soon. This is, for all its patience, a time-sensitive destination. You will be visiting during the tank's breathing era — the decades when there is still an air gap, still a distance for the drops to fall, still the tick. When the meniscus reaches the glass, the stock transitions and № 7 becomes a solid green lantern, full to the mullions, and the place's entire music ends. The water is already ahead of schedule — two stencils, maybe three — which means the silence may arrive early. If you mean to hear it, do not postpone the trip for a more convenient decade.

**TIMING AND WEATHER**

Arrive at the ridge in mid-afternoon and plan to stay past dark. The crown takes the flat sun beautifully, the honey hour comes on around evening, and after sunset the tower does its actual work — the louvers open the place to the night wind, the cold north panes condense what they catch, and the drip tempo quickens by a degree you'll feel more than measure. You want to be inside for at least one full transition: light to dark, stillness to drinking.

Check the sky first. You will be climbing the highest point of a bare ridge, inside a steel tower on braced legs. If there is thunder anywhere in the day, do not climb. The tower's discipline extends to its grounding, no doubt, but you are not part of its specification.

**THE APPROACH**

Leave word with someone at whatever settlement you came from — the pencil in the log is bright, which means a keeper comes on rounds, but rounds may be weeks apart, and you should assume no one will look for you. The walk is a bare hour from the new shoreline: no shade, humid heat that makes metal tick, and the footing of a young coast — raw cliffs, unmixed beaches, sediment not yet sorted by any patience of tide. Boots for the walk. Two liters of your own water, minimum, and salt. Not one sip of the tank's water is for you; it is dosed, it is accounted, and the meniscus audits every gram.

Do not follow the buried conduit lines as if they were footpaths, and do not pry at anything staked or surveyed. The conduits carry power and perhaps data, nothing wet — which is to say they are the tower's only tethers to the world, and the world is entitled to them.

At the plinth, stop. Rest in the shade of the drum until your heart slows and your sweat stops running — partly because you'll want your pulse quiet later, when the place gives you silence enough to hear it, and partly out of courtesy: arrive at the hatch cool, not dripping. Change into soft clean shoes here and leave the boots at the concrete's foot. It's a small ritual, but the place deserves it, and you will find you want one.

**WHAT YOU CARRY UP**

A small soft bag, nothing that dangles: an analog watch (nothing that beeps — nothing, anywhere on your person, that beeps), a pencil and a pocket notebook, since graphite survives humidity and ink feathers, a small thermometer, and a cord knotted at centimeters — your witness kit, for the drip-fall measurement. A dive mask for your eyes; the nutrient water stings. A towel. Dry clothes sealed in a bag for the walk back. Thin clean gloves for the hot hatch metal. A red-shaded lantern for the return down the ridge.

Eat before you climb. No food past the plinth — crumbs are fauna-adjacent. No fragrance, no sunscreen, nothing that sheds. And no camera flash; you'll understand why by dusk.

**THE SPECIFICATION**

This is the part an ordinary traveler is most likely to fail, so take it seriously. Hung beside the hatch is a log whose commissioning checklist contains a line that is, in its entirety, the local law: *Fauna: none intended.* And none present — the water holds no fish, no snail, no larva, no egg-clutch. The one animal in № 7 is a moth sealed at assembly between the gasket and steel of an inspection port, pressed flat and perfect, an accident that arrived at exactly the wrong moment of construction.

You will be the second animal. Be a good one. Rinse in the sea before the walk up. Inspect your boot treads, your cuffs, your seams — you are a vector for seeds, spores, and small riders from every world you've ever walked, and this place is a *specification*, a monoculture propagated from a single mother-clone, and whatever fence keeps it pure (the dosing, the copper in the lines, which is quietly algistatic, the sheer newness of the water) is not yours to test. Cover any cuts — the nutrient salts will find them anyway. Spit nothing. Shed what you must and no more.

And observe the reverse quarantine, because you are a multiverse traveler and this is the rule that matters most: the clone propagates. It has been propagated into ten thousand bodies from one stock; a fragment pressed into your cuff will root wherever it falls, including other universes. Take no cuttings, obviously — but also, at the plinth on your way out, check your seams for green. The plant does not want anything. Wanting implies impatience. But it *executes*, and a viable fragment in the wrong world is an instruction you will have carried out on its behalf.

**THE CLIMB AND THE HATCH**

The stair inside the leg is riveted and it will ring underfoot, and it will be the loudest thing you make all day. Do not try to silence it — no oil on the rivets, no cloth underfoot; the tower has its own acoustic budget and your footfalls are permitted. Climb slowly anyway. The metal may be sun-hot; the gloves are for this.

The hatch opens upward. Mind your head, push it gently, and prop it with the wedge you brought and will take away — leave no foreign object behind. While that hatch is open, you are the place's only open door. Especially at dusk, work it like an airlock: open, pass, close behind you if moths are about, because the world's small animals are looking for lit glass, and the entombed specimen in the gasket below is the record of what one open moment can cost. Read the commissioning plaque on the frame — a serial, a very recent date, and no name at all. Notice that the log's pencil is bright. Someone comes. You are between rounds.

**THE AIR GAP**

Crouch. Let your skin and eyes adjust. The air is bath-warm and saturated; sweat will not evaporate here, so limit your first stay to half an hour, then sit on the stair in the leg's shade, drink your own water, and return. The smell arrives in layers — warm metal, wet paint, the faint iodine of nutrient salts, and under everything the green exhalation, vegetable and dense, the inside of a stem. New paint off-gasses in heat; if your head aches, that's your cue to descend the stair.

Read the wall. This is the exhibit, and you can take in everything essential from right here: the vertical column of painted years, each with its line, climbing toward the glass; the meniscus at your feet touching its weld seam exactly, all the way around, as if soldered; the figures in the schedule's margins recording how far the drips were expected to fall. Measure. Time the tick with your analog watch — a drop, a small flat tap, rings going out slow and wide, then a long silence in which your own pulse presents itself. You will find what the log does not mention: the water is ahead of its painted schedule. Record this in your own notebook. If the log's convention permits, add one line — date, meniscus position, fall distance — and nothing else. Do not editorialize. The log does not comment; learn its manners. The discrepancy is data, not an error for you to fix, and it may simply mean that the future is arriving faster everywhere, the sea below the ridge included.

Voices off. Speech inside a steel drum is a vandalism of scale. Whisper if you must; better to point.

**THE DESCENT**

Some travelers should stay in the air gap: anyone with a heart condition, anyone prone to panic in warm enclosed water, anyone alone without a line. There is no shame in it — the wall tells you everything. But if you go down, go down properly.

Tether to the hatch coaming, with a companion holding the line, or a fixed line you can follow back to the square of light. Never hyperventilate before a breath-hold descent; the blackout that follows is the one way this gentle place can kill you. Enter along the braided trunk columns where the stems wind the structural stanchions — that is your route and your handhold. Everywhere you reach, your hand will close on either steel or leaf; choose steel. Do not pull on foliage, do not put fingers through the perforations of the planting racks, and do not rub against exposed substrate — the slag wool irritates skin like the mineral wool it is, and you'll itch all the way down the ridge.

Touch nothing mechanical. The gauges teed into the xylem, the flanged couplings bedded in grafting wax, the valve wheel standing proud of its trunk in a collar of living wood — the bark grown precisely around the wheel's travel, polished where the rim passes — these are grown, not fitted, and the plant's allowance is not your permission. The wheel can still be turned. That is the tree's concession to the office, not to you.

Fifteen minutes at a time, then surface and cool. The water is body-temperature; the boundary of your skin will go to rumor, and that is the place working on you — do not mistake the comfort for safety. You cannot shed heat in there. Headache, a pounding heart, nausea: surface. Between immersions, sit at the waterline with your legs in and the meniscus at your shins, and let the place be louder than you.

At mid-depth you will hang in light the color of bottle glass held to the sun. At the floor, put your face to the porthole: a meter of warm water, and beyond it the machinery level — dials glowing dull amber, a flywheel turning with monumental slowness, everything humming below hearing. You will not hear the pump. You will feel it, a soft double pulse through water and steel, against your sternum, unhurried as sleep. Somewhere on the wall is the inspection port with its entombed moth. Look. Do not touch. It will outlast, in its way, everything living around it — including, on the relevant timescale, you. That is the place's whole sermon, delivered in one flattened specimen.

**THE SHORT LIST OF NEVER**

Never drain — and take **DO NOT DRAIN** in the wide sense: no siphons, no samples, no bottles, no sips; every gram is audited. Never turn the wheel, though it is turnable. Never free the moth. Never paint, scratch, initial, or *correct* the stencils. Never feed the water anything — no crumbs, no coins (the copper already has a job), no wishes. Never use a flash. Never carry a cutting out. Never close the hatch and leave it undogged, and never dog it while a stowaway rests on the rim — check, every time.

**AFTER DARK AND THE WAY BACK**

Be outside at sunset if you can: one by one along the ridge the glass crowns catch the low sun and hold it — № 6, № 5, № 4, a line of green lamps dissolving into haze, each filling, each quiet, each on its own slow way to full. Resist the urge to hike the line and collect them. One tank a day. Each is the same sentence at a different word, and the discipline does not scale by repetition.

Then go back inside for the night's work: the louvers breathing the humid ridge wind, the cold panes taking their toll of it, the drip tempo quickening by that almost-nothing you will now, after a day of listening, actually be able to hear. Stars arrive through the crown, distorted by pressed leaves. The amber room glows up through the floor porthole — the only lit thing on the ridge. If you descend once more, do it then, lantern off, in the green-dark, feeling the place drink.

Leaving: close and dog the hatch as you found it. Descend the stair softer than you climbed it — you will have learned how. At the plinth, check your seams for green; pour your rinse water at the concrete's foot, where it joins the world and not the tank. Walk back with your light shaded red and low, and turn once at the ridge's shoulder. The crowns are dark now. Nothing down there is waiting — waiting implies impatience, and № 7 has none.

**IF THE KEEPER COMES**

Be plain. You signed the log. Offer your readings — meniscus, fall distance, tick interval — and admit the rest. The engineering office is, by its own typeface, uncompromising, but it has built an instrument that teaches patience, and it is hard to be entirely ruthless in the vicinity of one's own best quality. Expect forms, or none. Either way you will be the second entry in a very short book.

The gear, in the end, matters less than this: bring time, and the ability to hear your own pulse without reaching for a distraction. Nothing here will happen. Evening will come on without event. You will be tempted — this is the true test of the place — to *make* something happen: to hurry the drip, to turn the wheel, to fix the schedule. Resist. You are listening to something being finished. An instrument completing itself. A breath held for sixty years and most of the way in.

And since the record of the place ends where it ends — *It has* — let the keeper's silence and the painted years supply the word. It has time.

Pack the same commodity. It is the only coin № 7 accepts, and the only souvenir it gives.
