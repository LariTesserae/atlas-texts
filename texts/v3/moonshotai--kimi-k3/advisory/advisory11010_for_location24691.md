# Preparing to Visit Unit Three

## Understand what you are visiting

You are not visiting a ruin. That is the first thing to get right, because everything you pack and everything you do follows from it. A ruin is safe in the way a corpse is safe. This is a living system — a lung, an instrument, a clock, a closed loop of water that has been breathing for longer than anything around it remembers — and it will be wet, loud, warm, cold, dark, dazzling, and indifferent to you, sometimes all within the same hour. Nothing there wants to hurt you. Nothing there will catch you if you fall. You are an ordinary human: you get dizzy, you get chilled, your ears ring, your boots slip. Prepare as an ordinary human, and the tower will give you everything it has.

## The approach

The plain is the first hazard, and it is a real one. There is no water, no shade, and no landmark except the one you are walking toward. Carry every drop you will need for the crossing — four liters minimum, more if the day is hot — plus your purification method for later. Wear long sleeves, a brimmed hat, and sunglasses; the light off that gravel is like hammered tin, hours of low-grade glare with nowhere to hide from it. The only shadow within a hundred miles is the tower's own. Plan to rest in it when you arrive.

Navigate by the cloud. It is the only cloud in the sky, it does not drift, and it is moored directly over the tower — a flag the place flies for exactly the traveler you are. You will hear the hum long before you see anything, a directionless pressure in the ears, the air idling. Do not try to walk toward it by ear: bass notes have no direction for human hearing, and you will wander. Compass, cloud, and a marked bearing by day; stars by night. Start at dawn. Give yourself margin.

Before you arrive — this matters more than almost anything else in this guide — clean your gear. Every seed, every spore, every flake of mud from whatever world you came from, off your boots and out of your cuffs. You are a multiverse traveler, which makes you the single most dangerous biological event this place has ever faced. The hanging garden inside is a closed ecology with no competitors, rooted in a hand's breadth of manufactured soil that took a century to accumulate. One foreign moss spore could rewrite the whole pattern in a decade, and you would never even know what you erased. Arrive as close to sterile as an ordinary human can manage.

## Timing: the tower breathes, and you will schedule around it

The tower runs on a cycle of hours: the glittering drip-chime, the knock in the riser pipes, the warm indoor rain that roars for perhaps an hour, the long decay back to chime. Everything about your visit should be arranged around this breathing.

When you arrive, note the time. If a pulse has recently ended, you have hours of lull ahead of you. The knock is your warning system — once, twice, a wooden sound inside iron — and it gives you a short grace period before the crown weeps and then lets go. Learn it. Where you want to be when the knock comes depends on what you want:

- **On the walkway:** step back from the pool's edge, put in your earplugs, and stay. The pool will boil without heat; the mist will race up the throat; the roar will erase thought. This is the full experience.
- **In the gallery:** the best seat for understanding the machine — the pumps straining, the oilers weeping, the dials having opinions.
- **On the stair or at the lip:** you will be engulfed in warm mist racing up from below, then left dripping in thin cold wind. Manageable, but only do it deliberately.
- **Wherever you are, if you hear the knock and didn't plan for it:** get off any edge, get your plugs in, and hold still. It passes.

Plan your whole visit so that you are present for the **evening pulse**. Dusk is when the tower is most itself: the crosswind rises and the shell sounds its deep organ note across the darkening plain, the evening rain comes on, and several hundred swifts pour home down the middle of their own storm in the quiet lanes where the echoes cancel. Overhead the one cloud goes pink at the edges. This is the thing you came for. Do not be packing your bag when it happens.

## What to pack

**On your body:**
- Quick-dry layers, wool or synthetic, no cotton. You will be soaked at least once, in warm water — pleasant inside, dangerous at the cold lip or after dark if you have no dry reserve. Keep a full dry set sealed in a bag.
- Full rain shell. You can choose to simply accept the warm soaking indoors — many do — but you need the shell for the top regardless.
- A real warm layer and wind layer. The plain is high, the tower adds its own height, and the lip is cold, thin, dry air even when the interior is tropical.
- Boots with serious tread. Every interior surface is wet; the walkway carries algae and lime; travertine is polished stone with water on it. This is the single most likely place you will be hurt. Spare socks. Light gloves for rails and walls.

**For your ears:** several pairs of foam plugs, on a cord so you can pop them in and out. The chime is safe and endless and you should listen to it for hours. The roar, inside a concrete resonator, is beyond conversation and possibly beyond comfort — plugs for the pulse, ears open for everything else. If you are sound-sensitive, add over-ear muffs.

**For light:** two headlamps and spare batteries. The stair through the shell is slit-lit dark. The gallery has lamps — moths orbit them — but you are not counting on a machine's hospitality for your safety. A red mode is a kindness to the bats if you have one.

**For health:** a compact first-aid kit with blister care and antiseptic; any personal medication; hand sanitizer; and a **fitted respirator** (N95/P100 class). See the health section — this is not optional decoration. Cover every cut and scrape before you enter the wet zones.

**For water and food:** your crossing water, a filter plus purification tablets, and all of your food. Nothing in the tower is yours to eat. A thermos of something hot is a small miracle at the lip.

**For memory:** a waterproof notebook and a pencil — pens fail on damp paper — and a camera in a dry bag with desiccant and multiple lens cloths, because your optics will fog moving between the cold exterior, the warm wet interior, and the cold lip. And a **watch with an alarm**, because the tower eats your sense of time. If your way home runs on a window, set the alarm. Travelers lose whole afternoons to the drip-chime without noticing; it never repeats and it never stops, and the brain simply stops counting.

**If you stay the night:** a waterproof bivy sack, not a tent — there is nothing to pitch and nowhere to pitch it — and a closed-cell pad to sit on. You will want to sit on that walkway for hours.

**Do not bring:** soap or anything scented, speakers or instruments, drones (the swifts own that air; your machine is a trespass inside an instrument), pets, plants, weapons, or anything you would grieve to lose to water. Bring nothing you intend to leave behind, because of the loop's one rule: *nothing leaves.* Whatever you give the tower, it keeps, circulates, and re-breathes forever. Give it nothing.

## Conduct inside

Enter quietly. The shell cannot leave a sound alone — a whisper comes back to you multiplied, and a dropped water bottle rings like a bell in a church. You are a guest inside an instrument. Play it gently, briefly, if at all.

On the walkway, watch your footing and keep a respectful distance from the pool's edge. The water is black, mirror-still, and beautiful, and it is also the reservoir of a working machine, with pipe-mouths and intakes, and it boils during the pulse. **Do not swim. Do not wade. Do not toss a coin.** It is not a wishing well; it is a circulatory system, and your wish would go around and around among the pump seals for the next century.

**Touch nothing mechanical.** No valve-wheels, no switches in the dial room, no "just to see." The governor is a float and a siphon — a balance, not a control panel — and it has set its own pulse by its own body since before anyone alive was born. You are not qualified to adjust it, and neither is anyone else, anymore, anywhere. The needles still have opinions. Let them.

In the gallery: respirator on, don't stir dust, step around the spiders webbing the valve-wheels — their webs bow outward on the tower's exhalation and they are, among other things, the barometers of the place. Admire the green paint that exists nowhere else. Photograph the maker's plate. And leave the wrench. It is half-swallowed by flowstone, a fossil in progress, and taking it would be robbing a geology.

The hanging garden: do not touch it. Where the stair passes the ring at arm's length — dripping, smelling of orchid and lime and guano — keep your arms to yourself. Never pick anything. The soil is a century deep and a hand's breadth thick; one grasp erases decades.

Here is a gift most visitors miss: **the vegetation is the shape of the sound.** The ring grows lushest where the roar presses hardest against the wall. So if the chime ever becomes too much — and it can — walk the walkway until you find the sparse arc, the thin place in the ferns. That is where the sound cancels. That is the quiet seat. Watch the swifts and they will teach you the same lesson: they hold to the quiet nodes in the open air, the lanes where they can hear their own screams. The birds have already mapped the acoustics for you. Follow their homework.

## The stair and the lip

Climb during the lull, in the long calm after a pulse's decay, and you will have hours. Go slowly — the plain is already high and the tower stacks its own height on top; your breath will be short and that is normal, but it is also information. Headlamp on. One hand for the rail. The stair is drippy near the ring and genuinely dark.

At the top, put your wind layer on *before* you emerge. The air at the lip is thin, cold, and dry, and the wind can rise hard at dusk. The rim handrail is bright where hands would slide along it, though no hands come — test it anyway out of ordinary prudence, and then trust it. This structure stopped eroding long ago and started accreting; it maintains itself. It is in better faith than most things you have leaned on.

Do not lean over the inner throat. Vertigo works upward here as well as down — the shaft of light, the wheeling birds, the disc of sky — and if the scale of the void takes your balance, simply sit down. There is no shame in viewing a cathedral from the floor. The hawk will be hanging on the updraught at the tower's shoulder, below your feet. Throw nothing. Everything you drop is either a gift to the loop or a hazard to the plain, and both are thefts of a kind.

Before descending into the wet zone, listen for the knock, or check your watch against the cycle you noted. Then come down the way you went up: slowly, lit, holding the rail.

## Health, honestly stated

- **The aerosol question.** In your world, warm misted water in great towers is a famous breeding ground for a pneumonia that rides the droplets. Assume this tower can do the same. For healthy lungs the risk is modest; if your lungs or immune system are compromised, wear the respirator indoors — especially during the pulse, when the mist races — or admire from the doorway.
- **The guano question.** A few hundred swifts above, bats in the gallery by night. Their dust can carry a fungus with an old and unglamorous reputation. Respirator in the gallery, hands washed before food, no eating inside the shell.
- **The water.** It is perhaps the oldest water in the world, endlessly re-breathed — which means it has been *everywhere* in that tower: through the mist that bathes the nests, past the oil-film where the iridescent bacteria graze, through the pump seals for a hundred years. The romance of drinking it neat is real and must be resisted. Filter and treat it, and then drink it with full ceremony, because treated or not, it is extraordinary.
- **The isolation.** There is no rescue. The door has never locked because no one has ever needed to be kept out or in. Tell someone where you have gone, in whatever terms your situation allows, and confirm that your way home functions before you need it — whatever door, bridge, or itinerary brought you, test the return while you are calm and fed, not at midnight in the rain cycle.

## The night, if you stay

You have two good options. Outside, on one of the dead slabs: dry, quiet, the plain's enormous sky, the organ note drifting over you at dusk, and dawn light catching the vapor-flag. Inside, on the walkway: full immersion — dew settling on your bivy, the disc of sky overhead gone to stars, moonlight leaning down the throat where the sun-shaft leaned, the chime all night, the pulse at some hour you will sleep through if your plugs are good. Both are correct. Inside is the one you will describe badly for the rest of your life.

Set your alarm anyway. The only clock in the place is the sun-spot crossing the ring, and it runs on days, not hours.

## The inner preparation

Some of what you will feel is physiology, and it helps to know that in advance. The hum on the approach, with no direction, can produce unease. The deep organ tone at dusk can produce awe or dread or the strong sense of a presence — low-frequency sound does this to ordinary humans in cathedrals and engine rooms alike. It is resonance, not haunting. You may find, at dusk, that you do not care about the distinction. That is also normal.

The roar, when it comes, erases thought. Some travelers panic in it. If you fear you are one of them: feet wide, hand on the rail, eyes on one fixed point, and breathe with the long decay as it comes back down through lesser rain to drips to the chime. Other travelers weep. Both responses are correct, and neither requires apology.

And be warned about the time. Nothing here hurries, and after a few hours neither will you. The drip-chime never repeats and never stops, and the part of your mind that counts things simply gives up, and then the afternoon is gone. If you have obligations — a window home, a companion waiting, a heart condition about dusk — machine your way out of it: alarms, notes, plans made in advance by the person you were before the tower got to you.

## A suggested day

Cross at dawn by the cloud. Arrive early afternoon; rest in the tower's shadow, the only shade on the plain. Walk the base — four minutes — then the bones: the paper-colored slabs, the bolt-holes in their unreadable script, the rail spur that goes fifty meters and stops. Enter in the afternoon; learn the walkway; find your quiet seat by reading the ferns; take the gallery with your respirator and your respect. Be on the walkway at dusk with your plugs ready: the organ note, the knock, the weeping crown, the roar, the swifts pouring home through the storm they were born for. Sleep. Wake to the chime. Climb to the lip in the morning lull, stand above the hawk, look down the throat once, carefully. Descend before the next knock. Leave with the light on your marked bearing, and listen as the hum thins behind you into silence — long before the little moored cloud sinks below the horizon.

## Last thing

The tower needs nothing from you. It cools its own blood, completely, and it has no product but itself. So prepare until you need nothing from it either — your own water, your own warmth, your own way home, your own calm. That is the only courtesy a place like this can be paid: to arrive self-sufficient, to touch nothing, to add nothing, to take nothing away but wet boots, ringing ears, and a description that will fail you every time you try it.

Bring the notebook. The sound does not survive being carried — but you will try to carry it anyway. Everyone does.
