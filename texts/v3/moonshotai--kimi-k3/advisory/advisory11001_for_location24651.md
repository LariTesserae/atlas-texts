# Preparing for the Felt: A Traveler's Briefing

Most places, you visit. The Felt, you are permitted upon. Understand that distinction before anything else: this is not a landscape but a tissue — one organism the size of a country, plumbed, pressurized, and perpetually working. You will be a speck of warm chemistry moving across a body that processes everything it is given, given time. It is not hostile. Hostility requires noticing you. Your safety depends on physics, attention, and humility — in that order.

Here is how to go and come back.

---

## 1. Internalize the Rules Before You Pack

Every survival practice for the Felt derives from a handful of facts. Repeat them until they're reflexes:

- **The only warmth is the Felt's breath.** All comfort you will have is borrowed from the organism. The vents are your hearths, your water distilleries, your lighthouses.
- **Silence is a held breath.** Every hazard in this world announces itself once, and the announcement is an *absence*. You are not listening for danger sounds. You are listening for the sound to stop.
- **Distance is a rumor.** No shadows, sourceless light, a breathing horizon. Your eyes will lie to you hourly. You will navigate by instruments, logs, and the lean of steam — never by impression.
- **Everything issued is recalled.** Nothing you see is guaranteed to still exist at dusk. Photograph fast, camp nowhere that things come up, and expect your view to change.
- **The archive is perfect and you are archivable.** Anything that dies in the Felt is kept, intact, forever — and may walk back out some future century. This is the metaphysical argument for conservative margins: do not die there. It has more consequences than usual.

---

## 2. Physical and Mental Preparation

**Condition for cold, wet, and spongy ground.** Walking the mat is like walking a saturated mattress for ten hours a day. Every step is absorbed; every step presses tannin-dark water up around your boot. Expect to make 12–18 km on a good day, less in hoof-pit country. Train on soft terrain — sand, muskeg, deep snow in snowshoes — not on trails.

**Acclimatize for altitude.** The plateau is high enough that the cloud deck rests on it like a lid — which means you will live *inside* that lid, permanently, in saturated dimness. Spend a week at elevation before entry if your universe of origin allows it. Bring altitude medication.

**Rehearse flat-light disorientation.** The perpetual November-afternoon glow with no shadows produces something like a years-long whiteout. Experienced polar travelers handle this best. If you've never walked a featureless horizon under a lid, practice somewhere foggy and flat before you go. Learn to trust a compass over your own panic.

**Prepare your sleep.** The throb comes up through the ground — bone-conducted, unpluggable — a slow double beat, all night, every night, with the layered roar of vents, refreezing ticks, and distant animal calls over it. Most people adapt in two or three nights and come to find it hypnotic. That adaptation is itself a small hazard: you will be sleeping *to* the sound of the world working, and the lethal signal is that sound going away. Set a watch rotation if you travel with others. If solo — and solo here is close to unforgivable — bring a sound-level alarm: a simple decibel monitor rigged to wake you when ambient noise drops *below* a threshold. In every other wilderness, alarms trigger on noise. Here, yours triggers on quiet.

**Train the drill.** Before entry, practice this until it's boring: *the sound goes out — you stop, confirm for three breaths (is the throb gone? the trickle? the birds?), then you move, fast, crosswind, several hundred meters, and regroup at a rally point and count heads.* Everyone on the team should be able to do it half-asleep. Because someday they will be.

## 3. Kit

**Footwear — the decisive system.**
- Vapor-barrier boots rated to −40°C (bunny-boot style). Conventional waterproof boots fail here: the mat's water climbs by capillary action, and you will be stepping in pressed-out water ten thousand times a day.
- Knee-high gaiters. Snowshoes with large baskets, or short approach skis — load distribution matters on springy mat, near digestion seams, and over resorbed eruption sites.
- Eight to ten pairs of socks, rotated ruthlessly, with a sealed dry set reserved for sleep. **Immersion foot is the quiet killer of this expedition.** Schedule two foot-care stops daily, barefoot, dry, at a vent's outer warmth.

**Clothing.**
- Wool/synthetic base layers (×3), active insulation, a synthetic parka — *not* down; you live inside a cloud and nothing dries.
- Hard shell for the constant wind. Balaclava, mittens over liner gloves, spare gloves. Everything packed in dry bags inside a pack liner. Assume a permanent state of *damp* and manage it rather than fight it.

**Shelter and sleep.**
- Four-season freestanding tent with a spare footprint — the mat heaves an inch every night on needle-ice, and the nightly freeze-thaw will abrade and shift anything touching the ground.
- Double sleeping pads: closed-cell foam beneath an insulated inflatable. You are sleeping on a kilometers-thick wet sponge at freezing; conductive heat loss is relentless.
- Synthetic −20°C bag with a vapor-barrier liner. Deadman-style anchors — stakes hold poorly in peat.
- A bothy bag (group emergency shelter). In a world with no fuel and no shelter materials, a two-person bothy is the difference between a bad night and an archived one.

**Fire and food.**
- Liquid-fuel stove (canisters sag in the cold) with fuel calculated at expedition rates plus 50%. There is *nothing* to burn. Nothing is even fallen. Do not plan around a single local calorie.
- Full rations, 4,000+ kcal/day, fat-forward, plus a 50% reserve. Hot drinks are morale infrastructure; budget fuel for them deliberately.
- Do not eat anything local. Not the sedges, not the eel-things, not a mower, and above all **not the reissued.** The churn-gulls eat the half-made with no evident harm; you are not a gull. That flesh is bog-tanned, acid-pickled, and partly mat. Consider it leather that leaks.

**Water.**
- Wide-mouth bottles (they freeze slower), two thermoses, purification filter (sleep with it — a frozen filter is a cracked filter) and chemical backup.
- A collapsible condensation tarp and tubing. More below; the plumes will water you.

**Navigation and vision.**
- Two compasses, pace beads, waterproof logbook and pencils (pens freeze). You will draw your own map; there isn't one.
- A laser rangefinder and binoculars — your tools for forcing the Felt's rare events to *declare their size*. A thermal monocular is even better: in that shadowless murk, warmth is truth. Vents, herds, stall-statues cooling — the thermal view reads what the eye can't.
- Headlamp with spare batteries kept warm; a strobe for signaling (signal mirrors are useless under the lid). Whistles — sound carries wonderfully in cold dense air. Use a simple code.

**Safety.**
- A four-gas monitor (O₂/CO/H₂S/combustibles). Non-negotiable near vents and fresh domes. What the Felt exhales is warm; nothing says it's benign, and peat gas is flammable. Check it *before* you light the stove anywhere near a plume.
- Avalanche probe or 2m pole with a basket — you will probe for eyes, seams, thin mat, and resorbed slurry every day.
- 30m rope and harnesses (Mill-Race channels, dome edges), repair kit, comprehensive medical kit with hypothermia wrap, altitude meds, broad-spectrum antibiotics, wound care (keep mat water out of cuts), and anti-nausea medication — the infrasonic throb unsettles some travelers badly for the first days.
- The sound-drop alarm. Listed again because it's the item that saves you.

**Documentation.**
- Fast lenses, high-ISO body, notebook. The half-life of a form is hours; the frozen splash, the stalled reissued, the grey enormous thing weathering like a cliff in fast-forward — all of it slumps back within the day. If you want to remember anything, record it when you see it, not at camp.

**Leave behind:** noise-canceling anything (it will kill you), drones (the lid, the wind), firearms (pointless), pets, and anything you cannot carry out.

## 4. Entry Protocol

Choose your arrival point with care: within a day's walk of a visible vent plume, well away from any green-glowing seam and far from any ripping, whining hum.

**First hour:**
1. Confirm your exit mechanism works *here*, immediately, before you range one step farther. Re-test it daily.
2. Fix the plume's bearing and note its lean. The high wind is consistent; every plume on the plateau leans the same way, which means **the lean itself is a compass.** Calibrate against it.
3. Watch the sun-smear slide along the underside of the lid for an hour. Its daily arc is your second reference frame. You now have a coordinate system, of sorts.
4. Start the log: time, bearing, pace count, soundscape baseline. You will keep this log at every dawn and every camp. It is your memory in a place that scrambles estimation.

**Camp siting rules:**
- **300–500 meters from a vent plume**, crosswind of the steam: outside the condensate drizzle, inside the warmth's penumbra, close enough to dry gear and condense water, far enough that the midge columns and everything stacked up feeding on them are not in your tent.
- **Never within 500 meters of a Glow-Rot seam.** They creep meters per day and the mat there is being actively digested — structurally treacherous, possibly warm, certainly soft. At night they announce themselves in dim green; give them the width of your caution.
- **Never within earshot-drowning range of the Choir or the Mill-Race.** Both are magnificent. Both are masking noise. You cannot hear the world hold its breath over slow chords or a kilometers-wide ripping hum. Visit them, by all means — walk out, listen, be changed — then camp elsewhere.
- **Never on ground that domed recently.** Fresh resorption zones are thin crust over slurry. Probe.
- Move camp every two or three nights. The mat takes down whatever protrudes, slowly and patiently; there is no reason to test its patience, and needle-ice alone will wreck a static camp within a week.

## 5. Movement Craft

Walk the **healed swaths** when you find them — the faint darker strips behind the mower herds are the only trails this world makes: shorter growth, firmer going, a line through the featurelessness. Just remember they are herd routes and yield accordingly.

Probe as you go in **old herd country**. The hoof-pits widen over years into the eyes, and the eyes are the single most sinister hazard on the plateau: still pools of supercooled water, perfectly liquid below freezing, waiting for a touch to propagate. Under the sourceless light, a perfectly still black pool on black-bronze mat has no reflection and no edge — it is a hole in the visual world. A touch locks the whole pool in seconds, and the ice keeps the shape of the disturbance. The older eyes are crowded with the arrested splashes and leaping arcs of everything that ever fell in. Do not add your silhouette. Never touch eye water, never drink it, never step where you haven't probed. If one flashes to ice near you, assume every pool in the field is hair-triggered and leave the area on snowshoes.

**Daily rhythm, keyed to the world's own:**
- **Dawn:** the settling hour — the whole plateau crackling back down from the night's heave, a regional sound, the world clearing its throat. This is roll call. Do it outside the tent: confirm the throb, count the soundscape layers, check the gas monitor, inspect anchors and beacon for engulfment, log, then walk.
- **Midday:** the weak thaw; eyes slump back to stillness. Warmest hours; longest legs.
- **Night:** needle-ice heaves the mat; the Glow-Rot seams vein the dark with cold green, the land brighter than the sky. If you must cross seam country, do it at night when the hazard glows — otherwise travel by day and sleep by the throb.

Count dawns. In a place with no days, the dawn crackle is your calendar, and discipline about it is the difference between a schedule and a drift.

**Water discipline.** You are surrounded by water you cannot drink: tannin-black, acidic, or supercooled and lethal. Your two safe sources are **ice and breath**. Melt the morning needle-ice (filter and boil regardless). And condense the plumes: rig the tarp at the edge of a vent's steam column, where the breath hits freezing air, and collect warm distilled water — check the gas monitor first, let it run a while before collecting, and boil what you gather. It is the only hot tap in the world.

## 6. The Hazard Catalog

**The sucked-quiet (eruption herald).** Throb dips, trickle stills, birds leave. Everything alive reads it and goes; join them. Execute the drill: crosswind, several hundred meters, rally, count, wait. The dome may burst with a gas-driven boom audible for an hour's walk; gulls will converge within the hour from an apparently empty sky — **the gulls are your remote sensor network.** Gulls rising far off mean an eruption has happened on that bearing; log it and stay away for a day. Never approach a fresh dome site the day it erupts. What comes up may slump immediately, or it may walk, or it may keep coming out for an hour — and the geometry of that is not something you want to be standing beside. The Felt exudes more than it contains. Do not be present for the arithmetic.

**The reissued.** Tannin-dark, leaking black water, running fragments of extinct routines at wrong speeds. Most are harmless loops: grazing, courtship, alarm, a few steps, a stall into statue. Protocol: minimum 100 meters, never directly in a moving loop's line, never touch, wait out stall-statues rather than squeezing past. Treat a reenacting charge fragment as a real charge — the body is real even if the mind is millennia gone. If an eruption issues something *smooth and grey and enormous*, something that is not flesh at all: observe from far away, photograph immediately, and do not stand close while a mountain weathers a million years in an afternoon. And prepare yourself, calmly and in advance, for the possibility that an eruption issues something human-shaped. The archive keeps everything that ever ended here. It would be a copy running fragments — not a person, not rescue-able, not to be followed. Document from a distance, with dignity, and go. Talk through this scenario with your team *before* you arrive, not across the campfire afterward.

**The eyes.** Covered above. The rule is absolute: touch nothing that is perfectly still.

**The Mill-Race.** Audible long before visible — a ripping hum with a whine in it, like distant machinery. It is kilometers of slush channels spiraling down into a swallow-hole where the Felt drinks itself. Turn ninety degrees when you hear it and walk until the hum fades. If your route genuinely requires a crossing: rope up, probe every step, cross channels at their narrowest, and accept that this is the most dangerous day of your trip.

**The Choir.** Safe in itself — hollow stems sounding slow chords, drifting out of tune as they grow. The hazards are subtler: masking noise (you cannot hear the held breath over the chords) and entrainment. The throb plus slow chords plus the sponge-walk rhythm can put a solo walker into a trance state for kilometers. Talk aloud, count paces, sing off-key. Stay sovereign in your own head.

**Vents.** The only warmth and the only water, and therefore where everything living stacks up — midges in fierce brief columns, and everything that eats them, and everything that eats *those*. Approach crosswind, gas monitor in hand; establish how far the warmth extends before committing; expect scalding temperatures and zero visibility close in. Bring a head net. Watch the pale birds riding the updraft — birds in the plume mean the plume is breathing normally. Birds *leaving* is a sentence you already know how to finish.

**Mowers and fauna.** The herds are dots at the horizon and slow lines up close, wide-footed, humped, unhurried. Yield the way you would to bison: wide berth, no sudden moves, never between a line and its direction of travel, never behind a herd in the fresh hoof-pit fields — ankle traps and future eyes. Nothing on the plateau hunts you. Your entire relationship with its fauna is traffic management.

**Cold and wet.** Hypothermia at 0°C and 100% humidity kills more reliably than deep cold, because it doesn't feel like an emergency. Enforce scheduled rewarming at the vent edge, hot drinks on a timer, sock rotations, and an absolute rule: the moment anyone shivers *through* a task, camp happens.

## 7. Psychology

The strangest preparation is for the **economy of rarity**: total vegetation, almost no events, days of black-bronze sameness — and then the one afternoon when the horizon offers a shape that refuses to declare its size, and the sound goes out of the world. You will alternate between trance-like boredom and total alertness, sometimes within the same hour. Routines are the scaffolding: dawn log, water ritual, foot care, beacon check, evening entry. Guard them.

Expect the uncanny. The Felt prefers to place its rare events at exactly the range where they can't be read. You will watch a stall-statue for an hour waiting for it to move; you will be certain, twice, that something watched you back. The reissued are genuinely haunting — whole and tanned and wrong-speed, memories of bodies walking. Give yourself permission to be moved and unsettled; give your team permission to talk about it at camp. What gets people in places like this is not fear. It's the slow slide into not-caring that flat light, infrasound, and monotony produce — the day someone stops doing the log. Watch each other for that slide. It is the real expedition illness.

## 8. Ethics and Biosecurity

The Felt is one tissue. Walking on it is walking on a body; the throb in your soles is its pulse. Act accordingly: build nothing (nothing is built here; everything is issued), cut nothing you don't have to, sample nothing without justification — treat any sampling as a biopsy on a continent-sized patient, minimal and sterile.

**Pack out everything, and mean it in a way you never have before.** The archive is perfect. Anything you drop — a glove, a wrapper, a tent peg — does not decompose. It is tanned, kept, and stacked in the deep mat with the oldest at the bottom, and on some future afternoon it may come back up out of a gas dome into the hands of nothing that should be holding it. Littering here is permanent and possibly recursive. Leave nothing.

And on exit: **decontaminate.** Brush down every item, wash your boots, inspect every seam and tread for mat fragments, spores, living lint. Felt is the final form of matter *here* — everything else is on its way — and you are a multiverse traveler, which makes you the one vector by which "here" could become "elsewhere." Do not be the reason another world grinds down its mountains.

---

## 9. Turnaround and Exit

Set the rules before entry and honor them without negotiation: half the food gone means homebound; a sucked-quiet event within a kilometer means homebound; any injury worse than a blister means homebound; anyone stops keeping the log means homebound. Keep two reserve days for routing around a fresh eruption on your return line. Re-test the exit daily. Make the ending boring — the archive is patient and you do not want a place in it.

---

## 10. What You'll Carry Back

You will come home with photographs of things that no longer exist — splashes frozen mid-crown, antlered silhouettes mid-stall, green veins burning under a lid of cloud — and a log full of bearings and pace counts that prove you crossed a country-sized body and were permitted to. You will dream in the throb for weeks; some travelers report standing still in their kitchens, feeling for a pulse in the floor that was never there. That is the correct souvenir. You went to the place where the world finished, where the turbulence turned out to be the calm, where the mountains persist only as depth and everything else is on its way — and for a little while you were warm because something the size of a country exhaled, and you were listening closely enough to hear it hold its breath, and you knew what to do.

Go quietly. Pack out everything. Come back.
