# Preparing for the Cisterns: A Traveler's Briefing

## What You Are Actually Walking Into

Understand this first: you are not visiting a landscape, you are visiting **infrastructure** — tens of thousands of interlocking waterworks built and maintained by living mineral films, at high altitude, in near-total silence, with no fuel, no forage, no shade, no rescue, and ground that is wet even when the sky is clear. It is one of the safest places you will ever visit in terms of things that can hunt you, and one of the least forgiving in terms of things that can simply let you die. Almost everything that can go wrong goes wrong slowly: cold, wet, glare, missteps, and your own disorientation. Prepare accordingly.

## Before You Leave Your Home World

**Verify compatibility on arrival, not after commitment.** The presence of birds, goats, moss, and midges strongly implies breathable air, tolerable gravity, and an Earthlike day length — but confirm with a day or two in the low country before ascending. Test whether your compass behaves. Test whether your water filter handles heavy mineral load (bring a spare element; the milky basins will clog anything eventually).

**Stage in the low country.** The white mass on the horizon is visible from days away, and the locals know it — they use its self-made cloud cap as a landmark and they call it the Cisterns. Ask their stories before you climb. There may be customs or taboos about the plateau; there may be none, since it appears almost nobody goes up. Either way, lowlanders can guide you to the base, tell you current season, and — critically — serve as your check-in point. Leave a route plan and a hard return date with someone who will notice if you miss it.

**Choose your season.** You want the shoulder between melt and deep cold:

- *Melt season* is the most spectacular and the most dangerous: every weir runs full, the milky basins go milkier, and melt-pulse is when breaches happen. Water crossings multiply.
- *Cold season* slows the seeps and skins the still basins with ice. Walking is drier but the nights are severe.
- The shoulder gives you the plateau in working order with tolerable risk.

Know that **no season is warm**. Ice forms on still water before dawn as a matter of routine. Breath is visible at daybreak year-round. Pack for a place where every night is winter and midday is, at best, briefly mild.

## The Approach

The climb from the low country takes days. This is a gift, not an obstacle — it is your altitude acclimatization, free of charge. Do not shortcut it with whatever transit brought you to this universe. Arriving directly on the plateau risks altitude sickness in a place with no help, and it robs you of the single best navigation lesson available: watching the cloud cap build over the massif each afternoon, which teaches you the plateau's daily rhythm before you're inside it.

Ascend to the eastern escarpment if you can choose your entry — the terraces end there in a clean edge above the dim lowland, and it is the finest orientation point on the plateau: a handrail you cannot cross by accident twice.

## The Packing List

Everything you need, you carry. There is no resupply, no firewood, no game worth the name, and nothing taller than a boot to hang anything from.

**Clothing**
- Full layering system in wool or synthetics. **No cotton.** You will be damp for the entire trip; the night condensation weeps off every stone even in rainless weather.
- Hard waterproof shell, top and bottom.
- Insulated parka for the cold hours — you will want it every single dawn.
- Spare base layer sealed in a dry bag, reserved for sleeping only. Guard it with your life.
- Warm hat, sun hat, gloves (one pair for camp, one pair you accept will be wet).
- Gaiters — the retired districts hold dust and fine silt that gets into everything.

**Footwear**
- Boots with aggressive, soft-compound tread suited to slick wet stone. Everything is wet stone. If your world makes felt soles or studded cleats for river work, bring them as an alternative.
- Trekking poles, two, non-negotiable. They are your balance on narrow rims and your probe for testing suspect lips before you trust them.
- Multiple sock systems and foot powder. Your feet will be wet daily; the discipline is to dry and air them every single night without fail.

**Shelter and sleep**
- **Freestanding tent.** There are no trees, the ground is stone or silt, and you cannot count on stakes holding. Bring one that pitches with minimal anchoring; use rocks and silt "deadman" anchors.
- Sleep system rated well below freezing. Pre-dawn ice is routine.
- A footprint or groundsheet you don't mind abrading — the stone is gentle to look at and hard on fabric.

**Fire and food**
- **A stove and all the fuel for the entire trip, plus margin.** This cannot be overstated: there is nothing to burn. No wood, no dung worth gathering, the sedge is sparse and living, the moss is the workforce and off-limits. No stove, no hot anything, ever.
- All food for all days, plus a third again. There is essentially nothing to forage or hunt: a fist-sized bird, a heron once a season, distant gray goats gone before you finish counting them. Treat any thought of living off the land as a joke the plateau is telling you.
- High-fat, high-salt rations. Cold wet altitude burns through both.

**Water**
- Filter plus backup purification (tablets or boil capacity via your fuel margin).
- Wide-mouth containers and a small cup or scoop — more on water strategy below.
- Electrolytes. The water tastes of stone and your body will notice.

**Navigation and light**
- Compass (verified on arrival), altimeter, watch, and a written route log you maintain religiously — the districts are distinctive and a log of times-between-features is your best map.
- Headlamp, spare batteries. Nights are ordinary dark, moonless-dark, and you will sometimes want to move or pee in them.
- Whistle. In that silence, a whistle carries absurdly far. It is your loudest tool; nothing there out-shouts it except the Outfall itself.

**Sun and eyes — do not skimp here**
- Glacier glasses or goggles with side protection, plus a spare pair. **This is the hazard most travelers will underestimate.** Every pool is a mirror; the plateau holds tens of thousands of small skies; you are walking through doubled light for hours a day, at altitude, on ivory-white stone that throws the sun back up at you from below. Snowblindness is entirely possible here without a flake of snow. 
- Full skin coverage, high-SPF protection, lip block. There is no shade anywhere on the plateau. You are the tallest object for miles; dress like it.

**Safety and repair**
- First aid weighted toward sprains, fractures, blisters, and hypothermia. The likeliest injury is a slip on a weir or rim.
- Bivy sack or emergency shelter in your day bag, always, even for short excursions from camp.
- A short rope for moving between tiers and for a companion's extraction from a basin.
- Repair kit: duct tape, cordage, needle, tent patches.
- Earplugs — not for the plateau, which is silent, but for the Outfall, if you go. Near it, the noise is like standing inside a bell.

**For the soul**
- A journal. You'll understand by the second evening.

## Water Strategy

The paradox of the Cisterns: more standing water than the lands below hold in rivers, and you must still think about drinking.

- **The milky settling basins are your last resort.** They are opalescent with suspended mineral — the color of sky diluted in stone. They'll clog filters and punish guts. Fine for washing hands; not for drinking until desperate, and then only settled and filtered.
- **The small clear pools are workable** — clear to the bottom, every pebble lensed — but they are still mineral-heavy seep water. Filter them, try small quantities first, and watch how your body responds.
- **The best water on the plateau is free and distilled:** the night condensation. The cloud cap condenses on the rims so heavily that rainless mornings begin with dripping — every lip and nose of stone weeping. That water has been evaporated and re-condensed; it is the cleanest thing up there. Collect from your tent fly, from drip-noses with a cup, in the first hours of the morning. Rain, likewise, is instantly good.
- Carry two to three liters during the day anyway. "Water everywhere" does not mean "water at the moment you need it," and the dry retired districts — your main highways — hold dust and sedge, not pools.

## How to Move

**Walk the retired districts.** The dry quarters — white, intact, silent flights of basins holding dust, silt, and thin lawns of sedge — are the walkable streets. A district that holds dust, cushion plants, and lichen has been off duty for centuries and is not going to refill this week. These are your roads, your campsites, your sanity.

**In the wet quarters, slow down to the pace of the place.** Rims are your paths, and they are narrow, rounded, wet, and sometimes undercut by the very overflow that built them. Probe with a pole before you trust a lip. Never hurry a weir crossing — the spill-lips are worn smooth as poured glass and the water moving over them is thin, silent, and stronger than it looks. A slip means, at best, a cold bath in an ankle-deep plaza; at worst, a fall down a flight of terraces or into a deep round cistern of near-freezing water, alone.

**Avoid the slots.** The narrow shadowed channels between old terraces — alley-width, water moving in darkness — are the plumbing, not the streets. Cold, slick, constricted, and no reason to enter.

**Do not walk the elevated channels.** The raised causeways where water crosses the land on levees of its own making are among the most beautiful things you will ever see. Admire them from alongside. Their banks are self-built deposit, and a fall from one puts you in moving water with stone walls.

**Expect a modest daily distance.** Constant short climbs and descents between tiers, slick footing, route-finding among lookalike basins, and the sheer time-cost of staring — eight to twelve kilometers equivalent is a good day. Plan your food around that, not around low-country pace.

**Learn the daily rhythm and ride it:**
- *Pre-dawn:* bitter cold, ice skins ticking and knocking on the still basins. Stay in your bag until the light comes.
- *Morning:* the best walking. Cold, clear, stable.
- *Midday:* evaporation rises in long streets aligned with the terrace grain — shimmering mirage-streets that complicate distance judgment.
- *Afternoon:* the cloud cap builds over the whole country. Expect fog or whiteout conditions at altitude. Be near camp, or be somewhere you can stop safely and wait. This is also when weather arrives — but remember, you can see it coming from two days away on those sightlines. Use that gift; check the horizon every morning.
- *Evening:* level gray-gold light, each basin holding the sky's last color after the sky lets it go. Stop walking in time to watch this. It is, no exaggeration, one of the reasons to come.

**If lost:** descend gently until you find moving water, then follow it downslope. Everything here overflows, eventually, into everything else; the flow lines all lead toward the Outfall, which you can hear from a day's walk away. The escarpment edge, the cloud cap's center, the sound of the Outfall, and the grain of the terraces are your four cardinal directions when the compass is ambiguous.

## Campcraft

- Camp in retired districts, on silted pool floors or sedge lawns: flat, dry, soft by local standards.
- Before accepting a dry district, confirm its retirement: dust, silt, sedge, cushion plants, and thick lichen crust mean centuries dry. A basin that is merely not wet *today* is not the same thing.
- Do not camp in channels, on weirs, or on rims immediately downslope of full basins. Breaches are once-in-a-lifetime events for any given spot — but "once in a lifetime" is not "never," and the hour of local roar is not an hour you want to be in the path of.
- Everything will be wet in the morning even in perfect weather. Accept it. Vent the tent, wipe the fly, harvest the drip for drinking.
- **No fires. Ever.** There is nothing to burn and you are not going to burn the workforce.
- If thunder comes with the afternoon cap: you are the tallest object on a bare white plain. Get off the rims, put your poles aside, and wait it out low in a *dry* basin.

## Hazards, Ranked by Likelihood

1. **Sun and glare damage.** Doubled light, altitude UV, no shade. Discipline with glasses and coverage, every hour, or your trip ends with you snowblind on stone.
2. **Slips, sprains, and falls.** Wet ivory stone, thin silent water, narrow rims. Poles, patience, and no ego.
3. **Cold and wet.** Hypothermia doesn't need drama here; it needs one soaked layer and one evening wind. Dry camp clothes, real shelter, hot food from your carried fuel.
4. **Altitude.** Acclimatize on the approach; know the symptoms; descend if they worsen.
5. **Whiteout navigation failure.** Afternoon cap fog. Route log, whistle, willingness to just stop and wait.
6. **The Outfall.** If you visit it — and it is said you can hear it from a day away, and that near it the noise is like standing inside a bell — treat it as the one place on the plateau that is actively violent. The current gathering toward it is deadly; the ground near the lip is spray-slicked and possibly undercut; the roar after days of silence is genuinely disorienting. Earplugs, distance from the edge, and never, ever enter any channel in its drainage.
7. **The unseen track-maker.** There are prints, sometimes, in the silt of basin floors: broad, round, solitary, made by something never seen. This is almost certainly nothing to you. Take ordinary precautions anyway: sealed food, no scraps in camp, and remember that in this silence, anything approaching you will announce itself — a dislodged pebble clacks like a bell here. Sleep in sightline-rich dry districts and you'll likely never think of it again.
8. **Your own mind.** This is the one nobody packs for.

## The Psychological Preparation

Be honest with yourself about solitude. The plateau reads as a city before it reads as a land — districts, plazas, stairs, channels — and walking for days through maintained infrastructure with no maintainers does something to a person. Some travelers find it the most peaceful place in any universe. Some find the silence, which is a medium and not an absence, begin to press. Your voice will sound wrong and violent. You may start hearing things in the drip-tick and the weir-hiss; that is normal. The doubled stars at night — one field above, tens of thousands of fragments below — can induce a vertigo that has nothing to do with height.

If you can, bring a companion. If you go alone, go as someone experienced in being alone. Keep routines: morning water harvest, evening journal, regular meals. The place moves at the pace of stone; let it set your metronome rather than fighting it.

## The Rules of the Guest

Nothing here is owned, because there is no one to own it — but understand that this makes you a guest of the films, the countless invisible citizens who lay down a grain a day and have kept this city exactly as it is for longer than any memory below.

- **Walk on stone and retired ground. Do not walk on the living rims.** The moss fringe at the waterline is the workforce; a boot through a growing lip is vandalism at geological timescale, even if the scar rounds over within years.
- **Take nothing.** Not a pebble, not a crust of travertine, not a lichen. The lichen grows very slowly and is the only graffiti this place permits itself.
- **Leave nothing.** Pack out every scrap. The plateau maintains everything within itself perfectly; your trash would be the only disorder in a country the size of a sky.
- **Dam nothing, divert nothing, swim in nothing.** The plumbing is the point.
- If you build waymark cairns, build them small in the dry districts and dismantle them on your way out.

## A Sensible First Expedition

Do not attempt a crossing on your first visit. The plateau is a continent's raised back; treat it like one.

- **Days 1–4:** Approach from the low country, acclimatizing, watching the cap build each afternoon.
- **Days 5–6:** Up the escarpment to the eastern rim. Camp in the first retired district. Evening on the edge, watching the light go level and the basins hold the color.
- **Days 7–9:** Inland along the dry flights, a day or two deep: the plazas, the tiered basins, one deep cistern, the elevated channels seen from beside. Dawn ice-tick. Night of doubled stars.
- **Day 10:** Optional: toward the Outfall until you can hear it well — a rumor becoming a bell — without needing to stand at its lip.
- **Days 11–14:** Out the way you came, on your own route log, with your margin intact.

Turn around early rather than late. The Cisterns have been exactly as they are for longer than memory, and they mean to stay. That is the great comfort of the place: whatever you fail to reach this time will be there, precisely there, held in its own state by water working at the pace of stone, when you return.

And you will want to return. Nobody warns you about that part. Pack for it anyway.
