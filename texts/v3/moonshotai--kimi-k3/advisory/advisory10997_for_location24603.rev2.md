# Preparing for the Kettle (Ministry Designation: Column One)

## First, understand what you are actually signing up for

This is not a building tour. It is not a garden visit. It is a **dive** — a forty-meter descent into a warm, vertical lake at high altitude, inside a working industrial facility that has no staff, no visitors' center, and no margin for your improvisation. The moment the lock closes over you, you are a guest in a drowning room that happens to be beautiful. Every preparation flows from that fact.

If you are not a diver, you can still go — the exterior is worth the journey on its own, and through a handspan of double glass you can see the tiers, the carriage, the light. But the interior is the point, and the interior requires certification. Get it before you travel. Advanced open-water at minimum, and if you can add an altitude-diving specialty, do. This is exactly the dive that specialty exists for.

## Timing: three calendars to consult

**The steeping calendar.** Four times a year, the instruments judge the batch ready and a tenth of the column is drawn down into tankers while cold spring water floods in at the top. Do not be in the water during draw-off or for several days after — there are currents toward the base valves, and the cold influx sheeting down through the warm column creates shimmering thermoclines that will toy with your buoyancy and the gyre's patient clockwork. Ask the ministry for the schedule. If the ministry is slow, ask the tanker drivers. The drivers know everything the ministry knows, sooner, and they call the place the Kettle, which will make them like you more.

The week *before* a draw-off is the connoisseur's choice: the steep at its most mature, the water tasting most strongly of mineral and cut stem. Mid-quarter is the safest and most stable. Choose according to your temperament.

**The weather calendar.** Go in the dry season. The tower's ceiling is open water — a rooftop pond — and weather happens *overhead* there. Ordinary rain is a marvel (a soft applause you feel in your sternum rather than hear), but a mountain thunderstorm while you are submerged in a forty-meter alloy-and-glass lightning rod full of electrolyte is not an experience to collect. Check the forecast; hold a weather abort criterion and honor it.

**The light calendar.** Plan your dive to straddle noon. Morning comes through the east glass in blades; dusk is a long green afterglow; but noon is the cathedral service — the sun driving a bright core straight down the tower's axis, a second column inside the first, ten thousand fronds leaning toward it like a crowd facing a speaker. Be mid-column at noon. You can do a second dive at dusk if you want to be inside the glow while the plateau outside goes dark — bring lights for the exit.

## Altitude: the invisible hazard stacked on the visible one

The Kettle stands on the rim of high country, above the valleys, with clouds pressing its glass at mid-height. Assume at least 2,500 meters until someone tells you otherwise. This changes everything about the dive physiology:

- **Arrive and wait.** Ascend to altitude, then give your body a full 24 hours — ideally 48 — before you enter the water. Camp near the pump-house (nights are cold; bring real layers) or in the nearest high settlement. Do not sleep in the valley and drive up to dive the same morning.
- **Use a dive computer with an altitude mode**, and know how to confirm it's set. Sea-level tables will lie to you here.
- **Dive conservatively.** Treat 30 meters as your working depth, with brief excursions to the base at 40. The nearest recompression chamber is far away, down switchbacks, in the valleys or beyond. Your safety margin is the only one you'll have.
- **The drive home is kind.** Descending to the valleys after diving is physiologically benign — it's ascending afterward that would hurt you. The geography of this trip is conveniently arranged. Take the hint.

## The gear list

**Dive kit:**
- Full scuba, your own cylinders filled in the valleys — do not count on a fill station at the site
- Dive computer (altitude mode) plus a backup timer and depth gauge
- **Two cutting tools**, carried in different places — a line cutter and shears. This is the single most important line on this list
- 3mm wetsuit or dive skin — the water is blood-warm — plus thin gloves for the bright new weld beads on the racks
- Primary and backup lights (the dosing chapel at the base is dim; dusk and night are dark)
- A slate or wetnotes
- Mask that seals perfectly; a spare
- Everything streamlined, everything lanyarded. *Nothing dangling.* More on why below

**Topside kit:**
- Warm layers, rain shell, sun protection (the mountain UV is real — but never wear sunscreen or lotions into the water; more on that below too)
- Water and food; there is nothing to buy at the rim. A thermos. A dry robe for the cold wind on wet skin after the dive
- Satellite messenger — you are beyond reliable help
- First aid, and an oxygen kit if you can possibly arrange one
- Ear drops for after; warm curated broth in the ear canal is an invitation
- Permits, paperwork, cash for the vintage afterward

**Multiverse-specific:**
- **Quarantine your gear.** Clean and fully dry everything before you arrive. The tower's biome is curated to the microbe — every strain dosed, filtered, logged — and you are a contamination event wearing a wetsuit. What you carry in from another world, their filters were never designed for. And reverse the protocol when you leave: the broth's engineered microbes don't belong in the next world's water either.
- Ordinary, unremarkable kit. This is a world of ministries and tanker drivers and quarterly vintages — low magic, high craft. The tower is engineering, not sorcery. Treat it as infrastructure, respect its operators, and don't assume any rule from any other world applies. You cannot breathe the water. The plants will not commune with you.

## The people

- **The ministry** issues the permission and holds the drawings. Use the words "Column One" on forms.
- **The drivers** haul the vintage and know the road, the weather, the schedule, and the truth. Use the words "the Kettle" with them. Buy one lunch.
- **The filling crew** left a yellow rain-slicker hooked by the lock a year ago and never came back for it. Leave it there. It is the site's only human artifact and, practically speaking, a landmark.
- **The harvest carriage** is the only resident keeper — a slow yellow machine on an overhead rail that prunes constantly. You will feel it before you see it: a low chord through the water. When you feel the chord, find it, and yield the aisle. It will not yield to you. It is not cruel; it is a tram.

Bring a buddy. Bring, if at all possible, a **topside tender** — someone who stays dry in the pump-house, minds the schedule, and can call down the valley if things go wrong. The site is unstaffed. The log-plate prints lines no one reads daily. If you get in trouble, no one will know until the tanker next comes up the switchbacks. Two or three divers total is the right size; the aisles are a body wide and you will move single file.

## The dive itself

**Entry** is through the pump-house lock — a flooding chamber, like stepping into a closet that becomes the sea. Cycle it properly. Prop nothing. The water closes over you warm as a bath, tasting faintly of mineral, cut stems, and — still, a year on — cement and new gasket. Don't swallow it on purpose. Don't take your regulator out to taste it. You'll taste it anyway.

**Route:** go deep first. Descend the open axis to the base — the only room with a voice, a hum you'll hear in your teeth. See the dosing manifold hanging like a pipe organ's chapel: glass bulbs of concentrate, green and amber and rust, a drop a minute. Read a line off the log-plate; it's the tower's diary. **Give the intake screens a wide berth** — the gyre's slow terminus, where all drifting things are gathered and pulped and returned to the broth. Then spiral upward through the tiers, *with* the lean of the fronds, rising through the green corridors as the light builds, and finish with a long shallow hang among the lantern kelp just under the ceiling. That hang is your safety stop and also the best view in the place: the silver underside of the surface trembling overhead, the sun through it like a coin in a fountain, the whole garden doubled in the mirror, hanging upside down in reflected light.

One question to ask the ministry before you go: **is there egress at the lantern rim?** If yes, your profile is perfect — deep to shallow, out at the top. If no, you exit the way you came, which means a final slow descent down the axis to the lock after your shallow stop. That's manageable, but plan it, vent your buoyancy early on the way down, and pad your numbers.

**Hazards, in order of how much they'll try to kill you:**

1. **Entanglement.** The garden is packed the way a held breath is full. Rope-leaf hangs in curtains; veil-moss furs every mesh; the swim-ways are leaf on both shoulders. Stay in the pruned aisles and the open axis. If you stray into the racks, you are in a hedge at depth. This is what the two cutting tools are for.
2. **The intakes.** Suction at the base. Anything loose — a gauge, a fin strap, a lock of hair — goes to the screens. And understand the full horror of it: *anything you lose in this tower gets pulped and becomes an ingredient in a beverage that sells for more than wine.* Streamline your kit not just for your life but out of respect for the vintage and whoever drinks it.
3. **Narcosis.** Forty meters is deep enough, and this place amplifies it. The green light, the hum, the silence, the slow rotation — narcosis here won't feel like impairment. It will feel like *belonging*. If the garden seems to be speaking to you, that is nitrogen. Ascend ten meters and it will stop.
4. **The trapped air pockets.** Small silver mirrors pinned under beams and hatch coamings — the last of the builders' breath, shrinking week by week. Photograph them. Do not breathe from them. It's year-old construction air, oxygen-poor and rich in cure volatiles, and breathing from a pocket at depth invites an embolism on the way up.
5. **The dosing manifold.** Those glass bulbs are concentrates. A drop a minute into forty meters of water is broth; a cracked bulb against your arm is a chemical burn. Look, don't touch.
6. **Disorientation.** The whole column rotates once a day. You won't feel it, but over an hour the room moves fifteen degrees and everything free drifts with it — including you. Orient by the axis, the light core, the pump-house below. And near the ceiling, the mirrored garden hanging upside down can flip your sense of vertical entirely. When in doubt: **follow your bubbles.** They are the only things in the tower in a hurry, and they are never wrong. Watch them flatten under the surface and slide away in mercury sheets toward the rim — your breath joining the tower's breath, ducted away to nobody-will-say-what.

**Emergencies:** the saving grace of this place is that the ceiling is open water. Direct ascent to the surface is possible from anywhere, foliage permitting — this is a silo, not a cave. If anything feels wrong — a sound that isn't in the short catalog, a vibration that isn't the pumps or the carriage — ascend calmly to the middle of the column, away from walls, and surface. The worst case here is a crack at depth, and even then the top is always there. You are far more likely to be the tower's first failure than its pumps are. Don't be.

## How to be in it

Move slowly. Everything in this place moves at growth-rate or machine-rate; you will be the fastest thing in the tower, and it will feel faintly rude. Fin gently. Don't shed leaves — debris becomes broth.

Take a short first dive. Not for the gas — for your head. The catalogue of sounds is short enough to memorize: the tick of oxygen leaving a leaf, the soft gulp of a dosing valve, the carriage's chord, a trapped pocket surrendering with a soft apologetic sound, rain on the ceiling felt as pressure in the chest. And your own heart, loud by comparison, an intruder. Nothing darts. Nothing startles. There are no fish, no birds at the glass, no eagles — there were never going to be eagles. A garden with no garden-sounds is quieter than any empty room, because everything in it is alive and none of it speaks. Some travelers find this holy. Some find it an exquisite loneliness. Both are correct. Expect awe, expect a strange dissociation — the vertigo of watching a cloud press the far side of the glass *from underwater, from above the weather* is not like anything else — and expect to need a few minutes topside afterward before you can talk about it.

## Afterward

Rinse your face and eyes with clean water; use the ear drops; drink something — warm water pulls more out of you than you notice. Then stand outside at dusk and watch the tower hold its green light long after the plateau has gone dark. You've been inside that glow. Now see it as the drivers see it: a shard standing on the rim, patient as weather.

Drive down to the valleys. Buy a bottle of the vintage through the ministry's channel, not a driver's siphoned jug. The drivers say it sells for more than wine; they do not say what it does. Treat it as an unknown strong thing: if you open it, open it seated, among friends, with nothing to drive. Or keep it sealed — the essence of the place you swam in, steeping quietly on a shelf, is not the worst souvenir a traveler ever carried between worlds.

Sign the log if there is one. Take nothing but the bottle. Leave nothing — it all becomes broth. And go gently: you were, for an hour, the only warm-blooded thing in a held breath the size of a cathedral, and the tower will not remember you, but you will remember the tower.
