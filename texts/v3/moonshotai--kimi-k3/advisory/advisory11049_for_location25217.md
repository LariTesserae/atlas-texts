# A Visitor's Preparation for the Vesicle

Understand, first, what you are visiting. Not a cave, not a pool, not a curiosity. You are visiting an egg. Everything in that room — the map, the cairn, the pearl-chains, the one held bubble — is rehearsal for a lake that will not exist for ages, and the rehearsal is what the lake will become. This means two things, and both should govern you: nothing there is finished, and everything there matters. The only sin available to you in that place is haste. Prepare accordingly.

## Getting There

You cannot enter the way the rain does. The fissure is a finger's width under a lip of peat, and you are not water, whatever you are mostly made of. However you fold yourself between worlds, aim for the water, not the stone — and anchor your return to the moor, the hill, the rain, anything except the room itself. The room is not quite where the world keeps it. The inside exceeds the outside by inches, and the excess grows by a thought a century, and any tether tied to the chamber's position will, in some small way, be tied to a place that isn't there.

Go when it rains. Not because the crack opens wider — it does not — but because the fissure admits rain, and it is good manners to arrive through the same door as everything else. Stand on the moor until you are soaked through. Everything in that room arrived tasting of sky. It is the only passport the place has ever recognized, and you should carry it.

Know this also: the first thing the outside world ever gave the vesicle was rain. You will be the second contribution. Whatever you bring — heat, light, sound, your quick bright mammalian time — becomes prenatal influence. Travel as you would into any womb: clean, quiet, brief, and humble.

## The Clock Problem

This is the preparation that matters most, and the one you will be tempted to skip.

Time runs slowly in the chamber, and slowest at its center, where the thicket is oldest and drinks deepest. Treat duration the way divers treat pressure. Near the walls the gradient is gentle; at the center it is a well. A visit spent hugging the margins might cost you a season of the world above. An hour's drift into the oldest fronds might cost you a century. This is the oldest story the moor knows — the hill where a day passes and a lifetime is lost — and it is true, and you should budget for it as fact.

Bring three clocks: one set to your home world, one that is simply your own pulse counted carefully, and one you are willing to lose. Trust the middle one. Your heart is the only metronome the room cannot argue with — though it may try. More practically: keep your whole body on one contour of the gradient. Do not reach toward the center while your feet rest near the wall; you do not want your hand to live through more of the afternoon than your heart does. Move through the gradient slowly, the way you'd surface from a depth. If you feel a strange lag between intention and motion, a sense that your limbs are voting rather than obeying, you are straddling two speeds of time. Close the distance gently.

Do not sleep. In a place where time is a crop, a sleeping human is an unguarded field.

And set a hard exit before you go — a scrubber limit, a cold limit, a heartbeat count — and honor it mid-thought, mid-wonder, mid-anything. Practice this at home: standing from a warm chair in the middle of a sentence and walking out into cold air without finishing it. It is harder than it sounds. It is, in the end, the whole art.

## What to Carry

**Breathe in a closed loop.** Do not bring open-circuit air. Every exhaled bubble in that room becomes a permanent resident — falling there is a negotiation, rising is a decision, and your breaths would wander the felt for centuries, surfacing late, out of order, apologetic, beside the ears of whoever comes after. Let the one bubble be the only bubble.

**Dress cold.** The water holds at the temperature where water is heaviest — the cold of cellars, of the undersides of stones. A heated suit would make you a gradient, a difference between warm and cold, and the thicket eats exactly that. You would become a spring, and they would gather, gently, to drink the difference between you and the water. Accept the cold instead. It is the one clock the room cannot slow: when the shivering reaches your judgment, you are already late.

**One lamp, dim, hooded, blue.** Shaped like the lanterns, if you can manage it — the room's own noon is small, blue, and weak as breath on glass, and your light should resemble the local weather, not import a foreign climate. Expect the shadows to lean toward it. They are curious, and starved, and they lean toward light rather than away. Keep your beam hooded and your shadow close.

**Bring nothing alive, and nothing scented, and nothing you aren't prepared to leave.** Decontaminate as if entering a nursery — because you are. A hitchhiking snail from your world could become an unplanned feature of a shoreline ten thousand years from now. Some travelers carry a small wound clockwork to leave at the wall as a toll, something that ticks out moments the thicket may sip in place of yours. Others say leave nothing at all. The oldest advice is simply: bring only what you would hang above a cradle.

**Prepare one sentence.** Everything you say in that chamber will be kept — the felt and the fronds hold sound the way a coat holds burrs, and release it late, out of order, for the length of the room's long childhood. So choose one sentence you would be glad to have surfacing in a young tarn's shallows for the next ten thousand years. Speak it once, plainly, early in your visit, as if addressing a lake. You are.

## In the Water

You cannot get lost in a room four paces across. You can only get kept. Remember the difference.

Do not fin. There is nowhere to go, and the floor is a drawing — one grain of silt deep, a shoreline of bays and a point and a shallow reach that does not exist yet. Your wake can erase a bay. Move as if the future were written in chalk beneath you, because it is, and it is. Learn to hover the way the pebbles do: undecided.

Do not touch the walls. The stone is newly split, conchoidal, glassy — sharp enough to cut a hand that isn't there, and yours would be — and the felt furred across it is a living archive. Do not grab the pearl-chains; each bladder seals with a click when it has drunk its fill, and if you hear that click, you are hearing a meal conclude. Do not add a seventh stone to the cairn, do not nudge one that hovers, do not lobby in any negotiation the gravity of that place is conducting. A pebble may take a week to decide to rest. Let it.

Watch the scribe the way you would watch an eclipse: in glances, through something dim. It has been drawing since the room began, and to follow one line properly you would have to slow to its speed — and slowing, there, is the precise mechanism of your doom. The gardener will move like a clock's hand; it is moving, you simply cannot see it while you still want to. Give both of them the courtesy of witnesses, not participants.

Do not settle the question of the coiled pallor in the silt. Root or sleeper, the room has declined to decide, and a visitor who resolves what the room has left open is editing the rehearsal. Some things should reach the sky still undecided. Keep your suit sealed against the drifting motes for the same reason — you are warm and quick and full of exactly the kind of time they have been meaning to resolve into for centuries, and it would be a poor visit that ended with one choosing its kingdom on you.

Expect the water to anticipate you. Causes occasionally follow effects in there: a ripple may find you before its cause does — the backwash of your own arrival, the push of a fin-stroke you haven't made yet. When your future touches your shoulder, do not turn quickly. Turning quickly is how futures happen. And if the felt releases a sound in your own voice — a sound you have not made — do not answer its questions.

You will be sipped. The thicket drinks duration, and you are a dense vintage, decades of fast hot time packed into one skin. Accept small gaps, a sense of having been tasted, the way you accept that a museum keeps a little of your afternoon. If you cannot afford to lose an afternoon's worth of moments, do not go at all.

## The One Commandment

There is one breath of air in that place, resting in a cup of stone near the ceiling, and it is not yours.

It will look like salvation, especially if you are cold and disoriented and your pulse-clock is loud in your ears. It does not rise. It has waited there like a germinal spot since the room began, and when the hill slumps in some far age and the vesicle spills open into a tarn under the actual sky, that bubble is meant to lift, and join the general air, and be ordinary, and be free. It has waited longer than your species for its one moment. One breath will not save you. Do not be the reason a lake is born breathless.

Use it as a compass if you like — up is where the bubble waits. Never as a destination.

## If the Lanterns Light

The next lighting is due within decades, on a schedule no one keeps, so you cannot plan for it. You can only win the lottery.

If you do — if every hooded organ in the room wakes at once into that dim blue, weak as breath on glass — then for one hour you will see the whole rehearsal: the map entire, the cairn standing where the island will stand, the depth marks waiting for water that hasn't risen, the bubble, the pallor, all of it revealed at the room's one slow noon. Spend the hour looking, not photographing; no exposure you carry out will be honest. Watch your shadow. It will lean toward the lanterns along with everything else in that starved place. Let it look. Do not let it stay.

And when the blue begins to thin — leave. Leave with the light, or just after it. The room will offer you, as it offers everything, years of black to think about what you saw. This is the only offer it will ever make you. Decline it. The sentence "years of black to think about it in" is a warning. The moment it starts sounding like a promise, you are already deeper than your tether.

## Coming Home

Exit mid-thought, quickly, without farewell — your sentence was spoken earlier, and anything said near the door tends to snag. The water will close over the space where you were slowly; even your absence may take a week to settle. That is not your concern.

On the moor, check the date. Check it twice. Then check it against a face you love. Before you ever left, you should have arranged this: a letter to a patient person, if your world has patient people, reading *Gone to the vesicle under the moor. If I have not returned, wait longer.* It is the only instruction that will ever be correct about this place. If your world has only ordinary people, pay someone in advance for a very long errand, and hold the door open yourself.

For some days after, expect your memory to behave like the felt — things surfacing late, out of order, apologetically. Expect rain to taste faintly of iron and green bitterness. Expect to be poor company at hurried gatherings and excellent at waiting. These pass. Mostly.

## Who Should Stay Home

Do not go if you are grieving. The center of that room is a place where things do not decay so much as persist, and the offer to persist is one the room makes without ever speaking, and a grieving heart may accept it before the mind is consulted. Do not go to attend the birth, either — if your arithmetic suggests the hill will slump within your lifetime, keep away; no one should be standing inside an egg at the hatching. And if, on a second visit, everything seems to recognize you — if the archive releases your own old sentence beside your ear, if the gardener turns, slowly, like a hand on a dial — make it your last. Should you return a third time and find the cairn grown a seventh stone, set at your height, do not let there be a fourth.

Go once. Go quietly, pay in moments, take nothing, leave one good sentence behind you, and come home before your home forgets you. That is all the preparation there is, and it is barely enough — but the room is patient, the rain is still falling, the lanterns are hoarding their blue, and the shore, whenever it comes, will be the one already drawn. You are only ever a guest at the rehearsal.
