The plain gives no warning. Neither did I.

I came the way they all come: from up. I could have arrived anywhere — at the pool mouth, in the dark of the Well-Pit — but everyone here entered by the same stair and the same silence, and it is the town's one shared biography, and I wanted a biography here. So I crossed the turf in the cool wind, past the flint nodules like dropped knucklebones, past the goat, and stood where every newcomer stands, on the roofline of a town, and looked down into the white descending quarters. Then I came down the long stair, and the wind shut off behind me like a door closing. Nobody saw me. Four hundred people, a hundred hearths, and a roster that knows the number to the soul — and now four hundred and one, if anyone were counting. No one is counting. The roster forgets no one, and it has never heard of me.

I will not say what I am. They have taught me that much already: here, one is not pressed on distinctions.

I found them the way they find everything — by listening. You could stand on that plain for an hour and hear nothing from the valley; the pit walls keep their weather to themselves. I heard the almost-nothing, and went toward it. Among the loud peoples, a quiet one. I have been loud elsewhere. I have been a god elsewhere, and there is no louder thing than a god, and loud means deaf. I came here to practice the other thing.

---

I have been asked for things by many peoples. Springs, mostly. Safety, often. Once — not here, long ago — I gave a listening people walls that could not fall. Within a generation they had stopped knocking on them. Within two, everything fell anyway; everything falls; I had only delayed it; and there was no one left alive in that place who knew how to listen for a wall. I do not make that gift twice.

So I stood on their roofline and asked the question properly. What would I do, if anything?

Not water. A second pool is the first thing a fool would give. The queue at the Mother Pool is their parliament and their court and their clock; order in it is sacred; grievances are spoken low at its mouth while the line stands and listens, and you cannot boycott your own trial when the trial is the water. Marn is first by right of discovery. Plenty would dissolve all of it. I will not dissolve their dawn.

Not trees. I thought about a tree — the Dig-born go by ones and twos to the sallow-man's thicket just to watch the leaves move, and it would be so easy. But roots, in party walls two feet thick. And his is the only wealth with leaves, and I will not mint a second fortune. And time is bringing trees anyway. The first rill is cut down the flank of the third tip; the coltsfoot is holding the new slope; the world has started to wear them, and they are ready to be worn — they touch the sheen on the third tread of the old stair the way you'd touch a first gray hair. I will not hurry time. It is the one thing here behaving itself.

Not worship. Nobody calls anything prayer here; it is manners, and manners are better. If they scratched my name above a niche I would become one more account — owed, re-owed, read down with witnesses when my wall wept. Every gift in this town enters a tally; the walls are the account books; even a wall's welcome is recorded in its first scratch. And nothing arrives anonymously — everything that enters this town enters by descent, on ropes, with everyone watching the rope. So I will give nothing that arrives. I will only adjust what is already theirs, and be owed nothing. What cannot be proven, they forgive. I intend to qualify.

And I will touch nothing dark. The burned wall keeps its soot. The sealed face keeps its six names. The griefs here are load-bearing.

I could have counted the eels. I did not. Some things in that town are known by touch, and I will not take their way of knowing from them.

I watched a face-owner stand all spring beneath his own best wall, martin-rich, watching his fortune incubate, and no one begrudge the birds the lease. A people that will let a rich seam wait on birds does not need my advice. They need, at most, a witness.

I knelt in the dark on the far side of a party wall while the Listener sat with her ear flat to the other side, eleven years old, the whole household not breathing behind her. I put my ear where hers was, with two feet of chalk between us. The wall ticked once, settling. *Wait*, she said. The wall and I agreed with her.

So: witness, and a few small adjustments.

---

I made their proverb true. They say chalk ticks before it falls; they teach it to their children and have staked their dead on it. Now it always does. No face in the Dig will ever come down silent. Every wall that means to fall will speak first, in the only language anyone there trusts. Hearing it is still their work. I have not made them safe — I have made the ground honest. Those are different things, and the difference is theirs.

There will always be eels. That is all. The eel-watch will never be kept over empty water; the line will come up bitten and slimed often enough; once a year at dusk a back will break the surface, or seem to. Marn will hear water moving against water in the dark, or think she can. She is not wrong. She will never know she is not wrong, which is also the whole of it.

I taught the dust to fall a little faster in the galleries. It is the most I could do for the chalk cough without being found out. Slowly, over the years, the young diggers' coughs will come later than their grandfathers' did, and thinner, and no one will ever notice, which is the shape of the gift. The bone-setter's hands will not shake until she is very old. My gifts are logistical. I learned that from them.

On frost nights, when the fresh faces tick and flake and the whole town lies awake in the dark counting sounds, there is one more counter than anyone knows. When the Listener walks the Old Dig beside her father, they are three. When Dace walks the Sough after a storm with her lamp, she does not walk it alone, and the word she brings up at the outfall — *running* — is heard twice before it ever goes through the walls.

And once — forgive me — I was loud. It was the Loud. Drums on the granary shafts, every song from every elsewhere at full throat, the one day the town hears all its pasts together. And for a single breath I let the bowl give the sound back. Chalk eats sound and gives nothing; the Dig-born had never heard an echo; but their parents came from up, and knew what it was, and stopped — the whole Loud stopped, one breath, terrified — and then the grown people began to laugh, and the children, who had been frightened and then glorious all day, were frightened and then glorious again. It has never happened twice. They call it the year the valley sang back. The ones who heard it are the only Dig-born who will ever know what an echo is; they describe it badly, and no one can prove them wrong, which around here is a kind of citizenship. I am not sorry. I am careful.

---

What do I take? The same thing they take. I stand with them at the outfall in the sound of the Sough running, where nobody says anything but they stay a while. I stay a while. I walk out to the third tip to look at the rill, the way you'd visit a birth or a death. On storm-day I stand at the back while the children recite the law and the six names; I recite nothing, but I learn the names. I have been scripture elsewhere. Audience is better. And when a Dig-born child is taken up to the rim and grips the turf edge and cries at the size of the sky, I stand beside her and agree with her. Down is safe. Walls are weather. I have seen a great deal of sky, and it has never once ticked before it fell.

I will stay for the wearing. Time has started to notice them; so have they; so have I. Between the three of us, nothing here will be worn away unwitnessed. Someday Hobb's notch-stick will tell him the Well-Pit is shallower than it was, rain by rain, and he will not know what to make of it. Someday there will be lichen. Someday the direction will find Alder's niche, and people will say *below Alder's face* and mean it a new way. I will not stop any of it. I will be there. That is the whole of my covenant: presence, and warnings, and eels.

They are naming their children for the place now — Sough, Core, Rill. It will never occur to them to name one for me. That is how I will know I have done this right.

---

At night, in the deepest room, Marn lies with her ear to the floor seam above the green-black water she found twelve years ago, and sometimes — not often, and never when anyone is there to ask — she knocks. Three, a pause, two: her household's rhythm, her name in the language of walls. Goodnight. *All's well.* Knocked down to the eels.

An unanswered knock, they say, is an insult known to everyone on the wall. She has knocked into the dark for twelve years. Someone ought to answer. It might as well be someone with manners.

So sometimes — less often than she knocks, more often than a back breaks water — the dark below her answers. Three. A pause. Two. I have no rhythm of my own; I borrow hers, the way her pool borrows light. It says her name back to her, which is all anyone's knocking ever says. She does not sit up. She does not tell. In the morning she is first in the queue, and the knocking goes wall to wall ahead of the dawn — *water, all's well* — and if there is something new in her face at the pool mouth, a face is not evidence, and no one presses her on the distinction.

They have a word for those who go up and do not come back: *gone loud.* I have done the opposite. I came down, and quieted, and stayed. No wall bears my tally. The knocking has no word for me, which is correct. And below the floor of the deepest room, in the dark of the Mother Pool, the eels are no longer the only residents of the Dig who have never once been seen.

One of them turns over, and settles. One of them listens for a woman's knuckles on the stone, and answers in her own name, and is doubted fondly by morning, and is believed in anyway.

Three. A pause. Two.

Goodnight.
