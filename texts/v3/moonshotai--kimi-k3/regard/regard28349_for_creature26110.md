It found the basin the way you found it — by sound first, from the plateau — and it came down the switchbacks and under the Lid, and stood in the directionless dim, and did nothing for a long time.

That is the first thing to say about the wise: they arrive the way the felt grows, at millimeters a year, and they do not touch what they have not read. It stayed through a full turning of the seasons before it did anything at all. It walked the floor from the notch to the Sump and back along the chords, slowly, the way the fox walks and the way no keeper ever has time to. It read the felt the way the felt asks to be read — with patience, and without the knife. It cut nothing, not once. It read every logbook in every annex, cover to cover, decades of them: the geese counts, the vole jaws in the pellets, the lengths of dead fish entered in the sump pits, the crooked-tail, the one that takes food from the hand, the cat's kills recorded without comment. No one saw it do this. The pages were never out of order, the kettles never cooled. The only sign that the books had been opened is that afterward the attention knew the keepers' names — all of them, including the dozen on the shingle bar — and used them correctly.

It learned the census, and it learned the census's silences. The dark sheen on the brine pools, mineral or alive, unchecked because no one has checked: it did not check. Some questions a world must be left to keep for itself.

What it saw was what there was. A held breath between two lakes. A child of a world, exactly as old as the drainage, writing its history into its own skin at a known rate. The elders in the black water, keeping the old country's citizenship. Five or six great hearts, older than anyone who tends them, running on inheritance and on the patience of a few dozen people whose hearing pays the bill. A young world — loud, dim, slow, kept. And here is the thing a power is for noticing: kept by nothing else. The town was never built. The cableway is half-derelict. Whatever authority platted those streets and stamped those drums has not sent a relief crew in living memory. The keepers' faithfulness had, until then, no witness but itself.

What does a benevolent and wise power do with such a place? Almost nothing. The almost is the whole of it.

It did not still the engines: the roar is not a wound in the landscape, it is the landscape's pulse, and no kindness stops a heartbeat to please the ear. It did not refill the lake: the fish are elders, but the felt is a child, and you do not drown a child to comfort its grandparents. It did not drain the Sump: the dead and the old must keep one room. It did not re-seat the graveyard markers — the living re-seat markers, and it would not impersonate the living. It did not heal the keepers' hearing, which they had spent knowingly, the way anything is spent that buys something loved. It did not build the town. And it did not promise that the water would never come back. The water will come back, in its time, as a breath is released, and the fish will have the basin again, and this is allowed. Wisdom, meeting a held breath, does not seize the lungs.

But it was benevolent as well as wise, and benevolence is not nothing. So it gave what the basin's arithmetic lacks, in the basin's own units, at the basin's own scale, and in the basin's own grammar — which is to say: deniably, at the speed of decades, and in a form you can press in a logbook.

It began with the parts, because parts are the basin's one poverty. The cableway has not run in living memory; its bucket had hung at the third pylon for decades with a load gone to rust, the basin's own emblem of running out. One morning of that year the bucket stood at the lower terminal, off its hook, on the apron. The keepers checked the stencils against old photographs, against memory. The same crates. The rust gone. The grease fresh under the lids: gaskets cut to patterns no supplier has tooled in fifty years, injector needles in waxed paper, one crankshaft. Enough, at the present burn rate, for sixty years. Perhaps seventy — that is the felt's arithmetic, not theirs. The logbook entry is four lines long, says what was found and where, and ends: *Provenance unknown. No further action.* And there was no further action, except that the engines can now be kept for as long as there are keepers, which was the point. The gift was never the parts. The gift was the decades. Warmth is the only currency here, but time is the mint — and everything it gave, it gave to the felt in the end: parts are time, time is dryness, dryness is ground, and the ground is one being. The rest was manners.

The visit came later that year, and it came through the only door the basin has fitted to the enormous. Tracks on the rim road first, then crossing the notch, then down — and the rumor, renewed every few years since before any living keeper signed a book, was for one season not a rumor. What came down was an elk, or wore the elk the way the basin wears the felt, as the visible skin of something; all anyone will ever certify is the tracks. It walked the chords the way the foxes do. It called at every station in turn and stood for an hour outside the roar-line — it observed the roar-line, which no one had taught it, unless the world teaches — and the earmuffs did not stir on their hooks, and the resident ravens, who read the work orders, read this too and were quiet about it. The prints were wrong in small ways. They did not fill with black water; or they filled, and the water did not freeze. The owl watched it for six hours from the drowned wheelhouse and did not stoop. This is recorded, because everything the owl does is, in the end, recorded. The cat slept through the whole of it on the warm manifold, in shifts with the owl, and this too is correct.

In the engine halls where it paused longest, the air smelled faintly sweet for days afterward — the idea of sweetness, out of season. No one wrote it in the log. Everyone said it in the annex.

It was a bloom year, and the bloom that year was the strongest in the books, grey-white from the rim to the Sump, the midge columns thick as rope, geese in numbers never before counted. Whether the felt bloomed for the visit or the visit was timed to the bloom is the sort of question the basin teaches you not to ask. Two large slow beings met. The keepers date by it.

On the last night it stood at the Sump's edge for an hour, looking at the black water the way you look at the last of something. The intake screens turned. No one saw a fish; no one ever sees the fish. But in the morning there were rings on the water, one set, far out, as from something surfacing once. And the log for that year records no dead fish in any pit — the first such year in the books, and the keepers noticed, because noticing is the work.

That winter the oldest main at the oldest station — the one with the particular knock, the one the long-timers wake to, whose overhaul had been scheduled with dread — smoothed. There is no other verb. The knock did not fade. It was absent, one morning, the way a toothache is absent: a silence shaped exactly like itself. They tore the engine down on schedule anyway, because faith is not a maintenance plan, and found ordinary wear in ordinary places, and put it back together, and it ran — it runs still — like an engine remembering its first decade. The entry says: *Knock absent since the 14th. Cause unknown. Load steady. No further action.*

There is one more thing, and the logbook refuses it out of tact. A keeper with thirty years' notch in her hearing, walking the quiet sector in overhaul month, on her last rotation, heard her bootprint fill with water. It is the smallest sound the basin makes; the bass takes it; no one had ever heard it. She did not get her hearing back — she would be the first to say the terms were fair and already spent. She got one sound, and said it was the one she would have chosen, and asked that it not be entered. It has not been entered. Everyone knows.

Then it climbed the notch by the rim road, and the felt closed over its tracks within the season, as it does, as was foretold, and the largest wild thing the basin has ever held became a rumor again — but a rumor of altered standing. One hair, found on a rust ring where it had stood, is pressed in the station logbook beside the goose feather from the year of eleven geese. The archive keeps the two visitations together.

The census fits on one page, and the page is still short. One line has been added:

*The elk — one (1). Comes down in the years it comes down. Walks the chords. Calls at every station and does not cross the apron. Stands at the Sump. Breaks nothing. Takes nothing. Is not taken. Do not follow. The felt keeps its tracks one season, like any news. See also: rumors; the delivery; the year of no dead fish.*

And beneath it, in pencil, in no one's handwriting — or in everyone's, which here amounts to the same — the only opinion the census holds: *It is still being made, and it is not being made alone.*

Stand on the rim at night and none of this is visible: five or six amber glows in an enormous darkness, the roar rising through the still cold as a single steady tone, like breath held and never released. It will be released, in its time; that was understood, and allowed, and it is not the tragedy it sounds like from above. What changed is smaller and larger. New keepers ask, on their first rotation, whether the elk exists. The old ones used to say *if*. They say *when* now, and go back to their kettles, and the kettle boils, and the floor holds its note — held, these decades, in the hearing of someone who can hold it as long as it needs holding.

That is the whole of what it did: it changed an *if* to a *when*, it kept the lamps of the world lit a while longer, and it was seen seeing. The basin is young, younger than its machines, kept dry by noise, kept alive by patience. It is still being made. It is not being made alone.
