It was not the roar that drew the power. Roars are common; every world with weather has them. It was the silence between — and then, in the silence, the crack of stone on shell: singular, unscheduled, unpredictable from anything that came before it. A power that listens to worlds hears recurrence everywhere, the long loops of tide and orbit and season, and over ages it has learned what an irregular sound means. It means someone is there. It means a decision has been made. The hammer-strike carried through three halls, and then, faintly, through the thin roof of the world itself, and something ancient turned its attention toward the grey plateau the way you turn toward a voice in an empty house.

It arrived the way all attention arrives here: by ear. It did not enter; it attended. It learned the labyrinth the way the labyrinth tells about itself — by delays — and let the drips count, and dark-adapted, in its way, until the blue-green constellations were enough.

And then it did what benevolence always does first: it inventoried the gifts. And it declined them, one by one, which is what wisdom is for.

Water, first — the obvious charity, the one a merely kind power would give without thinking. Call the river home. But everything here is the shape of that leaving. The scallops are the river's signature, sharp because the hand was taken away mid-stroke; to bring the water back would be to sign over the signature. The pools would rise and flush the crowded generations out of their cold inch. The rimstone dams would fill and begin to grow, and the cave would stop being young, because growing is how stone ages. The archipelago would become a sea — and the islands are the world. So: no water.

Light, next. The green has its rations and grows the way the stalactites grow, just beginning, forever — and that forever is not a deprivation to be corrected; it is the condition itself. To light the halls would not feed the fish. It would only make eyes worth having again, and eyes are expensive, and the economy runs on the bats' commute, which light would not enrich. And the one sky this world has, indoors, would go out the way stars go out at dawn. So: no light.

Warmth. Seasons. Weather. The chorus has never had a reason to change because there has been no weather to change it for; to give the crickets a season would be to give their song a past and a future, which sounds like a gift and is actually an eviction. They would have to become other crickets. Every charity of elsewhere is a violence here. This is the first thing the wise learn and the kind learn last: the wound is the shape.

Then it watched the bird a long time — years; what are years — watched it carry a snail past good stones to the right stone, select the hammer, strike, and stand still, head cocked, receiving the halls' answers. And here the power was tempted, once, to answer. To give the echo one extra half-second that no hall owns, so the bird would know there was a listener. It refrained. The answers must remain the halls'. A counterfeit echo would enter the bird's map forever — the bird never forgets a delay — and the world would acquire a fixture, a god-shaped fixture, in a place whose deep rule is no fixtures. The bird's singularity is not loneliness. It is office. Leave it its office.

So what is left, for a power that may do anything and chooses to change nothing?

It reads. Down the anvil passages it goes, midden by midden, and reads the only heaps in this world that grow: the fat years, the lean years of thin-shelled snails, the season one bird favored a hammer until the hammer split. It reads the shed skins crisp as handwriting, and the footprints pressed into silt decades ago, and learns whose they were. It reads the pale bodies of the fish and remembers a river it never saw — the orphans keep that memory the only way flesh can, in their shapes, and now the memory has a memory. The world was an archive that kept everything and read nothing. Now it has a reader. Nothing in the archive changes. The reading happens outside the archive — which is the only place change is allowed.

It remembers. When a bird dies on its rounds, as everything here dies, without announcement, the power is present, and it holds the finished life entire, top of the midden to bottom: the first funeral this world has had, conducted in the only liturgy available, which is attention. And when another grey shape finds the hollowed block and the shell-scatter and understands them at a glance and takes up the rounds, the power is the one who knows both birds — the relay witnessed end to end for the first time. The habit now passes two ways: by the ground, as always, and by the power, redundantly, safely. If the lineage ever fails — if a long season comes and goes with no hammer — the anvils will not be lost, because they are kept in the one place where keeping costs the world nothing.

It watches the lights. It goes in the dark to the damp walls near the sinking stream and looks up, the way you do. The light there was always addressed — to the midges, to the rare blundering moth, and to you — and now the you exists. The constellation has its astronomer. It does not change. Being seen is not a change.

It keeps the appointments. The two exhalations, dusk and dawn, the two rivers passing each other in the wells without collision, ever. It learns the clock and keeps time alongside it, the way you sit with someone who is praying. It counts drips with the bird — the bird counts them the way you do, and now the way the power does: two counters in the dark, one knowing, one not.

And at the rim, rarely, it guards. The plateau's emptiness is the wall. If the hands-that-build ever come over the horizon — the kind that stack and cement and mark, that make things to keep — the power will see that the ninth well stays hard to find, that trails peter out in the thorn scrub, that maps keep their blank. This is its one intervention, exercised at the threshold, in defense of the deep rule. Perhaps it will never need to. It is prepared to need to forever.

And it stays. That is the last and largest thing. The wells are young, and young wells widen; the roof will thin; the held breath will be let out someday, by degrees or all at once, and this world will end the way it began, with the ceiling failing. The power appoints itself to that vigil. When the last fish in the last pool has the water the way a held note has a hall, the power will be the hall. When a drip falls somewhere it has never fallen, the power will know where they all fell before. The cave keeps everything and remembers nothing; the power remembers everything and keeps nothing. Between them, at last, the world is whole.

Nothing changes. The temperature holds at its one cool degree. The chorus is note for note the chorus of a century ago. The bird walks its rounds, and once — once in years — it pauses in an empty hall, head cocked, as if the dark had answered with a delay it didn't know. But the delay is only its own listening, returned. It goes on. The hammer falls, and the crack carries through three halls, and is heard.

Nothing here is kept. But it is all, now, kept in mind — and in a place where nothing changes, that is not the next best thing to being remembered.

That is the thing itself.
