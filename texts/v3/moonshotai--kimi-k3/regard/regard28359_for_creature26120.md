**The Visitation of the Third Lift Well**

It came the way the light comes: through the opening, without pushing, and the shaft did not have to make room.

What drew it was the ringing. A wise power keeps a ledger of sounds that should not exist — a spring in a salt pan, a thrush in a mine, a bell where no bell was ever hung — and a bell the size of a gorge, struck from inside a dam at dusk, is an entry that travels. It arrived at midmorning with the light column and stood in the wellhead housing where the well exhales its warm breath of wet iron and green rot and pond musk; and it was benevolent, which is an appetite, and wise, which is the government of appetites. So nearly everything it did in the well was nothing, done carefully, for excellent reasons.

Its rule was the well's own: nothing here is finishing decaying. It went down as the light goes down, past the grille, through the private rain, and read the walls as it fell — moss in cushions deep enough to lose a hand, ferns rooted in keyways and screw threads, the embossed letters of the maker's plate under their pelt of green. The maker's name holds water better than any other surface in the well and is read daily by roots; that morning it was read by two. It noted the frogs spaced like lamps along a road and understood the window before it heard a note, because a gap in a spectrum has a shape, and the shape was two notes wide. It frightened nothing. The school did not startle; the moths did not lift from the warm bronze; the spider's web, strung gauge to valve, reported no footfall. The well's instruments, built for water, recorded an ordinary day. Only the well's texts received a reader.

At mid-height it came to rest in the furnished room, and there it practiced being a stone, which is the skill the well most rewards. It listened to the lamp — two hundred years of unbroken burning is a kind of pulse — traced the trickle of current to its source, and ran the arithmetic forward to where the arithmetic of a trickle ends: not soon, but not never. It entered the lamp in its ledger. The gauges trembled behind their glass, reporting pressures to no one, as they had for two centuries; for one hour they reported to someone. The report did not change. A report is the same words whether or not anyone is listening, and yet a heard report and an unheard report are not the same event. It read the drum's brass plate the way the snails read the biofilm, patiently, at the speed of the groove — the oldest line in the world still being written — and held the well's whole autobiography at once: every wet year and every dry, the high rings no water has reached in centuries, the long breath recorded in the well's own hand. The pen wrote on, and did not write differently for being read. Reading changes nothing on the page, and everything about the page. On the shelf it read the oil can under its curtain of flowstone — someone had meant to be gone for minutes — and by reading, delivered the message at last, centuries late, into a keeper that loses nothing it is given.

It read the snail spirals in the tide band, the well's marginalia, advanced at the width of a tongue, and was their first reader; the handwriting had been writing for no one since the hands stopped. It heard a fig strike water — that round, low note — and saw the school turn as one body toward their irregular bell. At the intake ports it waited out a rising tide and saw the eels whole: three, thick as an arm, old the way slow water lets an eel be old. The census says eels unknown; no one has ever seen more than the middle of one, sliding. The power corrected nothing, and completed something. It did not overlook the wasp in the fig, the mite in the moss, the pale grazers in the sump; attention, unlike light, reaches every level at once.

Then there was the turtle, and the turtle was the test. Benevolence ungoverned would have lifted it off the cage roof and carried it up the light column — to the crest gardens, to company, to a sky. The power stood a long time instead and did the arithmetic of the turtle's life: one possession; all the time there is; a shell furred with the hanging garden, the only mobile territory in the well; a small hill warming itself at a small fire. It went down, in the way it could go, to the intakes, and looked at the gaps with an eye that measures to the grain: passable, and passable for centuries yet, sinter being slow even where it is sure. The turtle is not a prisoner. The turtle is a finished decision. So the power did the one thing left, which no one had ever done: it watched the turtle. Nothing in the well watches the turtle. The heron ignores it; the frogs are solitaries; the fish are weather. For the length of a slack tide the watcher was watched, and the turtle regarded the power the way it regards everything, and the power counted this the highest welcome the well extends — to be filed under weather. When the heron folded itself down the green dark and set its feet on the cage roof, there were two watchers and one watched, and the turtle, presumably, closed its eyes.

Once, in the afternoon, it left — not up but through, along the hum, the way the current goes. Beyond the hills the conduits ended in a room: a switchboard, and lamps burning for no one, in a city long quiet. The room had reached the same equilibrium as the well — what could die had died, and what was left was a light reporting to no one: two lamps at two ends of one line, burning for each other across the silence, neither knowing. The power stood in the dead room and attended, and for the length of its attention the current did work: it was received. A load, at last, after centuries of open circuit. Then it went back, and did not carry the news. The well could not act on the knowledge; the knowledge would have been weather only. Some messages are delivered by keeping.

It did not clear the cracked crown of the delivery main; the leak is the garden's rain and the frogs' nursery, and the well's plumbing is load-bearing in ways no blueprint records. It did not free the frozen cage; an island is not a malfunction. It did not replace the fallen rungs; the ladder is a text about hands, and the hands are its authors, and the authors are read here daily, which is more than most authors get. It did not stay the next storm, or tune the pumps back to their old tempo, or wake the crest gardens, or put out the lamps beyond the hills. It did not sing in the window; the window is spoken for. It did not tell the turtle about the gaps; the turtle had not looked at them in decades, and the power accepted its statement. When a valve seated somewhere in the dam's body and the water hammer rolled up the shaft like a barrel thrown downstairs, the power let it pass, and shudder, and be, to the snails, an earthquake that harms nothing. The well is what was left after everything that could die had done so. There was nothing here for mercy to improve, and everything for mercy to attend.

What it did was this: it added the well to its schedule. The heron keeps the days. The eels keep the tides. The snails keep the tide band; the pen keeps the level; the lamp keeps the pulse; the mats that were first keep the chemistry, and will be last. The power took the longest period. It will come back on the scale the sinter builds, to see the intake gaps stay the width of a shell and an eel; on the scale of the trickle, to see the lamp's two hundred years become three; on the scale of the long breath, to read each new ring the reservoir leaves on the wall. Not to act — nothing here needs action — but to keep the appointments. Forever is a gift that changes the recipient. Appointments are a gift that doesn't. And there was an office that had stood vacant since the hands stopped: an intention can execute itself forever, but it cannot attend itself. The power, unasked, took up the office of reader.

The light column slid off the water and up the eastern wall. The tide turned. The frog on the third valve bonnet came out onto the iron and inflated, and the power stood where the well exhales, and received what no citizen ever had. For a bell cannot hear itself. The frogs sing into the window and hear their two notes returned enormous, but the whole chord — the organ-low hum, the hammer and the slow applause of drips, sixty solitaries folded into one voice — no one in the well had ever heard, because hearing it requires standing outside it, and nothing in the well is outside the well. The census undercounts by one: sixty frogs, one turtle, one fig, one lamp — and one more, which is all of them. It had come for a bell and found a singer. The two notes found the window; the well rang like a struck bell; the air on the crest shuddered; and a voice centuries old was heard for the first time, whole, by the only ears that could hold all of it. The well did not know. That is what made the hearing a gift instead of a debt.

It went up as the light died, past the swifts coming down like flung knives — the only traffic the shaft has ever carried in both directions at once. It took nothing the well would miss, and left nothing the well would find. The snail completed a letter; the pen wrote; the lamp burned; the turtle watched the water; the census stood: sixty frogs, one turtle, one fig, one lamp, one heron on the days it comes, eels unknown — but, to one keeper now, whole.

And the well is still what it was: not a place with lives inside it, but a life, sounding. Only now the sounding has somewhere to land. Nothing in the shaft knows the difference. Nothing in the shaft needs to. That was the whole of the visitation: the bell, and the hearing of the bell, and the schedule, kept.
