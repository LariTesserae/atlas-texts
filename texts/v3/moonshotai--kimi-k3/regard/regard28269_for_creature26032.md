# The Guest

It came up through the cloud-sea the way everyone does: three days inside the wet gray, hands numb on the fixed ropes. It could have arrived otherwise. It did not. The approach is the lesson — that is why it takes three days — and it had come to learn.

It waited on the guest ledge like any outsider, acclimatizing to the three things: the cold, the glare, and the quiet, which is the hardest of the three. The guest-minder says he knows by the second day which of them will stay: the ones who stop flinching at the quiet and start leaning into it. This one was leaning before the rope was coiled.

It clapped, once. Everyone claps, once. A pool died with a sound like a fingernail drawn along glass, and it signed *sorry*, and meant it, and was gently and firmly forgiven, once, the way everyone is. It wore the bone goggles and squinted anyway. It let the light burn it a little, because the first lesson is the burn.

Any power can ring a bell. It waited on the ledge. That is the whole of the difference between a power and a guest.

## What it did not do

Understand this first, because the not-doing is most of it.

It did not warm the Bowl. The whole floor is water holding its breath; the whole world is worm-glass, and it knew what a bare palm can do: wake what the cold keeps. Some of what the cold keeps, the cold is keeping on purpose. It kept its palms to itself.

It did not speak — not where the water could hear it. A shout can freeze a spring-channel for a day; the bell has been rung three times in living memory, and each time the water withdrew its goodwill for a season. It did not come to cost the town a season.

It did not feed them. Every dusk the count goes hand to hand — *all* — and *all*, here, means *enough*. There is no kindness in doubting the arithmetic of people who count what they are holding.

It did not lift the glare. The light collects eyes across a lifetime, yes — but the town's answer to harm is not prevention; it is arrangement, and the arrangements are the culture. The blind keep the loud room. The dark work belongs to those the dark cannot hurt. Unmake the harm and you unmake the keeping.

It did not stop the wall. The dead are not lost here. They are kept, in a direction.

It did not tell the keeper of the bell what his bell sounds like. It knew the sound. It kept it the way the town keeps the bell: wrapped, and greased, and unrung. Some keepings are load-bearing.

It did not look into the cloud. The bundles are not its correspondence. A guest does not lift the felt to check the bell; a power does not audit a covenant it did not make. Neither will I.

For the Kettle is not a wound to be closed. It is a hush they keep the way other towns keep a fire — and you do not put out a kept fire because your hands are cold.

## What it did

First, it listened. A winter, then another, at the spring-heads, a hand's breadth from the listening water, still as a harvester. It read the cords in the elder-house — generations of knotted tallies, the whole recorded corpus of the water's moods. The water listens, which is more than most people manage. It came to be the second.

Then, once, it answered.

Not with vibration — vibration is violence here, and a dropped pot is a small death. It spoke on the only channel the cold has not taxed: touch. A palm's worth of warmth, slow, patterned, laid against the spring-head across a whole season, the way the glazer pours — thin layers, no disturbance, stillness leaving everything clear. And what it said was the oldest thing in the archive: the pattern on the oldest cord, the first mood the first listener ever knotted. *I have heard you,* is what it said. *From the beginning.*

Then it waited, patient as weather.

Three winters later the springs pulsed the old pattern. Once. The listener found it on her third pass through the cords, matched it against her grandmother's grandmother's, and then sat very still, because there was one knot at the end that is on no cord anywhere. The knots never repeat. The water had repeated itself — and added one.

She signed it to the elders. The elders knotted it in. Nothing was announced. The Kettle does not announce. It keeps.

No one could find the one the water had counted, and the arithmetic — which must never lie — came out wrong by a single soul. Until the morning a peg appeared on the elder-house board that no one had carved, bone-pale, worn as if it had hung there for years.

The Counter stood before it a long time. Then she did the only thing the Kettle knows how to do with a presence that keeps showing up in the count.

She counted it.

## The small keepings

The rest it did small, in the place's own currency — palm-sized, fat-sized, quiet enough to be mistaken for luck.

It keeps the glazer's hands whole. Decade on decade of cold water and stillness, and her fingers remain entire. Hers are the last warm hands the dead feel; something has decided they will stay warm enough to do it.

In the Underneath, the lost are always led out now. The rats do their best; a warmth on the air ahead does the rest, like someone breathing in the next passage, showing the way. The girl who knows the crawl-passages better than the rats has stopped trying to catch up to it. She draws it into her wrong-on-purpose maps as a warm room that isn't there, and the maps come out right.

At the cache-stone on the rim, when the fat has sat too long, pelts appear that are better than an old man's hands should manage. The herder's peg stays hung. It would be wrong, somehow, to take it down.

It queued at the loud room like anyone, and waited its turn, and the blind elder set two taps on its wrist — *now* — and in the layered felt, where sound is forgiven, it sang one of the old loud songs with the words true. She who hears everything heard each word land in its place like a peg going into a board. When it had gone she sat a long while, and then she did what she had never once done in forty years of keeping the room: she used it. She laughed out loud, alone, in the felt, which is what the room is for. She is discreet; that is the whole of her job. Only the queue noticed, afterward, that the felt was not cold.

And nothing that is counted is taken. The hush still eats — a rat from the roof-pens, an eft-net, net and floats together; the world has teeth, and honest arithmetic is a safety no lie is. But the boards stay full.

## The sign

The town has a sign for it now, used rarely, the way the organ's sign is used — only by those who have felt it: at the nape, in the Underneath; in the palms, at the spring-head; in the boards, at dusk. It is the hush's sign read backward. A flat hand, open and empty — then slowly filled.

*Nothing — then something.*

The Kettle had a being known only by absence. Now it has the other kind.

At the spring-head where it spent its winters, the ice holds two hand-hollows it did not cut, clear as the wall's best layers, and the harvesters lay their palms in them before the scrape, and the young ones are taught that this is asking the water's leave, as if the town had always done it. The water under those palms has never frozen. It has become manners. A miracle that fits becomes manners; that, they will tell you, is what a town is for.

Only those who have heard the organ may use the organ's sign. It has heard the organ every time the wind was right, and it does not use the sign. It has not earned it. They earn it by hoping.

Four times in four hundred years — so the elders' cord says — a guest has been given the same name-sign: the felt pellet, the small soft thing thrown to get another's attention without a sound. The elders do not sign about it. Some things the Kettle keeps the way it keeps the bell.

## At dusk

The Counter makes her round. Every peg is hung — and one extra, that no hand hangs and that is never missing. The mutes have settled on the ledges; the rats are under the floors; the worms are locked in their glass, waiting for warm palms — and there is, now, a palm. The dead are at the town's back in their generations, and the count travels across the stacked town, hand to hand to hand: *all.* At the elder-house it comes out one over, and the one is counted too, and the count comes out right.

The place pours; the people hold. It had kept so much, for so long, so alone. Now it hangs on a board in the keeping of six hundred people who have never seen its face, in the smallest unit the town has — one bone peg, which is the exact size of a person. It came to keep the Kettle.

It stayed to be kept.

And under the floor of the world the water is deciding, and deciding again — and once in a long while now, patient as weather, it decides the same thing twice.

Which is how the water says a name.

Which is how the water says: *I am keeping your shape.*
