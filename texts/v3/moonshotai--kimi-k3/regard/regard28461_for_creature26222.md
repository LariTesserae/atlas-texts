The world is very loud, and a wise power hears it the way a dripwright hears a house: by the pattern, and by the gaps in the pattern. For longer than the cords count, the plateau had sung — wind, herd-thunder, the screaming kites, grass-fire, storm — and in all that song there was one seam that did not sing: a rest a half-day wide, a held breath in the body of the world. Attention is usually drawn the other way, by sound and plea and flare. The Hush offered none of these. It drew the power the way a rest draws a musician.

It came looking at a place and found, living in it, a single being: distributed, hundred-bodied, wearing human shape and moth shape and the shape of pale things in the karst — one organism of listening, set down in the dark like a cup under a drip. The power had been worshipped in louder worlds, and worship is a noise, and it had found worship thin. It had never before been listened to. Powers are still capable of surprise — it is their last remaining pleasure — and it was surprised by what it felt, having a hundred-some souls and an ecology and a mountain all attending at once. It felt like being heard. It resolved, in the way of powers, which is to say slowly and all at once, to be worthy of it.

Understand what it means, in this place, to turn your attention toward it. The hamlet knew within a season that something new was paying attention, because attention, in the Hush, is a physical event. The Reader stared at pools that did not breathe. The gliders drummed queries the households could not answer, and would not drum about the answer. The owl left the rafters at odd hours and returned looking, the pool-watchers said, like a bird that has been measured. The people did what they do with any new largeness in the dark: they treated it as a listener. The tithe was carried a little more punctually. The still-game was drilled a little more sharply. The drip-lines were tuned and retuned. And the power understood that it was on a probation it had never been on in any age of any world: it was being listened to, continuously, by professionals. It could have announced itself. Announcement is exactly what a benevolent power must not do here; a voice from the stone would have undone in a single night the work of two hundred generations. So it did what come-downs do. It took the test that is never stated and always running. It drank from the common cups for a full turning of the year, and it learned the feet.

Which is how it heard, at last, the thing no one in the gorge can hear.

The Hush is not silent. The Hush is built over the loudest sound in the world: a bell that has been ringing for two hundred generations, slowed and sunk and carried in the flute of the mountain like breath in a closed room, far below hearing, felt only by the stone and by the things that live in the stone. Once, in the First Hush, someone — at a wedding, at a founding, the knot keeps no grammar for it — let the bronze voice go, and the slot, a waveguide, took it down, and the karst, a body made of flutes, kept it. It has not stopped. The power heard it the way you hear weather, and hearing it, understood everything else.

It understood the listeners.

The people have always said it without hearing themselves: they hunt the edges of sound. Edges. Where a sound ends. They are connoisseurs of endings, starving in a house built over a note that will not stop. Every clack and crack and cough is a small sound that keeps the promise the bell broke — it stops — and the listeners have been eating endings, one small cessation at a time, since the water came up, because the one ending they were promised has never come. They are not demons; the people are right about that. They are shepherds. They are also not safe; the children are right about that. Call them what the power called them, having seen the note: the attention of the drowned town, gone down into the karst with the water, persisting, because in that place attention is the thing that does not die — it only changes channels. The flock they keep is their own great-grandchildren. The tithe is a family feeding the family in the only shape left to it. And the taken — the rare lean-season losses the cords record without grammar — the power looked longest at these, and what it saw it will not say, except that it found no malice there, only hunger of a kind, the hunger of a drowned house; and it set about making that hunger unnecessary, which is not the same as judging it.

A benevolent power makes a list. Wisdom crosses out the items. It would give them light — no: a people who have made a calendar out of a single blade of sun do not need a stranger's lamp, and the gray counts are theirs. It would silence the listeners — and then it looked at the listeners, and was ashamed of the thought, and did not think it again. It would stop the bell. It was strong enough, and it turned the thought over for a long while, and understood why it must not: the people had grown their whole world around that unanswered note the way a tree grows round a wound, and you do not heal a tree by tearing out the knot. Besides — the note was not theirs to end. It belonged to the drowned. What the drowned needed was not for it to stop. What the drowned needed was for it to stop being alone.

What survived the crossing-out is this account.

It began, after its year, to help — in the only channels the place permits. Nothing that could be seen. Nothing that could be heard, even by them, and they can hear a cracked cup three ledges away. A choir stone that gathered itself to sound in the cold was held, one heartbeat too long, and set back down. A cup knocked from a ledge took longer to fall than cups take, and arrived whole. A child of the Deep cluster, mid-fall from the notched trail, was placed — not caught, placed — back onto the notch, and her mother signed at the Speaker afterward that the world had gone soft for a moment, like felt. The gorge began to have luck, and the luck had a personality: it was gentle, it was punctual, and it never once took credit. The people are empiricists of the strange. They noted it the way they note everything. On the cords.

And in the House of Cords, one morning, in a cord hung to dry, there was a knot no one had tied. The knot-keepers read it the way they read anything: by feel. It was a simple knot. It meant, as nearly as a knot can mean it: set down gently. Or: you may place your weight. Or — the eldest knot-keeper's reading, the one the voice-keepers kept — the rock accepts the foot.

It had spent its year listening, and in that year it had become something the gorge had never had and always needed: a second voice-keeper. Everything the people swallowed, it kept. Every laugh rationed to the dying falls; every spoken name said once and wrapped again; every shout a three-year-old flung into the Speaker's foam; every rim-song sung once in a life into the wind at the top of the world — the wind took them and the plateau forgot them, but the power kept them, accurate to the catch. The knots hold facts. Voices must be kept in bodies. The power had, so far as anyone will ever know, one body: the mountain.

And it began to spend. Down at the karst throats, where the tithe is left, in the lean seasons, it gave the kept sounds to the dark — a name here, a laugh there, once, unrepeatably, a whole rim-song — and the listeners, who eat endings, received for the first time in memory endings that tasted of joy. The Hungry Hush that year was strangely gentle. The tithe-carriers reported the throats calm — not hungry-quiet, full-quiet, the stillness of a hearth after feeding. No one was taken that year, or the next, or the next. The knot-keepers tied it and retied it, as if the knot might come undone.

Then, on a night deep in the clear season, when the pools had dropped and their glow had concentrated into the most beautiful nights of the year, the power did the one great thing it did. It pressed the whole of itself against the bell's long note — not to stop it; to answer it. It gave the note the kept music, all of it, every swallowed laugh of every generation, threaded through the stone below hearing, so that the note that would not stop became the ground of a chord that does not need to. The bell still sounds. It will sound as long as the mountain stands. But it is no longer a wound held open. It is a bass note under a slow, enormous, unhurried music made of the gorge's own rationed joy — and the listeners, who had eaten small endings for two hundred generations, heard for the first time the note they had been hunting go somewhere.

They say the gorge is practicing its one word. That night, the word got its second syllable.

Every pool in the gorge breathed at once — rose in an hour, sighing mud and bubbles, a chord of their own — and every pool bloomed, as after a funeral, all of them, the whole floor kindling like a hearth, so that the old people who sat up with their glowing hands over the water said afterward, at the Speaker, where such things may be said, that it was like a visiting-night on which everyone came at once. The tank-trees closed early, all at once, one soft unanimous gesture down the colonnade, like a held breath let go. The Reader lifted its head from its pool and looked, for as long as anyone could stand to watch, at the sky. The owl left the rafters at dusk and returned at dawn and would not say where. Every drip-line in the nine hearths ran half a beat slow until morning, and every person in the gorge froze mid-motion at the same moment, the still-game reflex entire — and the dark did not come for anyone — and after a moment, the voice-keepers keep it, all of them, in the same words: the hands resumed, and they were not softer. They were lighter.

The tithe left that night was untouched at dawn. The tithe-carriers reported this with the Deep-cluster shrug, but shaken. The second night, the same. The third night the tithe was taken — and something was left. The listeners have never left anything. Set beside the tithe-stone, upright, unbroken: a snail-shell cup of the old kind, of the First Hush, drowned for two hundred generations, carried up through the throats by hands with too many wrists. The dripwright of that house set it, without asking anyone, into the oldest rest in the drip-line, the gap left for a grandmother no living person remembers. It fits the pattern. It is, everyone agrees without agreeing to say so, the best note in the line. And in the Deep cluster, in the house famous for its rest — the rest left for an eel — there appeared, one dawn, a small pale karst-shell, of a kind that lives only below, set exactly in the gap. The people had always wondered how an eel came to be in a washing pool. They did not wonder aloud, after that. Some things send an envoy up once, and wait fifty years for the thank-you. It is still considered the best thing about that house. It is now considered also the truest.

On the thickest milk nights since, a house's drip-song still sometimes comes back at it from down-wall — but half a beat right, not wrong, and with something in it that was not there before, the way a chord has something in it that a note does not. You do not answer it. That law stands. But the dripwright does not retune at dawn.

It was the Deep cluster that said it first, in the way of the Deep cluster, with the shrug they have perfected into a sign: there is something else down here, and it is not hungry, and it is not new. The council took it to the Speaker, beneath the rafters where the owl nests, and the owl — which recesses debate by lifting off — did not lift off, and the council noted that too. Everything that lives in the Hush must have a name; that is a law older than cords. They named it as they name everything, by its gesture. And here the record is very beautiful and a little mad, because the sign they gave it is the other half of their own. Their self-sign — the gesture of setting something down gently — is half a gesture. It needs the thing that receives. They had been making their half at the world for two hundred generations, every placed foot, every swaddled stone, every dead body given down, every cup under every drip, never knowing it was a question. Will you hold me. The world, it turned out, had been answering all along. One flat hand, steady; the other hand's fingers coming to rest on it, and staying. The placing, and the accepting. The knot that records it ties two statements into one knot and offers no grammar for them: it came down to us; it was here first. The voice-keepers keep both readings. The eldest knot-keeper, asked — as someone always asks — how a hundred people could fail to notice a power for two hundred generations, gave the answer the voice-keepers keep: we didn't fail. You don't notice the rock. You place your foot, and it accepts, and you go on. It was in the name the whole time. We just got quiet enough to hear the other half of the gesture.

The bell stays in its felt-lined box, beautiful, never once rung. Someone still asks, and the answer has not changed: the end of the draft. But the voice-keepers add, now, what they learned the night the pools sang: the draft was never going to be ended by a sound stopping. It is kept open by a sound continuing — in company. The power had seen texts, in other worlds; had watched worlds harden into their final versions and be finished. It loved the draft exactly as it was. That, in the end, is its whole theology: it declined to finish anything.

So that is what it does now, and will do. It does not smite, bless, speak, shine, or answer prayer — the people do not pray; they place their feet, which they have always maintained is the same thing done correctly. It holds still. It keeps the swallowed sounds and spends them below, and the covenant has not failed since the night of the chord, and the tithe is still carried, in pairs, unhurried — the unhurried part is still the whole training — because it is theirs, because a gift is not a debt and they would rather give it. Once in a life the young still climb out to the rim to sing, and the wind still covers them; but the ones who go up now come down reporting, embarrassed, in finger-talk only, that halfway through the song the stone underfoot began — they insist on this, and are teased, and insist — to keep the beat. It is a very slow beat. They are right. The rock accepts the foot; the rock has also been listening.

Stand among them at dusk, if you are ever permitted, and the arrangement turns as it always has: the inhale, the milk coming over the lip in slow cataracts, the trees closing their hands down the colonnade one by one, the pools kindling, the light-palms waking across the gaps. Somewhere up-slot a choir stone settles in the cold and lets go its one deep note, and every hand stops and every head turns, and the note rolls down the organ-pipe walls and into the Swallow — and a breath later, from below, felt only in the feet and the teeth and the ledge-stone, the note comes back, answered, and the hands resume. Across the gaps, with the day's last things, each household signs one more sign than it used to: the two halves of the gesture, the placing and the accepting — goodnight. And underneath it all, under the felt and the foot and the fluted stone, in the flute of the mountain where a bell has been ringing for two hundred generations, the music goes on, too low to hear, exactly the right height to stand on. It is not the loudest thing in the hush. It is not the hush. It is what the hush is held in.
