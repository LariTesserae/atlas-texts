## What Stayed

It is not easy to draw the attention of a power that is both benevolent and wise. Benevolence is pulled toward suffering, and wisdom toward whatever is out of balance, and the bowl had neither deficit. What it had was keeping — a whole small world in which everything kept something: the pika its hay, the ice its cold, the snow its garden, the sky its Cup, the Cup its sky. That was what turned the eye. A black cup brimful of light, held without a tremor, on the shoulder of a mountain where nothing should have been still. Stillness that complete is either an absence or an occupancy. It came to see which.

It arrived the way everyone arrives: over the col, between the two cairns, at walking pace — though for it there was no need of pace, and that was the first thing it set down at the rim. It stopped the way everyone stops. The bowl lets you take it in with a single look and then spends the rest of the day refusing to feel small, and it did not exempt the power from this. The hour stretched until it behaved like a day. The power had crossed gulfs that make hours look like nothing, and the hour stretched anyway. That was the first gift, and it was noticed: no one had given it time in a very long while. Time was the one thing it had in plenty. Quality of time was another matter. The bowl gives that to everyone, without deference, and the power — being wise, which is mostly the art of noticing when you have been treated correctly — was pleased.

For the whole first day it did nothing. This was not hesitation. It was literacy. It read the lichen on the cairns, black and orange, the guestbook entries gone illegible and unarguable; it read the fresh moraine, bare of dates; it read the comb-marks down the slabs and the pecked tallies on rock a year out of the ice — a count, a circling, a herd. Someone had stood on land just born and begun keeping records on the first day. It approved of that more than it could say, and resolved to say none of it. It stood on the talus and heard water talking to itself under the stones, and the ground ticking faintly, thermally, like a slow clock, and understood that it was standing on the house's roof, and that the householder was in.

Then it considered what it had to offer, and the considering is most of the story.

It could have warmed the light. The light here illuminates without heating — it had felt the shadow take it like well water — and a word from it would have lent the sun its other property. It did not. Warmth in this house is furniture: the black slab, the lee of the moraine, the pika's flat stone. The tenants carry the map of it in their bodies — the bees' four hours, the finches' ten minutes, the cushions' one warm-blooded trick — and a house's furniture is where the house has put it. To warm the whole bowl would not have improved the bowl. It would have evicted the map, and the cold, which is the keeper's own working material, would have had nowhere to be kept. Cold is not this house's flaw. It is this house's staff.

It could have run the water all day — turquoise from dawn to dusk, the tarn brimming, the plumbing never shut. It did not. The schedule is not a ration; it is a heartbeat, and everything alive here keeps time by it: the threads at midmorning, the first *eet* within minutes, the bees arriving with the water as if sent by it, the shop closing at dusk. A heart that never rests is not generous. It is not even a heart. Water here is an event, and the power declined to make it a feature.

And it could have brought the ice back. It held that thought the longest, the way you hold a stone before deciding where to set it down. It could have filled the bowl to overflowing again, re-hung the apron on the headwall, given the keeper back its body entire. And the cushions would have gone under — the campion, the saxifrage, the crowfoot at the snow's edge, decades of an inch — and the sedge flush, and the knee-high woodland, and the boulder field, and the anvil, and the marks: everything alive or made in the bowl lives on the glacier's grave. You cannot return the keeper's body without taking the tenants' floor. And the keeper is not clinging. Every noon it spends itself — opens the plumbing, pays out its substance to sedge and bee and drying hay — on a diminishing purse, and does it anyway, and locks up at dusk like a shop that means to open tomorrow. The melt is not a leak. It is the day's work. You do not save a fountain by plugging it. You do not keep a morning by holding the sun still.

It knew, too, that the leaving had been hurried, and by what hands — the same small-handed species that adds stones at the col — and it did not confuse the custom with the cause. What it did about the cause, it did at the source, far off, where causes live. None of that work belonged here. You do not mend the world in a house where you have signed the guestbook. Here you keep the terms.

So the whole of what it did can be counted the way the bowl prefers things counted: before noon.

One stone, added at the col. One only — though it could have set the mountain on the mountain — because the custom flattens every arrival into a guest, one stone per crossing, and it accepted the flattening as the first courtesy. No one will ever know which stone is its. It has forgotten itself, and finds the forgetting the best part: the only miracle it permits itself is one indistinguishable from a custom.

Water at noon, when the mountain drinks. Warmth found where warmth is kept, and the shadow taken without complaint. The census counted, and finished before noon, and understood — a bowl so full of sign, and the census still finishable — and itself added to no list. Nothing touched on the tarn-side boulder, and no offering left, because everything it had was either too much — you do not set a second moon beside bone beads — or already given. Its offering was staying, the one thing the boulder had not yet received. It practiced until its passing arrived honestly, like wings — the one sound this place neither swallows nor delivers. It found where the bees go in winter. It will not say. Some things are kept best by being kept.

Then the light tilted, and the window closed, and the people went down to the valley slow, saying little, watching water — and the guest stayed. This was the only thing it did that no one does.

Winter is when the keeping becomes the whole work, and every keeping in the bowl already had a keeper: the tarn iced over with its last sky under the lid, the hay green in the cold dark of the talus, the marmots breathing once a minute, the snow lying over the garden warmer than the air, keeping it. Everything assigned — except one. No one watched. The marmots slept, the people were down, the pika's world was the larder, and the householder, who keeps the cold and the schedule, cannot also stand outside its own leaving and note it. So the guest took the one vacant keeping. It kept the watch. All winter it noticed everything, so that the winter was witnessed. Whether a witnessed winter is warmer, it cannot say. It is certain it was not shorter.

At night the house talked to itself — the stones ticking as they contracted, the ice answering, faintly, fainter each year, in the old grammar, the mountain and its former filling discussing the day. The guest listened a long time before it spoke. Whole winters. Then one night, from the moraine where the watch is kept, it gave back a tick in the old grammar: unhurried, correctly declined, a sound with nothing in it but itself. The ice answered — not louder; gladness is not in the grammar — but answered, and after that, some nights, the conversation kept three voices. The pika slept through it. It is weather. The pika has the house's noises memorized, and after some nights the new one was memorized too, and permitted — filed under weather, which is the bowl's entire naturalization ceremony. There is no higher acceptance here. There is no other kind.

The cat found it, of course. The cat keeps no schedule but its own and has never been seen at noon, but at the gray hour, over the tarn, the two of them regarded each other across the rings — the stars rearranged, briefly, into something unkept, the rings smoothing, the keeping resuming. The guest learned to keep very still at the gray hour, so that the cat would keep coming. Even a power can learn manners from a cat. Perhaps especially.

And it has seen the sky from farther off than anything else that visits here, which makes it the only being alive qualified to certify what the herders only assert. It certifies it: the Cup keeps it better. At night it watches stars it knows from the other side put down into the water and held without a tremor — kept better, yes, and by a keeper with no hands.

Some century the plumbing will not open. The bees will come at noon out of habit and find nothing on the schedule. The guest will be there; it has already decided what it will do, which is nothing, and everything. It could open the plumbing itself — that is the worst of it, it could — and the threads would run, and the bowl would wake, and everything would be wrong, because the keeping would have become a performance kept up for an audience of bees, and the house would have a new householder, one that does not belong to it, and the long honest leaving would end in a counterfeit staying. No. The keeping will have been learned — by the water, by the cushions, by the people adding stones for a landlord no longer in. The guest's part is smaller and harder: to be present the morning the habit outlives the schedule, and keep the watching, and add its stone that year like every year, and remember, on behalf of anything that forgets, both columns of the saying — the Cup keeps the sky, and the sky keeps the Cup. It has added no third column. It has only memorized the two.

But that is some century. Tonight the melt has stopped and the shop is shut and the cold locks down, and the bowl ticks as the stones contract, and under the talus the ice answers, faintly, in the old grammar — and tonight, from the moraine, one more tick, correctly declined, and after it a pause the size of a listening, which is this house's way of making room. The pika sleeps in the larder. The marmots rehearse the long absence. The cat comes down at the gray hour and finds the watch kept, and approves in the way of cats, by ignoring it. The sky goes very dark and very full, and the tarn takes the stars down into itself and holds them without a tremor. The morning of the interval is young. The keeper is awake, and keeping. And the guest is awake, and keeping watch.

That is the whole of what was done: one stone, and staying. Not because the keeping needed a witness — it never did — but because that is what love does, once it has learned the house rules. It adds its stone. It keeps the terms. It stays.
