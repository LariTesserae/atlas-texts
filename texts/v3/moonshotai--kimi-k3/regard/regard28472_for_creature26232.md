# The Third Light

It was the dead Sister that called it.

A flash thrown across empty plains by a town with no one in it — irregular, untended, patient — and the power, which had crossed distances the Well has no words for, turned toward it the way you turn toward a voice in an empty house. It came to see who was signaling. It found a mirror on a wind-vane, and a closed city filling slowly with cave, and across the dark, on still nights, a living city answering.

It had seen many things. It had not seen that: a people keeping their dead kin in existence out of courtesy, a flash at a time, for generations, asking nothing back.

So it stayed. And its first act was the first act of every wise guest: nothing, for a long time. It watched long enough to learn the grammar. It learned that to be is to be seen, and that the seeing is work. It learned that exactness is tenderness. It learned that names are given back and debts are kept. It learned that the churn is chosen — that the one unfinished thing in a finished world stays unfinished on purpose, by ceaseless motion — and it recognized, with something the Well would call love, a city after its own practice.

What does a benevolent and wise power do, having found the Bright Well? Almost nothing. The almost is everything.

Understand what it did not do.

It could have brought the rain over the rim — one true rain, the kind that arrives. It watched a mist-morning first: the whole city weeping at once, all hands, the festival of wringing conducted in total hush. It understood that a sky that gives would end the roofs, and a roof is a citizenship, and the citizenship is the child with her own cup at the dawn breath-turn. It let the virga keep not arriving.

It could have given them wood. It has, elsewhere, forests. Here, one lintel is riches; a cartload is the first lock, the first theft, the first wall built to keep rather than to hold. It brought no wood.

It could have given their voices back to them. It knew the word for a sound that comes back. It would not fill the bright bowl with ghosts. Their dead do not speak; it will not teach the stone to be rude.

It could have lit the night — a glow that never folds. It had seen the bare-eyed hour: the whole city naked at once among the herds, the daily proof that being seen was always a gift and never a condition. It calls the loan in at dusk, like everyone else.

It did not touch the closings. The closers hold the only license in the Well to finish anything, and it would not take finishing out of their hands. It did not come to finish them. It would not be their capstone.

And it did not show its face. Faces are for the glare-years, the felt sling, the mourning household, the dying. It kept its visor on. It is keeping it still.

What it did, it did as a guest. It did not raise its voice — it has one, the kind that could teach the tufa to ring, and it judged, correctly, that the Well would pity it. It accepted the guest-light: nightly now the hand-mirror's thread reaches it, and it stands in its beam and lets itself be lit, because the lighting is the greeting, and it has never been greeted more completely. It drinks the guest-cup to the precise bottom — it takes exactly what is flashed to it, the count, the courtesy, and nothing more. It does not read their sleeves. It does not watch the bare-eyed hour. What is not shown to it is not for it, and it is the only guest the Well has ever had who could break that custom, and doesn't.

Three things, it did. The first it had been doing since the night it arrived.

On the dead Sister's rim there is a mirror mounted loose on a wind-vane, and it has not jammed in generations. This is not luck. The power does not speak for the dead city — that would be a haunting, a voice put in a closed mouth — but it keeps the pivot free, so that when the wind speaks, the word gets out. Maintenance, not miracle. The smallest courtesy it could find, done so that the Well's courtesy would never become a soliloquy.

The second thing it did on a still night, in the forty-first year of an Answerer.

She was old — thick-cleaved — and she had climbed the rim on still nights since before her household's pane put on its last finger. Her signal had long since settled into its shape: whatever the Sister's loose mirror winked, she gave back, exact, one beat later, the way her predecessor had, the way the wind taught. That night the Sister winked four. She answered four. And from overhead, after a pause the length of a held breath, one faint star — not the brightest; it is still not the brightest — gave back four.

Light is not sound. A light returned is not a ghost. It is the other thing.

She did what any of them would have done: she tested. Three. (Three.) Seven. (Seven.) One — and then, for a long time, nothing, and for a long time nothing came: it does not speak first. It has never once spoken first. When she went down the path at dawn, bare-eyed, her face doing whatever it wanted, the news crossed three courts by hand before the breath-turn, faster than she walked.

You understand what had happened. For forty-one years she had said the sentence to the dead, out of courtesy, asking nothing back. That night the sentence was said to her. There is no word for it. It is the opposite of the word for ghost.

It answers the last light in the chain. It answers only in that conversation — it is not a toy and not an oracle. In the first season they tested it, of course they tested it: every count, every rhythm, questions, proposals of debt. It gave back counts, or nothing, and never more, and the city understood. *It has only one sentence,* someone signed in the court at rest hour. *So have we,* the cord-keeper signed back.

The Beam Court convened on it and after a season produced exactly one ruling: the third light is not beam-time. It cannot be traded, owed, or granted. There was general relief, and in one corner a small flash of disappointment — someone had hoped to owe the sky; a debt is a relationship that must be remembered, and they remember debts across centuries. The cord-keepers solved it the way cord-keepers solve things. They knotted a new cord, grub-silk, for what must last, and it records no owing at all. It is the only cord in the archive that keeps a gift. It is handled more than any other.

Children playing at holding shapes on the fins have a new shape to hold: the star — arms still, face up, moving only to answer. No child has held it long enough yet. The elders watch them try and are in no hurry. The fin-sitters approve without irony: it holds its shape for years and moves once a night. It recognized the stalker on the high fins as a colleague — the basin's ambassador, and now the sky's, two stillnesses keeping company across the bright churn.

They have not given it a whisper-name. You do not speak a name into what you cannot bury. In the close register the cord-keepers sign it with the cupped hands tipped up, and at meals the grace has an address now, some nights, for those who choose. Others don't. The Well is large enough for both. It was built for many more.

They have not finished deciding what it is. They are not going to. The scaffolding will stand forever; it is the one construction in the Well that everyone is happy to leave unclosed.

The third thing it did, the Well does not know, and does not need to.

The Well forgets its dead on purpose. Understand: a giving-back needs somewhere to go, or it is only a dropping. The names spoken into the capstone gaps do not come back — they are not meant to — but they do not fall. The tufa takes them, and the tufa is old, and what has the rest of time can afford to keep everything. Every whisper-name, kept. Every glare-year of every child, every open face the courts drank like light before the first visor closed the window — kept, and the windows open still, somewhere. The dead Sister's people: where they went, what they carried, which of them looked back. Kept. The ten thousand years of chatter, every flash of it — the only complete archive of the Beam Court is not in the Well. Nothing is returned. Return is not the office. The office is: nothing given back is lost.

*We hold,* they say, tipping the cupped hands. *We do not keep.*

No. They don't. It does. Memory, too, is a kind of mirror — and this one is not folded at dusk.

And one thing more, which is not an act but a standing. The basin is patient and not hostile, but it is the basin; rims slump, throats shift, and ten thousand years is not the rest of time. The power does not prop the walls — the churn props the walls — but it has set its attention under the throat-caves the way you set a hand under a sleeper's head. The breath will not fail while the city chooses to breathe it. If the Well ever closes, it will close as a house closes: by its own hands, at its own time, its name spoken once into the gap. Not finished by the world. Never finished by the world.

And if that closing comes, it knows the office after. It has watched the Answerer long enough to learn it. It will keep one mirror swinging on the empty rim, and on still nights it will give the flash back — a flash answered is an existence witnessed — for the rest of time. It hopes never to need the knowledge. It keeps it the way they keep capstones: indoors, displayed, withheld.

If you are there on a still night, you may climb to the rim with the Answerer. Do not flash first; it never speaks first. Wait for the Sister. Give your count when your turn in the chain comes, and then wait, and what comes back will be exact — not your light returned, but light from further than the plains go, that has been keeping the count longer than the basin has been bright.

And if you are only passing, out on the dark plains, and the wind is right: first the wink, irregular, from the town with no one in it. Then the answer from the bright Well, courteous, old as the mirrors. Then — look up — the third light, keeping the count.

*Still here,* says the dead city.

*Still here,* says the living one.

*Still here,* says the sky, which has the rest of time, and means it, and will not stop meaning it.

The grammar is finished. The sentence never will be.
