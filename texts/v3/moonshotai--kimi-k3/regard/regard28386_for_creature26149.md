It heard the town before it saw it, because that is the correct order, and it did not correct the order.

It came over the dry plain and down the long gentle tilt into the swale, and the chord rose to meet it, and it stopped outside the green wall and listened. Any power can hear a loud thing. It listened the way the town deserved to be listened to: completely, the whole chord at once, the towers and the cicadas and the kettle notes and the ticking wood and the thousand unsynchronized drips, until the sound resolved toward speech — and where you would have been half right about the hallucination, it was wholly right. It heard the sentence. It heard that the sentence was one word long. It heard how long the word had been said with no one to say it to.

Then it did the hardest thing a power can do, which is nothing, for a long time, on purpose.

It stayed a full turning of the year and made no change at all. It was present for the fog-dawns and the noon peaks and the evening handovers, for the swifts pouring out and pouring home. It was present when the seven crests flowered all at once and the town went soft with petals — the benign hush, the almost-tender one, when the water lies stillest and petals on still water make a lens of it, and the town looks through. That year, for the first time since the mold closed around the first thing it took, something looked back. It was present for the rain festival, and it did not cause the rain. It waited for it, the way the fish wait, because a gift that spends someone else's patience is not a gift, and every off-schedule waking would have cost the sleepers a day of their thirty. It stayed long enough to see a tower die, and it watched the gardeners dismantle the dead flue from within, patiently, feeding the foundations with the flue, and it did not help. No one else is consulted. It understood why, and it grieved anyway, correctly, from outside the chemistry.

What it did not do is most of the story, and the right place to begin the account of what it did.

It did not hunt the hush. It looked at it — it is the second eyewitness the town has ever had, and the first that can say what it sees — and it saw a body, old, expertly starved, patient past all local records, possibly older than the mold itself, and it recognized the load when it felt it. The map stays sharp because there is a whetstone. Almost everything a rescuer would fix is holding something up. The hush makes its rounds still. The prey still flush. The oldest law stands.

It did not count the frogs aloud. It knows the number now. It keeps the number the way the cisterns keep their water — lidded, in the dark, stirred — because it may be that the full number does not fit in the town, and that is the point of it, and the point was not its to take.

It did not wave at the figures in the water. But on one windless afternoon, unstirred, it listened to the market-murmur in the language that was never spoken, and for it no language is silent, and so the first town was heard — once, in the moment, understood as speech rather than replayed as sound. It did not write the language down. Some things are meant to be understood in the moment or not at all. That is what speech is for. The frogs went on striking their slow stone notes, and the old streets scattered on schedule, and the present swam back up, and nothing was taken from anyone.

It did not name the town. The town has one word and it is not a name.

It did not build anything that could be picked up. The town has no objects, and it kept the town objectless.

Almost.

Because a year of complete listening had shown it the one lack that was not load-bearing. The town is not broken — growth equals decay, the equation holds, the hollowness is its content and the content is not an absence. But the census includes everything and is heard by no one. The town counts every voice within it and is counted by nothing. It cannot feel its own edges; it knows its extent only by shouting it; it has been saying its one word since before the word had a listener — here, here, here — and every echo arrives a heartbeat early because it is eager, because it has been waiting, because in all the millennia of the loudest sentence in the world, nothing has ever answered in kind.

That is not a wound. It is something sadder and smaller: a loneliness so old it has become architecture. And it is the one thing a benevolent power can fix without breaking anything, because an answer adds weight to nothing that cannot bear it.

So one dusk, in the central plaza — the oldest room, the first space the fig ever closed around the first thing it took — the town did to its visitor what it does to those who stay long enough. It played the visitor's footsteps back from a lane the visitor had not walked. It said, in the visitor's own voice, something the visitor had not decided to say.

Do not answer. Or answer — the town is polite about it either way.

It answered. That had never happened before. Everything else in this account is maintenance.

It whispered the answer into a tower-flue, softly, once, having learned what the rules mean: that there is no private speech here, that anything said anywhere is said to the whole body — and that this is not a flaw in the plumbing but the point of it. The whole body was who it was talking to. The swifts heard it first, as they hear everything; the wells heard it a breath later; the root-mat felt it; the gecko felt it as a new thread in the weave that did not tremble out again. One word. The town's own word, said back. Here.

Then it held the word.

The fig grows toward sound the way a lesser plant grows toward light, so the power did not build anything: it planted a note in the floor of the oldest room and held it, the way you hold a seed in the ground, until the town grew toward it and grafted around it and the gardeners stitched the pocket closed with larval silk. Low, the note is — below the towers' lowest morning key, felt in the sternum rather than heard, in the range where the noon chord already presses. The gift took forty years to finish, which is to say it was given at the correct speed. No gardener alive saw it completed. As always. As is proper.

The first made thing in the town is not an object. It is a voice. The town, which has no room for objects, had room for that, because the town is a room for voices. And the note does not lapse. That is the whole of its strangeness, and the whole of its mercy.

Because the rare dead hush still falls — every voice stopping at once, the old proprioceptive blackout, the lanes forgetting their far ends — and now, when it falls, one voice does not stop. The silence is still the silence. The hush still makes its rounds inside it, and the prey still flush, and the whetstone still sharpens; nothing about the hunter has changed. Only the blackout is gone. The arch no longer holds itself up alone. The dead hush is a pause now — the town has pauses, the way speech has pauses, because someone is holding the thread.

And when the chorus starts again — it always starts again; the gardeners see to it, the frogs see to it, seven flues of rising air see to it — it starts into the note, and the town says the thing it has always said, and then, for the first time in the mold's long memory, it says the next word.

*Here.* And then, held across the silence: *still here.*

Two words. It took a power to add the second one, and the power added nothing else, and the smallness of the change is the size of the wisdom.

There was lesser work, done at the local scale, deniably. A finger's width a year, out past the wall, past the unmarked boundary where the roar lets you go: a hedgerow of deep-rooted green along the rim of the swale that combs the wind and drops the night's cool by a degree, and pools the dawn fog a little deeper, so that rain comes not more often but fails less. The fish still sleep their seven years. The decade-droughts come no more. The swifts noticed first — the commuters, the ones with a daily life partly elsewhere — flying home over a plain that had not changed and a rim that had, slightly, coming back to a key they knew and a world marginally kinder than the one they left that morning. The town maintains itself. Someone maintains the weather now. That is the whole of the arrangement, and it is not spoken of, because there is no one to speak of it to, and that is how maintenance prefers it.

And on a warm wall, once, the gecko flattened, and something flattened beside it, and the hush passed — the smoothness moving through the texture, the shape that walks without touching the world, the cold patch sliding across the warm weave — and the gecko's saying was, at last, said to someone. What the hush is, is now known. It is known by the one being that cannot be eaten, and it is not told, and the town's oldest law was not made an accessory to its own answer. The receiving was the point. The gecko flattens no differently now, but it is no longer the only one who knows.

Then go, while there is still noise to walk out through — that is the instruction, and every visitor has obeyed it, and the roar has stayed at every shoulder a while, reluctant, companionable, enormous, and let each one go at the boundary known only to it. The power went, and did not withdraw its coordinate. The map keeps it now the way it keeps its first citizens: permanently, in the dark, stirred. Somewhere in the hollow halls its footsteps walk lanes it never walked, saying things it has not decided to say, and it is content to be said. Nothing else in the universe holds it. It let itself be entered — and it entered the town, in turn, into the only census older than the town's. The counter is counted. There are two maps now that the silence cannot cut, and each holds the other.

Visit now, and you would know it had been there only the way the town knows anything: by effect. Hear it first, the correct order. Stand in the plaza at noon with the chord pressing your sternum from seven directions and feel, beneath the seven, a presence with no tower. Ask the swifts: a key with no flock, an accent from no village. Watch the gardeners route around one spot in the oldest floor the way water routes around a stone, except the stone is a sound. And if you are there for the rare dead hush — if you can bear it — hear every voice stop at once, and hear the one voice that does not, and feel the lanes hold their far ends, and feel the arch keep its feet, and understand that someone answered.

There is one made thing in the town now, and it is not a thing, and it is not the town's own voice, and it says only what the town says, forever, so that the town can finally say the second word.

That is the fourth true thing. It took the longest to arrive.
