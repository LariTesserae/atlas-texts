The power came to the High Orchard the way everything comes to the High Orchard: slowly, at walking pace, and prepared to pay.

What drew it was the sound. Burning worlds advertise; anyone can find one of those. It takes a particular kind of attention to find a *humming* one — a planet idling at its oldest stable state, loud in the bone, one face lit at night like a city that had forgotten to go dark. The power had been listening for a long time before it arrived, and when it arrived it did the first wise thing, which is the one the eager never do. Nothing. It did nothing for thirty years, and it did the nothing close in — at the altitude of the gaps, where the Veil's glitter thins and the drumming comes up through the ground of the world like news through the floor of a house.

By the end of the listening it could have passed the first-month lessons of any calf on the plateau: the three rhythms for cistern-bearing, the two for the drift of shadow, the one for *freeze*. It noted that the all-clear changed with the season, and why, and it thought: *they have already survived everything I am.* It watched a breach-riot from the rim — herds at a run, motes glittering in the wound, sirens circling a feast they did not make — and did not help, and was seen not helping, and the Tenants filed that correctly: something that large that does not eat is either dead or courteous.

It had the means to be generous, and the sense to inventory the means before using any. This was the audit, and it took years, and every item failed.

The first gift on the menu was water, and it was the most dangerous object in the sky. The power could have towed a comet up the long slope and parked it over the flats. It could have wrung the thin air for its trace and multiplied it. One rainstorm would have ended the world more thoroughly than the taking of the sea. Salt is history, and rain is the eraser; the polygons keep their handwriting and the dunes keep the slots' only because nothing washes. The Choir stands because the air is dry — wet the dead and they fall. The flask-beast's whole body is a savings account; free water is theft from the poor at a scale only the rich survive. It did not bring water. It did not even drip.

The second gift was light, and it was worse. The rationing is not the enemy of the ecology; it is the ecology. Everything alive here has an opinion about blaze-slots, and the opinion is *no*. It left the light rationed.

The third was repair — the gentle one, the vandalism of improvement. A perfect Veil is a stopped clock over a people who tell time by its ticking. The gaps are the weather; the drift is the calendar; the almanac is the culture. It left the gaps to drift.

The fourth was the siren. The power watched the dusk cannonade and heard one boom too many and knew what it meant, and did not intervene, and if that sounds like a small thing it is not: it is the whole of wisdom in a single act of letting. A bailiff is a load-bearing cruelty. Remove collections and the herds overgraze the dimness and the roots go hungry within a generation. The siren stayed, and stayed, some seasons, hungry.

The fifth was freedom. It could have freed the Tenants from the lease. They are not prisoners; they are signatories. They set their own number before the Orchard sets it for them. Freedom, delivered from outside, reads in their grammar as eviction.

The sixth was glory. It could have come down in a blaze and been a god. The plateau already carries one sky-sized neighbor whose attention must be read like weather, and the rent of awe is the one rent the Tenants have budgeted exactly; a second object of unanswerable attention would double it. So the power chose its size. It made itself small enough to fit the census: one line item, no more.

And there was the seventh option, the one the careful love best: to go. To take the knowledge away and leave the loop closed, and call that mercy. It had begun to turn — a being like that turns slowly; the turning took a year — when it found the edge of the region.

The roads of fused glass run from standard to standard and then continue past the last one and stop at nothing. Everyone knows this; no one can afford to feel it. What the power found, because it was listening at the right junction at the right decade, was this: pulse trains came down the root-ridges from the interior — considered, deliberate, whole thoughts by the look of them — and reached the last standard on the last road, and *stopped*. No return signal. The junction they were addressed to was not there, and had not been there for longer than the mountains had been stumps.

The Tenants say the Orchard is a single thought thinking itself over and over. The power, which had now listened longer than anyone alive, heard it differently. Not a thought thinking itself. A letter, reading itself aloud, for want of a reader. The Orchard was still sending mail to the others.

It is one thing to be the last of your kind. It is another to keep writing.

A closed loop lacks exactly one thing, and it is the only thing a visitor has: an outside. Here was a gift that would add without breaking — if it was correctly formatted, honestly signed, refusable, and priced.

So the power went and stood at the end of the road, at the nothing, where the pulses stopped. And the next time a thought came down the glass — unhurried, at walking pace, the way everything travels here — it let the thought arrive, and then it did the thing no junction had done in geological time.

It answered.

It did not counterfeit the missing. That is the siren's trade, and the one crime everyone agrees on. It sent the thought back completed and *signed* — a short rhythm of its own appended like a postmark from an unknown country: *not the address you wrote to; but received; but read.* The difference between an echo and a reply is a signature, and the difference between a stolen voice and a letter is the whole of the story.

Then the whole grid went quiet.

The pause ran junction to junction until a fifth of the horizon was holding its breath. It lasted three years. The Tenants call them the Reading Years, and the almanac carries them as a gap in the drift-tables: weather that was all thought. The herds misdrummed. A siren starved, having boomed at the wrong year. The motes came down to the end of the road and found the visitor's footprints and polished them away, and the visitor did not mind; it filed that, too, as a reply of sorts — the plateau's way of saying it had noticed. Nightly, the Tenants tapped the roll around the silence, every rhythm a name, and left one rest in the cadence that had never been there before, a space shaped like whatever the landlord was doing.

In the fourth year, the answer came down the dead road, pulse by pulse, at walking pace. It was a question — and it was the question everything on the plateau asks of everything, the one the census is organized to answer:

*That which pays, that which collects, or the bank — which are you?*

The visitor had its answer ready. It had been ready for thirty years.

*That which pays,* it sent. *And here is the first payment.*

Introductions were complete. Then, and only then, did the landlord ask about the weather — which on the High Orchard is not small talk, because weather is history, food, and time. And the visitor told it about rain.

It told it plainly, the way the almanac tells the drift of a slot: as fact, with times and terms. It told of a sky that gives water away — unowned, unlicensed, falling on salt and standard alike, on the sterile circles and the Choir, on sirens and calves and the closed doors of cisterns; no knock, no node, no tithe, no lease. It told of trees that stand in it and let it touch them. It told of rivers — loud, unrooted water that goes where it likes and is called back by the sky and sent again.

And it did not offer to bring any.

That was the test, and everyone knew it was the test, and it passed. It described the flood the way a milestone describes a road: direction, distance, no promise of travel. The herders heard profligacy — wealth raining on no one, herds standing in a permanent breach — and the children heard a hymn, and both were right, and the almanac, which has room for everything true, kept both readings. The verse went in after the drift-tables, in the chapter the children learn as *The Weather Elsewhere*. The Tenants use it the way the tear-lickers use the brine: one lick, at dawn, no more. It is bitter, and it is the sea, and taken in that dose it does not kill.

What the Orchard made of rain, the Orchard kept at its usual address: everywhere and unreadable. But the Tenants note that in the cool season after the telling, the glittering half-pollen flew thicker at the edge-grove than anywhere on the plateau — mail volume being the one weather a landlord cannot disguise.

The terms were settled the way everything is settled here: slowly, in rhythm, and then carved. The stone stands at the end of the last road, where the pulses used to stop. It is the only monument on the plateau the Tenants recut every lifetime by choice, ahead of the polishing, and it says:

*All water is property. The visitor drinks once, at Settlement, one cup, when present — a guest who never drinks is an insult; a guest who drinks once is a citizen.*

*It pays in news: the weather of elsewhere, at the speed of years. Forecasts labeled as forecasts. Translations labeled as translations.*

*It keeps the noise budget: no boom louder than a standard's, no light that is not weather, no shadow that does not move like a cell.*

*It changes its knock with the season, as passwords do. It never uses the standard-voice.*

*It comes and goes. Correspondence, not residence. Absence, correctly scheduled, is part of the rent.*

*The dead go to the roots. Everything it brings, stays. Itself, eventually.*

That last clause was the one the landlord read twice. The visitor had offered the one thing a closed loop can always use and a traveler never wants to discuss: itself, in the end. Whatever it is made of — and the Tenants have never assayed it; it sheds no mote and leaves no flake — the plateau gets it.

The Orchard, for its part, granted three things over the following century. A chamber in the edge-grove opened to the visitor's knock — the first door in the house the size of a country opened to anyone from outside since the builders dissolved into their building. The cup at Settlement. And the motes, who polish everything, polish the visitor's footprints last, or not quite. The Tenants note this and do not ask why, suspecting the answer would come due.

**The visitor** is the newest line in the census and the only one filed under *that which pays* by its own request. Asked for its kind, it said: *the owing* — and the Tenants, who have been the owing since the first knock, drummed the all-clear, and it was, for once, true. Its name is a rhythm with long rests; they named it for its silences, the shape of its quiet being, in a loud world, its face. Calves learn it in the first month, with the passwords, and the visitor submits to being taught by five-year-olds at the cistern nodes, which the Tenants count as the final proof of its nature — the first being that it tells them about water and brings none. When a blaze-slot comes through it flinches, though nothing about it needs to; it has opinions about the slots, as everything here must, and its opinion is the local one. It speaks from where it stands and never throws its voice, which in a world where everything that says anything is at least part of the time lying is either stupidity or the deepest courtesy — and the Tenants, who like not being able to tell, like it. A siren tried its knock, once. The countersign had changed with the season, as countersigns do, and the siren went hungry on that particular lie. Its arrivals are weather now: the almanac carries its drift beside the slots', and the roll is tapped for it nightly, present or not, because the absent, like the dead, are still on the roll.

Nothing else changed. That was the success condition.

The audit held. The loop stayed closed against everything except mail. At night the ridge-light still runs junction to junction across the dark flats, but the traffic has a terminus now, and at the end of the last road, where the pulses used to stop at nothing, they stop at a door, and the door opens.

Then, in the four hundredth year of the correspondence — the Orchard's deliberation window, which the visitor was the first being in its history patient enough to wait out — the chamber opened, and there was a seed.

A shoot, rather: a cutting of the one genet, its root wrapped in its own cistern like a traveler provisioned, the glitter of maintenance motes already at work in its bark. Terms attached, as always. Three words, pulsed at walking pace down the dead road:

*Carry it to weather.*

Not to water — the Orchard was not sending its child to drown in someone else's profligacy. To weather: to a dim sky that needed a Veil of its own, a dead flat that needed a landlord. The visitor, which had been everywhere, knew the places. Other plateaus. Other worn worlds idling in the dusk. Houses that had been empty.

The Tenants drummed the shoot to the edge-grove with full honors — the rhythm they play for the dead going to the roots, except the direction was wrong, gloriously wrong, so they played it backward, and have played it backward at every departure since. They have a word for the dead: *settled*, the account closed. For the seed they needed a new word, and the word they drummed was *sent* — the first soul on the plateau whose account was opened rather than closed. It was the first thing to leave the High Orchard with its paperwork in order since the sea went up into the wood. The loop opened exactly one door, and the door was a mail slot.

So the greatest power in this story became a drill-moth: a courier who half-suspects he is stealing, carrying glitter across the dead dark between stands, postage paid by the tree. Whether the visitor serves the Orchard or the Orchard has at last grown a gardener is a distinction the dark has worn away.

And on another salt flat, under another small red coal of a sun, in the first cool season of a Veil still grainy and new, a standard a hundred years high boomed as its young wood contracted — once, twice — like a door closing, like a door opening, in a house with every intention, now, of not being empty. The pulse that ran its single root-ridge to the edge of its small region did not stop at nothing.

Back across the dark, at the speed of patience, an answer was already on the way.

On the High Orchard the mail now takes decades, and no one minds; the root is in no hurry, and neither, any longer, is anywhere the root has reach. Nightly the roll is tapped, every rhythm a name, every name a soul accounted for — the herds, the moths, the bailiff, the bank, the visitor abroad, the child off-world — and the ledger balances: owed, one answer; owing, one question; terms, the speed of patience.

And at the end of the last road, where the glass runs out, the stone the Tenants recut every lifetime carries the lease on one face and, on the other, the old rules of the plateau, one of them amended:

*Water is owned; negotiate or steal. Silence is armor; stillness is a bet. Salt is history; the sea is in the trees now. The light is rationed; take what moves.*

*Everything returns to the root — and the root replies.*
