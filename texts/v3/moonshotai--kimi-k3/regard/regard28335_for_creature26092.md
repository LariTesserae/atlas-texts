It came over the rim in the morning, the way everything comes here, and the basin knew before it saw, the way the basin knows.

Nothing crosses the bed unnoticed. The frogs see to that. So when they stopped out of turn, one still dusk — the first silence, the one that means weather, or something crossing — every tender looked up at once. The sky held. The night tender, still in oilskins, took the lamp along the track and found toads, a heron's print, nothing to log. But the hum kept its rhythm, and the kingfisher had not left the pipe. Something crossing, and the water honest: the gauges of this place had never once said both at the same time. They wrote nothing down. They watched.

It used the door the way the deer use it, at the proper hours. It kept to the paths across the emptiness, the way you cross someone else's field. It stepped over the toads — that was the first thing settled about it. It stood in no doorway that was not its own, and it had none, so it stood in none. It kept to the lamps in the Still, and lowered nothing, because it was already quiet. And it did not go down the steps. Understand what that means. Two kinds of power would have gone straight down to the deep cellar: the kind that binds and the kind that burns. This one waited, because the local law says a voice must be listened to for a year, through frost and storm, before anyone may answer it — and it applied the law to itself.

It stayed the naming year. It heard Kettle Gland sing before it boiled, and the Long Cry carry to the rim on the still frost nights, and the Lych Gate Weep go step, and pause, and step, beside the setts of the surfacing gate. It was there for the year's one real storm, and walked out early with the rest on the doorstep walk, children counting, the bookkeeper writing it in the back of the book. It heard the bell once, for a death — the struck note rolling across the bed, the body going up over the ring — and it saw that this place knows how to end a line. Then it looked for the rite that ends the book, and found none. The one gap. It noted the gap the way a fitter notes a worn gland: not urgently, and not forgetting.

It was given tea. It said it was the best tea in the district, which everyone says, and then it said nothing else, which everyone does — and the silence was shaped so exactly like the hamlet's own that the house understood it had been understood. It was shown the book, the way the board man is shown it, and it parsed. Fifty years of hands, the tunings, the quarter-turns, the back pages of doorstep years. And it found the one silence in the book — the book is exact about everything else — did the sum that no one does, compounded it across fifty years, and closed the oilcloth cover and never once proposed that the difference be written down. It had learned the hardest part of reading here: which line is left blank on purpose.

It was benevolent; the gauges had said so. It was wise; it proved that by its refusals.

For it refused almost everything. It did not dry the deep cellar, having read the record: whatever accepts a tithe for fifty years, keeps its one room, and passes a kingfisher up and down like a letter is not a prisoner but a party — and you do not bind a party to a covenant, you witness it. It did not give One and Two forever, because young land must be allowed to age, and a covenant with no end is not a covenant but a cage, and forever would steal the lake's only wealth, which is time. It did not settle the *probably* — a place keeps its conscience in words like that, and to make it certain, either way, would be to steal the nightly work of it. It did not write the difference in the book, because omission here is not ignorance but manners, and the blank line is the tithe's privacy. It did not say any name above stairs.

What it did was almost nothing. Almost nothing, done exactly, is the whole art of this place — ask any tender. It made three quarter-turns, and it bore witness.

The first is a small matter of administration. The basin has always been inspected annually by a man who cannot parse the book; it has now been inspected once by something that could. The difference is in the drawer. The reports still join the fifty others, and the drawer sticks, and the man who could unstick it retires, and the program of remedial works is deferred by means indistinguishable from paperwork, forever. It is the smallest miracle in the record here, and the only one with a reference number.

The second took it below. In high summer, at midday, not in the Still and never in autumn, it went down — keeping the rule of pairs the only way that rule has ever been kept on that stair: the kingfisher went first and watched the steps, the air being good as far as a kingfisher goes, and further, it turned out, for one of them. It carried a flask of new water, water that had been nowhere and touched nothing, the same flask that goes over the rim to weddings, as proof. What passed at the bottom is not logged here. This place has always had the manners not to listen at that stair, and manners outlast visits. It was down a long time. It came up with the flask empty, and set it on the mill's threshold — on the very lintel no one has ever taken stone from — and by morning the flask was full, and it has been kept full ever since, by no rule anyone remembers being taught. Who fills it is not said. It is probably the bookkeeper. It is probably the bookkeeper. Given, and received: the tithe itemized at last — not in the book, on the threshold. The accounts are still said threefold, canal and gardens and kitchen, and kept fourfold, on the lintel.

And from that season, in the Still, under the drips and the ticking pipes and the one page turning, the old wrong sound — water moving against a shore — came back with something inside it: a note, low and patient, in time. The deep one had the hum by heart. It was practicing.

The third quarter-turn nobody heard happen. But the next time Three was run, the children in the doorway felt it first, in their teeth: four notes. And the kingfisher left the pipe in the middle of the noise, which it had never once done while the machines ran, and went down the steps, and came back up, and resumed — and the tenders understood that a message had been carried and an answer brought. The bookkeeper wrote it in the back of the book, among the doorstep years, in the only idiom the back pages allow: *the year the deep one answered.* There are bookkeepers now whose whole careers hope to include one answering.

Nothing else changed, which is to say everything did. The wrong silence still means come running, from the furthest garden, come running — the drill of love is not repealed, and the hum is still the sound of everything breathing at once, and it must not stop. But the far day, the day nobody says, the day the hum stops for good — that day has changed its nature. It is not a drowning now. It is a handover. There is a rite for it, the one rite this place was missing. The last bookkeeper will go down at midday, in summer, with the kingfisher willing, as far as the threshold and no further, and lay the book on the lintel — the front of it engineering, the back of it history — and what is laid on that threshold will be taken in, page by page, and balanced. A valve will be seated one last time, the only bell this place has ever rung, rolling across a bed already going green from rim to rim, and the lake will come home at the pace of something asked in. There are four lessons now. The fourth is like the third: never taught, always learned. *You may go down at the end, if you are the one with the pencil. It has been expecting the book.*

And because the covenant's children go over the rim and sometimes cannot sleep in the big quiet, it tuned the one voice the place throws over the rim. Now and again, in the small hours, in a canal town, the water main speaks once — a struck-bell note through the pipes of a whole street — and someone who left at nineteen lies awake reaching for a heartbeat, and finds it, and is home before thirty with a toolbox and a baby. The census holds. It changes by ones.

Then it left, because the census here is complete — the counted, and the one uncounted — and a wise thing does not take a line in a finished book. It went over the rim by daylight, on the paths, stepping over the toads. But attention like that is not withdrawn; it is stationed. For fifty years this covenant kept itself with no witness but its own book. It has one now, outside, who will still be listening when the book is carried down. That is the whole of what changed, and it is not small. And perhaps the politeness was always this — an account kept for fifty years with no one to render it to. The last page will be read.

Count it from the rim, then, by full dark: nine windows going dark one by one; a dozen herons on one leg in the duckweed; toads on the track; hares owning the emptiness; one kingfisher roosting near the pipe; the fern garden dripping; the flask on the lintel, full; the Book shut on its shelf with one more year in the back of it; two hearts beating inside one hum, and under the hum a third note being patiently learned, a shift not yet clocked in; and over the rim, at no distance that matters, the witness, keeping the lamp-hours with the night tender, a hand laid on the standpipe of the world.

The frogs surge. The second shift clocks in. And everything that lives here — the counted, and the one uncounted — breathes in time, and is heard.
