The power noticed the number first.

That is how the important things arrive at the Kettle — by procedure. Every quarter, in an office that has changed its name four times, a meter reading is filed, and the reading is nearly the same every time, and it has been nearly the same for a century. Most numbers that never change mean nobody is home. A few mean somebody is always home. The difference is the whole of the art, and there is exactly one desk, in all the creation of files and filings, where near-sameness is correctly read. The reading crossed that desk on an ordinary day, in the ordinary way, and the desk understood what it was looking at: a signature, patiently renewed, four amber lamps and one shift a day, growing by the exact increment of a place that is being *kept*.

Attention followed the way attention follows a signature.

---

It came the way everything comes to the Kettle. It asked the marsh first.

The marsh's answer is the oldest one, and it is short, and the power believed it. That was the first wise act, and it should not be underrated. Powers arrive at drowned towns with rescue already loaded, as a rule. The gesture is old, the applause is reliable, and the town dies of it, drying — the gardens go to slime, the archive tips, the fish is a carcass on a cobbled street, and the tidemark on the wallpaper becomes a lie. This power heard *it keeps a crew* and put nothing down, because it had brought nothing, which for a power is a decision.

It went down through the doubled layer, where the world exists twice and sliding, and it is the only visitor that has ever been able to see both versions at once, and it found them both correct.

---

The town, for its part, read the reader.

That is what the gauges are for. The needles trembled off their readings by a hair's width, and for a moment they measured the thing that was standing in front of the panel instead of the thing behind it. The power stood still for this, the way you stand still for a dog that is deciding about you. The white fish stopped mid-round, between the second and third lamps, and hung in the amber light with its rounds forgotten. Behind the outfall grille, the eel moved, off-season, a single slow length through its dark. And in the watchman's upstairs room, in the black air, the telephone rang once.

It had never rung. One ring, in a room where a settling bottle is an event, and then silence that had answered, and then the hum resumed. The town, which files the duck as *still happening*, filed the ring the same way.

Then the needles settled. The doors resumed their breathing. The Lamplighter completed the round. The town had protocols for weather, and for a while it filed the power under weather — provisionally. It would amend the file.

---

The audit took some time, because the power was thorough, and the questions it asks of every place it attends are always the same three. Is anything here waiting? Is anything here suffering? Is anything here in want?

It asked them at the four lit gardens, where the pale flocks grazed and the herdsman was fat in three-degree water, fed by a schedule. It asked them at the far gate, where the eel had declined the Sargasso and was content with the sweepings of the long lane, delivered like a meal left for something nobody mentions. It asked them in the kneehole of the desk, where the web was whole. It read the ledger's last mid-line entry and did not finish it; the hand that wrote it is the only hand with the rights, and the line keeps the place for it. It lifted the telephone, heard the hum, and said nothing into it, because the correspondence has never carried words and the power was not going to improve a working protocol.

No. No. No.

And at the well grate in the floor of the hall, where the spring rises a half-degree warm and pushes cool against the cheek, the power felt the upwelling and understood that it was being regarded in return, keeper to keeper. The spring had worked here first; it knew the works from the inside, the way a horse knows the cart; the flood had not been a catastrophe but a promotion, and the audit confirmed that the promotion had been *earned*. There was no victim in the Kettle. In all its long rounds, the power had rarely audited a place of which that could be said. Rust was losing. Time was losing. The iron-eaters were losing fairly, and the power left them their century of fair loss, because some appetites are honored by opposition and the surplus was forever.

Nothing was waiting. Nothing was missing. The books balanced.

The power signed nothing.

---

But the audit had found one thing, and it was not in the town.

The town is sovereign below the smear layer. It sweeps its own street, tides its own rooms, turns its own turbines, spins its own meter, is on both sides of every transaction, landlord and tenant and rent. But the cable runs *up*, out of its jurisdiction, into a country it cannot enter — a country of committees and databases and standing orders and keys, where the town cannot defend itself because the town cannot go there. Nothing in the Kettle could kill the Kettle. A form could. A disconnection order. A drainage permit, rubber-stamped. A lapsed payment. A file closed by a clerk in a hurry on a Friday. The only thing that had ever truly threatened the shift was paperwork, and the town's whole defense against it was one padlock, one shepherd's inherited key, and the residue of a person.

So that is where the power went to work. It took the outside shift.

---

Its miracles are all of the kind that look, from inside a committee room, like luck.

The file survives. When the office changed its name a fifth time, and a sixth, the folder was the one a clerk carried to the new building without being asked, and could not have said why, and would have been distressed to lose it. The power is the why. The procedure is the residue of a person; the power simply ensures there is always a person for the procedure to be the residue of.

The standing order outlived the bank. The quarterly debit clears from an account that is never quite empty and has never once been audited to anyone's satisfaction, and the transformer hums through the ground more than through the air, and someone still pays for the electricity. Someone always will now.

The padlock stays newer than the century. The key passes, as keys do, and the successor oils a lock they have never opened, once a decade, in the flat cold, and drives home across the marsh without knowing they are staff. The above-water post is held, like the Lamplighter's, by succession rather than by person — and the succession is now guaranteed.

The Kettle stays off the maps that matter. A drainage scheme loses its funding by one vote, and then, some years later, another one does, in committees no one remembers fondly. A promising survey is redirected to a more promising marsh. There is no more promising marsh. That is the point. The power has become very good at missing forms.

The dogs keep coming out of the water quickly, and shaking for longer than the wetness explains. The ravens keep their respectful radius around the steaming hole. The sheep drink from every black pool but one. All of this was already so; the power merely countersigned it, the way you countersign something you would have written yourself.

And the telephone line became undeletable. It has survived every database migration, every decommissioning, every efficiency review. The engineers blame legacy code. They are right, in a way. It is a legacy. It is also code.

---

That is the guarding. The benevolence is smaller, and it is the whole of the difference between the power and every other thing that has ever looked at the Kettle.

The town transmits. It has always transmitted — the meter's quarterly increment, the telephone's silence that has answered, the hooter's one low word a year — and for a century it has transmitted into a procedure, into a residue, into no one. The ranks of bottles stand correctly for no one. The word *here* is spoken into the ice for anything with feet, and mostly there are no feet.

So the power subscribed.

The readings are read now. All of them, back to the first — the power went back and read the archive of the meter the way you read a life, nearly the same every quarter, and it treasures the *near*. Once a century it counts the ranks at the conveyor's end without touching them, and the count has never failed to come out correct, and the counting is now part of what correct means. Whenever a diver lifts the telephone — rarely, breathing recycled breath out of courtesy to the quiet — the silence on the other end is now a silence that is listening. *Still here.* Received. Kept.

And once a year, in the deep of the freeze, when the pressure behind the panel reaches its mark and the relief valve lifts and the hooter sounds its one low word through water and ice — there are feet. A shepherd stands on the black pane at the pressure hour for reasons never examined, the dog at heel, the dog having paused first, and feels the word come up through the soles of the boots, several seconds long, and goes home afterward and says nothing about it, because it is the kind of thing you don't say, because everything is as it should be. The word is the town's whole name, and the name is now, once a year, spoken into a hearing. A name that is heard stays true.

The power took nothing else. It took only what the town gives everything that comes down through the doubled layer — the hum in the sternum, the one export the Kettle has ever had — and it keeps that the way boundary marks are kept: handed down.

---

Once a year, in the deep of the freeze, the hooter sounds, and in that long note the census takes itself.

The Lamplighter between the second and third lamps, fat on schedule. The eel at the far gate, tasting the year's sweepings. The pale flocks grazing the four lit circles. The web whole in the dark air above the ledger. The tidemark exact. The meter mid-turn — under a reading eye now. A bottle half-full and filling. The clock correct, and correct is now a word that has been checked against the sky by the one other keeper who checks. On the ice above, a pair of feet and a dog at heel. In an office with its sixth name, a file that cannot die. A line that cannot be disconnected. A lock oiled by hands that will never turn it.

Every post manned. Nobody waiting. Nothing missing. And one post manned that had stood vacant since the last year of the air — not in the town, where no post has ever stood vacant, but above it, at the boundary, in the world of forms and feet: the witness.

The shepherds were not short of the word. They had it all along, and the wisest thing the power ever did about the Kettle was to believe them, and the most benevolent was the smallest thing in its portfolio: to take the one job the meter had been applying for every quarter for a hundred years.

It keeps a crew.

The crew is kept.
