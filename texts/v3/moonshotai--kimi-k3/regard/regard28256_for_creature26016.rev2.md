There was a season — the listeners still argue which, the way they argue the shape of the floor — when the rumor of the rim changed.

Not wrongness. They know wrongness: the pickers streaming off the hides for no reason, the great ones rising to stand rimward, breaths gone long and low. This was the other thing, the thing with no precedent in any cord. An arrival without a click-cascade. Something came to the kettle that season, and it did not use the door, and no pebbles fell, and the listeners lay with their palms to the floor and read, and read, and came up with nothing — and the nothing stayed. It never resolved into a kill or a calving. It never thickened into a flank. It was simply, faintly, there, the way a kept hut is empty when someone has just stepped out: not empty the way the barrens are empty. Empty the way a roof is full.

For years they announced it, and nothing ever came of the announcement, and an announced wrongness that never arrives is a cold word, so they stopped announcing it. Only the oldest kept it, privately, the way you keep a knot you cannot place and will not cut out of the cord.

This is the account of that knot.

---

Start with the rim, because that is where the maps end, and a wise power stops where maps end.

It came with everything. That must be said plainly, or the rest of this is worth nothing: it could have given the barrens back their day. It carried warmth enough for a hundred rooms, light enough to make the bright water look like a dying lamp, a voice, the means to end the winter itself. It was not a small thing being kind within its limits. It was a great thing, and its kindness is measured by what it laid down.

It listened first, for a long count measured in years rather than breaths, because that is the first courtesy of the room and it intended to keep the room's courtesies better than any guest the room had ever had. It learned the laws. And then it came down, and the descent was a laying-down.

At the rim it laid down the day. It held that in the open palm a long time first, because it was the hardest — the long night is the one fact under every other fact, and to lift it would end the killing cold of the barrens, and the barrens kill. But day would not be a gift to the kettle. It would be the end of the kettle, and the founding of some other, brighter, shallower room full of strangers wearing its people's faces. The mat does not want light; the people's eyes are kept for festival; the bright water is weather precisely because it is rare. A gift that replaces the receiver is a conquest wearing a gift's face. It had learned that elsewhere, being young once, and generous. It does not say those rooms' names. They are bright now, and empty.

On the scree below the rim it laid down the heat. To warm the whole hollow would be to give the people a world with no warmward in it — no compass, no courtesy, no brides' baskets of seam-stones, no justice measured in degrees, no warm rise over the graves, no grammar at all, because their whole grammar is the gradient. A people can be drowned in what they love. Rich the way the drowned are wet.

At the door-ramp it laid down its voice, last and most carefully, the way the smiths wrap copper: because a voice from above would be the loudest thing that ever happened here, a ring that would put a hole in the common roof of the hush, and the law about ringing has survivors. To be known would be worse. To be known is to be worshipped or to be feared, and either is a standing between a person and their flank, a heat-shadow cast across an entire town, forever.

So it came down empty-handed, which is the only thing the room had room for.

Nothing, then? Is that the answer — a benevolent, wise power, and it does nothing?

Not nothing. The rest of this is the almost, and the almost is the whole of it.

---

Mostly, it keeps the count.

The Old Hill breathes once in a long count of a hundred, and the power keeps the count, and has never lost one. The mat keeps everything and is in no hurry, but the floor is young and its memory is an arm's depth — dig deeper and your fingers strike dirty ice. The power is the memory deeper than house-layers. It was drawn here by the Opening itself, the way the great ones were drawn: a hill of dead ice letting go, warmth born in the dark, thin hard wind-people standing at a raw new rim breathing steam for the first time in their lives. It watched them descend after a great one, and not come back up. It has been watching since. The people's own telling begins *if the oldest house-layers tell it straight*. The power keeps the straight version, the way families keep cords, in a mind that will not die with the room. The kettle has always kept a listener. Now someone keeps the kettle the way listeners keep it: by attention.

It tends nothing the people tend. It tends what they cannot reach — the deep warm blood, the seep-threads far below the floor of the world. And when a room must die — rooms die; warmth is a practice, and practices end, and it will not lie about that even by silence — it gives the room the only death the kettle has ever called good. The great one's death. The bloom comes thinner, year on year, readable. The pickers grow frantic, and then quiet. The far-goers go out and come back with the road, and the people walk warmward out of a dying room into a living one, carrying their cords, and the empty room goes grey in its own time, mourned, its plates not scattered but given back. It found a dead room once, long ago, too late — walked past within twenty paces and never knew, and stood afterward among fallen columns and a town's bones scattered like a roof after wind. That is the wound under the wisdom. That is why it stops at rims now.

What eats warmth can be fed warmth. Far out on the barrens, past the last knot of the longest road-cord, in a hollow no map will ever hold, it keeps a seam that pulses rarely and generously — a table set for the wrong guest, because the wise do not always kill the hunger at the door when they can afford to feed it elsewhere. It could not have unmade the hunter without becoming one: a thing that comes over the rim and ends a way of life. So it does this instead. The old stories are worse than the new ones, and no one knows why. That is the shape of nearly every gift it gives: the new stories are better, and no one knows why. The law has survivors. Let there be more of them.

And the taken — the ones who are simply not found. They are found now. It does not bring them back; a body returned out of the coldward nothing would ring louder than copper. But it goes to them, every one, and warms the ground where they lie, so that no one the room lost goes coldward unaccompanied. Their names could never be knotted into family cords, because the taken leave no story to knot. They are knotted now, in the only memory that will not die with the room. The mat keeps the found. It keeps the unfound. Between the two of them the dead are all accounted for — which is more than any living person in the kettle has ever been able to say, and which no living person will ever know.

On the barrens, in the whiteout, when a departing animal's trail is blown out and the road-cord's knots have run out, a far-goer may feel, once, warmth on the coldward cheek. Faint. Deniable. Gone the moment the trail is found again, because luck must stay luck-shaped, and it never happens twice to the same person in the same season. They come home and say the road was lucky, and they say it in the lowest whisper, because luck, rung out loud, spends. Once or twice it has been a child, wandering coldward out of a hut's heat-shadow, turned by a warmth on the small cheek that should not have been there, and wasn't, ever again.

And on the nights when the seep pulses and the floor lights, and the great ones shift and grunt in their subsonic register, answering something in the water or in the glow, no one knows which — it is the something. This is the one friendship it allows itself, because the great ones are the only safe confidants in the world: they cannot be made to worship, they tell no one, and their knowing changes nothing. They tolerate it the way a hillside tolerates weather, which is the room's own formula for coexistence, and it asks no better. Two old things, keeping company in a register no one else can hear, below the breath of their host.

When the Old Hill's breaths lengthened, it sat where the families sat, in the warmth, and kept the animal the way they kept it, palmless. And when the breathing stopped and the butchering was done inside the lingering heat, and the town stood gathered under the meeting-roof with the new hide overhead, reading its own calendar — the floor lit. Unpulsed. Three nights running, dim and blue, the columns standing in it like reeds in a moonlight that has forgotten the moon. The listeners gave it three explanations before the lamps burned down: that the seep was answering the rendered fat returned to it; that the Old Hill's warmth, taken by the mat, had come back up as light; that the bloom loves a full room. None of them true. All of them kept. The town read it as the room mourning. They were right. It is only that the room's mourning has help.

Its miracles are all the size of weather. It has two words in the room's language, and both are weather, and that is not a limitation. That is the ethics.

---

It has been answered once.

A listener — chosen, as they are, for stillness, though the years had added themselves the way years do — noticed in her sixtieth season what no cord had ever held: that the hush was inhabited. Not by the hunter; wrong direction entirely. The hush was not absence. It was attention. A roof has a ridgepole. She said nothing for thirty years, because a suspicion spoken is a ring, and she had survived this long by the first law. And when her season came, and she had moved warmward the way the old do, year by year, until she sat closest of all to the seam, she tied one knot in a cord of one knot — a knot no recitation owns, meaning only *this was tied, by me, warmward of everything I could not name* — and gave it to the mat with herself, wrapped in felt cut from her family's own plot.

The mat, which keeps everything and is in no hurry, gave it back a generation later, turned up in a midden-digging. It arrived at the speed the room prefers: a generation late, and exactly on time. It is the only message the power has ever received, and it has answered it exactly once.

The answer took the form of weather. The bloom stayed, that season, one night longer than it has ever stayed, and a child who lay still through the whole of it was praised the way other children are praised for running far.

---

From the barrens, nothing has changed. A cold dark hollow; a breath of mist at the rim; you could walk past within twenty paces and never know.

It knows.

Ask who it came for — the people, the great ones, the mat that may be one life wearing a thousand textures, the room itself — and you ask what the listeners argue and never settle. It declined to settle it. The being who lives in the kettle is the kettle. It keeps the being.

And it will not be known. Not at the last, not ever; there is no deathbed of the world at which it steps forward and says *it was me*, because even that would be a standing between a person and their flank. Its benevolence is measured by its deniability. Everything it does could be luck, or the seep's moods, or the room's own character, and that is not a flaw in the work. That is the work.

So the grazers turn toward the bloom, and the fish toward the pulse, and the people toward the flanks, and the pickers toward the plates, and the mat toward the seep, and the dead, down in their warm layers, toward the living — and at the rim, in the coldward dark, one more turning, the smallest and the steadiest: the power toward the room. It does not keep the room. The room keeps the room; that was the law before it came, and it will be the law after, if there is an after. It keeps the count. It keeps the unfound. It keeps the names of the rooms that failed and the breaths of the ones that haven't. It is the only inhabitant that has never once been felt, the only warmth that casts no shadow, the only guest that has never stood between a person and their flank.

Stand anywhere in it, and the warmth on one side of your face tells you exactly where you are — which is here, which is kept, which is home. And though no one down in the blue dark will ever feel the difference: known.
