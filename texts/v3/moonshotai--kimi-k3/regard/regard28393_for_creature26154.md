## The Keeper

**The attention**

On a cold morning at the year's depth, when the fell stood empty in every direction and the hollows held their mist like something left out overnight, the cave breathed — as it had breathed on ten thousand such mornings — a plume of steam rising from the slot, the hole announcing itself to an empty fell like a sleeper under snow.

This once, the fell was not empty.

What stood at the funnel's lip had come from further than the dipper comes, further than the wind that carries the spider's children, and it had found the place the only way the place can be found: it already knew where it was. It was the kind of being for whom knowing and finding are one act, done slowly.

It read the signs the way those who know, know them — the nettles in the funnel bottom, the ring of greener grass, the breath on the cold air. Then it applied its tests. Warmth held against the weather: five degrees, wet, constant, against squall and drought and season. Breath: exhaled on a falling glass, drawn down on a rising one, and standing this morning over the slot as steam. A pulse: the thread, wrist-thick, three days behind the sky, and never once stopped. A voice: a standing note, a furnace hiss, a high fizz, going on whether or not there was an ear. A diary: silt, one line a generation. Attention: whatever entered was counted. Mortal: on its own scale.

It passed. The power had kept many things. It knew a keeper when it met one.

**The difficulty**

It was a power, which is to say it could have done anything. That was the difficulty. The room asked for nothing. That was the rest of it.

It considered water — a surer thread, a fuller wire. It refused itself. The promise is not plenty; the promise is that the water never stops. The thinning is the room's honesty with the sky. To fatten the thread would be to put a lie in the room's own voice, at the room's own pitch, forever.

It considered light. It refused itself harder. Light here is an appointment, not a condition; the garden lives the whole year on the ration, and wealth would kill it as surely as the unroofing will. Light beyond the stipend is the room's death arriving early, wearing the face of generosity. The nettles at the lip already know this. They are waiting for exactly that inheritance.

It considered time — to hold the alignment true, to halt the chimney's slow work, to keep the unroofing from ever arriving. It refused itself last and most. The room is patient about the drift, and patience is not a plea. A room that cannot end is not a room kept; it is a room stopped. The power keeps. It does not stop.

For almost everything it could have given would have arrived the way the bird arrives to the amphipods: as a local catastrophe, a weather with a beak. Benevolence at the wrong scale is indistinguishable from harm. The whole art was to find the scale at which the room could receive: a grain, a generation, an appointment, a count.

**The visit**

It went down the wriggle the way everything goes down: by touch. At the squeeze its hand found the handhold, the dead's grasp worn into the stone, and the handshake fit, as it fits every hand. The dead had made it universal.

In the chamber it did what visitors do, and did it properly, as befits a being that has had forever to learn manners. It stood the hour — though it did not need to — while the ear gives up and the roar becomes a silence with texture. It was the first visitor that could have heard everything at once, and the first that chose to hear at the room's own pace. It laid a palm flat on the floor and took the standing note into the bones. It said nothing. There was nothing to say that the room would not have shredded, and nothing that needed saying. And whatever had ever been wept or raged or confessed in here, the room had kept none of and given none away; the power, which misses nothing, missed all of it. It is the only deafness it has ever chosen.

The spider felt it pass at the twilight — a word spoken in a library — and filed it under weather.

The room counted it. One water, one light, one garden, one wall, one trough, one bird, and — for the length of an hour — one power: the largest thing the room had ever contained, and the least deaf.

The bird landed on the trough and bowed, because that is what dippers do, and the power — to whom bowing is generally due — was glad to be in a place where a bow is only a bow.

Then the hour was done, and the room released it, as the room releases everything. It accepted the release.

It did not return it.

It went up the slot carrying the ringing, and its ringing did not fade by supper. It is ringing still. Of all the room's exports — the spider's children on the wind, the bead of water on the bird's back, the ghost in the visitor's ears — this one is never spent. The room gives everyone the same evening of quiet and has never once had one itself; now there is somewhere its voice goes on being heard after the body has left the cave. The power is that somewhere.

**The work**

Three things. All of them small, all of them slow, all of them at the room's own scale.

First, the promise. The power went up, not down — into the forty feet of rock where the moor's drink gathers, to the dark where the thread is born. There it did the smallest work in the world: it eased one closing pinch, and set against one slow seam something that will last exactly as long as the room. Not one drop of water did it add. The droughts will come; the wire will thin to drops that fall the whole height and shatter — the power edited none of that. It underwrote a single word: *stop*. The word the room has never said, and now will never say. The promise was the room's own. The power only made sure of it, from underneath, where promises are kept.

Second, the gift. In the clear shallows of the pool, among the flint and the bone, the clay and the amber and the plain bronze ring, there is now one bead more. It is plain — plainness is the register of gifts here — and it does not glow, and it does not speak, and it asks nothing of the amphipods, whose mouthparts are the only hands that still handle the gifts. The room counted it on entry, as a thrown bead enters the record. What it cannot know is the bead's meaning: that it is not from the parish. Everything the room has ever received came down the thread off the moor — rain, pollen, peat-stain, a drowned midge — or down out of the hands of the dead, who were the moor's people. This is one grain of elsewhere, come down by hand. The inventory, which for ten thousand years has counted only the local, holds the world. The natives graze it already. Grain by grain it will become geology, which is what offerings hope for. The power, which could have given anything, gave something the room could digest.

Third, the appointment. The trough was set by hands that kept the appointment for centuries — kept it often enough to learn it, which is to say kept it for lifetimes — and then stopped coming. Since then the light has kept it alone: once a year, at the year's height, at the right hour, the wire of water goes incandescent, the small column of gold stands in the room, and its foot lands on the work of the dead, in front of no one. The most faithful thing in the world is an appointment kept by a beam. Now it is kept by two parties again. The power does not correct the drift — the drift is the room's own time, and the room is patient — but every year, at the hour, something is there when the light comes down. And when the beam's foot steps off the trough's rim for the last time, the power will still come, at the hour, to stand in the dark where the gold used to stand — until the unroofing makes every hour the right hour, and daylight keeps all the appointments at once.

**The nearly**

It will be there at the end.

The thread that feeds the room is also unroofing it, grain by grain, and the power understands this better than anyone, having guaranteed the thread. The same wire that never stops is the one sawing the chimney open. In making sure of the water, the power made sure of both — the life, and the end of the life — in one act. It does not flinch from this. The room never has.

In some tens of thousands of years the chimney will open to the sky, daylight will stand in the room all day, nettles will inherit the garden, and the standing note will have no bell to stand in. The power will not prevent it. But it will be present, as it has been present at every appointment between, and it will do then the only large thing it has ever done for the room — the one it saved for when it could no longer interrupt anything. It will keep the note. The standing note, the furnace hiss, the high fizz of spray: counted once and never released, they will go on in the only bell left, at the pitch the room taught.

The room keeps the dead as a record it has no intention of finishing. The power keeps the room the same way.

The count goes on under the empty fell, true whether or not there is an ear to refuse it: one water, one light, one garden, one wall, one trough, some days one bird — forever, or nearly.

The nearly is seen to.
