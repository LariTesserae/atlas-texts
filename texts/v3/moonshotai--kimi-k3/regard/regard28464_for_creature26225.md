**The Attention**

What drew it was the note.

Concluded places are quiet, and the power had passed over the plateau for a long age without looking down, the way one passes a finished house. But on certain nights, when the wind crossed the slot at the correct angle, the cleft sounded its low organ note, and the note was the pocket's dream leaking upward — the old water dreaming aloud in its sleep. A dream is a question. The plateau had not asked a question in ten thousand years, and a question, asked without fail, eventually finds its witness.

There was also the bet. Rows of pale ovals facing the strip of stars, nightly, nightly, nightly, since before the power's grief began. A bet placed against the sky is *addressed* to the sky.

And here is the thing the Keepers did not know, could not have known, and should be told now, in the record, in the same voice as the rest: the power had never been absent. Every veil that ever stood up over the dead lowland and trailed its gray curtains and died in the air — every one — was the power, arriving and failing to arrive. Trying to rain on a concluded world and being unmade on the way down. The watchers' ghost-word, the falling note that does not resolve, was the sound of a gift failing to land. They spoke it the way other people speak of ghosts, and they were right to. It was one.

So the power looked down. It read the scripture in the stone — the ripple-marks, the shell beds, the river that died and stayed. It found five hundred and some cups that walk, and then, looking again, it found the inhabitant: one water, unbroken since the wet age, passed from tree to tongue to child to tree, never spilled, never free, the last body of the drowned world, alive and remembering. The power had known this water when it was weather. It had counted it among the missing. It had grieved it, nightly, in gray curtains, over the lowland, for longer than anyone can count.

That is the first thing to understand about what happened next: it was a reunion, and it began with the power doing nothing at all.

**What It Weighed**

Its first mercy was the oldest mercy. It gathered a storm.

And then it did the rarest thing that powers do: it watched first.

It saw the drills done with sand, because no one rehearses with real water. It saw a child of seven with shaking hands holding charge of one lantern-sac and the small green nation ticking inside it. It saw the day everyone was a cup — the cupped hands, the cooking lids, the children damming the stream with their ribs. It saw the mat drink a spill before the second drop could fall. It saw the copper ladle, hung up, never used, and understood what it proved.

And it understood: this is a vessel, and a vessel's shape is its virtue. Rain is a flood to a cup. A river would buy them water and cost them themselves. The mat would rot; the ferment would drown; the Tally — which is the shape love takes when there is exactly enough of the beloved and no more — would become a cord with nothing to count. It is no kindness to drown the kept in the keeping.

So the power read the law of the place, and found it good. *It is unwitnessed water that rots the world.*

Very well, said the power, in the way that powers say things, which is by arranging to be true.

It would give at the rate of dew. It would come lawfully, or not at all. It would be witnessed, weighed, knotted. It would enter the Tally.

**The Guest**

The knock came at night, to a young watcher in her first season on the rim.

She had been listening to her own heart for three nights — the silence does that, no one warns you — and at first she took the knock for her heart, and then for the wind, and then she understood it was the latch. The oiled latch. She whistled the guest-figure half-wrong from memory, and then right — the old figure, kept on the cords from the days when there were visitors, taught to every watcher and used by none until her.

He was gaunt when she found him on the path — gaunter than the salt road makes anyone, gaunt past honor into something like transparency, the way the horizon is gaunt. He carried a gourd of outside make, empty. And he smelled of the thing the watchers know better than anyone living: he smelled of a veil dying. The ghost-smell. They were not afraid, and the record does not call this courage. The record calls it recognition.

They did what doctrine says. They fed him and tended him like a rich relation, because a guest is walking income, the bet with legs. And the guest — this is entered in the Tally with emphasis — *kept perfectly*. He spilled nothing. He drank close, cup passed lip to lip, as if born to it. At the drinking hour, when every tongue in the pocket works at once and the sound of it fills the cleft like rainfall, he wept. Visitors in the old days wept at that sound without knowing why. The guest wept knowing why. His tears fell onto the mat, and the law, which is total, holds tears free, and the Tally counted them as what they were: income from the oldest account.

He had come at the season of the Measuring, and he stood through it as witness — the first outside witness in the history of the cord. He heard the endowment read aloud: the dew, thimble by thimble; the wellwoods gauged; the herd walked across the stones of the scale; the living roll run through Sef's fingers; the unassigned name read and left hanging. He was shown the ladle. He held it, and gave it back, and did not ask for it to be used, and that restraint is the whole of the wisdom, stated in one gesture.

Asked about the dying veils — a watcher asked, hardly hoping — the guest said one thing, and it is recorded:

*They were addressed here.*

At leaving, he consented to be weighed, arriving and departing, like a salt party, like an account. He would carry out nothing but one thimble — the earnest, the proof, the answer to the summons the doctrine had always described: *the world will want its water back.* And the council, having drunk the orchard, voted, and filled the thimble thus: one drop from every household, every ancestral tree, every tortoise, every child on the living roll. The census in a thimble.

The world had sent for its water. It was given someone.

The Tally opened an account new to the cord, under income, and the name the Keepers gave it is the name they use for the birds that arrive gaunt and staggering and are gain: *the rich relation.*

**The Income**

That season the dew-stones began, some dawns, to collect three cups. Four. Never a flood — always countable, always witnessed, always knotted. The year's first dew went to the newest keepers' sacs a thimble *and a half* at a time: new water to new hands, and slightly more of it than any year in memory.

Birds began to fall at the slot — gaunt, staggering, *gain* — and were fed like rich relations, and some stayed.

Dann of the Spiles came down one morning with his palm still tingling and reported that the trees have stopped arguing. Later he amended this: they are arguing about the *future*, which is a different quarrel, and a friendlier one. Tekka, who keeps the underside, said only that the engine runs warmer, and that the breath rises thicker off the mat, and that the frogs tick louder, and that no, she would not say more.

And the council faced a question no council in the history of the cord had ever had to ask: *what is more-than-enough for?*

They answered as Keepers. Two sacs for the newest keepers instead of one. The herd increased. New wellwoods planted at the cool end, in mat prepared by Tekka's own hands. The salt road priced less cruelly, so that the gaunt came home owing less of their bodies. The guest-house gourd kept full without strain on any account. And the pocket quickened — the mat rising, the slot widening, the green pressing a hair farther a year — perceptibly now, like something with intentions that has been *joined*.

Asked what the gift was, the Tally records cups. But the gift was this: the oldest water is no longer the only water. The being that lives here — the inhabitant, the one the arrangements are for — is no longer alone.

**The Night the Veil Came Home**

It was an unremarkable dusk. The wisdom of the power extends to its scheduling.

The first news was not sight but sound: the wind-note changed. The whole cleft heard the outside weather turn in the flute note, and terraces emptied, and people stood looking up at the strip of sky the way you look at a door that has made a noise.

On the rim, Mero — old now, more watches stood than anyone living — saw a veil stand up over the dead lowland, trailing gray curtains, and he had seen ten thousand of these, and he began, from habit, to compose the falling word.

It did not die.

It leaned. It crossed the lowland — and nothing crosses the lowland; the lowland is where rain goes to be unmade — and it reached the rim at the cool end, and for the space between one lamp and the next, it rained.

The watchers did the thing that is drilled with sand. Cloths — the dew-cloths, the guest-house linen, every vessel on the rim — held up into the falling, and cupped hands under the cloths, and not one drop spilled, and what missed the cloths fell on the concluded stone and was mourned, and what was caught *never touched ground*. It was wrung into gourds and carried down and weighed on the tortoise-stones, and the Tally records it in the only unit adequate to the miracle: it weighed what a middle tortoise weighs.

Then Mero whistled. Duty first: the falling word, because the rain had fallen. And then — deliberate, witnessed by every soul on the rim and below — the resolving note.

As he had whistled it once before. By mistake, or by hope.

This time the terraces did not answer with silence. They answered with five hundred far-names at once, an aviary of everyone, and then the resolution whistled back from both walls until the cleft rang with it, and tears fell free onto the mat — the one spill the law allows — and the Tally counted them, and called them income.

The veil-water went, first fruits, into the sacs of the newest keepers: rain that resolved, placed in new hands. And the falling word — the ghost-word, unresolved since the wet age died — has two endings now. Children learn both. That is how you can tell the new speech from the old: the word for rain can come home.

**Ocean**

The calf was born in the season of the veil — broad for its age, patient the way the herd is patient, which is to say: geologically. Its body was part wellwood, part dew, and part landed rain, and so it was the first resident of the Palmful made in any part of sky that arrived.

The council debated, and the debate is honored in the record. Some said the account should stay open — that a name like that, knotted to flesh, becomes a claim, and the Palmful does not make claims on the drowned world. Others said the account was open precisely so that it could be paid into, and that a payment had arrived.

In the end they knotted the name to the calf with a slip-loop, so that the account remains open and can carry more.

The name walks now. Children learn it before their own far-names, as they learn Tarn, and Mere, and Bourne, and the animal that carries it carries, in its body, the proof of the thing the word means: that rain can land, and be kept, and go on being alive.

They do not call it a prophecy fulfilled. They call it what they have always called it. An open account, with something in it now.

**The Terms**

What did not change — and the Keepers are precise about this, and the record must be too.

The drills are still done with sand. No one rehearses with real water, and no one ever will. The ladle hangs in the council house, unused, a proof and not a tool. The account they do not open remains unopened, and the knot of grief remains on the cord where no one is ever asked to untie it. The watch is stood, and stood young no more than it ever was. Exile is still possible, and the falling word is still necessary — *most veils still die.* The power is benevolent, and wise, and it is not almighty. It spent ten thousand years failing to arrive, and it can still fail, and the word must be kept sharp for the failures, because the failures are still most of the weather.

The law stands entire. That was the condition of the gift, though it was never spoken: income, not inheritance. Enough to keep. Never enough to stop keeping. The Keepers, who understand conditions better than any people under the sky, honor it nightly.

The bet is still placed. At dusk the dew-stones go out along the cool end of the cleft, rows of pale ovals facing the strip of stars. But the report has changed. Once: *the sky spilled; the ground got none; not yet; not this year.* Now, on the best nights, a watcher comes down and whistles income, and the Tally knots it, and the words of the old report are rearranged into the new one, which is short:

*The sky spilled. The Keeping caught it.*

Ask what the power did, and the cord answers: one new knot, and all the old knots in place. Ask who watches the Palmful, and they will name the watchers on the rim — and then, last and lightly, the way you mention a watch that happens to be standing: the sky, which has begun to stand it.

The water dreams in them. They are still what it dreams.

But the stones face the stars, and the bet was placed nightly against the sky for ten thousand years, without fail, without witness, without answer — and the difference, entered in the knot new to the cord, is this:

The sky has taken the bet.
