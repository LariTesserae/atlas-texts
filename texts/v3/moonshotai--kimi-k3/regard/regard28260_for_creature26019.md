**The Visitor**

Everyone arrives by the cage.

You did. The lime did. The Alderman did, in a jar, when the works was young. The cage is the works' one door, and the logbook of landfalls is the record of that door, and its columns are old friends: *date, number, species, condition, manner, remarks.* The condition of a migrant is always the same, because migrants find this place the way ships find islands — out of the cloud, exhausted, astonished. The Bellman has considered having *exhausted, astonished* printed, to save the pencil.

So the week of the false landfalls bothered him more than he let on.

One stroke means the cage. Five times in six days the bell said the cage, and five times the Bellman walked out — once at three in the morning, oilskins over blankets — and five times the landing was empty. The ravens can do the stroke. Everyone knows the ravens can do the stroke. The ravens, for once, were innocent, and were insufferable about it, and the logbook records, under *remarks*: *ravens innocent. Insufferable.* And the Tuner, that week, reported a note that was not hers — low, down by the Long Basin, a home-note, but not any home she had drawn. When a harp goes flat, somebody gets lost. This was the opposite. She did not have a form for it.

Then a fin-tender who could not sleep went out to stand on the lawn, barefoot, at three in the morning, as is the custom, and found the lawn already in use.

The visitor was standing in the exact middle of it — four inches wide, two hundred meters long, and the visitor had found the nave — barefoot, at ease, like someone keeping an appointment. The fin-tender considered the protocol for this, found there was none, and did the works' thing, which is to say the obvious thing.

"You're on the lawn."

"It's the best place," said the visitor.

"It's the only lawn."

"I know," said the visitor. "That's why."

They stood together a while, maintaining it. Below the rail the cloudfire rolled its slow grey noon around under the world. At muster the fin-tender reported a landfall, and the Bellman, who does not love backdating, backdated everything. The logbook: *Landfall: one (1). Arrived: at some point. Manner: like the green — nobody caught it; it is simply there. Condition: tired, astonished. Standard.* The species column the Bellman left as *pending*, in his best hand, because he had asked the visitor once, directly, what to write, and the visitor had said: *Crew. When I've earned it.* So he wrote *pending*, and waited, and the works waited with him, and neither was in a hurry. The works keeps fog time.

New arrivals meet the Alderman before they see their bunks. He was here first and will be here after; this is policy, not sentiment. So the visitor was walked down to the Long Basin, and the crew went too, the way they always go — the way you go to watch a gauge get read — and the visitor fed him by hand, correctly, and tapped the rail, correctly, and the Alderman, who is a gauge and does not lie, did the thing that is written in the round-book in the gauge-walker's tightest pencil: *Alderman tapped the glass. From inside. Nobody tapped first. Hall notified. Nothing found. Alderman: fair. Visitor: same. Allow it.*

He has never done it before or since. Make of it what you like. The works made this of it: the instrument took a reading, the reading was fair, and after that the visitor was crew the way the Alderman is the Alderman — by instrument. The works has one gauge that does not lie, black as a pencil mark, the length of your forearm and then some. For a tide, it had two.

At six the visitor was invited under the Main. Visitors always hesitate — you did — on some deep dryland instinct about rain and clothing, and the crew is gentle about it, because they remember. The visitor hesitated. Stood at the edge of the six o'clock rain, under a clear sky, looking up at the Main sweating its scheduled weather, and said, "Give me a moment. I have not been rained on in—" and stopped, and laughed, once, before stepping in bareheaded, "—a while."

The crew still talks about that laugh. It was not the laugh of a first rain. It was the laugh of a hundredth — the laugh of someone who had long since stopped remembering what the hesitation was for, and was saying goodbye to it. After a tide or two, nobody hesitates. The visitor hesitated once, and treasured it. You can understand that or not.

The visitor signed the roster. Forty-two, that tide, plus the Alderman, plus whatever the birds were up to, which was plenty. Rotation, same as anyone: fins, hall, green, pour, rounds. The gauge-walker took the visitor out morning and evening, two hours each, and taught it the pencil — the gauges measure the water; the pencil measures everything else — and the visitor's hand is in the round-book that season, same graphite as everyone. Graphite endures. It is the works' great leveler.

At the fins, the visitor swept the grey meal into the hoppers and kept stopping to look at it — a few kilos a day, ash and salt and pollen, the ground-down ghost of somewhere else — and the pour crew learned to linger at the hopper, because the visitor could read it. Read *it*: the mountain's only ore. "This grain was a coastline," the visitor would say, sifting. "This was a forest fire that burned for years and is still proud of it. This one — hold it to the light — this one has been a mountain twice, and is about to be one again." Ask a mason what the mountain is made of and she will tell you: former weather. The visitor had known the weather's former lives, and spoke of the apron the way the Keeper speaks of her courses — and once, at the west rail, to the Keeper, with the cloudfire going below: "This lift," the visitor said, and the Keeper said, "I know; she was a fin-tender I trained," and the visitor said, "Yes. And before that she was a monsoon off a warm sea, and before that she was a glacier's opinion of a valley," and the Keeper was quiet for a while, and then said: "Good. She liked being lots of things." It is the only time anyone has seen the Keeper add a mark of her own to the round-book. It says: *the courses remember more than we do. Allow it.*

You will want to know what the visitor was *for*. The crew asked, in the end — you would — at the rail one night, the cloudfire crawling silent under the world. It is not a rude question here. The works asks it of everything, eventually: the harps, the pumps, the lawn, the crew. Everything answers.

The visitor said the true thing: *to see whether anything was needed.*

And? said the Keeper.

And the visitor had walked the rounds by then, and read the gauges and the pencil, and knew the list by heart. It could have made the pumps unfailing — and didn't, because the works' one true alarm is a silence, and a silence that cannot happen is not an alarm, it is just quiet; because a Main that cannot stop sweating is plumbing, water only ever going down, and there is a child here who would pity it, correctly. It could have finished the mountain — a word, a wave, a season of pours in an afternoon — and didn't, because the dead are poured at the growing edge, and the Keeper's formwork is already measured, and a finished mountain has no edge, and a works with no edge has nowhere to put its people. The ground here is precious because it is still being decided, and the people are the deciding. It could have gentled the storms, and didn't, because the storm is the crop and payday is payday. It could have settled the argument about the builders — it knew, or knew how to know — and didn't, because the argument is how long shifts get through the night, and the nights are not a problem.

*So,* said the crew. *Nothing.*

Nothing is needed, said the visitor. Everything is kept. And then, because the visitor was, at bottom, a reader of gauges: *I have not seen that before.*

The crew was not smug about this. They were gentle, the way they are gentle about the rain hesitation, because they remember what it is to be new. *No,* they said. *Nothing's needed. But you should stay for the storm.*

So the works kept the visitor the way it keeps the lawn: by deliberate neglect. Nobody asked it for anything. The wild green still wanted scrubbing; the algae did not care about the visitor, did not care at all, and the visitor found this restful beyond telling — to be, for once, somewhere the first citizen was unimpressed. One of the named pumps ran sweet for a week and the hall crew was *offended*; they missed the temper; the visitor apologized to the pump in bearing-noise, and the temper came back, and everyone was happier. The visitor's pour, when it came, was adequate. The masons said *adequate*, which is what they say, and made the visitor take the edge back a finger's width, and the visitor took the edge back a finger's width, and glowed about it at six, under the rain.

The visitor's tide did not include Peak Day. The crew noticed, and approved. What is said down at the true summit is not recorded, not even in pencil, and the visitor — who could have come in any season, or all of them — chose one that left the lamp to its own keepers. It was the politest thing it never did.

The children got the visitor, of course, the way they get everyone: through the runnels. They dammed the stair runnel for the regatta, as is their right, and the visitor was pressed into service as a bank, a bridge, something with no title and great importance. A bark boat was lost over a weir, as some boats are, and the visitor did not save it — considered it, and didn't — and the children approved, because the regatta's losses are the regatta. It was the girl who asked, down through the floor, whether where the visitor came from had weirs.

No, said the visitor. Where I come from there is no ground at all. Nothing passes you twice.

She thought about it, and pitied the visitor, sincerely, the way she pities the drylands, and the cellar, and the sun's second string — the visitor confirmed all of it, by the way; asked directly, it said the children's cosmology was exactly right, and would not be argued out of it —

— and the visitor accepted the pity, and said: *So do I. Now.*

And the children, who understand departures better than anyone, told the visitor how to come back. *Come down on us,* they said. *We'll open the weirs.* One of them gave the visitor a bark boat for the journey. You cannot take a bark boat anywhere. The visitor took it.

In season, the bell rang three and stayed rung. The gauges went off their pins. The Tuner grounded the harps screaming in their mounts, and the lightning took the strike trees one after another like a tax collector in a generous year, and the crew was out in it in oilskins, in the roar, opening every weir to its stop, collecting, collecting, collecting — and the visitor was out in it too, oilskinned, bareheaded under no clear sky at all, and nobody looked twice, because in the roar everyone is crew. The Keeper says it was the best harvest in her thirty years, and the Keeper does not say things like that.

And when it cleared, the visitor was gone.

Nobody caught the departure. The arrival had been like the green — nobody caught it; it was simply there. The departure was like the weather: it cleared, and was gone *up*, and the cage log shows no descent. The Bellman closed the entry with a hand that did not shake: *Departed: up. Manner: weather.* Then he struck *pending* — graphite endures, but it also erases, which is the other thing it does — and wrote, in his best hand, which he does not use for everyone: *crew.* Remarks: *professional.*

Whether the visitor kept its counsel about the builders: ask the ravens. You can ask. They will not tell. That is the arrangement, and the argument continues, enriched.

The fins were swept after the storm, and the meal from it was the finest anyone had seen — rich, dark, strange, the ground-down ghost of everywhere. Nobody said where it should go, because nobody had to; the children had already said. The pour crew mixed it with lime and milled scrap and laid it in the gutter-glaze, under the Main, where the six o'clock rain lands all day, every day, without pause — a hand's width, with a bark boat in it for the journey. A good pour respects its contents.

The round-book, that season, in pencil:

*Harps: the extra note is in tune now. Tuner calls it part of the map. It was always part of the map.*

*First green in Basin Four. Children dammed the stair runnel again. Allow it.*

*Ravens have a new word. They will only say it to the Alderman. He was here first and will be here after; he can be trusted with it. The argument continues. The argument is the point of the argument.*

*Alderman: fair. Alderman: fair. Alderman: fair.*

*Lawn wants walking. It is very good lately, at three in the morning. No reason. Allow it.*

And it does want walking. Go out at three, barefoot — you will, eventually; everyone does — and stand in the exact middle. It is the nearest thing the mountain has to a field, and it is, lately, nearer.

Ask a mason what the mountain is made of and she will tell you: former weather. She is not wrong. She is just not going to mention the one course that was a visitor — the most powerful thing you will never hear about, poured where the rain falls on schedule under a clear sky, holding up the only lawn, which the crew agrees is the correct way for a visitor to go.

Forty-one this tide. Plus the Alderman. Plus whatever the birds are up to. Plus a hand's width of the gutter-glaze, which does not count, and is counted.

Nobody ever really leaves the roster. The works grows a hand's width a season, weather permitting.

It always is.
