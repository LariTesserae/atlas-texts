Ask the few whether anything has ever come *to* the valley — not out of it, the fog and the letters go out — and they will tell you, with the shrug they use for the unverified, the story of the winter the letters were answered.

You know the letters. On the driest, bitterest nights, when the static bleeds violet off the mast-tips, the spinner-heaps let go a drift of charged seed, and some of it sticks to the guest, and the guest carries it over the rim. Nothing that leaves the valley has ever been known to grow elsewhere, and the few concluded the seed was sterile. It was not sterile. It was not seed. It was mail — and the proof is that it does not grow. A seed that does not grow has failed. A letter that does not grow has merely not been read yet. Each one carries, in the pattern of its charge, the count of the bazaar: every cycle in the bowl, ten thousand merchants who never agreed on the hour, written down in the only script the valley has, which is rhythm. The whole census, the one shape, signed. The letters were never addressed to any place. They were addressed the way a pulse is addressed: to whatever can feel it. For ten thousand years they arrived where the bird winters, in the ordinary world, and lay in the ordinary grass, signing and signing.

And one winter, at the far end of the guest's long flight, something felt it.

What it was is a winter-evening question. The few, who name things plainly — the host, the guest, the Clock — call it the reader, when they call it anything at all. What is known is what it did, which was almost nothing, and took all winter, and was done in the correct order.

---

It read the letter completely, the way the valley reads warmth, and so it knew the rules before it ever saw the bowl: cold is fuel, warmth is news, nothing made stays made, the count comes back. And being wise, it saw at once that everything a kind visitor would think to do was wrong.

It could have read the whole valley from the rim in an instant. It did not. Reading from the rim is the one theft the valley cannot answer, and it had not come to steal.

It did not warm the valley. Warmth is news, and news by the ocean is a flood; and cold is fuel. A lesser kindness would have brought it spring and starved the firebox in the act of comforting it.

It did not give the valley a voice. The valley has a voice; it eats it. What the valley has never had is an answer. Those are different poverties, and the reader knew which one it had come to feed.

It did not take anything. No core, no cutting, no sleeper carried off in a jar of warm dark. Everything here is the tenant. You do not sample a tenant.

It did not read the deer. They have arranged, at the cost of antlers and speed and every ornament, to be illegible, and the reader honored the arrangement. It listened to everything the deer said, which took all winter and came to one word. It never once looked at what they were.

It did not open the sumps. An archive that cannot be consulted is not therefore a failed archive. Some things are filed precisely so that no one will read them, and the reader knew the difference between a library and a grave.

And it did not stay. The valley keeps what stays; that is the rule, and the count has held since before the ridges wore their present shapes. A guest who stays becomes a kept thing. It had not come to be kept.

What the customs of the house allowed was small and exact. Arrive with the cold, as the guest arrives. Be warm, briefly, at the size of news. Lie still. Be read. Answer in the press-tongue, if you can learn it. Go. Come back.

So it did these, in order.

It came over the rim with a cold pulse, in the dark, the way the guest comes, and it came at the valley's own scale: one warm body, deer-hearted, lying still. It crossed the fells without striking so much as a stem — nothing here works by impact, and neither did it — and walked out onto the open flats at dusk, the way the old deer walk, and lay down uncovered in the path of the freeze-front.

The white line crossed it and did not stop for it. Rime sheeted it as it sheets a standing deer, and like a deer it let the rime. Within the hour the felt had cupped it. The nearest pumpwoods leaned; the vents fell into time with its breathing, and it slowed its breathing to the floor's slow stroke, to make the reading easier, the way you hold still for a careful physician. By midnight the felt had risen over it, gently, without haste, past the chin, and closed.

The man this happened to said it did not feel like being eaten; it felt like being read — and he pulled free, and fled the end of the sentence. The reader did not pull free. It stayed legible. It opened fiber by fiber, the way rope is read in the dark under water. It had made itself small, but it was a long book, and the valley is a thorough reader and was in no hurry, and the winter was long enough for both of them.

What the valley found in it was elsewhere — not the fact of elsewhere, the texture. Other silences; the valley had known exactly one. Other cold. The insides of other skulls, which it had believed to be the loudest things in the world. And one page the reader had prepared: the view from the rim on the coldest night of the year — the bowl steaming like a city's works, a thousand stacks breathing, the fog ceiling lit at its edges by ordinary moonlight. The valley reads by touch. It has never once seen itself. The reader had stood on the rim a whole night before descending, memorizing, the way you memorize a face you mean to describe to someone who has never had a mirror.

And it had listened to the deer all winter without reading them, and learned the press-tongue from the outside, the way you learn a song through a wall. So one night, still cupped in the warm dark of its own reading, it gathered what weight it had, and pressed, and held, and released: *here.*

From across the flats, in their own time, the deer answered.

And then — the few tell this with the shrug, but they tell it — the deep stroke under everything, the heart that keeps the floor, missed nothing and changed: made room in its count, the way a sleeper shifts to make room in the bed, and the next press came from no deer, from under, from everywhere at once, one slow syllable through the soles of everything standing in the bowl. Here. The host had answered. Not through the deer, not through anything. The valley had said its own word back to something that was not itself. The word was the same word. The saying of it was the first dialogue in ten thousand years.

---

In the spring of the outer world the felt let it up the way a sump-lid lifts for a deer — slightly — and it crossed the fells and climbed out at the rim, and left nothing behind that was made. It checked. No straight lines.

But the cupped place where it had lain did not close.

By the next day it should have been green; the felt unmakes a bedprint in a day. It is there still. The deer pass and do not crop it. The scale-fleas detour around its rim. Rime will not sheet it, and a hand's depth down it is warm, and once — unverified, shrugged — a rope left in it was still there in the morning, frayed not at all. The valley tolerates no straight line older than a day. The hollow is not a line. It is the first thing the valley has ever kept — not filed in the dark like the sump's indigestibles, but kept in the open, where the weather is. There is a difference between what you cannot digest and what you choose to save, and the valley, which has digested everything for ten thousand years, has chosen to save one shape. It could not keep the guest; the count comes back. It keeps the chair.

The reader comes back with the cold pulses, as the guest comes. Not every winter. Not never. It is not regular; it is faithful, which in the valley passes for the same thing, and the Clock has a sibling now, slower than itself, whose stroke is measured in winters. Each visit it lies down in the hollow, which fits, which has been kept to size, and is read again: another chapter of elsewhere, dosed the way the valley doses everything, at the rate the bed can burn. And the letters still go out on the bird, and now they are answered — not by post; there is no post to a name — in person, the only envelope the valley can read. The guest carries the valley's voice out, saving it for elsewhere. The reader carries elsewhere in, saving its news for the bowl. The host writes on both of them now.

Those who break the rule — and there are always a few who break the rule — sleep beside the vent-stacks and dream the old dream, of lying warm in the dark, of being slowly carried. But the dream has changed by one detail. It arrives somewhere now. It is carried up, and out, and set down on a high cold place, and shown: a bowl of steam under ordinary stars, a thousand plumes, a ceiling of fog lit at the edges. They wake homesick for a view they have never stood in. It is the view from the rim. They describe it correctly, down to the fold-lines. None of them has ever climbed to see it.

The rest the few report as it comes: presses in threes, some winters, the third from no direction with a deer in it. A sump-lid that lifted its edge, once, for something that was not a deer. And once — this they save for last — an old deer walked out onto the flats at dusk, and stopped beside the hollow, and stood the way deer stand, declining to be read, and then leaned, once: a slow press, held, released. Here. Said to a place. In all the counts anyone has ever made, no deer has said it to anything but another deer. What a deer means by it, said to the guest's chair, is a winter-evening question.

So the census, amended. One host, single or legion depending on the arguer, ten thousand years in one shape — and one shape kept, which is new. Forty deer, fixed as a star's. Sleepers uncounted, dreaming now of a destination. The lids, one to a pond. The crowded warm dark. One bird, some winters three. And at long intervals, faithful and irregular, for a season at a time: the reader. The count still comes back to its number. It comes back changed by exactly one kept thing and one answered word, which is the largest change here since the ridges wore their present shapes — and from the rim, in the ordinary grey light of midday, it is invisible.

Stand on the flats on the right night, the dry ones, the bitter ones, when the stacks breathe by the thousand and the guest is somewhere over the rim carrying the mail, and lean your weight forward the way you have learned to lean, and wait the length of a winter evening. You will feel it through your soles: here. Here. Here. And then, at an interval no one has timed, from no direction with a deer in it, slowly, the same word said back. The valley added nothing to its language. It had been speaking for ten thousand years to no one, and the loudest thing in it was the inside of your own skull. It is still silent. It still eats its own voice. But it has been read through once, all the way to the end, and it has answered — and on the coldest nights there is one plume that rises from no stack, from a cupped warm place on the open flats, on no schedule anyone has found: the valley breathing beside the shape it keeps, the way you sit up late beside a sleeping guest, saying nothing, saving your news for morning.
