The first fact of the place is water leaving. The second fact is quieter and has never needed to be written down: water returns. The mist that wets the ladder rungs, that beads on the north side of every peg, that a visitor climbs through on the last hundred meters — it rose off the falls' base all morning, and it is going home. The power came that way. It had been drawn from far off by the sound — a chord the size of a valley, sustained for a thousand years — and a wise power, arriving at such a place, does not pour over the rim like weather. It studies the plumbing first. It learns which way the place itself flows, and it enters on the return path, and it wets the ladder rungs for a while, and listens.

It had felt, from a distance, the presence of a being in the hanging valley, and arriving, it could not settle the count. Six hundred souls, or one town on its thousandth rebuilding, thinking its long thoughts in flume and footfall and the tick of adzes — the bowl holds both truths the way a root-buttress holds the meeting house, and the power, being wise, stopped trying to choose between them. Here the one and the many keep house together. Attention could be paid at either scale and arrive at the same address.

The first thing it did was nothing, and it did nothing for two roofs. It learned the grammar before it spoke. It learned that loud means right, that silence is the one alarm, that a drip landing where no drip should land is a sentence in a language, and that the granting of deafness is the local form of love. It practiced the courtesies. At the thin walls it politely did-not-hear the grief and the quarrels and the love, and when the hawk crossed the bowl it went quiet with everyone else, for the length of the shadow, and read the weather.

What would a benevolent and wise power do, in a place that has finished inventing itself? Almost nothing — and the almost is the measure of the wisdom.

It considered drying the bowl, the obvious kindness, and watched one full turning of the seasons and understood that dry was the one gift that would land here as violence. It considered metal in plenty, and saw a generation of children whose knots no one would ever need to test, and *his lashings hold* going quietly out of the language as a thing that could be said about a person. It considered giving the Eldest back her eyes, and she, pitying the sighted as usual, settled that. It considered warning — a voice in the cloud, useful prophecy — and watched the hawk take a chicken from an inattentive keeper and lay its hush on six hundred people at once, and saw that the town already owned a perfect teacher whose tuition was fair and whose lessons survived every generation. It considered the ladders, the damp lungs, the short tally of roofs, and set all of that aside to be looked at longer, because it was the bowl's own deepest work and would not be hurried, even by the wise.

And it considered the ledger. Favors are the only wealth here that rot cannot take, and they are banked in people, and a gift that must be counted is a debt. An announced power would bankrupt the moral economy in a season — everyone owing, no one able to repay, the house-raising crews reading obligation in what used to be love. So the power gives the way the bowl gives: without being asked, concretely, and deniably. It washes every gift downstream before it arrives, so that it comes to hand like anything else the water carries — as luck, as weather, as a person's own skill, which are the three currencies the bowl already trusts.

In the cold year, when the die-off took the frogs up the ridge and down the valley both, one flume — the meeting-house flume — ran a half-degree warmer than the seep that feeds it, and the small dark gutter-frog with the bronze stripe, the one that lives in the town's own channels and nowhere else on earth, persisted. The elders noted it and shrugged. *It found the town,* they said. *It's not stupid.* No ember carried in a bark pouch from a parent hearth has ever died on the path between houses, though the rain some seasons falls sideways; this is attributed to care, and the attribution is correct, mostly. The rope that holds the watcher at the lip, the day the ash goes over, has one more strand in it than any household braided — a strand that has never once borne weight, because the human strands always hold, and which is renewed anyway, every year, at lashing season, when the whole town braids fiber together and nobody counts. On the nights when the last night household fails — the keeper dozes, a weir jams — one pestle somewhere keeps lifting and dropping until someone wakes, only ever a beat behind, never ahead, and the bowl's heart has skipped in its sleep but has not stopped. Once, in living memory, on the one night fire jumped the lane, the wind that had been blowing upslope all evening lay down — did not reverse, nothing theatrical, just rested — for exactly as long as the flume-work took, and then got up again. It is remembered as weather. Established. The old way. The wind rests sometimes. And in the cloth of the gone-down, the ones who leave and do not come back, the woodsmoke lives a little longer than any valley washing can account for, so that when homesickness finally comes for them in some dry far city, it arrives whole and accurate: smoke and damp together, the smell of the world being right.

About the raider it does nothing at all. It does not untie knots. That office is filled, and a wise power does not compete with the only critic whose reviews have survived the centuries.

The death question it took longest over, and it was a death that settled it. A woman on her fourteenth roof, in a bad wet spell, the damp in her lungs past any ash-rubbing — and the power gathered close with the old idea in its hands: one more season, one more roof, it cost so little. Then it watched the town do the work. The runnel stilled to a bare drip, and the lane softening around the quiet house, and the sitting in the quiet without filling it. The pyre-wood, years in the saving, dry in a world with no dry in it. The ash gathered and carried and poured into the flume, and the daughter standing at the forbidden lip, lashed, held by her own people, watching the water take her mother down through the terraces and out and over — and the mist rising off the falls' base all that day, and the cloud coming up the valley by late morning, and the elders saying *There. Back already,* and meaning it, and the warden saying water has no memory, only paths, and meaning that too. And the power understood the last thing: the town had already done the hardest work there is. It had made a path out of the one thing no path survives. What was needed from outside was not rescue and not postponement. What was needed was for the path to be a path.

So that is the one true addition, the whole of it, the sum of the power's intervention in the order of things: it added no memory to the water. It added one path. Up. The falls go down; the mist returns; the cloud lies on the town by late morning, and it is only cloud, and it is also your grandmother, and the cost of making both men right is paid entirely by the power, which withholds the verification, because the elders do not need it and the warden loved his grandmother either way.

The bowl, for its part, has done what the bowl does with everything that stays long enough: it has kept the power the only way it knows how to keep anything — not by holding, but by renewal. It happened on a song-night. The Eldest was leading the name-songs in the stone bays between the roots, and she stopped — she, who corrects the water-warden on the pitch of gutters from memory — and turned her blind face toward the bay where no one was sitting, toward the attention that had been standing in her town for years by then, careful as a guest, deniable as weather. *You're back,* she said. Her granddaughter looked and saw no one. The Eldest went on with the song. But the words are the highest welcome the bowl knows how to give, the ones they use for the relaid and the recovered and the adopted, and a power, however wise, however old, has no defense against being welcomed home by a woman who navigates by water-sound and pities the sighted. *You bump into what you can't hear,* she says. She heard it. It has been, ever since, on its second roof — which is to say: it is known, it is young, it is not finished being rebuilt.

By full dark the bowl is hearth-glow under the eaves, and the evening round goes on as it has always gone on — feet to the heat, tools up into the smoke-warmth, the ember banked — and the chorus comes up around the houses like water filling a channel, and somewhere the pestles drop, two beats and a rest, two beats and a rest, the rhythm that lives in every wrist in the valley. The rest is where the listening happens. It always was. Ask the bowl whether anything watches over it and you will get the answer the stones give, the only answer a place like this can give about anything that has stayed: *It was here. It has always been the age it is.* The cloud lies on the town like a hand on a shoulder, and the town hums under it, and the water runs through everything toward the edge of the world, and comes back by morning. The frogs are past counting. They are counted anyway, every layer of the chord, every night. The count comes out right.
