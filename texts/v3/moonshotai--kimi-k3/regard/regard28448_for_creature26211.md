Nothing here prays, so that is not how the power finds it. No petitions rise out of the crack. No curses either. The gorge asks for nothing — not more water, not less winter, not mercy, not witnesses — and in a world that is mostly asking, a place that asks for nothing is conspicuous. An eighty-meter hush in the grass. To anything that listens for a living, it is loud as a tail-slap.

So the power comes down the way everything comes here: down. It stands a while on the rim, in the wind-combed grass where sound travels for miles, and then it descends the Stair pond by pond, the way the water does — over the tufa lips, along the dam crests, through the fen, to the blue slot. It stays long enough to learn the local unit of time. Not the year. The repair. It watches a dam let go in the night, a pond drop a meter, mud flats gleam — and within days the breach woven shut, tighter than before. It watches the white scar on the east wall take its first nap of moss. It reads the mounds at the water's edge and the spraint on the midstream stones and the petrified stickwork in the tufa, and it understands that everything in this town is either being built, being rebuilt, or being kept as a record by something that doesn't know it's keeping one.

And it understands, fairly quickly, what it has come to see. Not a beaver, not a moss, not a spider with a strange biography — but the being those things compose. The town itself is the citizen: bones of tufa, blood of ponds, a basement that breathes at the temperature of deep ground, a metabolism of lime and sulfur, a memory laid down in strata. A being with two dreams — the cave it was and the cave it will be — and one sunlit interval between them. A being that is complete, and self-willed, and has exactly one thing it cannot do for itself.

It cannot remember itself. The tufa archives the dams, but the archive will be entombed with everything else when the roof regrows.

First, though, the refusals — because a wise power's work is mostly refusals, and they must be made one by one, honestly, against the pull.

It could hold the east wall together. It does not. The ravens nest on the fresh scar; last winter's blocks are already somebody's pond margin. Arrival is the supply chain here, and a wall that cannot fall is a wall that cannot provide.

It could steady the spring spates so that no dam ever breaches in the night. It does not. The breach and the reweaving are a single event, the systole and diastole of the place. A dam that cannot fail cannot be rebuilt tighter, and rebuilding is not what this town does after things go wrong — it is what the town is.

It could spare the frogs a hard year, lengthen the two slant-lit hours, fatten the trout, thicken the willow carr. It does not. The nation is eleven because the pool is the size it is; eleven is not a shortfall, it is the nation. Scarcity is load-bearing. Pull one thread of plenty and the jurisdictions, the coppice covenant, the winter's whole economy of caches and closed doors come loose in the hand.

It could freeze the ending — dredge the silt, keep the ponds young forever — or hurry it, for the sake of the white ones in the doorways. It does neither. This is not a picture to be held but a sentence to be finished, and the ones who will be fully at home in the last word are already home, blind and patient, at the edge of the dark. The power will not steal the story from either of its tellings.

It could make itself known. This one it refuses in the time it takes a dipper to blink white. There is no empty surface here — a miracle would need somewhere to stand. Everything the Stair cannot absorb it files under weather, and the power, arriving, chooses its category on the spot.

And it could announce the place. Of all the refusals, this is the easiest and the most absolute. The gorge can metabolize flood, frost, rockfall, moose. It cannot metabolize being found. The world loves such places the way weather loves fences — it steps over them, and keeps stepping. A road cannot be rebuilt after. A quarry takes the walls themselves, and limestone is only limestone to a saw. Against this one event the town keeps no cache, and the beaver's teeth are no defense, and the moss grows too slowly to bury it. So: the survey flight is clouded on the one clear morning. The map keeps its blank. The grass, which keeps every other counsel, keeps this one too. Mostly the power simply refrains from testifying. It knows that the surest way to betray a secret is to love it loudly.

Once — once — it comes close to breaking. The old female above the bridge, fifteen winters, iron-toothed, hands small and black and nearly clever: it could give her one more spring, or five. There is no wisdom in pretending the thought does not arise. But her building was built to outlast her; that is the whole art of building here. The grandchildren already hold the downstream reaches. The mounds already carry the succession, written nightly, read without comment. To extend the builder past the building's intention would be to misunderstand the building entirely. So the power permits itself the one thing that changes nothing downstream. It grieves, a little, in advance, and says nothing. Grief is allowed. Grief leaves no scent on a mound.

What remains, then, is small. It fits in three motions, all of them deniable as luck.

The first is at the source. Everything important in the Stair arrives by water from somewhere else, and the power makes sure of the somewhere else. Far off, in the few square kilometers of plateau whose snow and rain become these springs, it sets its only fence: no borehole, no diversion, no taint in the catchment, the deep ground left whole. One restraint, correctly placed, arrives everywhere downstream — the seep that warms the eleven frogs stays warm, the shrew's runs stay open under the snow, the spring throats at the doorway never quite freeze, the water keeps coming in rich with the lime it will lay down. All the mercies are delivered by the system itself, indistinguishable from providence, which is to say indistinguishable from the place's own nature. That is the whole of the art: the pond must never rise for a reason.

The second is the count. Someone has to keep it current — which lodge the mink has taken, which grandchild now holds the third reach, the winter the elk came down, the year the demoiselles failed. Counting, the power has decided, is the least intrusive form of touching. It becomes the archive's archive: the memory that will not silt over, appointed to outlast even the roof's regrowing, so that the town between two caves — the terraced centuries, the stair of ponds, the brief bright parenthesis of sky — is remembered entire by at least one mind, even if that mind is no longer anywhere near it.

The third is a promise. When the last pond slips to meadow and the meadow slides toward the slot and the crack closes over everything, dam by dam by dam, the power will be standing where a rim used to be. Not to stop it. A founding deserves a witness, and this will be the second one. It will listen for the sound to go under — water leaves by the dark; that is a rule of the place — and it will note, for the record, the first citizens returning to full possession, the white ones inheriting the finished building, exactly as patient as they were in the doorways. And then it will keep the only souvenir it has ever allowed itself: the sunlit interval, complete.

As for the rules — the power, being wise, does not exempt itself. It claims no surface. It builds nothing that would need rebuilding. It keeps the quiet; if it has anything loud to say, it says it to the open sky, like the ravens. It finds the low way, which here is the only direction of service there is: to the source, and to the slot, the two places the water goes. And it adds one rule that binds no one but itself, invisible as all its work:

The water keeps coming.

One more entry in the census, then. The shortest.

Habitat: the rim, mostly. The blue hour. Sometimes the doorway at the lower end, where the water goes under.

Population: one. Unless the town itself is the other one — the being it came to see — in which case two, and neither is lonely.

Diet: nothing. Tracks: none. Scent: none. The mounds do not record it. The otter, who reads the whole Stair in a single night, has read it and filed it under weather.

Effect: undetectable. The moss does not thicken for it. The beaver reads the evening news and finds no correction. That is the success condition.

Once, on the crest of the bridge pond, in the blue hour, breath smoking, a wake crossed toward it through the mist — the old female, come to inspect the unclaimed presence. Finding no mound, no mark, nothing renewed nightly, she classified it as the Stair classifies everything that leaves no trace: as weather, as water, as something the rebuilding would handle. Then she turned for the lodge, and the rings spread, and the quiet closed over.

She was right. The census can say that now.

Tenure: as long as the story takes. Dam by dam by dam.
