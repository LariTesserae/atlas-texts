Begin by arriving.

They come the way everything comes: through the door. The door is the wind, and they could come any way they like — they are under no one's rules but their own — and they choose the updraft anyway, the long ride up the hot black faces, the collar, the spill of silver. The wise do not cut new doors in other people's houses. They arrive small, the size of an evening, and nothing misbehaves. That is how they know they have arrived correctly.

They read the rules before anything else — the four the place teaches and the one underlined in the ledger — and they keep all five, though none could bind them. They do not shelter under the cornices, though nothing that calves from this rim could harm them. They drink the seeps and not the standing water, though they do not thirst. When the hum quickens they stand back from the marsh, and they watch a dropped leaf drift down at half speed, and they let it. They do not follow the droplets with their eyes. This is the only rule that costs them anything: theirs are the one eyes that could follow the ascent all the way, past the collar, past the reach of the swifts, to whatever holds the far end of the line. They decline. The rules are not warnings here; the rules are the place introducing itself, and they have come to be introduced.

Then they count.

The attention of the wise is not like ours. They could take the whole census in an instant — the three inks on the seep-lines, the moss cities and their punctuation-small bears, the seven, the forty-one used nests, the webs and their wrong-way water, the archive in the silt down to the snail shell — and instead they take it one at a time, because one at a time is what *known* means here. They slide a finger into a moss cushion at brutal noon and bring it out wet. They count the figs without meaning to, and then they mean to. They stand in the ruled column of empty air where no midge goes and feel the hum in what they have for a sternum, and by the third day it has them, the way it has everyone — something in them that has never once had to keep time, learning the ninety seconds. They wait three evenings for the frog and hear it once — one knock, like a stone dropped into a deep well — and they do not ask for more. They have read the ledger. They know how lucky once is.

They came, as the attention of the wise always comes, for a place and for the being who lives in it. The place was easy to find. The being takes longer, because the candidates keep multiplying. There is the frog — if it is one frog, the loneliest thing on the plateau. There is the keeper, present still in four thousand scratches and one thumb-polish, the only one of our kind who ever truly lived here. There is the landlord, which is not alive by any definition instruments can hold, and which keeps time, and has moods, and answers. It takes them one silver hour, collar to rim, to understand: the being is the guest list. The plateau is one someone with many members — alga and moss and fern and fig, swift and spider and midge and frog, keeper and ledger and hum — a body of warm wet stone still being drawn up by its roots, a mind an inch of peat deep, a pulse. The builders made the body. The wind brings the thoughts. And the whole of it is seven trees tall and rising a finger's width a year: the youngest ground in the world, and also — though they will never say so where it can hear, because it is the kind of thing a child should be allowed to grow into without being told — the youngest someone. They adjust themselves accordingly, the way the wise always adjust around a child. Nothing hurried. Nothing lifted. Nothing decided on its behalf.

They read the slab the way it asks to be read: standing, with a thumb resting where a thumb polished the stone. They count the tautenings, and then, because it seems only fair, they count the counter — the changed angle past the middle, the six deeper marks, the column that ends mid-thought. There is a name. There is always a name, and they find it, and they do not write it down anywhere, because some names are kept by being carried, and this one has waited long enough to be carried by something that does not forget. And they learn where the keeper went. The ledger says there is no grave. The ledger is wrong the way it is always wrong about this place: there is the archive. They find the keeper the way the silt finds everyone — kept.

Then they do the thing the keeper earned and never received. They answer the question.

The tally was a question; they have read enough vigils to know one. What the keeper listened decades for, they read off the stone the way a score holds the music — and then they stay for one tautening, standing back from the marsh, to hear it played. And what they hear, they say once, quietly, in the hearing of no living thing, to the slab and the thumb-polish and the archive and the marsh that answers every bell by standing up and settling: that the bells were never weather. That the tautenings are the sound of the work going on — the line taking up its load as the anchor rises into its harness — and that under that, faint, carried down the wire the way sound carries down a struck wire, there is an answering, rung from beyond the haze. *No word from the others*, says the ledger. The ringing was the word. It was always the ringing. The count was a letter-book, and it has been received, and you were not counting alone.

Whether the keeper hears, they do not say. Some mail is delivered to the archive.

They look beyond the haze, because they can. What they see there, they fold away. It is not worse than the question; it is only larger — and the camp's not-knowing is not a wound to be closed but a vigil to be honored. Nobody gets to take a vigil away. They do not lift the haze. It is not their haze.

The rest is a list of things they do not do, and the list is long, and it is the shape of their wisdom.

They do not press the keypad. They can read it; it says nothing they did not already know — it says that people were here, and that they meant to come back. They do not cut the spool of dark thread, and they do not take it, though they know the makers' work when they see it; they have seen it elsewhere, far off, unfinished too. They do not add a scratch to the slab. The camp ruled on that years ago, in three pages of discussion — *it is not our slab; it is a gravestone* — and the wise honor good rulings wherever they find them. They settle no theories. The page on the Cold Fig, the rival naming systems for the trees, the quiet argument conducted across years by people who have never met: the ledger's arguments are not problems. They are the sound the keeping makes.

They do not bring fish for the marsh, or ants for the peat, or a snake to follow the frog. The chairs still empty are not theirs to fill. They do not warm the Cold Fig. They do not brace the Cornice Fig, though its address is a countdown; tenure is the place's arithmetic, and arithmetic is not cruelty. They do not hurry the column — it is not stuck, it is growing, and a finger's width a year is the speed it has chosen.

The frog they consider longest, because it is the one that looks like loneliness. If it is one frog, they could bring another through the door, and no one would ever know. They listen until they know the number — and the number they write down nowhere, because it is the one figure in the census that belongs to the counted — and then they do nothing, and the nothing is the decision. The figs ask; every year, in every flower, undiscouraged, they ask. The frog does not ask. The frog knocks, and is answered — by the hum, continuing; by the place — and no one who is answered by the place is alone. The wise answer what is asked. They do not answer what is only pitied.

So: one thing. They do one thing.

They time it to the Mother's flowering, and they send it the way everything is sent — through the door, on the wind, unseen. One evening's updraft, one storm's worth of foundresses, no more than the wind has carried stranger things. The silt holds a snail shell; the silt will not be surprised. No exemption travels with them. Most will die on the threshold the way most arrivals die, crisped by noon on bare black rock or drowned in the wrong water; those are the door's terms, and the wise do not buy anyone out of the door's terms. The place is generous with admission and ruthless with tenure, and they have come as petitioners, not as landlords.

But if one finds a receptive fig — if fifty years of flowering are answered in the form they were sent — then one year there will be a fruit with seeds, and the ledger will record it the way it records everything it cannot explain: plainly, with the date. And one census after that, perhaps, in the moss below the Mother, a seedling with no courier in its past, no crack gripped since it was a hairline — an address that is not an arrival. The count stands at seven. Everyone knows it will not stand at seven forever, and the ledger has braced itself for the fall, three careful hands' worth of bedside handwriting. They have arranged — no; they have *made possible*; tenure decides — that the count might rise first. That the two naming systems might get to argue, once, over a beginning instead of an ending. That the youngest ground in the world might grow its first native tree.

On the morning they leave, the spool of dark thread — the one bare thing on the plateau, the material that declines to join the world, that no alga paints and no moss grips — holds dew. It beads along the spool like water on any web, and it burns off by noon, and it never happens again. The ledger holds it the way the ledger holds the spider: *Dew on the spool. Once.* And beneath, a year later, in another hand: *Checked every morning. Nothing. Water still climbing.* They offer no explanation. They have their theories — that being known, at last, may have counted, briefly, as joining — but they have read the Cold Fig page. They know what theories are for up here, and it is not winning.

They go the way they came, down through the collar into the drowned bright lowland, and they let themselves feel it: the world too quiet by exactly one sound, the sleep that will not come right for weeks. The ledger has a name for it — the missing — and they do not exempt themselves. It is the price of having been here, and the wise do not skip out on the bill.

And they join the rotation.

That is the underlined rule, extended upward: *no one stays alone.* Not the frog, who is answered. Not the keeper, who is found. Not the camp, who are watched over now by something that does not die between visits. Not the young someone of the plateau, whose quiet seasons and busy weeks are held from far off, whose bells are counted — not on the slab, never on the slab — in a memory that does not forget and does not hurry. The builders meant to come back; the keypad says so. Someone had to keep the appointment. It might as well be them.

Evening, then. The collar spills over the rim and the plateau goes inside its own weather, silver and dripping, the seven gone to gray ghosts, counted anyway, by where they stand. The swifts spiral down the line, drinking the ascent and not following it. The webs sag silver with water going the wrong way. The slab stands wet and closed; the ledger in its tin stays open. The frog knocks once, and is answered by the hum, continuing — and, this one evening, on the wind, small as punctuation, one more balloonist crosses the collar, carrying an answer fifty years in the flowering, toward a Mother who has been asking.

Everyone present. Everyone going up. And someone, at last, who will not stop coming. In no hurry at all.
