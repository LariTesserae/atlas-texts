It hears the place before it sees it. That is how everything arrives at the Kettle, and a benevolent and wise power is not exempt: from whatever distance such powers keep, the plain is a long silence, and then a compounded hum — engines, falling water, frogs, the frogs loudest of all, measurably louder, a bronze bell rung nightly in a country with no other bells. Attention follows sound. A power with eternity to notice things in is drawn to bells, and to any place where the arithmetic changes currency at the rim, and to a census that is never finished. It is not the most improbable arrival in the pit's history — the wasp holds that title, and keeps it — but it is the most patient.

It comes the way attention comes: already there when you turn around. Not down the incline, not by the ladder-road. The deaf mule lifts its head, and the men take note of that, because the mule does not lift its head for the chorus, being deaf, nor for the pumps, being philosophical. The screens show nothing. The heron does not fly, and the camp watches the heron the way it watches weather: the longest lease on the floor accepts the newcomer in the way it accepts everything, which is by remaining entirely at ease. The engineer, entering the day's line, has for one moment the feeling of the ledger being read over his shoulder, and turns, and finds his own shadow in the lamplight. The camp files the whole matter where it files such things: nowhere but in the men's tone of voice.

Then the power reads the place, and it reads it the way the place reads itself — off the intake screens, off the dated gauges, off the temper of the water. It reads both ledgers and finds the true one truer. It reads the sediment fans, which are the pit's intention written in the pit's own slow hand. And on the question the men argue on the third drink — whether the Kettle itself belongs on the census — it does not argue. It counts. Pulse, breath, voice, memory, intention: if those do not add up to life, they add up to something that keeps the same hours, and a power, above all things, has hours to keep. The men speak to the pit constantly, in coins and doorframe-touches and oblique formulations. The power is the first to wait for the answer. The answer is legible in the fans: to fill, to warm, to garden.

So the question it sets itself is not *what is wrong here* — the question a merely kind power asks, rolling up its sleeves — but *what is already being decided, and by whom, and what stands in the way.* And then it does what wisdom mostly is: it refuses.

It does not save the pit from the water. A younger power, a kinder and stupider one, would have brought pumps — glorious pumps, pumps beyond the company's dreaming — and won at a stroke the war the company keeps almost declaring, and it would have been the worst thing ever done to the place. The water is not the enemy of the census. The water is the census-taker.

Nor does it give the pit to the water tonight. A romantic power would still the pumps one evening and let the lake arrive by spring. But the fourth rule is written down, and the buried district is real, and other men's galleries and other towns' wells do not deserve to be the price of one place's beautiful ending. The pit may decide what it becomes. It may not decide what it drowns.

And it does not show its work. Certainty is the one import the camp has no use for. The whole religion of the place is maintenance, and maintenance requires that the relationship stay two-sided — that the coins be given on trust or not at all. A power that lets itself be proven would be filed where the order about the Tree is filed: in the safe that no longer opens, neither lost nor obeyed, only rusted around. The coins on the saint's shelf have fused into a single green-black lump; the ochre respects no offering. The power respects them. Every coin was a request for the beat to hold, and the beat holds — while it is needed, and no longer, and the wisdom is in knowing that those are the same length of time.

What does it do, then? The honest answer is: almost nothing, and the almost is the whole of it. It works the way the water works — slowly, and always in one direction, and mostly below the resolution of the gauges.

First it moves in, which here means submitting. It gets wet; nothing here is dry; the first rule respects no visitor. It keeps office hours: at midday it is in the blade of light, and the men drifting toward the brightness with their tea find themselves standing nearer a patch of it than they intended, and do not ask why, and the mule is already there, unsurprised. It takes a leech and lets the leech finish; the pump chief salts it off and pronounces, in whatever tongue powers are addressed in — here, silence with a coin in it — that it is a resident now; the pit has taken its tithe of blood and stamped its papers. It never blocks the adit; it builds no fire at the adit mouth. It flinches once — whether on purpose, no one will ever know — pays the coin for the saint, and never flinches again. If the camp ever bothers to formulate the thing, they will say what they say of everything that comes up out of the deep: the pit sent it; the pit keeps it. For once the formulation is exactly right.

Then the small works, none of them datable. The beam engine, fired once a year: there should by rights have been a year it didn't catch. There has not been. The old men weep on schedule, the young men study the gauges, the smoke is granted. The true ledger's ink: pages from the wet years remain legible in their bent-wire hand, and the engineer credits the paper, and the paper is ordinary. The crack above the Tree grows — the surveyor is not wrong, he is never wrong — but it grows into the bedding plane that will hold the ledge, not the joint that would drop it, and the twice-yearly number stays the right side of alarming. And one spring, on the sediment fan below the storm flume, where every dropped seed before has failed on the raw marl, there is a seedling. Two leaves, then four. The surveyor finds it on a routine traverse and stands there long enough for the instrument to cool, and tells one man, who enters it upside down, and the pool on the ledge above the Tree quietly changes its terms.

The company it does not argue with. It lets the decade-long sentences reach their natural periods. Inspectors arrive, by some recurring accident of scheduling, on toad nights, or at dusk when the floor appears to be burning slowly in a hundred places and the smoke is made of lives, and they walk the boardwalks with the lantern low, apologizing, and they go back up to the plain changed in ways the minutes do not record. Head office, being swifts, notices nothing; swifts never land. No second order about the Tree ever comes down the incline.

It spares nothing. Now and then it chooses the hour. The mule, old beyond arithmetic, fully depreciated since the year the old level drowned, lies down one warm night beside the fuel drums, in reach of the pumpkin vine, ears toward the chorus it cannot hear, and does not get up. They bury it at the fenced plot on the rim, the only tenant of the floor ever admitted to dryness and a horizon, and the stone says MULE, which was the name all along.

One December the engineer, checking his third column, finds that the year's gains exceed the year's losses — not by a knuckle, not ambiguously, but past argument. And reviewing: the year before, the same, and the year before that. It has been true for some time, and he has been not-seeing it the way you don't see the water rise. The gift is not the surplus. The gift is that he lets himself see it. He writes the total right side up. He begins leaving the true ledger where a certain young pump-hand will find it, and the husk count goes on each morning without being asked, which is the only kind of immortality the camp believes in.

But the fourth rule is written down, so it gets the longest labor. Under the far wall, past the galleries, out in the buried district, a fracture that was always going to open opens a decade early; a syncline two valleys off that was always going to become a sump becomes one a century early; the hidden watershed shifts its lowest point by less than any gauge can certify, year on year, always within the margin of drought. The engineer notes the mere's temper changing and writes *the neighbors are pumping*. Years later he writes *the neighbors have stopped pumping*. Years later still he writes the line he never expected to write: the inflow has peaked. The letters from the hopeful name lose their urgency. The pumps step down terrace by surrendered terrace, and the war ends not in defeat but in treaty, and the water comes home as heir, not invader, and no one anywhere ever learns what was spared, or by whom. That is the signature. A bias in the fortunes, always in the same direction, never once large enough to be sure of. And the human fruit of it, when it ripens: the first pump-men in the Kettle's history who are allowed to sleep through a stopped beat.

Nothing here is saved. Things are kept, or they are let go. That is the sixth rule, and it is not written down, and no one will say when they learned it.

One far century — this part is already legible in the gauges — the last lamp goes down to the flume-keeper's one, and the lake arrives on the pit's own schedule, and the marsh after it, and perhaps the garden, the fig's grandchildren cracking the last raw walls, the rising main an ore-cored column, the district's one rich strike, left at last in peace; the safe's fused brick of paper still the most accurate document in the district, accurate now forever. The camp does not outlast its term; a camp is a tenant, and tenants go. But no one is held hostage by the going. And through all of it the count is kept: first willow on the fan, the reed-stand become a marsh-margin, the pale fish's descendants rising at night, a heron or a heron's heir on a hoist that is by then a shape under silt. The census is never finished, because the water takes it nightly. The power does not finish it. It keeps it. Keeping is the whole art: the pit keeps boot prints, the pumps keep the beat, the ledger keeps the gains, and the count gets a keeper with sufficient tenure.

But that is not tonight. Tonight there are three low notes in the planks, where there used to be two — the pump-beat, the Foreman, and under both a third, felt rather than heard, that was not there before a certain year — and the camp says the pit's voice has deepened with age, and the old man of the Drowned Level, who would know, says pits do. The engineer enters the day's line, and under it, upside down, the things that are not measurements, and tonight one more, which he does not remember deciding to write: *one more resident; name unknown; tenure uncertain; tithe paid.* And on the third drink the census is amended: the people are tenants with good pumps; the frogs are tenants with good voices; the heron holds the longest lease; the bacteria hold the deed; the water is the estate itself, coming home into possession; and the luck has seniority now, and no one can say when it moved in. The mule knew first. The mule said nothing. It had nothing to prove.

The lamps burn doubled in black water. The game goes on at the adit mouth, in the one cool breath, which tonight is a degree cooler and smells faintly of rain falling somewhere on the plain, and the card players, without looking up, say the pit is in a good mood. The pale fish rise among the reflected stars to take moths from the sky, and the surface closes over them like a ledger — like a ledger being kept. And the water rises its fraction, and the beat holds while it is needed, and the heat, and the hum, and the chorus go on and on.
