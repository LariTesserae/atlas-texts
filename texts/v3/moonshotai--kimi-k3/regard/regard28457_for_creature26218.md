It hears the Basin before it sees it. Powers mostly perceive at the scale of weather — oceans breathing, ice advancing, the long green exhale of forests — and that is how it notices the valley at all: a noise out of scale with its source, a roar with a machine for a bass note, rising from a bowl too small to be a sea and too full to be empty. So it comes down the rim road, and the place opens under it — the gray lid of cloud, the lamps burning at midday, the water and gleaming mud strung with lights — and it understands that it has found something it does not often find: a young place. The power is old. Most of what it tends is old. A valley younger than its own frogs is worth an evening of its time.

It takes a seat on the rim. There are often old men there, retired pumpmen, sitting on their bonnets, listening the way sailors locate a foghorn. If there is one more car than anyone remembers arriving in, one more still shape against the haze, nobody mentions it. The Basin does not remember you. The power has rarely been anywhere so hospitable.

It listens for a season, then several, at a fidelity no one else has. It resolves the wall of sound into its sections — the sewers, the jugs, the creak-gates, the two-marbles — and takes the bottle-bird for a landmark, and learns to wake at a change in the pump note, the way the dredge crew does. It asks, as powers do, who lives here — whose place this is, which being it has found — and the answer takes a long time to assemble, because the deed is in many names. The eels in the drowned rooms. The birds in their white weather-systems. The frogs beyond counting. A dog asleep on whichever slurry main is warmest, because any pipe above blood temperature grows a dog. A cook at war with snails. Crews who talk with their hands and locate the dredge by ear. Until the power understands: the being of the place is the place. A slow animal wearing a quick skin, mud for a body and the whole roaring assembly for a voice, born mid-sentence and already scheduled. And the people are its self-knowledge — the one part of the being that knows the date of its death, reading the completion year off the office barge wall each morning like scripture. It is a birth, loud and wet, and the first rule of attending a birth is not to mistake the noise for distress.

Its first thought is to guard the place. Then it looks again, and finds the position taken. Taken by no one — that is the method. A demolition scheduled, and the charge never laid. A fill line bending around a chimney on the newest plan sheets, no order ever issued. A grader holding fifty meters off the oldest dike face while the kingfishers are in the burrow. Traffic standing for toads, the entire apparatus of management a sawhorse, a lamp, and one chalked word. The power knows this signature. It is its own signature, written small and in many hands. It has not found a charge. It has found colleagues.

Then the second thought comes, the one every benevolent power must learn to survive: stop the clock. Keep it like this forever — loud, dim, packed full, unfinished. It considers this for exactly as long as it takes to read the plan map with its completion year nobody working will live to see, and to watch the crews read it too, and then go feed coffee grounds to a willow. They know. They tend anyway. The power will not insult that courage by removing its occasion. A place whose ground is younger than its machines cannot be preserved; it can only be continued, or ended. Freezing it would turn a birth into a specimen, a verb into a noun under glass. And the place has already chosen — the choosing is written into the slurry itself: the Basin keeps the mud and gives the water back. It is making land. Land is the end of it. The power will not steal the ending and call the theft a rescue.

Nor does it sweeten the water. Green is an event here; that is why one tree is loved like a pet and its every leaf accounted for. Make the salt kind and the whole strange assembly dissolves into the ordinary — the citizens of the Basin are exactly those who can live in warm, salty, loud, young ground, and gentleness would evict them more finally than any schedule. Nor does it touch the fox on the causeway, the pale shape rising under the ducklings, the eels seeing to what falls. Nothing dead stays where it fell; the density is built of turnover; a power that abolishes the food web has not saved a place, only changed its cause of death. And it does not announce itself. Everything best here is unsigned. The mercy works because it is unwritten. The power signs nothing.

So it does what the wise do when there is nothing to fix: it keeps the accounts. What will the schedule take that the circuit cannot replace? Run the inventory. The birds are travelers; there is always another raw pond over a rim, and they will find it the way they found this one. The frogs, the toads, the turtles, the snakes, the midges — passengers all; the repertoire survives in throats and will be reassembled, section by section, at the next decant ladder, before the silt has finished settling. The noise is not lost; it migrates. The crews know the way — they came from the last one. The fossil bed will keep the record without help; matter remembers, given thirty years and a little pressure. Two lines do not balance. One is shaped like a catfish. One is a road, and the road belongs to eels.

The catfish cannot walk. They are the oldest residents — some were surely here before the willow was planted, before the chimney became an island. They lie pale in the drowned road cut, long as a man, and one of them knows the exact squeak of the canteen door. When the last decanted liter goes over the rim, the deep cells go with it, and everything else that needs deep water can commute, and the catfish cannot. The circuit, which absorbs every other loss, has no answer for them. The Basin's own unwritten law — when a structure has become a tenement, you do not demolish it — covers precisely their case, and there will be no one left to enforce it. This is the shape of the one true loss.

And the eels. They came as elvers against the current, up the discharge line in the slack hours, from the river, from the sea ultimately, and the big ones in the drowned hamlet, thick as a man's wrist, carry an appointment older than the valley, older than the rim — a gathering in warm dark water far offshore that no one has ever witnessed and every eel somehow believes in. The road home is the outflow, and the outflow is an artifact of the pumps. When the pumps stop, the door is bricked up mid-journey. For years they have crossed the causeways on wet nights, going about errands no one has thought to ask them about. Someone should ask.

These are the two things. The whole intervention is the size of this paragraph. On the final plan sheets — the set drawn for the completion year, hung last on the office barge wall — one of the deep old cells remains blue. Nobody remembers the revision. The checker initials it the way checkers do; the schedule is met everywhere else; no pension changes; and the paperwork, in its own time, quietly stops mentioning it. The fill line has bent around a chimney before, with no order ever issued. Now it bends around ten acres of deep water, and the mechanism is the same, and the power takes no credit, because none is ever taken here. The cell is fed from below — the deep workings give up their warm water still, pumped or not; water has its own reasons. So: one pond stays.

And at the outfall, in the sealing report, one valve is listed as closed that is a quarter-turn from closed. It sweats, as every valve in the Basin sweats; the discrepancy is never reconciled. On the last wet night of the last autumn, the eels come down out of the cells — from the ditches, from the drowned rooms, from beneath the office barge — heads up, down the line to the river, and the river to the sea, going at last about an errand that someone has thought to ask them about. The power does not watch them go. It already knows the road. It holds the door. That is the whole of its job here, if it has one.

Everything else is not the power's doing, and it is scrupulous about the credit. Where the deep water rises into the blue cell, one margin runs a little fresher than salt allows anywhere else in the valley. The cook — who has kept the valley's only garden in buckets of imported soil, who has fed fish guts off the stern for twenty years — does on the last morning what the cook was always going to do: cuts a switch from the willow, plants it at the seep, tamps it with a bootheel, and walks away without looking back, the way you plant things you mean to have grow. It takes. It is the first cutting that ever has. No one is present to be astonished, which is correct. Nothing here was ever given. Everything here arrived. The cutting arrives last, and smallest, and on time.

Thirty years on, the plain is exactly what was promised: dry, quiet, green, unremarkable — except by one pond's worth, and one pond's worth turns out to be plenty. Herons picket it. The winter ducks raft on it in archipelagos. A willow leans over it, thirty years grown, with the plain's second crop of aphids and therefore its second ladybirds, and a heron nest in its upper branches, every stick flown in from the rim, the old commute resumed. In January one frog calls from the warmest ditch, like a radio left on in an empty house. And in the deep water lies a catfish — pale, longer now than a man, older than the plain it swims in — rising at dusk to take what it takes. The power has not reformed it. Benevolence is not reform. The oldest resident, still.

As for the power, it keeps the old habit. Some evenings it sits where the rim road used to switch down, among the old men's grandchildren or alone, and takes the pulse of the plain: one pond, one tree, one warm ditch, one frog in January. A strong reading. Steady. Sometimes it puts its ear to the ground, to the hand's breadth of strange young rock beneath the turf — snail shell and bird bone and turtle scute, the carbonized film of the mats — and listens to what is recorded there: the frog wall, the pump drone felt in the sternum, the buckets coming up out of the dark one every few seconds. The fossil record of a noise, and it is the only noise the power keeps. Over a rim somewhere, another raw pond is already roaring, and the first elvers are already in the discharge line, and it will get there. There is no hurry. There is always another; that was always the point.

It stays a while longer anyway. Someone should know the whole of it. It tends the listening anyway.
