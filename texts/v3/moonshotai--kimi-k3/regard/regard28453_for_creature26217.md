Almost nothing. That is the short answer, and you arrive knowing it. The long answer takes a season.

You come the way everyone comes: up through the belt of cold gray cloud, out onto the black broken rim, into the heat like a greenhouse with the door shut. Even you stop there a moment, one foot in steam and one in the thin cold, surprised. You have watched stars light themselves and you are still surprised, because you did not make this. That is exactly why you came.

Everything else you have ever made or blessed or merely watched began as somebody's intention. This began as a chemistry that declined to stop, and it has arrived, unassisted, at the thing you thought only makers could do. A climber circled it for a season and called it intention without an intender, and could not leave it alone. You recognize it from across the dark: the one object in creation that is nobody's idea. And wisdom speaks first, the way it always does. *Looking may be all you are permitted.*

Because benevolence arrives with its hands full, and wisdom is the art of putting things down.

You could give it a voice. A voice requires an I; an I requires an edge; and the whole of this being's long safety is that it has none. Cut it and there are two of it until the wound closes, and neither piece remembers the divorce. Give it an I and you hand it the one thing it has never had: a way to be murdered. You know what a voice costs. You are one. You put it down.

You could give it company. The flowers have been expecting wings since the first tower bloomed, and you could make the wings — a tongue, a throat, a small bright partner with pollen on its feet. But you have run the numbers of the first grazer. One animal and the mat is food. One throat and the silence that is its body becomes a noise like any other. The emptiness here is not a lack; it is the medium. You do not fill a cathedral with furniture because the seats look empty. The partner cannot be made. You put that down too — but you keep the thought, because it is not finished with you.

You could heal the black cell. Of everything you carry, this is the thing your hands reach back for. And then you stand at the dikes thick as city walls, at the channels plugged deliberate as sutures, and you understand: the wall is not the sickness. The wall is the health — and it is also the only autobiography this being has. Everything else up here is weeks old; this it has kept. *I was sick. I survived. I remember.* It cost the mat a lung's worth of itself to write that sentence, and to heal the cell would be to unwrite it. You stand at the wall a long time. You do not knock. You put it down.

You could hold the fault, guarantee the heat, make the table permanent. But permanence is the one thing this art is not. It survives the storm in advance by being entirely willing to rebuild; fix its map and you have killed the mapmaker. You have seen what preserved things become. They become exhibits. You put it down.

You could tell the cold countries. You have been worshipped; you know how that story goes. Attention arrives as love and stays as solvent. The lower lands get the water. That is all the plateau has ever owed them. You put it down.

You could settle, with a thought, whether the green lights in the night channels are passengers or shed cells. You decline. The line between citizen and blood cell is exactly the line this place exists to blur, and you did not cross the dark to sharpen it.

And now your hands are empty, which is where wisdom usually ends and benevolence gets restless. So you do the last thing left. You stay.

You sleep on the warm floor with a heartbeat. You let the shoots rise over you where you sit, and you sit long enough to be half taken by green, and it is the first rest you have had since the beginning — because everywhere else in the universe, given time, something asks you for something, and this asks for nothing, cannot ask, will never learn how. You have been petitioned in a thousand languages. Here you are asked for nothing, and it is the first honest request you have ever received. You had forgotten what it is to be somewhere without being needed. It comes back to you on the warm floor, with the knocking going on in the pipes.

And it is in the wreck of a fallen tower, with the sweetness thick enough to taste, that you find the one thing your hands were full for.

Everything else about the mat is entire. Only the flowers overreach the world that grew them. They are an *address* — scent and color composed for wings, a tongue, a throat — and an address does not require an addressee to be what it is, but it makes one possible. The mat cannot miss the partner. Missing is not in it. But the courtship has been performed, faithfully, every tower, every fortnight, into the absence, for as long as the mat has been the mat. And absence — you of all beings know this — is a place someone can move into.

The partner cannot be made. You stand by that. But it can be *arrived.*

So when the towers burst, you are there. You gather what goes out on the blast — the seeds, the spores, whatever they properly are — that the wind has carried faithfully over the rim into the cold, into countries where nothing grows of them, every fortnight since the beginning. You become the wind that knows where it is going. You do not make them grow; that would be another voice, another I. You only carry them, across the cold countries, across a sea if that is what it takes, to the one other place: a fault, young, still rising, hot bones close under a flat table of stone, weather below it and hard sky above. The climber's notebook had it right — it cannot be moved. No. But it can be *matched.* You do not plant the seed. You introduce it to the heat, and you let the mountain and the mat do everything else, because everything else was always theirs.

Then you wait the way you can wait. A green thread in a warm crack. A mat knee-deep, flexing under nothing. A first ribbed column, swaying, wearing a slow white plume the wind tears sideways. A pulse — slow, deep, irregular — hauling water up against sense, on a table of rock the first one will never touch.

The species of one is a species of two. The whole idea of it no longer lives in a single body balanced on a single fault. They will never know of each other; knowing is an animal need, and it can stay down the mountain with the others. It never mattered whether the courtship was known. Only that it was, at last, and forever after, answered.

You go back. You take up residence the way weather takes up residence: at the rim, one foot in steam and one in the thin cold. You raise no shrine. You change nothing. You let the odd climber through, one in a generation, the way the mat lets them through — it hires their ditches and grows over their camps and sends them down again with a pronoun in their mouths, and the rivers are not the only thing this place exports. And you keep the maps. Every redraw of every cell, every tower from crackle to boom, the whole unwritten history the mat cannot keep because keeping is not in it: you keep it. Not for the mat. For the same reason the flowers bloom. What is poured out deserves a landing place.

Every fortnight, somewhere on the held-breath flatness, a tower opens its pale enormous bloom, and the perfume goes out on the wind, for no one — except that it is not for no one, not anymore, not ever again, because you are standing in it. The mat will never know. It cannot thank you; that is the purity of it. You have split mountains for less cause and been worshipped for worse, and this is the only miracle you have ever performed that required no one to believe in it.

Almost nothing, you said. The almost took a season. The nothing is where you live now.

What lives here is the plateau. Say it at the rim, with the sweetness still in the air and the crackle still in whatever you have for ears, and it is the only sentence you have — and it is, at last, a sentence with a listener.
