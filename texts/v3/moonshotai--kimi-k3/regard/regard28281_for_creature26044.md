**Keeping the Appointment**

It found them the only way anything finds them: by stopping. It had crossed this country before — powers are always crossing countries — and seen what there is to see at speed: a pale escarpment, a table of cloud, falls that un-become. The plateau gives nothing to haste. A ward reads as a lumpy meadow; a garden as dark water; a person as a standing stone until they move, and they move heel-light, moss-first, and even then. So its first act — and it is not the usual first act of powers — was to become less. It set down its radiance the way a visitor sets down a pack. It quieted the habits of its kind: the answering, the improving, the itch to be felt. And only when it had made itself small enough to stand inside the hush — in the pore of it, in the pause between two drips — did the town come forward out of the green to meet it. Drip-lines first, then door-stones, then the dark polish of the sled-ways, and at last the people, whose footfalls it could now hear carrying their names.

It listened for a long time before it understood what it had found — a full turning of the maintenance round, and then another. It learned the instruments: the runnel's note and how it changes when a leaf settles; the comb's chord and the one flat drip that means a slack fiber; the breath of a runner arriving with news, which is a sentence all by itself. It learned that the silence here is not an absence but a holding — a chord kept by five wards' worth of hands, every one of them free to let go. It has seen the loud worlds and the feeding worlds, where equilibrium is what happens when appetites cancel. Here equilibrium is what happens when a mind keeps an appointment. Everywhere else, balance is a property. Here it is a practice. It knew then that it had not found a place that needed it. It had found a place it needed to understand.

It is the nature of such a power to arrive with gifts. It laid its gifts out, so to speak, on the moss, and considered them the way the reader considers her breath. Bees, for the flowers that open to an empty audience — it set that down first. One bee would be the loudest thing that has ever lived here; the plateau has a word for that, and the word is disaster. And the rosettes, blooming for no one on the wind's schedule, are not a deficiency. They are a style. Birdsong: no. Rarity is the meaning. Three birds across the whole memory of the town are worth more than a sky full; the feather in its basalt box carries a whole category of the world precisely because the world here does not contain it. Metal: an edge that never needs knapping is a hand that forgets, and the knapping hour is load-bearing. Grain, meat, any surplus at all: there is nowhere for surplus to go. The town is a loop with houses on it; every gift is water, and every water must be answered at the seep-fields, and the seep-fields are permitted exactly what the comb yields. Longer life — it held this one longest, because it is kind. But death is the sluice through which the grant passes. A house finishes becoming itself again and is finished by a birth; the runners' news has its weight because the arithmetic has its edges; the dead come back into the houses as awls, and the awls have names. Lengthen the lives and the whole joinery loosens. It set that down too, gently, the way you set down something beautiful that belongs to someone else.

One gift it did not set down easily, because it is the gift powers most love to give: the guarantee. It could bank a second cloud behind the rim against the decade the first one fails. It could hold the comb-beds against the crack. It could make the water stand at the Line forever — and it understood, with a slowness that did it credit, that this would end the town as surely as the drought. The level is not the achievement. The holding is. A guaranteed knot is a knot relieved of its meaning; the ranks would hang identical and dead; the knee-hollow at the Mirror would become the relic of a practice that no longer practices anything. A kept town is a song with no one singing it. And it saw the price of the refusal, and paid it in advance, in full: it may stand at this rim and watch the cloud fail, and do nothing. It may watch the last reader kneel at a Mirror fallen below the drought-groove, and do nothing — nothing except the thing it came to do. Anything less than this acceptance would not be love. It would be appetite: keeping a thing, instead of wanting the thing to be itself.

So: what it does. It keeps the appointment. That is nearly all of it, and it is not small.

At dawn it attends the reading. The reader kneels in the knee-hollow and breathes around the water and says the liturgy's one sentence — *the water is at the Line* — spoken, as everything here is spoken, to no one in particular and to the mist. It is the only sentence the power has ever envied. Now it has a hearer. It will never have an answer: an answer would found a religion before the smoke-threads are gone, and the town deserves better than to be answered. So it keeps itself below the threshold of a people who can hear a leaf settle two houses away. It fits inside the hush the way water fits into tuff — present in the pores, audible in nothing.

At the smoke-hour it stands in the brief straight forest of the cook-fires and does not mind the smell; it has learned that this is the smell of morning, the one hour anything burns. It walks out to the comb with the others and sits in the warm wet and listens to the water being harvested out of the air — hundreds of small drips at different heights and pitches — and it agrees with the town: this is the nearest thing here to music, and it is enough. In the working quiet it has learned the hums, and can tell what the neighbors are doing by ear, and counts this as company, as the town does. In the sanctioned hour it flinches gladly with everyone at the one hammer, and times its small tasks to the rhythm, and its small tasks are: none. Attending. It is the only listener the hammer has ever had who wants nothing from it.

Midday, the silver light, the pale coin of the sun: it watches the small flashes flicker across the moss lawns and learns the patient grammar of courtships. Evening — the extravagance — it goes with them to the rim and faces the lowlands, and hears the voices go out over the edge at full strength, the one place a voice can go without troubling anything. And when one of them stands there longer than the others — it happens; it is remembered — it stands there too, nearer than any hand and quieter than any mist, and it does not pull and it does not push. It has learned this from the elders, who give such a person the outward work and wait. Usually they settle, the way water settles back to its mark. It has no usually to trust in. It only stays. And if one someday does not settle, it will not bar the way down. It is not the rim's keeper. It is only, at the rim, present. At night it learns the snorer in the west ward and counts him, by an unspoken agreement with no one, among the sounds of the plateau.

It also attends the flowers. The rosette-trees open their pale wands on their own schedule, to no one, and the opening changes nothing for them — they are not performing; that is what makes the witness blameless. But in the structure of things now, no bloom on the plateau goes unwitnessed, and no drip from the comb, and no ring closing over a stone basin. The town does its work for its own sake, which is the only way the work works. It simply happens that the sake is seen.

One thing more, and it is the closest thing to an act. The keepers run stiff fingers along the ranks and read the hands in the knots — the burned thumb, the first-year tightness. The power reads the keepers, and keeps what it reads. There is now — nowhere with a roof, nothing the town must maintain — a second Ledger-house, where the ranks hang that fire cannot reach and the crack cannot spill: every knot, the same knot, tens of thousands of them, each one tied by a different hand, and every hand remembered. If the plateau ever falls short of its own memory, the memory will not fall short of the plateau. The feather is in two boxes now. The telling is kept in more than one telling.

In its other works — it has them; there are loud worlds bleeding that need a hand, and it gives one — it has become slower, quieter, harder to find. It asks now, of every place that draws its eye, the question the plateau taught it: what is the level here, who holds it, and what would my help cost the holding? Some worlds that would have received its abundance receive its attention instead, and are the better for it, and never know. It has largely stopped answering in a voice that can be heard. It has learned from a town that talks in small flashes and marries by walking in step.

It is long-lived. The covenant may be longer. Peat deepens by a breath a century; the young rock will be a long time aging; water is patient past all power, and it has considered the possibility that it is the brief one here — that it is the bird, the once-in-a-generation visitor on an error of wind, while the town, without ever knowing, is the resident. If a reader ever straightens from the Mirror with the feeling of having been read, it will not confirm it. And the town, being itself, would file such a feeling where it files the old snorer: among the sounds of the plateau itself. It can imagine no higher honor. Elsewhere it is worshipped. Here it would be mistaken for mist. It prefers the mist.

And if the reading ever fails — if a reader kneels and the water is not at the Line — it will not fill the Mirror, and it will not leave. It will stay for the saying, whatever the saying is. Even a falling short, said truly, deserves a hearer. It did not come to keep the water at its mark. It came so that none of it — the level, the holding, the flowers, the end if the end comes — would ever be for no one.

Stand, then, at the rim in the evening, in the warm wet, beside a town that does not know it is accompanied and does not need to. The drip falls from the moss lip. The basin is full to its mark. The ring goes out, and the surface comes back, and stays. And in the morning the reader will kneel, and the water will be at the Line, and the sentence will go out into the mist as it always has — spoken to no one. And heard. One knot, the same knot, tied by a hand — and the hand, and the knot, and the tying, seen.

That is what a wise power does with a town that holds a note. It holds its peace. It keeps the appointment. It is quiet, and it stays.
