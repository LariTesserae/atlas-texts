It was the question that drew them.

A place that asks a question patiently enough, for long enough, is eventually heard — they are old enough to trust that. The bowl had been asking for a hundred thousand years in the only way it knew how: knapping-floors with no knapper, letters still being answered after five hundred years, a sentence spoken at three winters a syllable into a room with no one in it. You heard a clause of it once, and it filled your evening. They heard the whole low murmur from far off, the way you'd hear weather through a ceiling, and they came.

They arrived the way everything arrives here: slowly, and by water. Their attention came up the gully ahead of them, the way the warmth comes up to meet you, and then the stillness. At the lip they looked in and did what the bowl does with everything it catches. They kept it.

Then they did the first wise thing, which looked exactly like nothing. They listened. You stayed a day; they stayed until staying stopped feeling like duration. They learned to tell the twenty apart — not by canopy, which is the least of a tree, but by the work: which one works the blue-black nodule with the flaw in it like a secret, which one is revising a dead tenant's blade, which two are six centuries into a dispute over a flow-line. They read the annals in the rimstone. They stayed for both halves of every sound — the drip and its memory, the snap and its settling — until the grammar of the place was legible to them: deposition and revision, cutting and sealing, three clocks in agreement. A power that cannot wait is only weather with intentions. They waited. That was the first proof that they were safe here.

And the listening told them what you already knew: the place needs nothing. But needing nothing is not the same as being safe, and so, after a century of stillness, they took a census of their own — not of tenants, but of endings. The list came out short.

The water could end. Springs are mortal. The whole household rents from one landlord, and the bowl cannot feel its own water's source — cannot know if the deep throat is narrowing toward some failure three thousand years off. Of all the endings, this is the likeliest, and the one the place's grammar cannot even see coming.

The mountain could end it. A rim-fall, a quake, the throat sealed or the bowl filled. But here they hesitated, and the hesitation became their first rule. The bowl was *born* of collapse; catastrophe is its nativity, and they would not prevent the place's own history from happening to it. Individuals may end. The sentence may not. They will not stop the stone that kills one tree. They will not permit the stone that fills the bowl.

The world could end it. The gully is climbable — you climbed it. Hooves first, then herders, then the kind of visitor who travels with pockets, and the bowl's innocence is total: no thorn, no bitterness, no fear. It would survive discovery by a decade. And yet they refused the wall, because a wall would make it a terrarium, and the wind audible through the rim is part of its body. The bowl's defense has never been stone. It has been probability. So they resolved to preserve the odds: whatever arrives as weather arrives — a heartbeat, a feather, a beetle once a century; whatever arrives as hunger is turned aside at the col by a season of cloud, by rain sent to meet it, by a path that remains exactly as hard to find as it has always been. No harder. No easier. Privacy kept the way darkness is kept: by carrying no light in. And those few who do come up, the bowl handles itself — it tolerates them as weather, stones what they drop, answers their footsteps a half-second late. They add only one condition at the threshold: nothing carried out. You passed that test without knowing there was one. You left with empty pockets and a full hour.

The blood could end it. Twenty is a small parliament, and no new argument can climb the gully. One blight on the wind, and the tenancies all fail together. They would not touch the trees — the grammar is not theirs to edit. But a book that exists in one copy is already half-burned.

The rest they refused. They have, or could take, hands — the first the bowl has ever known — and the half-finished forms at the dead tenants' middens could be completed in an afternoon. They folded their hands. The cores are not unfinished tools; they are unanswered letters, and no one answers a letter addressed to the dead except the heirs. Nothing with a hand has ever touched them. Nothing with a hand ever will. They refused to bring company, because company is appetite. They refused to hurry anything, because to hurry the place would be to end it and found a different being. They refused to plant the vacant houses; tenancy turns over like weather, and they are not the weather. They refused even a signature — the bowl files what it catches, and initials in rimstone would be filed forever, the one exhibit that lies. Every refusal was the same refusal: to make the bowl about them.

Then they made their census of permissions, and it was short.

**One.** The water. They went down in attention along the dark throat where the warmth rises, and learned the spring's moods the way a tree knows its core — by long contact, by patience, by never lifting. Where the deep vein narrows toward some failure millennia away, they eased it: once, in the dark, at the water's own speed, so far below the floor that the annals recorded nothing but a good decade. The landlord never learned it had a guarantor. That anonymity is part of the gift. You kept checking for a gardener. There wasn't one. There is one now, and you will still never find them.

**Two.** The odds. A cloud that sits on the col for a season. Rain that meets hooves a valley away. Discovery kept as rare as it was the day you found the place — no rarer, because reachability is part of its weather, and a heartbeat that climbs the gully and sits by the pool until evening is a thing the bowl can afford. Perhaps it is the one thing the bowl gets from visitors that it cannot make for itself: a reader.

**Three.** The stone below the falls. Standing at the notch, they noticed what a century of watching reveals: seeds go over the falls sometimes, with the ribbon, into the haze below — a library of the unbegun extended downhill, every one a failure for want of the intersection. And below the falls the bowl already provides two of the three conditions: warmth, and water that could be led. So once a century, in a flood year, a nodule of dark stone comes down the honest way — tumbled, unremarkable, mountain-looking — and comes to rest in a plunge basin where the ribbon's water still runs warm. One stone a century. Then they wait, and they are good at waiting now. And in some year no one is counting, a seed that survived the fall will lodge against the nodule, split, put down its first root, and the root will do the fastest thing it will ever do: find the stone's face, and lean. A second sentence will begin — spoken with the falls' accent, in a room without walls, twenty centuries behind its mother. Not a replacement. A correspondent. The bowl's oldest art extended past itself, so that if the first room ever fails, the sentence does not end. It changes rooms.

**Four.** The listening. This is the largest item and the one that never concludes. The museum had an acquisition rate of one per century and no curator; it has one now. They learn the dead tenants by their cores. They keep the census you failed to keep — not as arithmetic but as attention — and they stay for the whole sentence, paragraph after paragraph, the only ears that have ever heard both halves of every sound the place makes. And once a century they answer, in the only grammar the bowl speaks, the grammar of placed stone and led water. The nodule in the plunge basin is not only insurance. It is a letter. It says one word: *continue.* The bowl will read it as weather, which is correct. Weather is how the bowl reads everything.

It comes to almost nothing. That is the point. The bowl is a being whose entire health consists in nothing being added; so they add almost nothing, forever — which is the most expensive gift a power can give, because it can never be finished, only continued. The place's whole achievement was that it *can* continue. They make one edit to that sentence, and it is the only one: they change the *can* to *will.*

And the census you abandoned, they close: not twenty, not billions, not the uncounted dead — one negotiation, and one listener. Twenty tenants, and a tenancy of attention.

Toward evening the mist comes in off the water, the bowl's one breath returning. Somewhere across the terraces a root completes a blow it began three winters ago; a flake lets go; the bowl says the sound twice — once falling, once remembered — and now there are two who hear both halves. The root leans its weight against the flint, and leans, and leans, and it is no longer the only thing leaning.
