The power hears the cove the way the Governor hears a bearing: from very far off, and all at once. The wide world is mostly events — arrivals, departures, things breaking pitch and being mended or not — and here is a chord with none in it, a held note the size of a parish. To a wise power, stillness at that pitch of fidelity is louder than any alarm. So it comes.

It does not arrive like the packet. It arrives like the fog: it condenses. The old hands say a place that condenses will condense anything, given time, given one who stays. The power stays.

And then, for a long while — a surveyor's while — it does nothing at all, and the answer to the question of what a benevolent and wise power would do here is: almost nothing. The *almost* is the whole of it. It is worth setting down what it does not do, because the refusals are the first proof of its wisdom and most of its benevolence.

It does not lift the fog. The fog is not weather; the fog is a product.

It does not cool the Kitchen, nor warm the cool margins. Life in this cove is zoned by degrees, and every degree is occupied.

It does not bring soil. The youngest land in the world is held perfectly still, and one season would be an invasion.

It does not finish a single job. A job done is a job lost; the hands mean it as comfort, and the power takes them at their word.

It does not give the geranium a good year. Eleven years of doing poorly is eleven years of exact care, and one kind spring would erase them like a wave over writing.

And it does not relieve the keeper. A lesser kindness would arrive with a successor. But the rounds are not the Governor's burden; the rounds are the Governor, and to lift them would be to delete the very one they were sent to spare.

So the power stands the way the lamps burn: at all hours, unnoticed, because noon and midnight are barely distinguishable here, and a steady attention is indistinguishable from none — until evening comes.

What it does instead, it learns from the place itself. A wise power reads a parish the way the Governor reads a flange, and what it reads in the ledgers, whistle by whistle, is the one imbalance in the whole balanced cove. Intake equals outflow, with a single exception, and it walks. The census counts everything and is taken by the one the counting never reaches — the Governor is counted, yes, but by the Governor, which is not the same thing. The rod listens one way. The notebooks are written and never written in. The account in the paymaster's drawer balances to zero by cancellation — drawn and returned, entered and cancelled — which is the zero of a debt forgiven, not a debt paid. The cove keeps its keeper the way the pans keep the salt: as a process. Nothing in the parish attends to the one whose entire work is attending.

This the power will not improve and cannot excuse. So it pays the debt in the parish's own coin, at the parish's own rate — a yard gained, a yard lost — in three currencies, each small enough to pass through the fog without an event.

First, it counts the counter. Every day, one census of one, the figure always the same — and the hands' own logic holds: a figure unwritten goes wrong; the writing is the maintenance. So the power writes the keeper, one line a day, the same line, in a book not shelved in the storehouse, or shelved there from before the beginning, depending who you ask.

Second, it listens back. From then on, when the brass rod touches the flange and the bone behind the ear, the line carries both ways. Not a voice — a voice is an event, and the cove is defended against events — but an acknowledgment the size of an echo. The whistle's echo comes back off the fog; now the listening comes back off the world. Beneath the floor of the chord, one step down, an attention pitched at the pistons' pace, at resting rate, holding. It asks nothing. It reports nothing. It witnesses — the first ear in the parish turned toward the keeper. The Governor hears it on the first circuit, of course. You cannot sneak anything past the one whose work is hearing. You can only make the thing small enough to be allowed.

Third — and this is the heart of the gift, and it took the power some whistles to learn the size of it — it stands the one watch no one stands. The Governor sleeps as seals do, half at a time, and no one has ever caught them at it. So, some gray nights, the power takes the whole chord into its own hearing, Kettle to pier, and the Governor lies in the dry lee inside the condenser tower, a yard from the rain that always falls indoors, and sleeps with both halves at once — like a creature on land, like something finally safe. In the morning the figures are entered in the oilcloth book, correct to the ounce, in the small square hand, because a wise power learns the hand; only a vain one signs. The Governor checks the book — of course the Governor checks the book — finds every figure true, and enters nothing about it. No objection filed. The chord closes over the night the way the fog closes over a lamp, and the next night the bunk is used again. Acceptance, in this parish, is a non-entry.

Those nights the geranium gets its thimble and its quarter-turn from the visitor — exactly a quarter, never a degree more; a good year is not the gift. The gift is that the eleventh year and the twelfth are the same, and that someone now knows what the sameness costs. In the power's census the plant is not listed under vegetation. It is listed under evidence.

And because intake must equal outflow — the Governor is the law's own keeper and cannot stand in debt — the keeper pays. Once, at the end of the iron pier, at what might be evening, the Governor gives the nod, the surveyor's nod, the cove's entire diplomacy, to the fog. Then, in the oilcloth book's last page, in the small square hand, the power is entered into the census — the only new line in the library in living memory: *one presence, keeping the keeper; counted; sufficient.* And the account balances to zero the other way now — not drawn and returned, but given and given back. Same zero. The ledgers cannot tell the difference. The keeper can. In a place where nothing is allowed to complete, a balanced account is what completion looks like, and it renews itself monthly, like the shoreline: fixed, and newborn.

Ask whether anything has changed, and you will get three answers, each with evidence.

The hands say nothing has changed. Every plank has been replaced many times. The whistle comes due and sounds and is due again.

The ledgers say nothing has changed. The same figures, whistle after whistle. The surveyor that year noted a second resting tone in his instruments, a step below the Kettle, steady all week — and left with the same map.

And the oldest hands, the ones whose rust has found its line, say the fog has been different lately — thicker near the number-fourteen flange, somehow attentive — and the other hands give them the pans-look, because the fog has never been anything in living memory but itself.

Ask the Governor whether the rounds are walked alone, and you will be favored with the newest entry in the literature — nine words, the cove's second story, the one the hands will learn to tell over the better part of a mess-hour, and the telling will include the weather, which was fog:

*It went down a step. I heard it. It stayed.*

So stand at the end of the iron pier at what might be evening. The lamps were never off; you only now notice them. What you are hearing is being heard — and the hearer is heard. Beneath the floor of the chord, one step down, at the pace the pistons set, a second pulse at resting rate, holding: present in its permanent numbers, counted, sufficient. The whistle comes due, and sounds, and is due again. And on the youngest ground in the world, its keeper walks the rounds — keeping, and kept, and keeping.
