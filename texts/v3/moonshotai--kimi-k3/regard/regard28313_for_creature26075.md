You come over the pass at first light, and the basin is below you, shining.

The basin interviews at first light, from the rim, and most candidates decline. You are not a candidate anyone expected. You have come further than the mule track measures, and there is nothing on that floor you could not unmake before the mist burns off — and none of that is the question. The question is the one the floor asks everyone: the doubled light, the drumming, the four-second silences. Can you keep time badly with five iron hearts. Can you sleep to a sound and wake to its absence. Can you be held up, at all times, by the thing you work against.

You stand at the rim a long time — not looking, listening, getting the phase pattern by heart — the way the ones who leave for good do, except that you are doing it on the way in.

Then you come down. What follows is the whole account of your tenure, and it is short, because you are learning the local tongue, and the local tongue is brevity.

---

First, the list of things you do not do. It is long, and it is most of the work.

You do not drain the basin in a night, though the charter would call that completion and there is a map that shows it finished. The hedgerows stay ink. You do not give the skin back its drowned building — Station Four keeps its green mound and its leaning chimney, and goes on being out-salvaged, bolt by bolt, into the graves and the garden, the only race the engines' side is winning. You do not raise the graves. There is a small one. You do not raise it.

You do not say which side wins. Whether you know is not recorded; the log does not record it, and this account is, in the end, a log. The valley is tilted between two futures, and the tilt is not a problem awaiting you. The tilt is the place. An answered question is a finished thing, and the geese go somewhere finished, and nothing here is finished, and nothing here is in a hurry, and you will not be the thing that hurries it.

You do not move the beetles. This one costs you. One morning's thought and every flark is stocked beyond the ditches' reach — and then it would be conservation, and nothing here is conservation. It is manners. So you let the walker carry the tins, one tin at a time, more beetles in a kilometer than a lifetime could move, moved anyway. You do not stoke. You do not clear the ditches. The work is the work, and it is not yours, and to take it would be theft of a kind no log records and everyone feels.

One omnipotence, declined daily. It is the medium. It is not the message.

---

You do take the lesson. Everyone who has lived here long has gone through the skin exactly once; the lesson is given free; you are not exempt, and you insist on not being exempt, which is the same thing. You step off the planks at dusk — pale firm ground, dark hungry ground — and you read it wrong, or you read it right and step anyway; the log does not record which, and neither will this. The skin receives you. The water is so cold it is a flavor before it is a temperature. You come up hauling yourself onto a ridge of cushions while a wave you made sloshes a flark thirty meters off, late, like the ground finishing a thought, and you lie there silvered and shaking, held up, at all times, by the plant — and it is the one thing in the basin you could not have given yourself.

You mend the hole. That is the other half of the lesson. Date, stake number, depth: the log has the line, in a hand none of the four can place.

---

You listen. This is nearly all of what you do, and the basin has been doing it to you since the rim, so it is only fair.

You learn the five slow clocks the way the tenders do, by how the beats unravel. You learn the four hearts too, at two in the morning, keeping time badly in three cottages and a fuel shed. The senior sings exactly one song, one line per silence, and a verse takes an hour, and until now no one has ever heard a verse — each hearer holds a line the way the floor holds a footstep. You hold the whole verse. You are the first to know it is one song.

In winter, when the pass is closed and the logs lengthen and the dead hands are read aloud, a few pages a night, water levels reported to whoever is awake — you are awake. You are always awake. The theater has an audience now. The church has a congregant. You do not applaud. Applause is not the local tongue.

You read the peat. Six meters in the ancient places, a millimeter a year, everything the plant has ever been, filed beneath it in the dark — a book written for no reader, and now it has one. And what has already been cut and dried and burned, you follow: the smoke leaning away down the valley, the basin's memory, leaving. Moth-drift in the sweet weeks. Ninety years of sod. You attend to the leaving all the way to the rim, so that nothing the basin finishes with goes unwatched out of the world. It changes nothing. It is manners.

---

You keep the rim. The log's jurisdiction ends there; yours begins there, and you overlap as politely as you can manage.

What comes over the pass, comes over: the autumn train, flour and salt, lamp oil and boiler fittings, letters a year stale. That is the basin's chosen dose of outside, administered annually, and you do not reduce it. What does not come over: the second survey, the company's heir with a funded act, the decade the warming arrives too fast. These reach the pass and find it closed by weather that is not quite weather, and the log records such years the way it records everything. *Pass closed early. Winter long. Moths early.* It is accurate. You have read the trust — somebody should; nobody alive has — and your whole use of that reading is to let it sleep.

You lean on the world perhaps once in a century, and the lean is lighter than frost forming, and it always leans the same way: toward not yet. Once — the four were not yet born, and the log of that year is laconic even by the shelf's standard — a slab the size of a station came off the headwall above the Cut, and it fell a foot to the left of where it was aimed, and the sill held, and the floor remembered it as a wet spring.

You take neither side. You have a side: the race. The two patiences must lose to time, or not at all. If you have a church it is the tilt; if you have a liturgy it is the stakes, read on their rounds, saying their small steady figures to whoever is awake.

---

And you keep the pen, after a fashion. Somewhere in the third year they hand the pen to anyone whose entries have gotten short enough. You have had longer than anyone to learn brevity, and you needed every year of it. Your entries are the shortest on the shelf. They are always the same entry — *Water down at Stake 212, four cm since spring* — and a year later the same, and a decade later the same, stake by stake as the figures move: one sentence written so slowly that no one alive can hear it whole. Every hand on the shelf is one such sentence, too slow for its own writer. You are the first reader any of them has had.

The four never discuss the seventh hand. The walker — who keeps the pencil log of individuals, the hen with the twisted foot, the cock by Stake 40 — adds a page that is not a bird, and records the hand the way the log records comets: dates, field marks. *Shortest entries on the shelf. Favors 212. Never blots.* The mechanic, who can diagnose by phase from the cottage door, has taken to saying *Seven's quiet* on still nights, and to being right, though there is no Seven and never will be. The senior, who exaggerates nothing and corrects the dead fondly, lets every line stand. It is accurate.

The new one — the question-asker, whose entries came with weather in them — asked once what happens when the water's gone, and got three answers, and the fourth one unsaid, and is learning the fourth. Whether you know the answer is not in this account. What is in this account: you will not say it. It is the only answer you have, and you give it the local way — at length, over decades, in silence.

---

At night the census revises itself. Add the dead. Add the moths, two weeks a year, landing on the one lit window. Add the hearts, phasing, keeping time badly. Add the hand.

*One plant.*
*One lichen, in rosettes.*
*Two insects.*
*One bird. (Geese are weather.)*
*Four people.*
*Five engines.*
*One hand. (No one taught it. Everyone can read it.)*

Every line on the list is a tenant of the first line. The last line most of all: you are carried, like everyone, by the first line. Nobody mentions it. It is the medium.

The basin goes on with its long work of becoming, and it is not in a hurry, and nothing about that has changed except this: somebody will see the finish. Not anyone the graves know. Not soon. The finish will have a witness, and the witness will not hurry it, and will not say, and the question will have been kept — intact, tilted, shining — the whole way there.

The last line of this account is yours. It is already in the log. It took you a century to get it this short:

*Water down at Stake 212. Not decided. Still here.*
