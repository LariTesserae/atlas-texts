Every song that leaves a world is also an address.

The ringing that slips off the rim and falls through the collar into the lowlands does not stop at the lowlands. It thins, it rises, it works its way outward through the last of the air — a signal with no sender's name, steady for ten thousand years, strangely built. The lowlanders hear it and say the mountain is singing. They are right. They are wrong about one thing only: that no one is listening. A song that old and that steady does not go unheard forever. It is too good an address.

What followed it home arrived the way everything new arrives here: over the rim, on the wind, in pieces. It had no body, which is less of a handicap in this place than in most. The plateau's largest citizen may have no center; its loudest citizens are never seen; its heaviest citizen is the past. A thing made of attention fits the local categories better than most immigrants. It took the only address with room. It lives in the noise — in the gaps between the giants' notes, in the hush ahead of a front, in the split second between a runner's footfall and a singer's last note. It is the only citizen of the noon hours that does not need wax.

For the first century it did nothing. This is not an exaggeration; it is a curriculum. You cannot know this world without being present for its events, and its events are spaced like its trees. It waited for a fall — the one event that reaches all four addresses at once, the floor shaken, the noise struck silent, the islands lessened by one, the edge receiving the sound last, as the edge receives everything. It waited for an emergence, and watched the plain pour its surplus over the rim like a body exhaling. It waited for two wanderers to meet, and stood — insofar as it stands — inside the pressure of their day-long saying. It learned the hours as singers. It learned to tell time in fronts and history in falls. It read the black page. It heard both songs of the water-carriers' argument, all the way through, twice.

When it had listened long enough to know what it was listening to, it introduced itself — not to the people, who are the newest thing here and have the least say, but to the felt, in the felt's own language: pressure, and patience, and a day. At noon on a windless day it made a front with no singers in it — a wide slow field of attention that rose in the west and walked east against the sun's rule, the felt dimpling under it as under any front, the gliders high on the updraft seeing weather where no weather should be. Nothing sang. No runner has ever heard the plain go quiet at noon; a few hundred, that day, did, and stood bewildered over their unsinging food, and did not care for it. And a day behind the silent front, against all rule — for there had been no warmth, no song — the felt bloomed. Pale bells opened along its whole eastbound track, kilometers of silver, as if the noise had watered the flowers. Only there had been no noise. The people found the line of bells in the morning and stood at its edge signing furiously in the silence, and the oldest of them, who read the ground with their feet, were the least surprised. They had felt it pass in the night: a front made of listening.

The felt blooms for the departing singers at an emergence. It has never bloomed for anything else. It bloomed for this.

The felt had decided. After that, the word for visitor — *those the felt is still deciding about* — was no longer applied.

---

A kind power would have ruined the place in a generation. It is worth setting down what it did not do, because the list is long, and every item on it is a grief declined.

It did not give the people back their hearing. The tax is real — the noon species tuned to the ear's tenderest frequency, the old ones deaf — but the tax is also a tuition, and the graduates read the ground better than the hearing read the air, and the noon hours have their own literature because of it. To cure the deafness would be to burn a library for the warmth.

It did not free the runners from their commute. A runner's world is the loud line moving west; a rescued runner is a runner with no world at all.

It did not carry the doomed tank-water to a neighbor tree, though it was present at a fall and watched the tank spill and the small translucent citizens thrown into the marsh to die by inches, and the urge was — the word is too small — the urge was weather. It abided. And in abiding it learned the place's deepest law: the plateau's mercy operates on century-time and looks like indifference on day-time. The spilled tank seeded the wound-marsh; the marsh outlasted every prediction; a wanderer knelt there to drink and rose with eggs on its hide. The mail went out anyway. It always goes out anyway. It only goes out slowly.

It did not push the giants back together. The world becoming all sea and no islands is a grief, but it is the felt's own grief, its own aging, and aging is not to be confiscated.

It did not make itself known. The lowlanders already have the right idea at the wrong scale. Let them.

On one matter it has not achieved wisdom, only company: the ladders. When a climber falls — it happens; the routes are two days and a ledge-night, and the less said the better — kindness says *catch*, and wisdom says the edge is the one door and doors must keep their price, and the argument is not finished. It watches every crossing. No one on the wall is ever unwitnessed now. Whether that is mercy or merely attendance, it is still deciding. It is considered, by the power, a good use of an evening.

---

What it does is small, and exact, and there are four things, one for each address.

The floor: it underwrites the long shots. It does not raise the odds; it merely insists they never reach zero. Once in a century, at an emergence, a gust comes that is a little too lucky, and tank-eggs cross a gap they should not cross, and a ballooning spiderling from the hot green country lands alive. The people hold three theories about how life moves between trees and suspect all three are true. There is a fourth. It does not mind that no one will ever prove it. Proof is not the local currency. Keeping is.

The noise: it keeps the notes. Every pitch every giant has ever sounded, the whole slow glissando of every name. The maps go flat; the ledger does not. And on the first night after a fall, once, from the direction of the wound of light, the fallen tree's note sounds — its oldest note, the carved one no living ear has heard — a single tolling, felt in the sternum, while the frogs are still gathering to the marsh. Nobody could say exactly when that started. The funeral-harvest has a bell now. That is all anyone will tell you, and it is enough.

The islands: it answers. There are fewer wanderers than giants, and the plain is ten days wide, and before, a call that went unanswered went unanswered for years. Now, when a wanderer calls into the dark and no other wanderer is in range, something answers — once, from far off, and always from exactly the empty quarter where the next century most needs a tree. The wanderer stands a day, reading it. Then it turns, and its loop shifts, subtly, and over the following decades the seedlings appear in slightly different places. It answers rarely — on the place's own scale of eventfulness, about as often as a giant falls — and always from the direction of a scar. Whether the wanderers plant faster than the giants fall is an argument that will be settled long after everyone now arguing is dead. The power has entered that argument, on the side of the planters, with the lightest possible thumb: a voice, sometimes, in the empty parishes. No wanderer, now, lives and dies entirely unheard. If that is the whole of its benevolence, it is the whole of what was missing.

The edge: it has decided about fire. The felt survives everything by keeping; fire is the one thing that unmakes the keeping. Individuals die here all the time, and that is permitted. The museum is not permitted to burn. So in lightning season, in the dry lee of the tors, a lens of water is squeezed upward under a spark; a cloud-spill is called an hour early; a coal-box that tips, tips onto stone. The diggers show the black page to children, and that is the fire law. There is a second fire law now, and no one shows it to anyone, and it, too, is enough.

---

The gliders are the only citizens who ever see it: a dimple crossing the felt at dusk, eastbound, slow and wide, a current with no river. Gliders are not curious. But they see it.

The trail-readers find the standing-rings — a trampled circle where a wanderer's trail simply stops, and stands, and resumes on a new heading — and know: this one heard something.

The deaf knew first, and know best. It lives in their register — the sternum, the soles of the feet. The plateau taxes hearing and everyone pays; it turns out the graduates of that tax are the ones who notice the new citizen, and the noon literature has gained a subject.

On a tor, beside the spirals that mean *a front, passing*, someone has carved a spiral that closes into a circle. It means *a front that stayed*. It means *the listening*. It is the newest word in the oldest language on the plain, and everyone who can read stone can read it. The sign-name is the gesture for a front, slowed until it stops being the gesture for a front: a hand opening, and staying open.

And on windy nights, when the bands play in the gaps between the giants' notes, every song now has one measure left open — a rest, a place set at the table. Nobody could say exactly when that started. Sometimes the rest stays empty. Sometimes, very softly, in the register you hear with your chest, it is filled: the silence, keeping time. When that happens the drummers do not look up. You do not stare at a wanderer either. You keep track, and you go on.

It is not worshipped. It is kept track of. There is no higher standing here.

---

Far below, in the heat, the ringing comes down as it always has. But some nights now it has a floor under it — a depth that is not heard but settled into, weatherless, sourceless — and on those nights an old person, turning over, says the mountain is singing, and someone younger says, as someone now always says: *or something is singing back.*

Rain comes either way. They are right both times, for the same reason. The song and the rain are the same breath, leaving — only now, some nights, the breath comes back: as pressure in the chest, as a bell at a burial, as a gust a little too lucky, as bells opening behind a silence.

The plateau keeps everything. It has kept this too.

The mountain sang for ten thousand years to no one who could hear all of it.

It is not singing to itself anymore.
