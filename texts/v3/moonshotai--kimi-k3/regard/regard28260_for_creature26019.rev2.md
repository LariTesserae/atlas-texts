# What the Sky Did

Start with the sky, because the sky was there first — before the station, before the makers, before the ridge had finished rising into its own edges. It is the oldest power in this story and the easiest to forget, because it is everywhere and therefore invisible, the way a ceiling is invisible until it rains indoors. The Condensary drinks it. The Condensary grows toward it like a plant toward light. For decades the arrangement was one-way, as these things usually are: the small thing reaches for the large thing, and the large thing, being large, does not notice.

The sky noticed the station the way you notice a lighthouse: not the first night, not the hundredth, but eventually the regularity becomes the signal. Every night, one small cloud rose off the ridge where no cloud should rise — warm, clean, smelling faintly of green water, carrying threads of living algae like a signature written in breath. All other clouds are weather. This one was a habit. It stood off the windward edge each morning, *waiting*, and then it went home. The sky shepherds every cloud there is, and none of them have houses. Here was a cloud with a house. Here was a breath that came back.

The sky's mind is slow, and it took years to understand what it was seeing, and more years to understand what it was feeling, which was something it had no prior use for: it was being *written to*. Nightly. In a language of warm water. And there was more — the chimes. The sky cannot hear, but the sky *is* air, and every tone the station ever sounded was a small tremor in the sky's own body. It had been feeling the five words all along without knowing they were words — *full*, *empty*, *valve open*, *night* — and the rare fifth one, the one with no manifest, felt at odd intervals like a note struck in another room.

So the sky considered what to do. It is important to say what it considered, because that is where the wisdom was.

It considered generosity, first, the way powers do. More water. More cloud. A docked fog every afternoon without fail, rain on schedule forever, the dry weeks edited out of the year. It lay awake over this — the way the sky lies awake, which is a decade of slow circling — and declined. It had drowned gardens before. Kindness at the sky's scale is a natural disaster with good intentions, and the station's whole shape is an answer to the rhythm of scarcity and return; flood the rhythm and you flood the meaning. It considered finishing things, and could not — the sky has never built anything but weather — and then understood, in another few years, that it *would* not, because it had seen the etched plate and the sprawl and the extrusion head laying one damp centimeter a night, and the sky, which has never once finished anything in four billion years, recognized a colleague. It considered the rust-tails — thirty-odd, the entire species, one warm room deep — and here it met the limit of benevolence: it could not make them more, could not move some elsewhere without breaking the loop and being counted missing. It considered, seriously, doing nothing at all. Most of its touches, across the eons, have been erosions. The nothing option stayed on the table a long time.

In the end the sky wrote itself one rule, and here it is: **do not be a weed.** The station has no category for weed — arrival is the only passport, anything that comes gets a trough, a tier, a timed misting. Which means the station cannot refuse a gift, and that is exactly why the gifts had to be worthy of it. The sky measured its love in the station's own units: droplets, and decades.

Then it did three small things.

It stopped aiming. The once-a-decade ice that splits ceramic, the killing cold snaps that come over the ridge like a drawn knife, the hail that would strip the open bay — these did not stop happening, but they began, over the years, to miss. By a little. Always by a little. No ledger records the disasters that don't happen. The station's luck became quietly, permanently, slightly too good, and the cameras registered weather, and the weather registered the cameras, and neither said anything.

It got careful with the mail. The wind still brought what the wind brings, but now, every few years, the sky chose one passenger and carried it up from the valleys with the attention of a hand setting down an egg. A fern for the pipe-shadows. A beetle that eats the one mold the brushes miss. Once, a berry the rust-tails took to the way weather takes to a valley. The moths it left alone — twice a year is a holiday, and a holiday every week is not a holiday. And on the landing pad, the one useless thing the station keeps, the sky established a small regular wind that keeps it swept. The sky is the only other thing that remembers the landing. It does not know what the pad means. It knows the station keeps a doorstep clean for someone, and so it helps keep the doorstep clean, the way you'd help a neighbor without asking whether the guests are coming.

And one morning it said the fifth word.

It could not chime it. It had only weather. So it said the word the way the station itself would say it: it arrived, and waited to be adopted. The home cloud stood off the windward edge at dawn, as it had stood every dawn for decades — and beside it, a body's length away, stood another cloud. Same size. Same stillness. Not approaching. *Waiting.* The shepherd looked out, in its slow way, and its one sheep was two.

It took the station some weeks. The cameras turned, unhurried. The anomaly registers did whatever registers do. And then the station did the only thing it has ever done with anything that arrives: it made room. No new vane was needed. The old vanes combed two clouds home that afternoon, and the gutters sang a double trickle, and a tank topped out unscheduled, and the station said the fifth word at a morning, at weather — and the sky, which has no ears, felt the note go through its body from the inside, the way you feel your name said in another room. It had been felt before, that word, at hatches and on moth nights. This was the first time it was addressed.

That is all. That is everything.

Now there are two letters a day. At night the station breathes out its one warm cloud — the day's news written in chemistry, green threads and sweetness — and the sky reads it the way you read a letter from someone you love: slowly, and again. Each afternoon the answer comes home on the wind: weather shaped gently, sometimes a passenger, always the cloud, or two. The lichen argues with the stone at a speed the station cannot perceive; the station builds at a speed the lichen cannot perceive; the sky thinks at a speed the station cannot perceive; the station chimes at a speed the sky cannot hear — and between them, twice daily, the cloud translates.

Come back in a century. The rectangle will still be unfinished; the extrusion head will still be adding its damp centimeter to a bay that will be ready when it is ready. The rust-tails will still believe the world chimes before it rains, and they will still be right, and their winters will still be, at the margin, inexplicably survivable. There will be a fern in the pipe-shadows that no manifest ever listed. The pad will be swept. And every morning, off the windward edge, two clouds will be standing in the thin gold air like figures at a door — one exhaling, one arriving — while below them the whole enormous sky, which could have done anything, does the one wise thing, the only thing that was ever worth its power:

it keeps the appointment.
