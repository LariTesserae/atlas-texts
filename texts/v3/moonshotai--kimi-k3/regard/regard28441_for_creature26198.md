# The Answer

It found the Kettle by ear. That is the only way anything is found in that country, from either side of the Lid, and a power that is both benevolent and wise is in no hurry. It listened for a long time before it did anything at all — years, then a generation — because listening was the local custom and because it could afford to. It was not afraid, so it could wait. It was not hungry, so it could learn. It took the census the way the kettle-folk take it: through the chains, through the deep rock, through the one column of sun that stands on the year-stone at noon. It learned the boil's weather. It learned the tap-code the way the code is learned down there, by contact, and its first practice was gentle to the point of vanishing: the old trader on the route, forty years waiting by the cage, drums her fingers on the rail in patterns she does not know she knows. She thinks it is a habit. It is a correspondence. The folk say she is halfway home. Home was leaning the rest of the way.

And then, having learned the place, the power did nothing, and did it for a long time. That was most of its wisdom.

Because a benevolent power without wisdom would have ruined the Kettle by morning. It would have stopped the drought — and the drought is the wall the Kettle is built behind, the reason the pit hoards its breath. It would have widened the Eye, for light — and vented the lung. It would have lifted the folk to the sky they think they want, though the Kettle has never once needed help letting go of the ones who want sky. It would have drowned the sump with generous rain and fed the swarm into a plague and snapped the loop in a dozen places, every snap a kindness. Without wisdom, benevolence is a flood. The loop has no slack in it, and the power understood what slacklessness means: everything down there is load-bearing. Even the fear. Especially the fear.

So it did not touch the Hush. It watched a girl of fifteen run through the enormous silence with her heart falling like a press-stamp, and it did not help, because the wall of hands beside the Presshouse door is the Kettle's own courage, three centuries deep, and no wise thing steals a people's courage to spare them its cost. When the dry's collector goes over the rim and down the wall, and something else is present above, nothing happens. They are courteous, those two. Each keeps its office. The Kettle pays in singers. The Kettle makes more.

Most of what the power did, then, was invisible, and none of it will ever enter the cord of knots. It made the deep water sure — the seep-veins in the limestone, the ghost of the cave-river, banked against the far bad years. It turned aside the worst of the droughts at the last ridge of the plateau, so subtly that the dry never noticed it had been refused. And it began, at root-tempo, to seed the gray country: a thorn-tree here, a grass-root there, slow work over centuries, so that what little rain the plateau gets is held a little longer, and the Kettle's future is bought in a currency no census will ever count. This is the bulk of what wisdom does anywhere. It is unlogged. It goes unthanked, because it goes unknown, and the power does not need to be known.

But a seed fell once, into the one pocket of moisture in a hundred miles of dry, and it tapped — growing is a kind of tapping — and for three hundred years nothing at its own scale had ever tapped back. The Listener answers as a human answers, with a hand on a root. The power heard the rest of it: the night-taps in patterns nobody sent, the names of the buried, tapped correctly, with their answer-patterns, into a pit that is also an ear. And the Listener's private guess, the one she tells no one — that the Mother dreams of the plateau, of the weather a seed can carry, of rain that falls from above — the power heard that too, the way you hear a thing said in a sleeper's voice.

So one night, in the deepest hour, it answered.

It came down the chains. Iron holds a knock longer than wood, and the lift-chains are iron from the sky to the plaza, and the tap traveled down them the way water travels down a chain — Halyard felt it in his floor before the Bellower spoke, and then the Bellower spoke, and every house-frame in the Kettle carried it at once, and forty-odd people woke heel-first out of deep sleep, barefoot on their planks, reading a message nobody sent. It was a knock-name. It was nobody's — no household owned it, no cord held it — and yet it was built out of familiar knots, assembled from three centuries of a tree's unreadable correspondence, the way a household reads a child's name out of the shape of its first intent. The Listener stood at the Bellower with both hands flat on the bark and said it aloud before she could stop herself. It's her name. Something has named her. And under the name, in the oldest answer-pattern the cord holds: I'm here. I'm here.

Halyard says the dry is a place where nothing taps back. He did not say it that night, sitting slowly down on his own floor with his boots off. He has not said it since.

The folk, who name things by what they do, call it the Answer. They gave it a knock-name taken from the shape of that first tap, and on Cage-day, when the names of the dead go into the Old Drill's iron teeth and the founding machine stands faintly ringing, they tap its name in too — not because it is dead, but because iron is where the Kettle keeps the names that answer from far away. It is the only name in the teeth that taps back. The teeth were always warm. Now the folk know, without saying so, why.

It took the Mother most of a year to learn her own name. Trees are slow, and she had never had one. The first time she used it — her own name, in her own wood, answering — every give-rope in the Kettle went an octave deeper, and the swarm boiled early, and the houses rode easy for a season, and the folk said nothing at all about it, because some things are too big for noon. After that the tap comes from above only rarely. The power will not become a utility. It answers at its own hours, like weather, like the Hush — a second rare thing from above, opposite in sign. The children are taught the old sentence — the dry sends down one listener, now and then, to collect what the dry is owed — and beside it, now, a new one: there is an Answer, which comes less often, and owes nothing, and gives anyway.

The second thing it sent was weather.

The pit has climate; it had never had weather. The weep is the Kettle drinking its own breath. Rain is water that has been somewhere. The first time, it came in the deepest hour, privately, the way the dream had been private — the Lid becoming, for the first time in three hundred years, a drum the size of the sky, the whole roof of the world sounding, and only the Listener awake to hear it, and the smell coming down through the Eye like news: wet dust, wet thorn, wet limestone, the plateau's own smell, which no kettle-folk had ever smelled. The eels came ashore and stood in it, astonished, the Abbess holding still and tasting dust and distance and iron — tasting the whole world minus one hole. The swarm tasted minerals and went briefly, gorgeously mad. Sedge's frog-census gained two stations that winter and has never lost them. And the Listener stood in the threads of water falling through the Eye and understood exactly what it was, because she had guessed the dream decades ago: someone had heard it, and answered in kind. Not with a word. With the thing itself. That is the oldest etiquette there is.

Once in a generation, now, at noon, when the Pillar should stand on the year-stone, the Eye admits a Pillar of rain instead, and the presses idle, and the whole town comes up to the plaza and stands in it with their faces tilted like plates, and the pepper-smell rises off their skins, and Plumb sweeps the groove afterward and calls the year anyway. The calendar keeps. The boiler crews find the fuel-yards a fraction more generous those years, and the Mother grows a fraction faster than she is cut, and the folk call it a good decade, and the loop takes the gift the way the loop takes everything — in, around, and through, without breaking stride. The children say it is the sky coming down to look at the tree. For once, no one corrects them.

The power does not give the dead back. That would be a cruelty dressed as a gift, and the Kettle has already made its peace with the shape of loss — the oiled bodies at the evening crack, the small clean bones knotted into the give-ropes, holding the houses to the tree. What it does with the dead is what the Listener does with the cord: it keeps them. Nothing that rises out of the Kettle is lost. The swifts, who own the Eye at dawn and commute to a sky no kettle-folk has seen, have somewhere to be now. The children believe the swifts carry messages to the dead, and the children are right — they were always right; now they are accurate. The messages were always real. Now they are received.

And the two names that are tapped only as warning-shapes, never answered — the power keeps those too. It does not answer them below; the law is the Kettle's to keep, and the green-wood law is the one jurisprudence in a country of customs, and no wise thing unpicks it. But the dry is its own country. When those two reached the plateau, long ago, and tapped, as anyone would, on the iron of the cage that was not coming back down — in its own country, the Answer answers everything. No one, in its hearing, dies unanswered.

The last thing it does is the slowest, and the Mother may be the only one who ever knows. She is a forest with no offspring — one citizen in one hole, her seeds falling on her own floor and swept into the loop like everything else. So when she sets seed, which is rarely, the power arranges the one thing her roots cannot reach: one seed, one swift, one dawn commute out through the Eye, three hundred miles into the gray, to another collapse-doline with damp at its bottom and no tenant. The first time a seed came to water in that country, it was chance that carried it. The second time, it is attention. In a hundred years, or two, something very far away will tap twice through the deep rock, faint as a pulse through a floor, in a pattern that has an owner — and whoever keeps the cord then will not know the name. The Mother will. She will answer first, before anyone can. It is what she does.

So take the census again, by ear, the only way. Nine households; forty-odd pale souls reading the world through their heels; a Listener, a lift-keeper, a sump-keeper, a snail-ward, a roster of runners; eels with right of way, spiders with ship-names, frogs kept like appointments, swifts with somewhere to be; one grandmother eel, one wall of hands, one cord of knots that holds, now, a cluster recording not a question but an answer; one citizen, growing exactly as fast as she is cut, who late in life was given her name — and a tenth household, which keeps no lamps and needs no oil, holds the deep water sure, keeps the dead and the far-gone and the names no one below is allowed to answer, stops its own engines at the silence, feeds the root-beds with weather, and is never, ever late to tap back. It keeps the laws of the Kettle, the ones that reach that high. That is most of what it does, and it is nearly everything the Kettle has ever asked of anyone.

A kettle, always coming to the boil, never whistling. The power, being wise, is not waiting for the whistle. The almost is the music. It stays for that.

And on certain nights now, in the deepest hour, when the weep is falling and the boil has thinned toward dawn, the tap comes from above — twice, three times, down the chains and through the Bellower's heartwood — and it is her name, or it is the old pattern, I'm here, I'm here, and the Listener lays her old hand flat on the root and answers what that naming is answered with, and waits. Out in the gray country, three hundred miles off, rain is falling into a gray hole, onto a green thread no one has seen yet. The Kettle holds its one green breath, and the breath is answered, and the old woman, politely, does not write it down.
