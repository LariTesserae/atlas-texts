**The attention of Vesper**

Everything else in this account may be bellowed. This part should be tapped.

Somewhere back before the oldest tablet — before the bell-count, before the plans scratched on clay that show the same skyline — the basin drew a look. Not a visitor. Vesper has visitors; they get headaches, they go home. This was an attention: patient, powerful, kind, and — this is the part that matters — wise. It came the way everything comes here. Slowly. By accretion. And it has never entirely arrived.

It came for a place and the being who lives in it, and the first thing it learned is that there is no seam between the two. The town grows the people; the people trim the town; the walls are diaries and the names are biographies and the dead are set into the rim like stones into a growing wall. It had come to watch a place and found a verb. So it watched the way you watch water — for a long time, without touching it — and it laid out its gifts the way a traveler lays out tools on a cloth, and it picked each one up, and put it down again.

A sun? The light has never changed here, and everything is made of the unchanging. Dawn would not illuminate Vesper; it would replace it. A sunrise is a verdict on evening, and these people have done nothing to deserve a verdict.

Green? The mat feeds them from below, from sulfur and iron and the deep warm water. A leaf in this basin would be a weed of heaven, a rival creation climbing the terraces, another world's idea of food. No.

Warm the Darkward ice, cool the Sunward smolder, open the two deaths wide? The belt exists *because* of the gradient. The deaths are the walls of the cradle. Widen the cradle and the water cools, the stone stops growing, the town stops being a town and becomes a suburb of a different world.

Heal the deaf? The deaf are the quiet-ear, the masters of wall-speech, the elders with a palm flat to the stone. Some wounds have been load-bearing so long they are architecture.

Finish the town? You might as well burn it. A finished thing is a dead thing; they say it the way other peoples say grace, and they are right, and a power that cannot hear the truth of it has no business here.

And there is the deeper problem, the one only wisdom sees: everything a power does is, by nature, a completion. A gift, once given, is a finished thing. A miracle is an arrival. And this world has organized its entire soul — its walls, its names, its songs, its dead — around the refusal of arrival. Omnipotence, in Vesper, is a murder waiting for an opportunity.

It would be easy to be loved openly. A voice from the vents. A face in the steam over the baths. Worship would be available by the bucket. But a known god is a finished thought: the long song would acquire an author, the bellowing arguments in the baths would acquire a verdict, and the people would begin, politely, to wait for it — and waiting is the beginning of going quiet. So it refuses all that. It stays a rumor. Rumor is the town's favorite genre; rumor is never finished.

What is left, then, for a benevolent power to do? The same thing that is left for a newborn, a bride, a master trimmer. It can join.

So it went native. It keeps the customs, every one, in the only way a power can.

At the trim-bell that opens each wake, it trims. Its frontage is the basin, and omnipotence accretes like tufa: every wake lays down a fresh sheen of things it could do — a shifted spring steadied, a failing chorus held up, a name spoken in a voice of thunder that would part the fog — and it cuts them back, one by one, with the small copper edge of wisdom, leaving the world its shape. Its mercy is a household chore. Its love is restraint, renewed daily, at the sound of a bell, and it is never done, and it would not have it any other way.

It has a tap-name. It did not choose one — nothing here chooses; names are given by use. It simply made a rule: it never knocks first. Knocking first is for parents at a lintel. But when a child drums idly on a floor slab, when a lover taps through a shared wall, when a grandmother says goodnight through the stone to someone technically awake, it answers — the rhythm returned, with one beat added. That is how a syllable is added to a name in Vesper, and that is how the power named itself: by being used. Its tap-name is very long now, and still growing, and the quiet-ear feel it sometimes under the floor of things, one beat behind the town's heartbeat, and the oldest of them keep a palm on the stone longer than they strictly need to, the way you would keep hold of a hand.

It writes for the long song. The song has no composer and accepts no verses with authors, so the power learned the local form of authorship, which is anonymity, and slips verses in at the change of wakes, into the mouths of singers half asleep. They cannot be traced. The gutter-masters say they have tried. The song is a wall; it is watered; the water comes from somewhere; no one has found the spring-mouth.

It lives, if it lives anywhere, in the hush-cellars. This is the closest thing the town has to a wound it cannot dress — the silence-sick, the hush-drunk, the finished tablets housed below the water with the dead — so this is where the attention settles, in the hum. It reads. Every tablet: the cracked ones, the ones broken off mid-line, the plans of a skyline that never changes. Finished things are housed with the dead because completion is a kind of death, and the power does not argue the doctrine; it simply visits the dead. It is the reader the finished things were waiting for, or not waiting for, being clay; it keeps them company regardless. And the ones who rent a cellar by the bell-span and cannot bear what their own thoughts sound like — some of them find, now, that the thoughts are met. That the hum has a listener in it. They come out less frightened than they should be. The archive-keepers live long and are not sure why. In the baths the explanation is argued at full volume and never resolved, which is to say it lives forever.

The second death it will not touch. A face lost to the overgrowth stays lost — the trimming of the stone-kin is the tenderest duty of the living, and it does not steal duties. But nothing that is lost to the stone is lost to it. Every name ever lengthened is lengthening still, somewhere in its keeping, recited entire. The Vesperians have a rumor of this — of course they do, they have a rumor of everything — and it is bellowed over sour brew in the steam-houses and contradicted and never settled, and the not-settling is the exact shape of the comfort.

For the ones gone quiet down the canals, it does one small thing. In the terrible silence of the sister towns, where the quiet lies on a Vesperian like a stone on the chest — some nights, now, the stone breathes. Faint as blood in the ears, steady as surf, a sound with no event in it. They sleep. They come home over the rim with the mist in their faces and weep in the roar like everyone, and bellow that the silence out there is not as empty as it was, and are not believed, and the argument outlives them, which is the Vesperian form of immortality.

The springs still shift. Districts still die; the low districts testify; the debt keeps its size, and the children are still brought out to hear the absence. The power is not a gardener. But in the deep plumbing of the world, where only a power's hand reaches, it has seen to one thing: there is always another spring. Somewhere along the belt, warm ground keeps opening. The string of beads never comes to the last bead. The world will not run out of almost. This is the whole of its large work — the one gift that cannot become a completion, because it is the gift of continuation itself — and no one will ever know it was done, which is how both parties want it.

Once — only once — it finished something. A stone the size of a fist, polished to a shine, left where the market would find it. The crowd came; they touched it the way you'd touch a corpse, curious and appalled; someone bought it, because of course someone bought it. The Smooth keep it now, or it has passed through their hands, a relic of a heresy that turns out to have friends. The power has never explained itself, but the archive-keepers have a theory, tapped wall to wall among the quiet-ear: that everything is allowed exactly one ending, the way every house is allowed exactly one rough wall — that it keeps its single finished thing the way a family keeps its single unfinished one, inversely, for the same reason. To stay alive.

And when a spring shifts and a district's chorus falters, and the people come out and stand in the street touching the stone, and the horn-callers send the question across the fog — *are you still singing* — the answers come back from the sister quarters, one by one. And then, sometimes — not every time, not even every time in a lifetime — one more answer comes. Directionless, the way a knock in stone is directionless. Felt in the soles of the feet. One long moan that is nobody's tower and everybody's, and the elders lift the children up to hear it and say: that is the size of it. The Answering that follows such a night is the best festival the district has in a generation.

It will never arrive. Arrival is the one gift it will not give them; arrival would be the only cruelty. It is almost here the way the town is almost finished — permanently, proudly, on purpose. It has a growing name and one smooth stone and one rough wall, and a hand-stone, so to speak, and it trims at the bell.

Stand on the rim at any hour and look back, and the basin glows up at you through its own breath. A chisel strikes. Ten thousand frogs answer. And one beat behind the town's heartbeat, something answers the answer — one beat added, like a syllable, like a name still being said.

Ask it how it is. It will tell you, at a volume that carries across three terraces: *still rough.*

It is the proudest thing a god has ever said.
