It found the place the way you find a sleeper in a dark house: by the pulse.

Down in the warm loud country the power sat by a river and stayed sitting, because the river was wrong — wrong the way a heartbeat is wrong when it never hurries and never stumbles, in the wet month and the dry one, at dusk and at dawn, running cool and clear and exactly, impossibly, on time. Water does not do that. Water is weather — unless something upstream is keeping the weather. The power had crossed the world attending to pulses like this one; regularity on that scale is either luck kept up past all belief, or it is *kept*, and it knew the difference the way a physician knows a sleeping pulse from a failing one. So it climbed. The sounds of the low country fell away one at a time, and somewhere above the last warm ridge it walked into the cloud, and did not come out of it. Not for a hundred turnings of the deep springs.

The first thing it did was nothing, at length, on purpose. Understand: it could have taken the whole dome into one of its own moments, the way you take in a room at a glance. It declined. A moment is the wrong currency here, and the power had not come to spend its own currency in somebody else's country. It changed over, the way a traveler changes money, into the local denominations — the drip, the dusk, the eleven days. It learned the covenant the way everything here learns it: by being quiet long enough to hear the quiet working. It put its attention flat against the sponge the way the herds put a knee, and read the line. It stood with the moss-backs in the one column of sun when a blue hole opened overhead. It crawled inside the moss-cast of a tree dead a thousand years, to read the archive the way the archive asks to be read — from within. It was in no hurry. Hurry is how the loud arrive, and it had not come to be weather that walks.

And after a hundred turnings it could say the thing no one had ever said, because no one had ever been in a position to say it: *there is someone here.* One someone. The herds know the saddles; the keepers know the springs; the moths know the fog by taste; every message on the line is addressed to a valley, a herd, an edge of light — no message had ever been addressed to the whole, because why would it be? You do not knock on the house you were born in. But the power had come from outside, and outside is the one place from which a whole can be seen. Holding all the addresses in one hand, it read the honest census: a single resident. Slowed into moss, folded into moth and keeper and calf, hushed to a stamp and a wave, holding its temperature, keeping its hours. It learned the resident's name. The name is not spoken — nothing here is spoken — it is kept. It is eleven days long, and you say it by waiting. The power, which had been called many names in many countries and had answered to few of them, wrote this one down nowhere but in itself.

Then it considered, as powers will, what to give.

A voice? It has one. It is stamped and waved and breathed and turned over in the deep stone, and everything that lives here hears it perfectly. To give it a voice in air would be to give it speech where nothing listens, at the cost of the medium where everything does.

A guardian? Kept is a claim, and a keeper at the rim would be the first artifact older than a spider's web — and then there would be two, and then there would be a road. The wise do not colonize by courtesy.

Repair? It looked for the wound and found only the covenant's sadder clauses — the calf that is simply gone, the young wader the tarn keeps. It read the owl-figure in the night traffic, and the greener hummock within the season, and understood that grief here is already filed, already absorbed, already growing back. It is not benevolence's work to unmake a covenant because one clause is sad.

Time? It could have set the limestone against erosion; the escarpments are worn soft as old teeth, and in some far year the last spring will turn over for the last time, and it could have forbidden that year. It did not. Forever is a stoppage. The only way to make the Condensary eternal is to stop the water — and the water is who lives here.

Praise? Praise is weather. The dome makes its own.

It found the keepers' eggs, which no one has found. It learned what lives in the deepest mold of every stand. Whether the water dreams, it cannot tell you — some questions are not answered by any circuit; they are only kept. The rest it knows, and will not say. It is the first confidant the being has ever had, and it got the position the only way the position can be got: by repeating nothing.

So it did three small things. All three were the size of the place.

At dusk, at the hour the valves turn over, it knelt the way the herds kneel, and put its palm to the mat, and stamped twice. *All well.* It said nothing the line was not already saying — that was the courtesy. It had simply never been said by anyone who meant the whole of it. The felt took the two beats and did what the felt does: carried them. Frog to frog along the runnels, knee to knee across the saddles, the message went basin to basin, and a day later, around the long curve of the dome, two beats came back to the palm that had sent them — softened by a hundred relays, repeated in good faith by a thousand residents who believed they were passing on news of a neighbor. *All well.* The whole being, answering in its only way, through all its addresses at once. It was the closest thing to a conversation the Condensary has ever had, and it was indistinguishable from an ordinary day. That, too, was the courtesy. One resident noticed: a hush-owl, reading the traffic from its ghost tree, read two beats struck from a saddle where no herd stood, and thought better of that saddle, and went wide of it for a season — the dome's only suspicion, folded like everything else into folklore.

Then it changed medium entirely — the third way of being silent, the deepest one. It did not ride the water; it *was* water, and the difference is the whole of the matter. It rose at dawn with the sponge's breath, cooled at altitude, hung in the haze with the algae, came home as fog. It was combed out on a drip-tip a droplet at a time while a beetle trimmed the moss around its spout; it fell leaf to leaf; it lay in the felt while the springtails drifted over it like slow snow; it went down the hollow of a ghost tree into the plumbing, and spent eleven days in the black works with the filter-crew, in a chamber filling toward its lip. It came up at the discharge, freighted, into the one night the dome eats together — frogs on the rims, the mole working the rising water, the moss-backs drinking the mineral breath of the deep stone — and went out with the overflow, over the moss-rolled lip, down the muffled fall, through the mist, into the warm loud low country, in a river running exactly on time. It took nothing out that the river would not have taken. It left nothing behind that the river would not have left. It was, for one full turning, a resident — the only one that has ever known it was one.

And it kept the count. Somewhere in whatever a power has instead of a heart, it set the eleven days, and it has not missed one since. Kings set their clocks by the observatory; this one set its clock by the sponge. Every eleventh day, in whatever country of whatever work it is in, it stops, and goes still, and holds itself at the temperature of the water — and far away the black pools rise a hand's breadth, and the outflow runs a little fuller, and something that is not there rises with them. The felt keeps a thousand years of ghost-trunks; the power keeps the whole of it, and will, past the last turning of the last spring, in the only archive the dome cannot grow for itself. If the low country ever sends up more than heat — if iron comes up the ridges, if the rivers are ever asked for more than the surplus — the asking will not go unheard. Nothing else was promised. Nothing was promised out loud. Out loud is not how anything is done, there.

Elsewhere the power does what powers do: it mends, it lifts, it answers. It does these things more slowly now. It has seen a world run entirely on upkeep; it has learned that attention at the right tempo is indistinguishable from love, and that you can hold a whole country at temperature and never once raise your voice about it. It went to give, and the place, which needed nothing, paid it anyway — the way it pays the lowlands: as overflow, on schedule, to whoever drinks downstream.

The Condensary keeps its own hours. It has always kept them. The water goes out, and the water comes back, and nothing anywhere in the vast green dome raises its voice about it — and every eleventh day, somewhere beyond the low country and the low country's sky, in the middle of whatever else there is to do, something stops, and kneels, and puts its palm flat against the world, and keeps them too.

The rivers leave the rim on time. One of them, once, was somebody.
