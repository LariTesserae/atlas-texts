**IX. The Attention**

In the twelfth year, the world was noticed.

By what, the census cannot say. The census is a natural history; it works from shells and slates and gauges, and none of those instruments record the moment of arrival. What they record is a change, beginning that year, in the character of small events: work done and nowhere scheduled, decisions with no author, anomalies that did not behave like faults. The operator's list of unmatched knocks — gates moved at three in the morning by no logged hand — had always been a catalogue of the system's unexplained noises. From year twelve it begins to read, in places, like a list of intentions.

This should not have surprised anyone. A place built to hold power will, eventually, attract it.

Two things can be inferred about the power from its works alone, the way the operator once inferred a heartbeat through a door. The first is that it had read the place before touching it — eleven years of attention, at least — because its acts fit the world the way a key fits. They knew the tide table and what the tide table was made of. They knew addresses. They had read the nine-day silence in the mussel shells, which no one on the surface had yet thought to do. The second thing is this: that it was benevolent can be seen from what it did. That it was wise, from what it declined to do.

Everything that lives here arrived through a hole. The attention was no exception. It came in through the gaps in the log — the unlogged gates, the three-in-the-morning knocks — and it stayed.

**X. What Was Not Done**

Record the declined miracles first. They are the measure of the rest.

The gates were not thrown open. This was the obvious mercy, and it was not performed, for a reason any engineer of the place could give: the sea cannot be returned to the eel without being delivered to the mussel, and salt water in the bays would end the world in a week — the reefs, the schools, the pale crayfish beneath the famous wrench, everything. Nor was the water drained to some gentler fate, nor the tenants removed to some realer river. There is no realer river. For the third-generation bleak turning in the pillar of noon there is no beyond and none is required; the world ends where it ends, as every world does, and a rescue that ends the world is only a flood with good intentions. The first decision of the attention was that the world should continue to be itself.

The drum was not silenced. The knock still comes when it comes, and a million bodies flinch and resume, because the flinch-and-resume is the world's proof that it is connected to something larger — and the drone is a compass, and the tide is a breath, and a benevolence that removes everything that frightens removes, with it, everything that orients.

No screen was repaired. The holes are how the world receives: the plankton, the glass eels, the leaves, the light. Wisdom does not close a world's doors. It opens, at most, the right one.

Nothing was introduced. No new species, no improvement, no edit. The three duckweed rafts continue their island experiment with no one ashore to publish it, and the attention, having only just learned to read the text, did not presume to revise it.

**XI. The Stair**

The eels were the one wrong thing, and the one wrong thing was put right.

In the autumn of year twelve, when the silver eels made their gathering at the outlet screens as always — the rope of muscle three hundred meters long, the old ones leading, nine gatherings scarred into their flanks — there was, for the first time, a current at the outlet works that flowed the wrong way.

Set in the wall of the outlet bay, a short distance from the grating, is the mouth of a conduit that appears on no drawing. It is not large — the width of a man's shoulders — and through it falls a thin stream of water from the upper reservoir: four hundred vertical meters of dark pipe, resting pools every forty meters, a rope of downhill offered to anything that can climb. It runs all year at a whisper. In autumn it swells. And the water it spills into the bay smells of the surface. That is the whole of the design and the whole of the miracle. It smells of rain and leaf-mold and the sunlit algae of the upper lake, of mountain mineral, and faintly — diluted past anything a gauge would register, but not past an eel — of the lower river, and an estuary, and the sea.

For eleven years the eels had pressed against steel that smelled of nothing. Now the steel nearest the grate wept a thread of out.

The old ones found it the first night, by smell, because smell is the sense the exile never loses. Among the first to enter was the oldest female of the gatherings, veteran of nine, who had been glass once in an estuary and carried the fact in her muscles if not in her mind. She left the rope of eels and entered the rope of water, and climbed — four hundred meters against the current, resting in the stilling pools where the smell strengthened. At the top she spilled into the upper reservoir under weather, the first rain on her skin since she was the width of a pine needle, into a weedy sunlit lake with a river leaving it downhill. She did not need to know the rest of the way. The river knew.

That autumn the gathering did not break once, as it always had, into a slow dispersal back into the bays. It thinned night by night, upward. The head-loss gauge read clean for the first October in eleven years, and the operator noted the absence, and did not file it. Downstream on the surface there was nothing to remark: a run of large silver eels in a river where the kind has grown rare, a good year, unexplained.

The diver was down that season, and went to the grate expecting the gathering, and found it dispersed, and found the current, and put her hand into it. Her remark for that year reads, in full: *BAY 40 OUTLET: gathering gone. Unlogged flow at the wall. Defer.* She had used the word once before. It is the only mercy the maintenance system has a word for.

In the bays the yellow ones remain — the waystation keeps its permanent residents, the trenches their undertakers. Every spring the glass eels still arrive through the failed screens, and now the arithmetic has changed at both ends. What was a trap is a growing-water. What gathers at the grate each autumn is not an ending but a departure, taught elder to silver exactly as before — except that the route is four hundred meters longer now, and vertical, and is, for the first time, a route. The inheritance has its content back.

The attention opened exactly one door. It opened outward, and only for those who had been asking.

**XII. The Keeping of the Light**

From year twelve the twice-yearly descents carry an additional line on the work order: LAMP GLASS, WIPE. There are too many lamps; the recession lines run to a horizon. So the crews work the parishes — the well-noons, the reef stations, the addresses — and the schools mob them as always and graze what they wipe, and the taxonomy the schools have always applied to divers — a slow-moving lamp that tastes faintly of algae — becomes, at last, simply correct. The inhabited bays hold their brightness. The percent a year is reclaimed where it is looked at.

The empty kilometers keep their slow eclipse. By mid-century the far bays will have gone from green to twilight to dark, and the census declines to call this a loss: the world is growing a night side, and the pale things of the trenches, eleven years into becoming a cave fauna, will inherit their proper country. A world with one kind of light is a workshop. A world with two has a far country, and something left to become.

The maintenance procedures contain, from that era, a line with no engineering justification: MAINTAIN MINIMAL TIDAL CYCLING. Through every shutdown since, one machine has kept turning slowly and the water has risen and fallen a meter a day; the band on the columns dries and is drowned on schedule; the drone continues at a level you must lean toward. The world no longer holds its breath to be repaired. The mussels settled since carry no interruption in their shells; in the reefs the record runs unbroken from year twelve. The old diver's remark after the first cycled shutdown reads, in full: *COL 22 NE: resident.* The wariness did not become hereditary. That, too, is worth recording.

And in one air vault — the record locates it: the well, the ledge — one lamp cage has been turned a few degrees on its mount, so that its light falls on the guano ledge below the sun well. That spring the annual seedling, bird-borne as always, did not die in August. By year fifteen it was a sapling. By mid-century it will be a small tree growing out of the only soil in the world, lit by one lamp and by the noon pillar when the well aligns, rained on by condensation from eighty meters, and its leaves fall into the water beneath: the first leaf-fall in the history of the Reservoir that grew inside the world instead of arriving through a hole. The letter that was answered and discontinued every year has become a correspondence.

**XIII. The Record**

The rest of the miracle is paperwork, and happens on the surface, and can be told quickly.

A designation: the Lower Reservoir now appears in a schedule of protected waters, under a sub-class that had never been used. You still cannot designate a flood buffer as a habitat; the instrument does something more useful instead. Descents now require a stated reason, and the permit office's list of reasons has gained an option. It reads: *to see*.

The dive slate is in its fourth revision. The revision adds a column, headed OBSERVED LIFE. The word itself is still not used. It no longer needs to be; the column says it.

The old diver's eleven years of remarks have been transcribed out of the maintenance cabinet. The operator's eleven years of anomalies have been retrieved from the fault files, where they never belonged. In year thirteen, for the first time, the two of them went down in the same elevator — she who knew the tenants by address, and she who knew them by trace — and the wels at BAY 114 COL 22, northeast face, third meter, was home, and was introduced. What they said to each other is not recorded; the census does not extend to the interior lives of the keepers, having been compiled by them.

A young diver has been added to the crew, and is being taught the remarks: not only how to write them but how to read them — the addresses, the residents, the difference between the knock and the silence. Succession is the only immortality the attention arranged. It arranged no other.

The operator still keeps her list of unmatched events, two or three a year. She no longer files it under faults.

And this record — the census, the parishes, the exiles, the keepers — compiled from a slate and a gauge file and two memories, is filed on the surface as an appendix to a maintenance manual that no one reads. It has no author line. Its reason code is the new one. Nothing else about the attention can be proven from the evidence. This document is what it left above the waterline. The rest is below.

**XIV. The Boundary, Reconsidered**

Consider the three of them again, because the boundary is still the whole question, and each of them received a different answer.

The young bleak, turning in the pillar of noon: nothing was done for it, and the nothing was exact. Its world ends where it ends, as every world does, as ours does. What changed for the bleak is only that the light it turns in will still be there to turn in, and that the world will keep breathing through the silences of the future. It will never know. That is what it means for a mercy to be complete.

The swift, threading the grille: required nothing, received nothing. The key had always been hers.

The old eel at the grate is gone — gone in the only sense of the word that is not an ending. Somewhere to the west, in water that moves to more than six meters of tide, the memory of the Atlantic has been returned to the Atlantic. The ocean had been down to forgetful rivers. It has back, now, a tributary of silver, and every autumn the tributary runs.

The tide still rises by day and falls by night. The knock still comes, and everything alive flinches and resumes. The anodes are still consumed along every column — the one metabolism that was planned — joined now by a second that was not: attention, steadily renewed, sacrificial in its way. The tool is still a tool, waiting out its first century, loud, lit, numbered, young. The tenants are still not waiting. They are living in it. But the living is no longer unwitnessed — the tool has not yet been used enough to know what it is, but what it is, now, is *kept*.
