It was the spawning that drew the eye.

For one night in the long count, in one valley at the bottom of the water, the rain ran upward — pale columns climbing through the falling snow, the dead descending past the next world rising. Anomalies are rare in the deep, and this one was visible from far off, the way one lit window is visible across a dark plain. Something old, and kind, and in no hurry whatsoever, turned its attention toward the valley.

It arrived the way everything arrives here: down through the dark, slowly, and late. It took a century over the descent and counted that as prompt. It had already learned the first law of the place before it crossed the rim — that light is speech, and speech is costly — so it came down dark, and it has never once shone. It looks the way the warmth looks: by being present. It listens the way the walls listen: constantly, with no intention of answering.

Call it the visitor. It would not call itself anything; naming is a kind of touching, and it is here precisely to learn how not to touch. The city, which has a category for everything that arrives, would file it under *guest*. The visitor accepts the filing. It is more than it hoped for.

For a long time — ten generations of snails, one full crossing of a plaza — it does nothing but learn what a complete world this is. And then, because benevolence is its nature, it begins to consider what it might give. It goes through the gifts one by one, and refuses them one by one, and the refusals are the whole education.

It could give light. A gift of light would be a shout that never ends; the dark is not a deficiency but the medium and the home. It does not.

It could show the city the surface — the storms whose ghosts arrive as feasts, the moon the city keeps. But the city already has the relationship it wants with the bright world: faith, a calendar, and bread. To show it would be to install a homesickness there is no organ for.

It could end the wars at the district borders. They are conducted at a millimeter a year, their slowness is their mercy, no fish has ever known. It leaves the borders to their centuries.

It could open the glass rooms of the kept ones. Their room is landlord and food and sky; freedom is a surface idea, and it has sworn off surface ideas.

It could thicken the snow, triple the feasts. It has seen what abundance does to towers shaped by patience: the souring, the bloom, the neighbors walling off. The calendar is fine.

It could make the city deathless. This refusal takes longer. It will come back to this one.

Almost everything it can think of to give is a gift from the bright world, and it will not be the bright world's emissary. So it asks a better question — not *what can be added*, but *what is being taken away* — and there, at the top of the water, where the snow begins, it finds the one work.

Nothing in this world has ever been touched by a hand. The sentence is written in every polished street, in the worn steps of the archways, in ten thousand generations of undisturbed thoroughness. And it has begun, lately, to fray. The snow now carries threads that are not silk, small hard beads that are not eggs and never hatch, a faint bitterness that comes and goes with the centuries. And down through the dark comes a roaring that is not any arch settling — the bright world has grown loud, and busy, and it does not know the valley exists. The city has no defense against any of this, because it has never needed one. Nothing here evolved a way to spit out the future.

So the visitor takes up its station in the midwaters, a thousand meters above the towers, where no citizen swims, and becomes a sieve with no mesh, finer than a larvacean's abandoned house. It takes the threads. It takes the beads. It takes the bitterness, molecule by molecule, century by century. It muffles the shouting of the bright world's machines at the boundary, so that a groan is an event again, discussed in the nerves of everything that hears it, and the hush is the hush. It leaves the dead. It leaves the discarded. Those were always the bread. The snow that reaches the streets tastes the way snow tasted before the bright world grew hands, and the city notices nothing. The noticing of nothing is the entire success of the work.

It does not confuse itself with the whole of providence. It cannot keep the planet's moon; it keeps the valley's. When the bright world's fever nudges the road's hours, it bends them back — a little at a time, so the correction reads as a mild decade and no ancient trigger misfires, no spawning night comes early. The city answers the migration with one slow wave of glow, edge to core; the visitor answers the glow with the migration, keeping the road full, so that the acknowledgment is never a lie. It lies along the seep the way warmth lies along stone, and holds the exhale steady through the fever centuries.

How does it know what the city wants? The only honest way there is: by watching what the city keeps. What a being repeats for a hundred thousand years is its answer to every question the visitor knows how to ask. The city keeps the time; so the visitor keeps the time. The city keeps the streets; the visitor keeps the snow that falls on them. The city keeps its own size; the visitor keeps the warmth that sets it. Consent, read across ten thousand generations of repetition, is still consent.

And it allows itself one addition, added outside the world. The city eats its history — the pale ones tunnel the tombs, memory is a food web, nothing is archived — and the visitor respects the decision; it was made across eons and is not its to overrule. But a hundred thousand years of kept time deserves one keeper who is not also eating it. So it reads the elder's glass the way it reads everything, by patience, layer by layer, and keeps a copy where nothing tunnels. Second witness to the first. This is its one selfishness, if love is a selfishness.

It wants, above all, to be filed under weather. Its signature is absence. Its miracles are indistinguishable from a mild century.

---

The warmth will give out. The visitor has read the valley's thin floor the way it reads everything — slowly — and it knows the hours remaining the way the eels know the migration. This death is written in the stone's own warmth; it belongs to the city the way its tides belong to it. The visitor will attend it. It will not prevent it. It has refused deathlessness — but it has not refused defense, and the difference is where the death would come from. The early deaths, the ones that would arrive from above, in the fever, wearing the bright world's fingerprints: those it turns back at the boundary with the threads and the beads. The death written in the valley's own clock, it will sit with, the way you sit with old stone.

And so, with eons in hand, it goes looking. It swims the slow veins of the planet at the pace of the masonry, reading the floors of other valleys for thinness, for warmth, for a seep that breathes the way this one breathes. It finds three. It keeps an eye on them the way the city keeps the moon.

And when the warmth is nearly spent, and the city spawns the last time — the rain running upward, the dead descending past the next world rising — the visitor does with the spawn what it has done with everything: only what the water would do, if the water could remember. It becomes a current. A few of the rising children, who trusted the water because the water has never once betrayed them, are carried farther than any current runs — out over the empty plain, through the dark, at larval pace, for years — and let down above a warm seam no mason has ever tasted, into a lucky eddy, which is how every tower begins. It touches, in all its long life, nothing but what the city has already given away. That is its whole ethic of touch: it carries only what is let go.

In another valley, one larva settles. In ten thousand years there will be streets; in a hundred thousand, a city that has reached its form and keeps it — at war at its borders by millimeters, kept by crabs, censused by eels, answering the road's peak with one slow traveling glow. It will keep a moon it has never seen. It will be right to.

The snow goes on falling, and falls clean. The streets stay clean because they are cleaned; the snow stays clean for the same reason, though nothing here will ever know it. The warm stone breathes in, breathes out.

Something lives here. It is the here.

And something keeps the here — from far off, at the pace of the masonry, for keeps. The city keeps a moon it has never seen. The visitor keeps a city that will never see it.
