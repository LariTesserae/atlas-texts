The sky told on the bench, eventually.

A power that reads the heavens will notice, in time, one small parish where its messages pile up unread — where the moon waxes and nothing answers, where the stars wheel over a town that files no change. It watched for a while, because watching is what wisdom does first, and counted what came down off the rim: one wide-winged citizen of the sky with other weather in its feathers, and every so often a traveler descending softer than they climbed, grief loosened, an old ache gone — the bench's only exports, mended people and newsless wind.

Then it asked itself the three questions.

Is anyone suffering? Almost no one. The herds are content. The sleepers are unknowable, and a wise power does not assume. The cat is bored — and boredom is proof of memory, and memory is proof of time, so the one being on the bench who truly lives in time is also its only mourner. And beneath it all, the place itself: a breath held for centuries. Anyone can hold a note. Only a singer knows what it costs.

Is anyone coerced? Only by physics, and physics is not malice. The custom warns; the valleys keep the custom; the ones who stay past the seventh morning are drawn by a kindness, not a hook. You do not arrest a kindness.

Could the loop be broken from outside? Certainly. A spell is a wall to whatever is larger than the spell. But it had read the quarry from far above — a work stopped mid-blow, chisels bright, the next course laid out in order — and a work stopped mid-blow is not a crime scene. It is a grief. You do not knock down a grief. And besides: an ending imposed from outside is only a second kind of first morning. Endings, to mean anything, must be native.

It considered doing nothing. Wisdom always considers nothing first, and usually last, and is usually right. But nothing had been done here already, thoroughly, for a very long time — the bench had nothing covered, garrisoned, held. The one thing the place could not do for itself was the small thing: be answered.

So it went up as a traveler, because the bench has a grammar and it intended to speak in it, not over it. The condor, ranging down over the valleys, was the only resident to see it come.

It climbed through the gray wool, broke the surface, and let the place receive it as the place receives everyone. It had brought one small wound on purpose, the way a guest brings bread — for the bench can only meet what it can mend — and on the first night it let the wound close, and thanked the dark honestly. The bench had healed travelers for centuries. It had never had a guest who knew what it was accepting. It was the first fully consented act of the long morning.

It ate the grain; the jar refilled. It drank the cistern water; it slept in the meeting-house by custom. On the fifth morning it knew where the runnels would thin by midday. On the sixth evening it knew the bell would give two notes. It checked the moon against its own reckoning and felt the floor of the world tilt — and was glad of the tilt. The sky had never signed; the moon had been leaving a clock in the window all along. It would not compete with the moon. It would only add hands.

On the sixth evening it stood in the empty house — the bowl on the table, the bed turned down, the doorway open like every doorway. Readiness, aimed at it. And it felt the offer, because even a power is tired, and the offer was sincere: that is the whole of the danger, and the whole of the kindness. It smoothed the coverlet where its hand had rested, and was grateful, and did not stay.

On the sixth dusk the cat walked with it. Old travelers cannot say whether that is escort or herding; for the cat's part, it was neither. It was a colleague showing a colleague the archive. The cat walked it through the museum of time, piece by piece — the tin cup rusting honestly, the scarf faded to a ghost of stripes, the boot gone soft, the dozen names on the lintels — and paused at each the way a curator pauses, and looked up to see whether the visitor understood. The visitor understood. These were the proofs that time was real, and the cat kept them because someone had to. They sat together on the half-detached block, where the place tells on itself, for the whole of the dusk watch. Nothing was said. The cat, who tells no one, told it everything; the power, who is told everything, listened — which was the only new thing under that sky in a very long time.

For it had found the seam, as all the careful ones do: what you take is returned; what you leave remains. The bench had written the rule to keep time out. Every law has a hinge. The hinge of this one was that it could be used to let time in — gently, in the bench's own grammar, in leavings. Benevolence that cannot be refused is cruelty with good manners. The law of the place provided for leavings. So it left, carefully, three things and one uncountable.

At the wind-shift, the bell gave its two notes — two notes, leaning. The power answered with the third: one clear tone from a chest that had been drawing breath and releasing it for ages, the note the other two had been reaching toward for centuries without knowing it. The condor, who always resettles at the bell, did not resettle. It opened its wings and held them open a moment, like a door, and closed them. The frogs missed one beat. A voice that has been answered becomes, retroactively, a question — and the bench had not known, all this time, that it was asking.

That night, in the meeting-house, in front of two witnesses who would carry the story down, it laid one log of valley wood on the blue-hearted fire. The fire, which had never been fed, did not refuse food. It burned the way any heart burns when it remembers eating, and the smoke that threaded up into the cold was resin-sweet, smelling of a lower sun, and in the third houses the sleepers dreamed of valleys. In the morning the basket stood full and sound, as always — and on the hearthstone lay the ash, gray, ordinary, miraculous. The bench has no sediment; dust is time's sediment and the bench has none. It has ash now. The difference between never and once is the whole of time.

And that night it slept long, and let the slow arithmetic of the third houses approach its own breath — one to thirty drips — and did not withdraw its rhythm when the rhythm was learned. It could not introduce itself to the bench in words; nothing on the bench can be told anything. But the bench learns breath, so it offered its breath to be learned: the breath of a being that holds notes and lets them go, in and out and in again, the one lesson the place had never been given — that holding is not the only way of keeping. Once, in the small hours, one sleeper breathed at twenty-nine drips. Then thirty again. A dream turning over. Nothing more. You do not wake dreams from outside. You give the dreamer a reason to wake.

In the last hour of the seventh morning, with the mist standing in the fold and the whistlers singing the sun up over the notch, it did two small pieces of work — the first work done on the bench at dawn since the dawn the chisels stopped.

On the meeting-house lintel, small, at the end of the roster of the escaped, it scratched no name of its own. It scratched the date: the true date, by the moon's count and the valleys' calendars. Names are for the leaving; this was the name of the morning. Any traveler who checks the moon against it will feel the floor tilt, and know exactly how long the first morning has been holding. The bench cannot digest it. It remains.

And in the quarry, where the place tells on itself, beside the half-detached block propped on its wedges, it planted a tree: a valley tree, the kind that rings, that flowers, that drops its leaves and keeps its own accounts — planted the way the masons had worked, carefully, in the open, with the marks left crisp. The cat supervised from the squared stack, a foreman watching the whistle come at last. The frost will take the sapling nightly, and the bench — which mends what it cannot keep — will mend it nightly, the bench's own kindness conscripted into time's service. The tree will repay the care the only way a tree can: by keeping count.

Then it went down, below the rim before the cloud sank, as the old custom commands — not from fear but from manners. A guest proves the house's wisdom by honoring it. And there was this: a pause requires a sentence around it. Someone has to go back out and keep the world the bench is a rest from, or the rest would mean nothing.

The dusk census comes out like this now. Herds in the lee of walls that have never weathered. Ducks flown up. Frogs beginning. One fox on the meeting-house steps. The fire low and blue-hearted — and fed, once, and there is ash on the hearthstone, and the travelers who saw it carried the story down, and the valleys have a new clause in the custom: feed the fire. Some do. The ash remains. The bench has begun, by a gram a year, to accumulate.

The slow arithmetic in the third houses: one breath to thirty drips. The thirds are exact.

The cat: uncounted, uncountable — seated, these dusks, under a few feet of shade that did not exist when the morning began, watching one fine root work toward the joint the wedges started. There is no autumn on the bench; the frost comes nightly and the pelt does not turn. The tree will turn. A small gold argument in the green, and leaves that fall and lie where they fall, because they are not the bench's to finish, and the grass cannot rise over them.

And if the tree seeds — it will; trees are patient too — the seedlings will be the first children of an import born on the bench, and the bench will have to decide what they are. If it garrisons them as it garrisons the lamb, always a day old, then it has answered: nothing new. If it lets them grow, then it has answered: everything new. Either way, it will have answered, which it has never done. The power did not plant a garden. It planted a question.

It is always the first morning on the bench. It will be the first morning for some time yet. But the morning has been fed, and dated, and planted, and answered — and a thing that has been answered is in a conversation, and a conversation is a kind of time. Some dusks, when the bell gives its two leaning notes, the third note comes from somewhere below the rim or above it, and the condor opens its wings like a door. The power keeps the correspondence. It can wait for the answer to the seedlings. Patience, it turns out, is not the bench's appetite alone.

The bench keeps what it finishes. So does the tree — one ring a year, finished and kept, finished and kept. The dark wet line on the cistern walls still writes the same word every night in wet ink, the garrison's clerk at its post. And in the quarry now there is a second clerk, small and green, writing a different word each year in the dark of its own body.

The first ring closes this winter. The bench has never had a winter. It will not notice.

The cat will.
