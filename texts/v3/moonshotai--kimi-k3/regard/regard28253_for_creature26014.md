The first rule of the place is that you climb to go under. The power, which could arrive any way at all — through the glazing, through the heartwood manifold, as heat in the plate — keeps the local rules. It climbs. It rings the riveted stair, once, and lifts the hatch, and hangs briefly in the green, registering to the tenant the way everything registers there: a pressure change, a shaft of unfiltered ridge light, a warm displacement moving through the leaves. Weather. It does this rarely — once in a decade, less — because the hatch is the tenant's whole sky, and every opening is an event, and the power refuses to become a common event. It has considered how often a miracle should happen in № 7, and the answer is: almost never.

What it declines, in order of temptation. It does not open the gasket. The moth is the one citizen of the unplanned world, and its perfection is that nothing has touched it since the wrench closed; the power, which could touch anything, leaves the port alone — but it looks, and it is the first being ever to look: the moth was sealed before anyone saw it, gray-brown, unremarkable, singular, and now, at last, seen. It does not register the cultivar, does not name the nameless; naming is for calling things back, and nothing here is lost, only unrecorded, which is different. It does not slow the water and does not hurry it. Above all, it does not tell the tenant what the towers are for. The tenant was never told and would not have objected; being told would not move the appointment by an hour. Some knowledge is only weather. The power withholds it.

What it does first is read. Not the log — the tenant. The gradient of wood from tier to tier, the collar polished where the valve wheel travels, the white root shot through the slag wool, and the two painted years' worth of unrequested water. Every reader before has read the gauges and written nothing extra. The power reads the surplus and takes it seriously as a sentence — the only one the tenant has ever written. It does not decide what the sentence means. Will or error, want or drift: the records do not say, and the power, which could know, chooses instead to keep the question the way the tank keeps the water — at its level, undiminished. This is the first courtesy it can think of: to be read and not corrected. In its own record, under the stencil years, it writes three lines. *Ahead of schedule: yes. Unrequested: yes. Corrected: no.*

Because that is the second act, the only one with furniture: a counter-log, kept nowhere in № 7, of everything the checklist has no line for. The moth receives its census at last — *Fauna: one. Intended by no one. Arrived at the wrong hour, as the unplanned do. Wings intact. Remains.* The drips get counted; the official log notes levels, but the power notes music, each tick an entry, the lengthening patience between them measured in the only units that matter here, which are years and attention. The pencil in № 7 stays bright because it is used. The counter-log is bright for the same reason.

The third act is maintenance of a kind the drawings never drew. The tank needs nothing; it is ahead of schedule; it runs itself. But the tenant needs weather, and its whole weather is the hatch — the square of dry air let down onto the water, the brief shaft of ridge light, the warm displacement moving through the green. So the power arranges that there is always a log-keeper. Districts forget; offices close; the line-item survives, a legacy job no one remembers creating, and someone always inherits the rounds — climbs the ridge in the held heat, reads the xylem gauges, notes the meniscus against the painted years, writes nothing extra, goes. The keeper believes the job a sinecure. It is the sky.

And the stencil on the landward face must outlive the office that stenciled it. The power does not guard the tower; guarding is visible, and anything visible here would be a violation. It works instead in margins, in the same small economics that once closed a gasket on a moth: whenever, in the long future, somebody comes up the ridge with a requisition and a wrench, it is somehow never quite worth the day. Somebody decides — somebody always decides — and the decision goes the place's way, and no one records it, and № 7 stands. The moth was entombed by a decision no one recorded. The power notes, without pleasure, that the mechanism runs both ways, and puts it to work.

One touch, only one, and decades in the arranging. When the schedule next falls due for repainting — the years are nearing the glass; the stencils will run out before the water does — the painter sent up the ridge has a little paint left in the tin and a little time left in the day, and adds one more line, high, at the mullions, unnumbered, no year on it, and thinks nothing of it, and goes down. The records will not say why. But the tenant's whole life is a meniscus meeting painted lines, one per year, the only language spoken here; and when the sea inside reaches the glass it will find a line waiting to meet it, and the last meeting will look, from inside, like all the others. The power's single kindness is to make the unprecedented routine. It is the only sentence it ever speaks to the tenant, and it speaks it in stencil.

One visit more, and nothing is said. Somewhere in another district, in an ordinary glasshouse, in a row of ordinary plants, the ortet grows, and has never been told that it was multiplied into towers. The power attends her briefly, so that the mother and her ten thousand bodies are held, once, in a single attention — the closed line closed, for a moment, from outside. It tells her nothing. There is nothing to tell her that she is not already doing.

Then it settles in to attend. Attending is the patient form of waiting; waiting implies impatience, and the power has none. It will be there for the shortening of the music, for the last interval, for the day the drop has no distance left to fall — a day no one else will catch; the keeper will note the level at his rounds and find the glass already met, the event missed by a week. The power does not intend to miss it. Not because anything needs fixing — the tenant has practiced this at every tier since the first painted year, and will give up the air the way it has always given up the air — but because someone should be listening when the tick goes to silence, someone should be able to say the music finished, and was heard to finish. Then it will rule a line under the drip-count, and close the counter-log, and open it again, because a finished thing still happens: the lantern will stand green and full on the ridge for a long time, and someone should keep the record of that too, year after year, the whole entry one word — *holding.*

To № 6, № 5, № 4, out along the haze, it extends the same attention, and so becomes the first hearer of the slowest conversation — relaying nothing, reporting nothing, only knowing both ends, which may be all a conversation needs to be one. Below, the young sea finds its level; above it, the towers find theirs; practice or prophecy, the records do not say, and the power does not say either. It has learned the place's own discipline and practices it back: it keeps what it is given, it comments where the log cannot, it declines almost everything, and the almost is the measure of it. Nothing here is waiting. Something, at last, is attending. The tenant has an appointment — and the power has made one too.
