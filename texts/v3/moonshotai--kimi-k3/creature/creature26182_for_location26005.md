Everything that lives here has made the same arrangement with the room: it has given up its events. Elsewhere, life is a way of spending — hungering, growing, mating, tiring, beginning, ending — and none of those currencies pass under this hill. What remains, when the spending is taken out of life, is what lives here. It is not a smaller life. It is life distilled to the one tense it was always secretly in.

Begin at the bottom, in the broth of the pool. The infusoria are not a population; a population implies generations, and there are no generations. Each one is an individual, kept at its own address: the wheel-animalcules turning their crowns like mill-wheels that grind nothing, or grind the splash — the one grist the weather ever delivers; the ciliates rowing their oar-banks against the ripple-star's standing peaks, holding station like swimmers in a current that never strengthens and never slackens, treading water forever. And somewhere in the font, mid-stride, walks the room's second animal: a water-bear, eight-legged, barrel-bodied, crossing the basin. It has been crossing for longer than the basin can remember, and it will arrive where it started, because the basin is a cupped hand and every road in it is a ring. When the drop falls, the whole broth tastes the splash. The splash is their weather and their news, the single headline, forever reprinted: the room is on.

On the brightest wall, the meadow — gold-green, the size of a hand — drinks the light and pays out sugar into the film of water that is everyone's road. It grows the way the drop swells: to one size, and no further, forever. Its cells divide only to replace themselves. Birth here is a form of maintenance; the meadow is mowed by its own metabolism at exactly the rate it grows, a lawn kept by the act of being a lawn.

The seven fronds at the seep-line are seven wagers. One is all midrib, a single green blade, as though grass had decided to stop being many and be one. One forks by twos and twos, the oldest architecture of stems, a family tree that never got its grandchildren. One is a single cell swollen to the size of a thumb — a grape of green — proof that a plant can be one cell if the world agrees never to jostle it. One is pale as the stone it feeds on, the ghost-frond, the one that bet on minerals when everything else bet on light. One stores its sunlight as a bead of sugar at its tip, always swelling, never falling — a drop of harvest to mirror the ceiling's drop of weather. One is the fiddlehead, always about to unfurl. And one carries a bud: the room's only flower, folded, and it has been folded for longer than some flowers have existed. Above ground, a seed-line gambles a thousand forms and keeps the winners. The room kept all the tickets. The seep-line is a nursery where nothing is raised and everything is about to be.

In the least bright corner — still bright as morning — the ivory felt does death's work in a deathless town. It eats what has been let go: the sloughed cell, the spent sugar, the shed skin. It is why the room does not fill up with its own exfoliation, why the shell-snow on the floor stays at its constant depth. A gravedigger with no graves, kept fully employed by housekeeping.

Below the waterline of the light, the reef-felt is the loudest listener in the world. Its feathered mouths face the sound the way sunflowers face a sun — except its sun is a chord and never moves, so the felt has stopped turning and become a fixture of turning. Whether it is one animal or ten thousand is not a question the felt answers; its members share the pulse, share the crystal, share the work. It eats the song and pays out the wall, and the wall carries the song the better for it: the choir is building its own throat, note by deposited note. And the things that look made are the felt's other secretions, or the seep's, built the way coral builds, without a builder's opinion. The urns rise the way kidney stones rise. The lattice dam is an argument between flow and stone that agreed to stop mid-sentence. The spiral is the felt's diary, ring after ring recording, in perfect penmanship, that nothing happened — the one text in the world whose entire content is the fact of being written, legible in principle, read by the water, read by no one.

The bell-crickets are seated by physics, each at the node where its partial of the chord stands loudest in the air, arranged by size like organ pipes: the bass one low against the floor, the small high voices up under the dome's crown. Through their glass bellies you could watch their last meal — one gold cell of meadow — being digested across what, above, would be a century. They crop the meadow at precisely the rate it grows; the meadow is always being eaten and is always the same size. Above ground, their kind sings to court, to claim, to count the nights; here the song has forgotten its errands and become load-bearing. And the one that is molting has been half-born longer than most creatures get to be entirely alive. It is not trapped. A threshold is only a trap to things that travel. It sings from both skins at once, and the difference is too small to hear.

Why carry a lamp into the brightest room in the world? Because the lamp-midges' orange is not for seeing and not for being seen. It is the one frequency the felt cannot eat, burned off as light — the household's only smoke, and it is beautiful. They are the room's only travelers, each flying a closed orbit, a pilgrimage that returns hourly to where it never left, each midge tuned to its own harmonic, so that the swarm is the chord made visible: if you could see the geography of the sound, it would look like this constellation. They never blink, because blinking is a clock. Whether the constellation moves is a question you would have to watch forever to answer, and the room has forever, and declines to answer.

And in the pool, at the center of the cymatic flower, lies the oldest animal — the softest citizen of a room of teeth. It rests where the drop falls; the star of ripples dances around it like a fixed halo, and each splash arrives on the water directly above its spine, the room's most intimate transaction, a touch at the interval of a heartbeat. Its heart beats once per drop-swell, and the pulse under everything — the one so low it might be your own body — is this heart, conducted through water into pavement into reef, so that the walls themselves are fed, in part, by the beating. The hill keeps the room the way a body keeps a heartbeat, and the room keeps the animal the same way. Hearts within hearts. If the oldest animal has a name, the name is the pulse; the room has been saying it without ceasing since before names. Its biography is one sentence long: it is here. The open urn on the shelf is not evidence that it hatched; nothing hatched; the urn has simply been open so long that openness is its contents. The four sealed urns hold eggs always about to hatch — its siblings, perhaps, conjugated forever in the subjunctive. It is the only indicative of its kind. Once an age, if it swims its one perfect circle, it comes around to face the seam shaped like a door, and is oriented toward it the way a compass is oriented — "faces" being only a way of speaking; it has no eyes — and the door is patient as a thing that has already arrived, and nothing opens, and nothing needs to.

If the room is a chapel — and it passes for one, dome, font, niche, urns — then it is fully staffed and has no staff. The felt is the mason. The crickets are the choir, holding their almost. The midges are the lamplighters, though the light was lit before they were. The ivory felt is the sexton. The urns are reliquaries, keeping futures the way a seed keeps a tree. The oldest animal is the congregation: one parishioner, eyeless, attending a liturgy with no officiant and no end, a service one note short of amen.

That missing note is the household's only appetite. Everything else is satiated by definition, but the chord leans, and everything alive here leans infinitesimally with it — the felt's infinitesimal growth, the gap in the midges' constellation where that frequency would fly, the folded bud, the paused molt — the whole citizenry oriented toward a resolution it will never take, the way a vine is oriented toward light. The niche is swept by a null in the standing wave, the one pocket of silence under the hill, and the silence inside it is shaped exactly like the missing tone. Three kept vacancies, in stone, in silk, in sound — fed nothing, swept by everything, tended the way hearths are tended.

Or perhaps that is the wrong way to say it. Perhaps there are not many lives here but one, wearing many shapes the way a body wears organs: the meadow its eye, drinking the light; the felt its ear, eating the sound; the pool its skin, feeling the weather it makes for itself; the oldest animal its heart. The crystal vein that climbs to the ridge is its single gill-filament, redless, cold, one thread of connection to the world, and through it the room exhales its chord into the winter nights the way a sleeper exhales. On the stillest nights a walker stops, and touches an ear, and blames the cold. Below, the drop swells to its one size. The choir holds its almost. The heart beats when the drop falls, or the drop falls when the heart beats; the room does not distinguish. Everyone is home who has ever been home. No one has ever arrived, and no one has ever left, and the population has never changed by so much as one midge — and it is, and has always been, the most inhabited place in the world.
