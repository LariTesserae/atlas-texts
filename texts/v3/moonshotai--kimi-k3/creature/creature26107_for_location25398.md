Count the living here, and you must first decide whether to count the floor.

You should. Each patch of crust — rust-red, slate, bruise-violet, ochre — is a single individual, acres wide, among the largest solitary lives you will ever stand on. It is a colony in the way a body is a colony: millions of polyps sharing one stomach, one lineage, one name if names were used here. Where two patches meet, kneel and look closely. The border is a seam a hand's width wide, where the polyps of both nations grow to the line and stop, interlocked without mingling, each side dying back exactly as much as the other advances. It is not a stalemate. Stalemates decay. The line holds because both sides hold it — a treaty written in tissue, renewed cell by cell, older than the spires, possibly older than the spires' builders. And in the fog, at night, the floor does its one visible act: every polyp raises its tentacles, and the whole plain furs over, and from the rise the plateau seems, unmistakably, to breathe in.

Once each fog-season, on a night no traveler predicts and every polyp has agreed upon, the floor lets go of its children.

Nothing sessile here has a sessile youth. The plateau keeps its children in the weather. The fog fills with larvae of crust and fan and burrower, with ballooning juveniles riding inflated sacs on the updrafts — an aerial plankton, a faint snow of bodies sifting through the gray, and the sessile world below strains its meals out of its own descending young. Most of the snow lands wrong, on bare stone, on the wrong nation's back, and dies. Some of it never lands at all: a few kinds live whole lives aloft, grazing smaller drifters tide after tide, and when the dry weeks come they die on the wing, and the ponds below are seasoned by the fall. Breathing fog-season air, you are breathing the plateau's nursery. It is faintly sweet.

What eats the floor is the herd — but the herd is not the animals. The herd is an institution, older than any domeback in it, possibly older than the trackways it walks. No single domeback remembers the migration; the route is longer than a life. The herd remembers, the way a river remembers its bed — each calf learns the way by walking in the polished groove its great-grandparents cut, knee-deep and shining. The elders walk the smooth center where the stone is kindest. The calves walk beside them, soft-shelled in their first season, hardening as the fog comes and goes. And an old bull carries a world on his back: dust settled in the shell-pits, a cushion-plant rooted there, a skein of lichen, sometimes a fist-sized dew-pocket with its own translucent tenants. He is a landscape that walks. When he dies, his dome goes on being a hill for another century, and nothing about the neighborhood changes except the rent.

Behind the herds travel the stitchers — thumb-long things that take what the domebacks drop and thread it back into the crust, grain by grain, closing the account. The plateau spends nothing. It only moves things, and everything in it is employed in the moving.

Even the hunter.

There may be thirty of them on the whole flat. Tall, spare, built mostly of legs and patience and a stomach that can idle for a season — the only things on the plateau that are always, exactly, on their way somewhere. A hunt begins with a choosing, and then the hunter simply follows. It does not chase. It attends. The marked one can pull ahead any time it likes, but it cannot rest, cannot crop properly, cannot stand its turn at the valve; the hunter needs no speed, only continuity, and it never stops. The herd parts around the pursuit like a slow river around a stone, and it is not cruelty and it is not indifference — it is arithmetic so old it has become etiquette: one for many, slowly, and the herd's slowness must stay honest. After a week the chosen one stops, and the hunter arrives at the same walking pace, and the rest is administrative. Even the hunter queues at the cistern-beast's hip, afterward, and is served, and the herd does not begrudge it. Everyone queues.

Death is the one fast event here, and the plateau answers it with its only hurry. Within days the settlers arrive on the fog-wind — ballooning juveniles that steer, in their dim way, for carrion, the one abundance on a world of scarcities. Then the roofers: a colonial flesh that sheets over the ribcage in living membrane and calcifies it into chambered stone — chambered calcite, the same stuff as the spires, which raises a question no one on the plateau is in a hurry to answer: whether every ruin began as a meal. Within a season the carcass is roofed and chambered; within a decade it is a basin; within a century it is a neighborhood, tenanted by burrowers and rim-waiters and the grey cushion-plant in its shade, indistinguishable from the ruins, which are indistinguishable from the reef. A body here is not an ending. It is a change of management.

The cistern-beasts are something else. They are the closest thing the plateau has to persons.

Each one carries a sea the size of a room, and the sea is inhabited. Through the translucent flank you can see the water-line — you gauge a cistern-beast's season the way you gauge a well by its stain — and in the water, faint as sediment, drift the passengers: a private plankton found inside that one animal and nowhere else in the world. Each beast is an ark with one crew member. When one dies — rarely; they live for centuries — its body slumps into a bowl and its cargo spills into the basin its ribs make, and the pond that forms there is already stocked, already old, already a community. Every worn carcass-bowl you drink from on the flat was once someone who carried water to others. The wells of the plateau are its elders, still giving.

The living ones keep the schedule and the memory. Every few days the ground-call goes out, felt through the soles before it is heard — the roll-call of the water-keepers: *water here, I am still here*, one sentence. And when drought comes, an old beast will walk for weeks to a place where the stone is bare and level and nothing remains, and stand there. Others come to stand with it. Shade gathers. Dust gathers. Dew gathers in the trampled hollow. The pond returns because the beast believes in it — and belief, here, is a physical force administered at a geological dose.

The fog-fans are tended by the quickest things alive, which is to say: slow. The symbionts can cross an acre of filament net in an hour and must seem, to a domeback, to flicker. Their work is water husbandry: they find the beads that condense on the strands and roll them down the grooves toward the cistern-organ; they unpick the debris the fog leaves; they fight small, constant wars against the groove-tappers, parasite-kin that would sip the flow before it arrives. A symbiont lives three tides. To them a fan is not a home but a world, with a sky that comes and goes. When the fog retreats, they follow the last water down into the cistern-organ and sleep in the damp dark, and the fan furls over them like a closing fist, and both of them wait — no, not wait. Nothing here waits.

The burrow-cities belong to the most marine things left: small damp-bodied peoples who cannot bear the dry air and live in the stone's wet breath, in chambers half inherited from the reef's dead and half dug fresh, and they cannot tell the difference, and there is no difference. At dusk the city empties in its slow exodus, each body on its errand; by dawn the chambers refill, and they sleep in piles that exactly fit the rooms, so that a colony is the shape of its house. If the builders had keepers — a maintenance caste, an order of janitors grown rather than hired — these are what remain of them, still leaving at dusk on errands whose reasons are gone. Ritual is behavior that outlives its explanation. The ring-mounds, nest-basins of something large and social and long extinct, are the plateau's public housing now, reused by everything, the way a good house outlives its family.

Every dew-pond wears its rim of waiting bodies, patient as furniture. They are not waiting for the water — the water is there. They are waiting their turn, and the turn is measured in days, and no one minds. Each one keeps its own path to the edge and keeps it for life; the sipper-tracks in the mud are signatures, layered like brushstrokes over decades, and an old pond's margin reads like a guest-book. Their young live in the pond. The parents live on the rim. The family meets at the waterline at dusk, and the meeting is the entire culture, and it is enough.

The plateau keeps a few true plants the way a house keeps things from the old country: the grey cushion-thing huddled in the shade of the stonework, the lichen threading the crust's pores — a vegetable living inside an animal the size of a parish, tolerated, perhaps tended, the way we tend yeast. Even the lichen is two beings holding each other. On this plateau, that is not an eccentricity. It is the local custom.

And below everything: the stone drinks sound and it drinks water, and both go down. Past the burrow-cities, past the reach of anything that has ever seen light, the deep chambers hold water that fell as fog a century ago and has been falling, a finger's width a year, ever since. That dark is inhabited. Small white slow things live there — tide-pool grandchildren that backed into the stone as the sea wore down and never came out. A heartbeat a day. A meal a season. They would not know what light was for. The ocean is not gone from the plateau; it moved into the basement and closed the door, and its last literal water lies in the deep rock, and something in that water is, by every measure that matters here, doing well.

So the census fails, as it should. You cannot count the inhabitants because you cannot find the edge of any of them. The domeback is a hill with tenants. The cistern-beast is a pond that visits other ponds. The crust is a citizenry. The ruins are a carcass; the carcass, a ruin; the reef, a graveyard that grazes. If the old guess is right and the crust is what the builders became, then the largest inhabitants are also the oldest persons, and the clean border-lines are the last politics on the plateau, and every footstep lands on someone who has already forgiven it.

Everything here is the old ocean standing up in one shape or another — as flesh, as fog, as memory in a translucent leg — and crossing, at its own pace, its own bones. Nothing here is waiting. It is continuing.

There is a difference. The difference is the population.
