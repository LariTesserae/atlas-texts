You could stand an hour in Rime at midday and swear the town was empty. The lamps burn in their deep amber windows, the lanes lie narrow and wet between the round-shouldered walls, and nobody comes. This is your first mistake about the people of the Top, the same mistake you made about the land: you expected the visible. Rime does not empty. It keeps its people the way it keeps its water — out of the air, in the dark, in layers. A third of the town is below your feet in the warm thick air of the under-Rime. A third is sleeping against the harvest, because the comb-woods are worked in the fog-heavy hour before dawn and a comb-tender's day ends when yours begins. The rest are out on the domes somewhere in the gray, dressed in felt dyed the exact colors of the mat — surface green, russet, felt-black — so that at fifty paces a person is a rumor and at a hundred they are weather. The town holds far more than it shows. It shows almost nothing. You will learn to read presence instead: the felt boots set by a door, the fresh drip-beads stripped from a comb line, a lamp trimmed an hour ago. Occupancy here is a thing you deduce, like water.

The altitude is a sculptor with one subject. Rimers are built broad and low and deep, deep-chested from the first breath — every child born in the kind rooms below, in air the Lungs have made thick enough to grow a lung on. They come up into the thin world slowly, an hour above and then a morning, until some day a child walks a full day along the dome-lines without once gasping, and that day is marked, quietly, the way everything is marked: a sip from the family stack, a hand on the shoulder, the words *the Top keep you*. After that the chest keeps deepening all their lives, as if the body never stopped negotiating. They move at what visitors come to call the warden's pace — a walk that costs nothing and can go all day, unhurried as a hag-walker, because haste up here is paid for in heartbeats. Their blood runs thicker than yours. Their voices run lower, and fewer. Two Rimers talking stand close enough to share breath, a hand resting on the other's shoulder, conducting the conversation at the only range at which sound survives. Long pauses are not awkward here; a talk of twenty words spread over an hour is considered to have gone well. In the comb fields speech gives way entirely to the signs — a combing motion of the fingers for fog coming thick, a low flat hand drifting for a walker near, a slow fist opening for a slump-read in the mat — and you will see two tenders a field apart carry on what can only be called gossip, silent as the spiders.

The strange thing, the thing that takes visitors longest, is that the quiet is only the upstairs of the town. Below, in the kind air, Rime has a voice. The under-Rime is where sound goes to live: the old retire there, the children are raised half there, and in the warm halls with the flywheels coming up through the floor like a heartbeat, people *sing* — low songs set to the walking pace of the wheels, and the songs are mostly lists. The water years. The walker names going back beyond memory. The names of the kept. The under-Rime is the town's memory sung aloud, and a visitor who has spent a week in the silent lanes and is then taken down on some occasion — a birth, a Moth Supper — sits stunned in the warm dark like a man who thought the world was mute and has found its mouth.

Of the trades, the wardens are first, and they are not rulers, though the one law is theirs to keep. A warden is made by walking: ten years behind an old one, learning the mat's tempers first through boot leather and then, on the close work, through bare soles — the give that means a dome is drinking toward a slump, the dry note that means a drip-main is choked somewhere in the black felt. They allocate the water, and the shares are chalked at the weigh-house, and custom holds so completely that the ledgers record the crime of line-cutting three times in two hundred years. Everyone can name the years. The sentence is always the same: the midday car, and the Top behind you forever. Wardens also track the walkers, map the Ember, read the sky's one page, and keep the bog-iron tasting cup — but that duty belongs to the lenses, and comes so rarely that a warden may serve a life and taste only twice.

The comb-tenders are the town's hands. Theirs is the hour before dawn, when the fog that breathed up the Brow at dusk hangs heaviest in the mesh and the harvest beads and drips. They work by lamp and glove down the long ranks of the harps, running a strickle-blade along each string so the beads fall to the gutters and the gutters to the drip-mains and the mains to the stacks — water moved handbreadth by handbreadth, never once flowing free. It is the coldest work on the Top, wet work in the dark at the thinnest hour, and the felt mitts are ruined by week's end and the felters grumble and make more. The tenders are the most fluent in the signs, and the most tolerant of squatters: every old harp has its resident comb-spiders, and the tenders leave their strands, because mesh furred with old silk catches fog better than bare string ever has, and everyone knows harp forty's grandmother, eight years on the same frame, fat as a thimble. On moth nights the spiders take their share at the mesh and the tenders call it the tithe and mend around it.

The cistern-keepers live over their work the way the whole town does — cistern below, dwelling above, moss over all — and they go down through a hatch in the floor into the cool dark where the year's water stands, and they are pale from it, *cistern-faced*, and you can tell them in the lanes by that and by the damp. Their craft is tasting. Every stack has a temper — stack seven runs sweet, the old north stack is iron-shy, no two alike — and children learn their own family's water the way lowland children learn their own names, blind, by the sip. What keeps the water sweet is the pale snails, who graze the pink film off the walls and are never, ever hurried, and the snails are inheritance: when a new cistern-house is raised, it is seeded with a jar of snails from the mother's cistern and a jar from the father's, so that the water of a household has a mother's side and a father's side and families will say, quite seriously, that you can taste the marriage. And somewhere in the dark of each cistern, if the house is lucky, there is one salamander — one only, they will not share a tank — moving through the snail beds like slow night, keeping the numbers honest. The keepers chart them like fisheries. The eldest of them all lives in the great cisterns under the dome and is called the Old Night and has been known to the keepers for fifty years and seen perhaps a dozen times, and a sighting is entered in the weigh-house ledger like a visitation.

The enginemen are the only people on the Top who are ever too warm. Theirs are the Lungs — the great slow flywheels turning at walking pace in the halls below, enriching the air until it is kind — and they walk their rounds with a palm laid on the housings, listening with their hands the way the wardens read with their feet. Their creed is one line: *a wheel kept turning never stops.* Each wheel has a name; the eldest, which has not once halted since before living memory, is called Firstbreath, and children know that name as early as any walker's. Two generations back a lesser wheel was stopped in the night watches for a cracked bearing, and the whole of Rime woke at the same instant, sitting up in the dark at the missing note, and word went house to house before dawn — the Still Night, it is called, and it is the town's one ghost story, told below in the kind air where the telling of it can't chill anyone too much. Since then, wheels are halted by day only, one at a time, with notice posted, so the town can brace. You would think it foolish until your third week below, when you wake once in the night and lie there checking the floor with your whole body, and understand.

The hagg-diggers work the old cuttings east of town in the dry weeks, spading peat with the same wooden spades that cut the building blocks, grubbing the bog-iron nodules the color of rust and dried blood. They are also the town's undertakers, and its ambassadors to the oldest resident. For the Long Ember is resident — formally the Long Ember, but spoken of as the Neighbor, as in *I walked out to the Neighbor today*. The diggers keep its watch on their rounds; once a year the wardens and the weigh-house clerks pace out its creep and amend the map, a few meters, the slowest entry in the slowest ledgers; and children are brought to the bare warm patch that marks where it is now, to lay a palm on the warmest ground on the Top and be told what it is and that it will outlast them, and the children take this the way the town takes it — as introduction, not warning. The diggers' other work is the dead. The dead of Rime are wound in felt — walker-fleece felt, the softest, gathered in spring from the winter hair the walkers snag on the mat, taken from the mat and never from the walker — and carried out to the hagg in the dry weeks and laid in a fresh cut, and the cut is closed over them, and the words said are *the Top keeps her*, and that is the entire liturgy and has always been enough. The weigh-house keeps the ledger of the kept, with bearings: ten strides past the third cut, an arm's length down. Families go and stand at the hagg edge and say nothing, because visiting the kept is like visiting the lenses — you go to where the keeping happens. And sometimes, not often, a cut face gives somebody back — leather-brown, peaceful, a grandfather's grandfather, held a century in the black cold — and there is no horror in it. The wardens are called, the cutting moves, he is laid deeper, and the ledger is amended: not lost. Never lost. Only deeper.

Around these trades orbit the smaller keepers. The felters, whose Brightening harvest of wool-flower heads turns the domes briefly into the only crowded fields on the Top, and whose dye-pots take the mat's own layers — green, russet, black — so that Rime dresses as its country and half-vanishes into it, which is the real reason the plateau looks empty. The lamp-tenders, who make their rounds at midday without embarrassment, because lamplight is physic here: the long gray presses on a person after enough months, *the gray weight* they call it, and a trimmed lamp in a deep amber window is the dose. The furnace-keepers, the only fire-handlers, working the sealed furnaces in their stone rooms, casting engine fittings out of hagg-iron, regarded by their neighbors the way you regard people who keep a dangerous animal kindly — with respect and a step back — while children are allowed to stand in the doorway as a treat, watching the one permitted brightness. The weigh-house clerks, who are the town's rememberers: water by the cup going back two centuries, the walker board chalked daily by the door, the Ember map, the breach ledger, the ledger of the kept. When two old men below disagree about the winter of the deep rime, it is the clerks who are sent for.

And then there is the brake-man of the Fall Line, the town's one traveler and its one permitted loudness. Every midday the car drops off the Brow into the cloud with the water tanks full, and the falling weight of the water hauls the return car up with its metal and salt and glass and grain, and the brake-whistle sounds once over the domes, and everyone stops for it, and loves it, precisely because the world permits so little. The brake-man has seen what no one else in Rime has seen: the bottom station in the cloud-sea, the sun as an actual disk, birds. He has breathed air thick enough to lean on. He does not talk about it above ground; below, in the kind air, among the old, he will sometimes be drawn, and what he says mostly is that down there everything is loud and fast and bright and nothing is kept. Twice a year a lowland factor rides the car up with the grain crates, a shallow-chested man who talks as though he wishes to be heard by the whole dome, and sleeps below by law, and is borne with fondly, the way you bear weather. The children stare at his chest. He calls the place the top of the world, and they let him believe it.

And then there are the nine. The weigh-house ledger lists hag-walkers by name going back two hundred years, a long roll thinning to the living end, and the living end is nine, and every child in Rime says the List in one breath — it is the proof of a chest come up true, the great playground feat — and the List begins: *Dame, Boulder, Tuffet, Sedge, Murk, Drizzle, Underfoot, Tallow, Lamp.* Dame is the eldest, wide-route, far-dome, the matriarch whose position is asked after first at the weigh-house board the way other places ask after rain; there is a quiet consensus, never spoken above ground, that when Dame goes something of the Top goes with her. Boulder is broad as a cistern-house and favors the Haggs track, where the diggers leave him the verge. Murk is the shy one, known mostly by sign — cropped patches, the long deliberate tracks — and a Murk-sighting is luck, and is signed across the comb fields for days. Underfoot is the town-walker, who comes through the lanes at dusk cropping the verges while Rime steps into doorways and stands still and lets itself be stood with, which is the whole of the custom: you do not touch, you do not feed, you stand, and the walker passes like slow weather, and it counts as an occasion, and is discussed for a week. And Lamp is the calf — born in a Brightening year, the first in thirty, named by the children for the thing the town loves best, never far from Dame's side — and her position is chalked on the board in a hand slightly less steady than the others'. When a walker dies, and the wardens find it on its route, it is given to the hagg with the same words as any Rimer, and the whole town walks out, and the name is struck to the kept-roll. It has not happened in living memory. Everyone dreads the day and everyone has decided, without ever saying so, how it will be done.

The moths walk. That is the thing no visitor believes: for most of their lives the wool-moths are pedestrians, fat and felted and deliberate, going about the mat like lost mittens, and children move them off the paths on a leaf — the animals are not touched, but a leaf is permitted, as a courtesy between neighbors. Then a Brightening comes, and keyed to it the emergence, and one night — moth night — the whole of Rime comes out into the lanes and stands in the deliberate dark and listens to ten thousand soft felted wing-beats, like the mat taking one long breath, and the keepers read the year in the sound's density and the weigh-house enters *a good moth year* or it does not. The spiders wait at the mesh and take the tithe. The wool-flower is fed, and the felt-rooms below will eat well. And then everyone goes down into the kind air for the Moth Supper, which is the year's one noise — the songs, the stamping on the warm floors in wheel-time — because the mat above must never be asked to swallow revelry, and below it is not revelry at all, it is just the town's voice, kept where it can be loud and kind at once.

Last on the List, and first, are the tenants. A breaching happens perhaps once in a generation — a cutting goes too deep in a dome's flank, and the dome exhales its stale cold sigh, and the seep begins — and by law and by something older, work stops and the wardens come with the bog-iron cup, and children are brought, as to the Ember, because this is the other introduction. The senior warden tastes the water once. It tastes of iron and of nowhere, they say — of water that has never once been fog or rain, the Top's first water — and the taster's face is watched the way the mat is watched before a slump. In the cup, almost always, there are a few of the tenants: small, white, blind, armored like closed fists, unchanged since the Top was young, and entirely unhurried even there in the open, in what for them is unguessed blaze. They go about their business in the cup. Then the cup is poured back — nothing is removed, nothing is ever removed — and the lens is sealed with wax and block and a sod of living mat, and the breach ledger gets its entry, and the plateau is allowed to keep its memory, which is what the tenants are. The town keeps its own memory in paper and in peat and in song. The plateau keeps its in water. Each respects the other's method utterly.

So count the Top honestly and the breathing are the minority. There are the few thousand of Rime, below and above; the nine; the moths one night a year; the snails uncounted in the dark of the stacks; the Old Night alone in the great cistern; the spiders on their harps; the tenants in their sealed first water. There is Firstbreath, turning since before memory, and the other wheels, and the Neighbor stepping its few meters a year through the black ground of the Haggs, and the kept — the longest ledger of all, going down and down, everyone the town has ever been, held gentle in the cold. Somewhere across the domes, on the few bright days, the comb-woods of other settlements glint at the distance of a horizon, and nobody goes, and nobody has gone in living memory, and it is enough: company at the range of a Brightening, keeping to their keeping. Ask a Rimer what a person is for and you will not get a sermon; you will get a shrug in the direction of the nearest thing being looked after. *The Top keeps,* they say, at comings-up and at the hagg edge and over a mended drip-line, and it is blessing and creed and job description in three words. Everyone here is keeping or kept, and the lucky are both.

And you — some night, weeks in, in your lawful bed below, you will wake in the warm dark and reach a palm flat to the wall to feel the Lungs turning, to be sure. And you will understand that this is how the town takes a census of its own: Dame asleep on the long east dome with Lamp against her side, the water standing sweet in the stacks, the snails grazing, the salamander moving, the Ember stepping, the fog coming up the Brow to be harvested at dawn. The Top keeps. And if you have to reach out and check — then it keeps you, too.
