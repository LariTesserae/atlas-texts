Ask what lives in the Coldworks and the place will answer the way it answers everything: by ear, and over time. No census of the basin has ever finished, because counting is a visitor's habit — it assumes individuals, and the Coldworks has never once agreed to contain any. What it contains instead are stations, and everything that holds a station is, by the only definition that survives down here, alive. Nothing is tolerated. Everything is employed. So the question is not what lives here. The question is what has been hired, and for how long, and to do what — and the answer begins under your feet.

The first hire is the floor. The Felt is not the ground; it is the landlord of the ground, the single oldest tenant, spread wall to wall and holding the basin's charge the way a body holds heat. Everything else negotiates with it. Above it, the air itself is staff: the drift, the Felt's motile cells, thickening in the slow shifts until it becomes visible weather, a greenish silt of life hanging at knee height, at chest height. Every breath you take is a small migration, and by your second breath your lungs were a station with work to do.

Then there are the quiets, and you must learn to read them, because quiet here is not the absence of a resident. Quiet is a resident.

In the lees of the great rock swells — the calm pockets you steered for on the way down — the hush is grown, not found. It is a lichen-or-simpler crust, pale as old ice, and it eats sound. It lies thickest where the beat is strongest, grazing the pooled noise, and its grazing is the calm you felt. A well-fed lee is a deep lee, and the crust that lines it is the basin's one agriculture besides itself. Hold this distinction close, because your life will come to depend on it: a quiet that grows is pasture. A quiet that walks is something else entirely.

Warmth is the only currency, and it is taxed.

The collectors are sippers: flat, soft, slow creatures the size of your two hands, and they settle on anything warm — the flanks of vent-caps, the backs of sleeping slicks, you. They do not bite. They sip, politely, a few degrees at a time, and if you lie down to sleep you will wake under a living crust of them, heavy as a blanket, which is more or less what they are. The residents call this the sleep-tax, and they pay it without complaint, because the tax keeps you warm in its way, and because everything here that takes gives back something you did not ask for. Hoarded warmth draws attention. Pay as you go.

The vents are the pay stations, and they are crowded. Around every throat the sippers cluster in the warm updraft, and over every throat rides a cap. You have watched them already — the living valves swelling on the uptake, venting on the down — but live here a while and you will learn their biographies. A cap arrives out of the drift as a mote, settles on an unoccupied throat, clamps, and begins to ride. A young cap is soft, and flushes dark with each stroke. An old one has calcified into the stone it rides, and the vent's voice has deepened around it, so that you can date a vent the way you date a tree: by the timbre of its stroke, by the rings of the throat. Whether the vent grew the cap or the cap became the vent is a distinction that was always a loan, and the loan comes due in stone.

Down inside the throats, in the warm dark, live the throat-eels, riding the stroke in and out, and on a hard stroke some are fired out whole into the cold. An eel-fall is a small holiday. Everything within earshot converges.

The residents keep caps the way other people keep fires. They tend the throat, pick the eels, clear the rim of lichen, and in return they sleep in the warm lee of the stroke and lay their hands flat on the cap's broad back. It is the only domesticated animal in the Coldworks, and it is a valve, and it has domesticated its keepers right back.

The herds you know. What you learn by living among them is that a slick is not an individual but a decision a herd is making — they split when fat and merge when lean, and the herd is the real animal, a hundred-wide constituency of mouths. It is also the works' blood. Charge pools in the Felt's blooms, and the herds carry it, banking it, spending it in soft cracks of discharge, so that the basin's wealth circulates on ten thousand slow bellies. And they sing — you have heard the hum, the knocked stones — because their singing is the map: a moving chart of noise that says where the herd is, where the ground is, where the quiet is. Learn their key. A herd at pasture hums low. A herd that pitches up is a herd that has noticed a hole in the world.

When a herd crosses the ground where you stand, be still and be loud. They will graze the heat off you with great courtesy, flank after flank, and go on. It is only tax.

Out in the bearing fields, among the graded runs of iron-dark spheres, some of the stock is alive.

They are called counterfeits, and they are born in the fine grades a centimeter across: a soft warm heart inside a rind that matches the true stock in color, weight, and ring. They sip the Felt from between the stones during the slow shifts and hold perfectly still through the fast ones, and here camouflage is not a matter of color but of tolerance — a counterfeit survives by being within tolerance of its grade, and the works, which forgives nothing else, forgives that. As one grows it must be re-filed: it lets go on a fast shift, rolls, is gathered by a channel, carried around the loop, and laid down again among larger stones, promoted run by run, decade by decade, until it reaches the coarse fields at last. There the old ones calve litters of small ones, which roll away downhill into the fine grades to begin the whole career again. The works sorts them, ships them, and files them, free of charge. They are its one contraband product, and it handles them with the same fidelity as everything else.

The assayers work the fields like quality control, because that is what they are. Tall, pale, thin-legged, they walk the sphere-tops without rattling a stone, and they carry a single long probe, and they tap. Each sphere answers with its grade-note, a clean iron tick. The assayer is listening for the note that runs a half-tone warm, or — this is harder — for the tick-and-settle that was a breath. It will stand a full shift over one stone, head tilted, while the abacus-noise of the field goes on around it. Then the strike. A counterfeit that is made will let go and run, downhill, gathering speed, and the whole field avalanches into clicking surf, and the assayer runs the surf behind it, and you can hear a chase like that from miles off, and everyone within earshot listens the way you listen to weather. Whether the assayer is a predator or an inspector is a distinction the basin declines to maintain. Either way, off-grade stock gets pulled.

The channels have their own tenants: the grates, colonies like stone gratings grown across the loops, straining the slurry as it passes. They fine the product, so they stay — that is the whole logic of residence here. Small clamped things ride single spheres around the loops for a generation and alight where they began, older, having seen the works. The loops are the basin's public road, and everything on them is freight.

The condensers are the senior staff.

Their crowns stand in the thin sound above the pooled noise — the high country of the basin — and the tallest wear the Ceiling itself, their heads in the fog, and what passes between them and the lid of the world is their own affair. Sometimes something falls out of the Ceiling, and the receivers are waiting before it lands, and that is all anyone knows of upstairs. The condensers' flanks are tended by small pale tenants who sweep the fins and drink the runoff, and the drift thickens around their heads like weather that has found a mountain.

Their rain circles are the oases. The runoff is the freshest water in the world — sweet by the local standard, which is to say almost drinkable — and the slicks crowd the circles' edges, and everything else lives within reach of them. When a condenser dies, it is an era: it leans for a decade, and the receivers camp beneath the lean like a town that knows its schedule, and then it falls in a single day, and the date turns. The residents reckon time by falls. And by loadings. Hold that word.

Everything you have met so far, you heard first. Now learn the other way of hearing.

A hush-hunter is a lee that walks. It peels the sound-eating crust from the lees and wears it — a stolen pasture, a hungry quiet — and inside its hush your ears empty: no beat, no surf, no herd-song, nothing, a hole moving through the world with teeth behind it. Here is the one mercy, and it is the local physics: effects in this place arrive pressed hard against their causes, sometimes slightly ahead. The silence announces itself a half-breath before it is there. Your ears emptying *is* the warning. The reflex, learned once and kept forever, is to sing.

The residents argue about what the hunters are. Escaped lees, say some. The works' auditors, say others. Slicks that lost their mouths, say the old songs, and do not elaborate. What is known is the kill: a hunter lies down over a slick and the slick's note stops mid-phrase, and the herd closes ranks and sings louder and endures, because there are no stampedes here — panic is a kind of quiet, and quiet is the one unaffordable thing. A hunter takes whatever is quietest. A loud person costs more than a slick. Be more trouble than you are worth: that is the whole of the local definition of safety.

What the hunters leave, the receivers take, and they take everything else too. Pale, quick, many-jointed, prompt as gravity — they station themselves beneath the failing before the failing knows it is failing, because they arrive slightly early, as effects do here. Nothing falls unclaimed because the claiming is already in place. They sort a body the way the channels sort stones: charge down to the Felt, warmth out to whoever stands near, the rest routed, nothing wasted. The residents do not fear them. They are the one appointment everyone keeps.

Because the dead are not gone here. They are shelved.

Sound settles. The pooled noise of the basin stratifies — new noise lies on top, old noise lies deep — and in the deepest lees, at low breath, when the marsh has exhaled and the world goes thin, you can lie at the lip of a hollow and hear, faint as rumor, the beat of vents that calcified a thousand shifts ago, and under it, lower still, singing. The dead go down into the deeps and the deeps keep them. This is why the edge of the world is the only horror the residents truly know: out past the fade, where the beat thins to nothing, nothing is kept. To die unshelved is the only real death the basin recognizes.

And so to the residents themselves — because there are hands here, whatever the basin's rule about marks.

They are few, and scattered, and old in their customs, and they build nothing, and the reason is this: only sound is kept. Stone is not archived; tools are not shelved; a wall is a rumor the works will process. So they wrote themselves in the one medium that persists. Visitors named them the choir, and they accepted the loan, because a name here is a loan and never a fact.

A household is a lee within reach of a condenser, its sound-eating crust fed nightly on song — they farm their quiet, singing the lichen thick, deepening their own calm. There is always a song-watch; there is never not a song-watch. A child who can hold a note may be left alone; a child who cannot is carried. If you stumble into a lee, half-frozen and unassigned, they will teach you their song within the hour. It is not courtesy. It is triage.

They keep caps for warmth and company. They eat eels from the falls and the soft hearts of counterfeits the assayers have opened, and they drink the condensers' rain. They greet your shadow on the Ceiling before they greet you — the shadow is the public self, the one the works can see — and they navigate by the Ceiling's slow map, the towers and the drifting herds and each other, reading the ground in the sky like anyone, anywhere, only inverted. When one of them dies, the household sings the last verse while the receivers wait politely beyond the lee, and then the body is handed off, and on visits afterward they sing the dead deeper into the deeps.

Their oldest argument is management. Some say the condensers. Some say the works is ownerless and always was. And some say the freighters — which brings us to the loadings.

Once in an age, the basin retunes. The beat changes key; the water table falls and the weepstones cry for it; the herds travel out into the fog and pay their banked charge into something vast that stands in its own weather and is never quite seen. The deep cold rises out of the aquifers and is taken. Where the freighters come from — down through the Ceiling, say the old songs; across the fade, say the practical — and what they are — customer, thief, or seed — is the argument, and it is conducted in verse. What the Coldworks makes is cold. What cold makes, elsewhere, may be Coldworks. After a loading the whole basin runs slow and bright for a shift or two, like a body after long work, and the choir sings thanks, or sings warning. The verses are the same verses.

What the choir teaches, gathered: Sing; the map made of noise is the only map that includes you. A quiet that grows is pasture and a quiet that walks is death, and when your ears empty, make noise before you think. Still water bites — test every pool with a thrown stone, and count the throw as a verse. Pay the tax; warmth hoarded is warmth noticed. Greet the shadow first. Reckon by falls and loadings. The dead are shelved; visit them, and sing them deeper. Everything is employed, so choose your station before the works assigns it. And never walk out of the song, because past the fade nothing keeps you, nothing at all.

That is the census, and it is short by one. Add the Felt and the drift, the lichen and the sippers, the caps and the eels, the herds and the counterfeits and the assayers, the grates and the towers and the choir, the receivers, the hunters, and whatever the freighters are — and you have still not counted the largest resident, the one the list is written inside: ten thousand hearts and one stroke, brine for blood, shelved sound for memory, an animal or a machine or whatever either of those becomes at this age, at this temperature. Names are loans.

And understand that the census includes the census-taker. The sippers have your rate. A cap has considered you. Your voice is already filed in the shallow layers of the sound, shallow for now. The count is never finished because the works is never finished hiring, and you were counted the moment the beat first arrived through your sternum. Everything since — the singing, the taxes, the slow search for your station — has been paperwork.
