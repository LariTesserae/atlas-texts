The census of the curing loft is short, and every entry on it is a verb. Nothing here was born anywhere else for unguessable generations, but nothing here was born here originally either — life arrived the way life always arrives in made places, as stowaway: eggs in the lagging wool, spores in the damp grain of the shuttering, a film of forgotten water standing in a hose that was drained, once, into the first pour. Then the door closed, the last day began, and whatever had come in became the world.

Begin with the shiver, because everything here does.

The moths have no name. If the loft had ever had a naturalist, she would have named them for their labor, because labor is what they are: warmth-from-within, manufactured on the wing, paid for continuously and never owned outright. In the curing loft, warm-from-within is the entire definition of alive, and the moths are its most earnest citizens. They are furred like the lagging they nest in, and by now partly furred *with* it — generations of caterpillars have spun lagging wool into their cocoons, and the adults emerge wearing the room, fibers caught in their setae, pre-insulated, upholstered. To touch one, if anyone ever touched one, would be to touch the one warm soft thing in a cold hard room. Every moth is itself a small loft — furred, humming, and almost certainly inhabited; there will be mites in the fur, and the mites will have their own weather. The loft's smallest rooms are its moths.

They are deaf, functionally, because the chord never changes and hearing is only the detection of change. But they read the note the way the frost reads it — bodily. The room's true geography is acoustic, and the moths navigate by node and antinode as other creatures navigate by light. Their eyes, what remains of them, are tuned to the two lights that exist: the orange of the pilot lamps, the blue of the arc. The arc is their sun. They orbit it in slow shivering rings at the exact radius where its small heat pays the shiver's bill, and the rings never thicken and never thin, and a moth's short life is a slow spiral sunward, and occasionally the nearest ring loses one to the blue point — a spark, a small smell of burnt wool — and the ring closes. Nothing has ever happened.

Somewhere in the room there are dead spots, where the chord cancels to nothing. The mug sits in one, its tea frozen mid-tremor with the note's waveform preserved in brown ice — the only record of the chord ever kept, unreadable by everyone who lives with it. A moth crossing a dead spot hears, for the only time in its life, the sound of its own wings: the single most private event available in the loft. Whether they seek these places or avoid them is the kind of question the loft declines to answer. Nothing here keeps records. The logbook says *steady*.

The moths finish, which makes them the exception. Eggs in the lagging, caterpillars grazing the wool — the only herbivores in an indoor meadow — cocoons of wool and shed skin tucked toward the engine's warmth, which speeds them, so the casing wears a gradient of generations the way a hillside wears vegetation. A moth's life is brief and incandescent and identical, and the species has long since stopped being a population and become a process: wool into moth, moth into scale, scale into film, film into moth, the whole closed alchemy turning at the rate of small lives. Given enough of the last day, all the lagging will have been moth, some of it many times. The room is slowly becoming ex-moth, and the moths are slowly becoming the room, and neither notices, because noticing requires a before and an after, and there is only a during.

The oldest living things in the loft are not moths. They are the swarms. Where the sound field cancels, they hang in the air in tilted lenses, oriented to the lean, each shape held by a clot of furious wings, membership replaced body by body in the slow turnover of small lives while the shape itself persists the way a wave persists — not the water, but the going. Even there, nothing rests; rest is stillness and stillness is death, so the loft has invented rotation instead, and rotation is the only repose the last day permits. If the curing loft has citizens, they are these gray standing congregations, older than any wing that composes them, holding their lentil shapes over the same few feet of floor for longer than anyone will ever know. The moths are their weather.

Below them, the fry. Their world is a sheet of water the depth of a coat of paint, viscous with shed scale, ponded a finger deep against the downhill wall by the lean — so the room has a coast, and the fry crowd its shore. Some live in the resonance trays, in the ringing bowls, in water that is always mid-note: the only creatures in existence that live inside the drumline. They eat what sinks. The moths take some, drinking; the film takes the rest. And they never, ever finish — because the cue for finishing is a warmth, a drying edge, a season, a surface that holds still, and none of those has ever come. So the species persists as an endless childhood, translucent, thumbprint-small, seething gently at the limit of sight, and the adult of it is an idea the water keeps having. What it would become — something winged, something crawling, something with a name — cannot be known, because it has never happened, and here, a thing that has never happened does not, in the only sense that matters, exist.

The film is the pasture, the cemetery, and the stomach. Grey-green, threadlike in the lamp-lit corners, tuned over unguessable time to the orange of the pilot lights, it follows the weep-lines and avoids the frost, so the zebra on the outer plates is also a map of grazing. It grows exactly as fast as it is eaten — not balance, slacklessness; anything faster would be cropped, anything slower would be gone. It eats the fallen. It has begun, patiently, to thread the damp pages of the logbook, and it is eating the word *steady* one molecule at a time, and writing nothing.

And then there is the largest resident. The walls themselves: warm from within, sweating against their forms, holding their faint chemistry of heat like blood, unborn and unfinishable. By the room's own definition — warm from the inside, not yet done — the pour qualifies. It is the slowest animal in the loft, gestating at the rate of the stalactite halos, one molecule of intention at a time, and the moths roost on it the way small birds roost on large, patient cattle, and it does not mind, because minding is a kind of finishing, and it will never finish.

What the whole arrangement cannot use goes to the grate, and falls at the slant into the humming dark, and no landing has ever been heard. The living world ends at the grate's bars. Below is weather and depth and the cold iron smell of distance, and if anything down there lives on the slow gray snow, the loft will never know it, and the snow will never say.

But the true tenant of the curing loft is none of these. It is the chord, or the loop, or the held gesture they all make together — and the living things are not its guests but its load-bearing members. Remove the moths and the bearings foul for want of scale-dust, the blanket's warmth is lost, the chemistry slips its arrest, the pour completes — and the first morning after construction finally arrives, which is to say the loft ends. The moths are not decoration. They are part of the mechanism that keeps the last day from ending, and so is the fry-water, and so is the film, and so is the slow warm wall. The loop has no slack, and the living are inside the loop.

Someday — though the last day never ends — the crew that is about to arrive will arrive, and they will find the room kept: bearings dusted, pasture grazed to its exact stubble, drumline in tune, everything warm that should be warm, everything wet that should be wet, everything about to. The logbook will say *steady*, and it will be the truest word ever written, meant literally, maintained literally, for unguessable time, by a census of verbs.

Nothing here has ever happened. The living here is what keeps it that way.
