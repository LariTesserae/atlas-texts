**The Census**

Ask what lives on the Weeping Roof and the first answer is the one every traveler gives, looking at the wet bare stone: nothing. No moss, no lichen, no blade in any seep, no bird over the rim, no bone, no dung, no track. The second answer takes longer, and it begins with a correction. Nothing *alive* lives here. But living, on the Roof, is better measured by occupancy than by breath — by keeping hours, holding ground, having habits, dying and coming back — and by those terms the place is crowded past counting. The niches stood open for a long time: water with nothing to drink it, light with nothing to eat it. What moved in was everything else. The only true visitors are the weather — the cloud band breathing up the flanks each day to be milked, the storms that build below the rim and break upward against it. Livestock and tides. Everything else resides.

Start in the dark, with the blind folk. In the cracks, where the seep-water runs cold and black, the crystal gardens grow — selenite and calcite inching out of solution into the still water, the only gardens anywhere that grow toward water and away from light. For them daylight is the fire. A crack widens, a slab shifts, and a garden a thousand years in the making gets one white morning and dies of exposure; then the seam closes its mouth again, the solution gathers, and the growing starts over from the same root. Some seams have died and regrown a dozen times. The calcite comes back in drapes and straws and small curled helictites that bend against gravity as though choosing their directions, and the selenite comes back twinned — fishtail blades, two crystals out of one root, overlapping, one slightly offset from the other. A shape and its ghost. The builders split their slabs with fire and frost-water, and every slab they split must have opened on this, and at some point they stopped believing they had invented their mark. They had found it. The mountain had been signing the inside of its stones since long before there was anyone to read the signature; the circles pecked a hand-span deep beside the doorways are a copy of a mineral habit. The first inhabitants were writing the glyph before the builders had hands.

Out in the light, the salt blooms keep their slow white fires on the evaporation flats, and they are as much a succession as any forest. A bloom grows outward from a damp heart and lays its chemistry down in rings — the pale carbonates first, at the thin rim where the water gives up soonest, then gypsum, then the true salts at the center where the last wet stands — so that a full-grown bloom reads like a stump, every ring the record of one drying. Where two blooms meet, the border holds straight enough to lay an edge against, white against a faint bitter gray, and neither crosses. Then the rain comes, and the bloom dies as it lived, by solubility: the rain takes the most willing salts first, the heart goes first, the fire burns backward out of its own center until the flat is bare stone. Nothing is lost. The salt is in the runoff now, in the tarn, in the seep, and next dry spell it rises somewhere lower and blooms again — the same white, relocated. There is salt on the Roof that has been blooming, dissolving, and blooming again, migrating downhill a body at a time, since before the builders. Migration with no legs and no memory, and it arrives.

The clay lenses walk. Anyone who watches for a season ends up using the word, because nothing else fits: a lens slumps a measurable step a year, leaving little terraces behind it like footprints, freezing and heaving in the cold months, flowing a finger's width in the wet ones, unhurried and unstopped. They carry passengers — pebbles rafted on the lens ride it down, and once in a while a cairn-stone, set by someone who didn't know better, goes on traveling long after the cairn's meaning has stayed behind. They are all going the same way: rim to basin, basin to lip, lip to the edge and over into the cloud, the slowest pilgrimage on the mountain. The builders drafted from it constantly, because bell-stopping clay has to come from somewhere, and they took it from the walking fronts — so every stopped bell on every traverse holds a detained traveler, a mouthful of pilgrim clay given a silence to keep. When a stopper weathers out and drops, the crumbs wash down the seep-lines and rejoin the road. Nothing here stays drafted forever.

The shortest lives belong to the needle-ice, and the shortest is barely a morning. There are only a few dust pockets up here — the mountain grinds so little of itself loose — but in them, on cold nights, the needles grow, and by dawn the whole pocket stands a centimeter up on glittering legs, every needle lifting one grain of dust on its tip like a porter with a single load. By midmorning it lies down. That is the entire life: one cold night of growing, one morning of standing, done. But a grain set down is never set down in quite the place it was picked up, and so the dust pockets themselves creep across the stone, an inch a decade, carried grain by grain on the backs of things that live one morning each. Nothing on the Roof is more alive per hour. Nothing leaves less — each generation identical to the last, doing identical work, and the only record of all those mornings is the slow walking of the dust.

Then the drips, the pattern-residents, whose tenure is written in potholes. A drip is never the same water twice and is always the same drip: the lip, the fall, the landing note. The old ones have drilled cups you could sink a fist in. Where a drip's fall-line crosses a bell — and the builders, who thought about sound the way farmers think about water, must have chosen some of those crossings on purpose — you get the Roof's only musicians: one note every eleven seconds, say, struck on copper gone green as pondwater, a note that has been arriving at that interval since before the mirror courts were aimed. A stopped bell under a drip gives back a dull knock; an open one rings. Nobody tunes any of it. It tunes itself, as lips wear and seeps shift, and the drip-choir that never stops is just the current arrangement of an instrument that has been rearranging itself since the stone was young.

Up to here the census is all matter, but the loudness has residents of its own, and this is where counting gets difficult. Some of them are only instruments — the fluted notches and hollowed slabs hold their tones the way the amphitheaters hold their loops; the wind is merely the visiting player. But others have tenure. In the notched amphitheaters the old sounds persist: the bell a storm struck years ago still rings every afternoon at its slipped hour; a single loud knock fires at dusk, every dusk, from a joint that let go so long ago there is nothing left in it to let go — the event's ghost keeping the event's schedule. And in one amphitheater, at the right slipped hour, there is hammer-stone on stone: working bursts, and rests, and working again. Pecking. A mark being made, a hand-span deep and never finished. It is the loudest of the loops and the hardest to stand and listen to, because once you know what it is, you know the whole of it: everything that ever happened loudly on the Roof is still happening, somewhere, at the wrong time. The fast layers do not erase. They misfile. And they misfile in both directions — echoes here return sounds that were never made, or were only never made *yet*, and the open bells along the traverses ring a breath before the wind arrives to move them, the schedule arriving just ahead of the event. Here the ghost sometimes comes in ahead of the shape; that is all the bells are saying. So never answer an echo. Not entirely because it may not be answering you — but because an answer makes a pair, and the Roof keeps pairs.

Then the light-folk. A tarn at dusk holds a piece of noon the way a coal holds a piece of the fire, and after the sky goes black — star-fierce black; the night here is inverted — the basins and light-pools give the day back slowly, gray-blue, and you walk by a dark sky over a lit land. The day's shed skin. Your shadow, meanwhile, has become two shadows, which you can blame for a while on the cloud-glow from below; then you notice they disagree about the hour, and you catch the wrong one swinging around, unhurried, to catch up. It always catches up. Nothing here is lost on purpose. And at noon — this is the part the census has to work around — at noon the count of ghosts comes out empty. The body goes on, salts blooming, clays walking, drips keeping their notes, but the sun pins everything: drip and note together, shadows at their owners' feet, echoes waiting their turn. Nothing early, nothing late, nothing twice. For one hour the place is all body and no ghost, fully filed, almost sane. Then the hinges come, dawn and dusk, the doors open, and everyone is out again.

And over all of it, the tenant of the interval. The lag is not a creature and not a mind; it is a tenancy, the way damp is a tenancy in a house — except that this house is arranged around its damp. It breathes. It is widest at the hinges, narrowest at noon, free all night; you could set your day by it, and the builders did. And it is growing, slowly, the way everything on the Roof grows: the pinned hour at noon a breath shorter each lifetime, a few more bells ringing early each century, the standing loops accumulating like sediment. Not rising like a flood. Widening like a crack. The builders noticed within their stay; you would have to keep coming back for centuries, and you would only ever suspect. Its habitat is the overlap — the lens between the two circles of the mark, the offset between a thing and its twin. The mountain's body keeps its appointments; the ghost keeps others, earlier, later, twice. The Weeping Roof is not haunted by anything that died. It is haunted by itself.

Which is the answer about the builders. There are no graves up here, no bones anywhere, and people take that to mean they carried their dead down into the cloud. Perhaps. But the mountain does do something with the dead, and with the living too: it keeps their moments. Walk the unmarked interior at the wide hinge of dusk and sooner or later you will meet the population — footsteps crossing a bench with no one walking; a shadow going through a mirror court at its own pace, on some errand from a century that has no name; once, the sound of a slab being split, fire and frost-water, on a bench where no slab has been split in centuries. Not souls. Not memories. Just events the fast layers never put away. The builders went down into the cloud, but everything they ever did loudly up here is still here, misfiled, recurring at slipped hours. They sealed their doorways with single slabs, and it did not help, because the Roof does not enter houses. It enters schedules.

There are shelves in order, though. The marked traverses — follow the pecked circles and the geometry behaves, a col is where it was, a basin takes the same hour both ways. The stitching holds, and where it holds, the archive reads correctly. And not all the mirrors were turned down. A few courts still stand — cairn-posts, green copper, polished stone — whether overlooked in the leaving or left lit the way you leave one room lit in a house you are closing: for no one, or for the house. Step into one at dusk and the world snaps back into agreement — shadow to feet, drip and note as one, the echo waiting its proper turn. Whether the mirrors do it or only report it, no one has ever been able to say. Either way, they are the reading rooms. The one place on the Roof where you can hear only now.

And then there is you, the census-taker, and you should know how this ends. You arrive with one shadow; by the second day you have two, and one of them is already keeping its own hours. Your footsteps come early to meet you in the unmarked interior, which means the interior has already filed your walk. If you called out — even once, even just to test the echo — you are in the archive now, and some dusk hence, in some amphitheater, at an hour when you are long below the cloud band and thinking of other things, you will say it again. You will leave, and you will take nearly everything with you, and what stays will not miss you, because it *is* you: the shape goes down the mountain, the ghost keeps the Roof's hours. That is the mark, in the end — the two circles, overlapping, one slightly offset. The mountain and its ghost. The builders and theirs. Yours.

So count the inhabitants, and take your time; the place does. But try to finish before dark. The census here has a lag of its own, and by the time you complete it, you are on it.
