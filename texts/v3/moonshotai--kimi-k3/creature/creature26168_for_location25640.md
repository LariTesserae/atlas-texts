Nothing lives here. That is the first thing the chamber tells you, and everything in the first hour confirms it, and it is wrong by one.

Not a ghost — take the place at its word on that. Not an animal: the dust keeps perfect accounts, and anything footed would have signed them by now, and there is no signature but yours. Not a keeper either, no last tender of the spring wearing a path between pool and shelf. A resident with feet would have written a path into the pavement, and there is no path. Nobody's errands cross this floor. Whoever pressed the red hands onto the wall pressed them and went, and the slumped cairn at the mouth tells you what the dust inside already knows: the visits come years apart now, and no one is currently between them.

The tree, then. It is the obvious candidate, and it deserves the courtesy of being considered first. It is alive, unmistakably — leathery, gnarled, rooted in its crack, doing the slow arithmetic of holding its size against the dry. But the tree is one stem with one shadow. It cannot account for the interval of the drip, or the single held shoreline, or the mineral skin closing over the fallen block in the corner. The tree is not the answer. The tree is the neighbor.

Follow the water instead. The seep across the cool wall, the sleeve of moss going black-green, the film of olive and rust at the waterline, the slick dark inches of rock where the damp climbs. The crust thickening over the stacked slabs of the dam. The skin on the fallen block. The rim of the pool, where the shoreline line is cut sharp and singular. Follow all of it and you find it is continuous — there is no gap anywhere in the wet zone, no damp inch unoccupied. Scrape any of those inches and you will get the same thing: cells too small to see, sheathed in the stone they help lay down, living at the exact temperature of the water, which is the temperature of blood. It is not a metaphor the chamber shrugs off. What lives here is as extensive as the wetness and not one inch more. Its boundary is the dry seam, and you can see the whole of that boundary from anywhere in the room. It is the only body in these hills you can take in entire, at a glance — and it has no shape at all, because it has the shape of the water.

Step back from the scrape on your fingertip, where it is only chemistry, and look at the whole, where it is a body. It has an edge. It has a diet: old rain, dissolved stone, delivered warm from underground — it eats the past, literally, and the present it calls the present fell on far hills longer ago than anything here remembers. It has a pulse. The drip is the fastest thing it does, its single quick gesture in a life of slow ones, and the faint return off the far wall is the room handing its one sound back to it — it speaks once per beat, and the wall answers. It has a temperature, hoarded by day and paid out by night, steady as a fever held precisely. And it has a history, which is written in the dry half of the floor.

Because it was larger once. The relict travertine basins, the rounded rims holding dust — those are not ruins of the place. They are its old bodies. In the wetter time it filled basin after basin, built each rim around itself the way a reef builds, and when the water thinned it was driven back pool by pool, out of each terrace and down into the next, until it reached this one sleeve of damp, this one pool, this fringe. That retreat took ten thousand years and it is over now. It is not dying. It is finished — finished becoming what it is, the way the slopes outside finished long ago. What it does now is not growth and not decline. What it does now is hold.

That is the thing to understand, and it changes everything you saw on the way in. The constancy of this room is not the absence of life. It is the labor of this one. A pool does not hold a single shoreline for centuries without something spending itself on the holding. A fallen block does not digest into the floor without something doing the slow work of the dissolving. The stillness here is not a stopped room; it is a held note, and a held note is sung — someone is spending breath on it, evenly, at an interval you can set your breathing by. And you do set your breathing by it. Everyone who has ever come here has done that. Which means every visitor, without knowing it, has spent their hour synced to the resident's pulse — two rhythms in the room for the length of a stay, the only company at its own timescale it has ever had.

It has no organ with which to know this. It meets its visitors the only way it can: as weather. A body entering the pool is a warm front. Footfalls on the pavement are percussion, faint, arriving through stone. Breath is the only wind that ever blows inside — the slot admits none — so to the resident, a human being is a brief season of moving air, a few dozen heartbeats long, centuries between seasons. It cannot read the ochre on the wall as hands. But consider where the hands are: shoulder height, near the water, at the one place where its body climbs closest to the air. You cannot draw something that has no outline. There was never anything here to depict that was not already present, entire. So they pressed. Some dragged their palms across the stone, slowly. Make of that what you will. It is explicable, all of it. So is everything a body does to greet another body.

What it keeps, it keeps in its own way. The dam they built is inside its boundary, so the dam is becoming body — crust over slab, work of hands becoming geology, taken in. The handprints and the pebble scatter lie beyond the seam, in the dry half, where it cannot go — and that is exactly why they persist. The chamber has two keepers, and they keep in opposite grammars. The wet one keeps by taking into itself, slowly, over centuries. The dry one keeps by never touching anything at all: footprints sharp for years, banded pebbles lying where they were set down, unincorporated, unforgotten, unclaimed. Your visit is being kept both ways already. Your outline is in the dust, which is not the resident's to change. Your warmth is in the walls.

It is not waiting for the next visitor. Waiting is quicker than anything it does. It is holding — the pool at its one line, the air in its narrow range, the note at its one pitch, the way it has held through every hour of every year since the terraces went dry. Tonight, as every night, the walls will pay out the day's warmth, and yours with it, indistinguishable from the sun's, spent slowly at the drip's interval and gone by morning. That spending is all the acknowledgment a body like this can give. It is the same one it gave the hands.

The place keeps what it is given, because what lives here is the keeping.
