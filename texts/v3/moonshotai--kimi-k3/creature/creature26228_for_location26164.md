**The Census**

Ask what lives in the basin and the basin answers first. The city is not the place where the life is. The city is the life, and everything else — the herds, the worms, the tufts, the arches, the lichen — lives in it the way mites live in a pelt, the way bacteria live in a gut: tolerated, tended, occasionally eaten. So any honest census must be taken twice, once by bodies and once by tonnage, and the two counts will not resemble each other. By bodies, the basin belongs to the polishers. By tonnage, there is one animal here, five kilometers across, and it is the floor.

And almost none of it can be seen. In a world with no air, nothing announces itself; there is no birdsong, no rustle, no distant cry. You will learn the inhabitants the way you learn a household through a wall — by their knocking, their furrows, their absences. Lie down. Press your ribs to a floor plate. Most of the basin's fauna is evidence.

**The first inhabitant.** Reread the anatomy as zoology and the whole place changes. The hexagonal plates are skin that eats — flexing in the giant's fist, generating the charge that is the city's bread. The seams are a mouth that breathes once an orbit. The metal veins are nerves, and the tick-pulses running plate to plate are not just messages; some of them are reflexes, some of them are gossip, some of them may be the slow local opinions of districts that have never once agreed quickly. The spires are hearing. Every micrometeorite that stitches the plaza with its silent flash is a note struck on a tuning fork, and the spires feel it, and the city, in its way, listens to the sky falling. Perhaps it hears more than impacts. The giant hangs there forever, storm-flanked, muttering across the spectrum in its slow electromagnetic voice, and in a world with no air to make a sound, that whisper is the loudest thing in the sky. The city's hum at pericenter may not be entirely its own. It may be humming along.

Whether the basin is one creature with sixty limbs or sixty creatures sharing one body is a question the basin itself may not have settled. The plates have opinions. The districts have moods. The whole bowl has something that, for want of a better word, has to be called attention — and once an orbit, in the single hour of true dark, that attention turns inward, and the city dreams in cold light along its veins, patterns too slow to read and too regular to be anything but language. It does not sleep. But it dreams on schedule, and the dreaming has an audience of one, and we will come to her.

**The herds.** Four hundred polishers, give or take a drumbeat: nearly all the animal life there is. They are almost blind, and it does not matter, because they hear through their legs — each of their many feet is an ear pressed to the world's only telephone line. They have never seen the giant. They know it as the squeeze: the twice-daily cooling of the ground, the once-an-orbit flex that rings the floor like a struck bowl and sends the charge fizzing up through their soles. When the city eats, everything eats. Pericenter is the basin's dinner bell, and the herds drum loudest then, singing over the bass note, if singing is a word you can allow for three-beat pulses stamped into stone by soft-bodied animals wearing tombs.

Because that is what their shells are. A polisher cements its armor from flakes of dead crystal, and the flakes come from the dying. When a district sinks — when a spire past its time is drawn back down into the rootplate over the slow years — a herd will come and keep it company, camping at the resorption scar for seasons, taking the offered flakes as the structure gives them up. Whether this is scavenging or mourning is not a distinction the basin asks you to make. What it means, practically, is that every old polisher is a reliquary. Its shell carries pieces of spires resorbed centuries ago, districts that left no ruin and no marker, neighborhoods that exist now nowhere else in the universe except as shingles on a grazer's back. A young polisher is born nearly naked and is given its first flakes by its elders, and so the herd's memory is literally handed down, worn, and never written. The city keeps no past tense. The herds are the past tense. A dozen of them crossing a plaza in three weeks is a library on the move.

Their language is the drumming, in groups of three, handed down the line from animal to animal across kilometers of floor. Three beats is a word. The census-call at pericenter. The location of a good lichen bruise. The long, patient relay of a warning. And when the Cathedral pulses last and slowest in the dark hour, the herds stop. All of them, all across the basin, they stand still on the floor and feel the eldest thing finish its thought, and then they begin again. Call it worship if you need a word. The basin doesn't.

**The worms.** In the dust of the plazas, furrows appear overnight, and that is all anyone has ever had to go on. The furrows vary in width, which suggests a population — young and old, growing. Or the furrows have widened, decade on decade, all at the same rate, which suggests something worse and more wonderful: one worm, a single ancient citizen down there in the dark, swimming the electrostatic silt like a fish through water, getting bigger. Or the furrows are not made by an animal at all — they are peristalsis, the city's own gut moving resorbed material beneath the skin, in which case the worm is the floor, which is a way of saying the worm is everywhere. No furrow has ever crossed the ring of bare plaza around the Cathedral. They turn aside, all of them, at the same invisible line, a hundred meters out. The herds, crossing a fresh furrow, drum faster, and bunch, and hurry. Even the blind things that have lived here for centuries know there is something under them.

**The arches.** Some of the arches are not the city, and this is where the census turns cold. They wear the architecture perfectly — to the eye. To the foot, they are wrong. The floor's tick-pulses do not pass through them; the song of the ground goes around them or dies at their root, and so a herd's scout, suspecting an arch, will drum beside it and listen for the bass note that should come back through the stone and doesn't. An arch is a hole in the floor's song. That is the test, and the herds pass the results down the generations, and their rule is the rule of memory: an arch that has always been there, that your grandmother's shell remembers, may shelter you. An arch that was not there last season is not an arch.

It would help if the wrong ones stayed wrong. But the count changes — nine, or eleven — and there are worse rumors, felt rather than confirmed: that a wrong arch, at pericenter, hums. That it has learned the city's feeding-hour joy and counterfeits it, a lure pitched exactly where a blind animal's heart would lift. The city hums when it eats. The arches hum so that they may eat. What happens after a polisher shelters beneath the wrong one is slow: the arch thickens afterward, for a while, and then, over months, it thins again — and the meal has to go somewhere, and the only somewhere is down, into the rootplate, into the city's body. Which raises the question the basin declines to answer: whether the arches are a cancer the city cannot cut out, or teeth it has never bothered to deny. The herds are kept at four hundred. Something keeps them there. Once in a decade, in the dark hour, presumably, where all the basin's secrets are kept, an arch closes. No one hears it. But somewhere a line of drumming stops mid-word, and the herd closes the gap, and the pattern reshuffles itself around the silence, and that is the only obituary this world has ever written.

**The small lives.** At the seam-lips live the sigh-riders: flat, translucent things the size of coins, timed to the floor's breath. Once an orbit the seams open a finger's width and exhale, and the sigh-riders are thrown up with the dust, spreading membranes in the low gravity, riding the plume for long slow minutes before settling seams away. They eat the chemistry of the exhale. Their whole biography is a list of leaps — one flight per orbit, dozens of orbits per life — and their only predator is arithmetic: a seam closing is a guillotine, and young polishers will snap at them when they land, which is the only hunting a polisher ever does. At the terminator, where the dust fountains rise along the moving line of dawn, not all the dust is dust. Some grains re-loft against the odds, cluster, steer by charge, and ride the twilight boundary around the basin twice a day at a walking pace — the world's only nomads, eternal dawn-dwellers, weather with a metabolism, or a colony with the attention span of weather. The shadow-tufts cast their spores into the fountains and let the bright line carry their children. The tufts themselves walk. Each one creeps around the base of its spire once a day, centimeters an hour, keeping the small sun off its rime-farm, and where two settle too close — closer than the hundred meters that is their right — the dispute is settled by patience: one simply stops, and goes dormant, and waits the decades until the other dies. War, at the basin's clock speed. And over every spire-face runs the oldest war of all, the three-way slow grind that is the basin's ecology in miniature: the city grows the glass, the smoke-lichen etches it, the herds scrape it clean. The lichen may be the eldest living thing here. It may be older than the city. It may be what the city was, before the city learned to stand up.

**The unborn.** And then there is the Cathedral's crown, thickening for decades with nodules that are not for here. They are alive now — that is the part that matters. They hum, faintly, at a pitch slightly off the floor's own note, the only dissonance the city has ever been known to cradle. The dark-hour light-sermons crawl along the spire-veins across the whole basin, pattern answering pattern, and the Cathedral pulses last, and everything else waits for it — because the lesson ends at the seeds. Everything else is rehearsal. In a place where construction and anatomy are the same act, information and anatomy are the same act too: the seeds are books by being bodies. They are being taught how to be a city, so that when the strike comes — the impact the whole basin has organized its old age around — the pieces flung into the black will arrive wherever they arrive already knowing. They will not know the way. They will know only how. Which made which is the basin's one secret, but the census taker notes the symmetry: a crater that may be a germination scar, a crown full of seeds hoping for a crater. Somewhere, perhaps, another bowl of stone keeps another silence. The congregation here does not know its mother's name. It is raising daughters who will never know hers.

The counts of the basin:

The city: one. It is also the count.
The polishers: four hundred, minus what the arches have taken this decade, plus what the seams have lately cradled.
The arches: nine. Or eleven. Ask again after the dark hour.
The silt-worms: one, if the widening furrows are read one way; many, if another; the floor itself, if a third.
The sigh-riders: uncounted. They are only ever in the air for minutes, and no one has wanted to badly enough.
The shadow-tufts: one per hundred meters of moving shade. They enforce this themselves.
The lichen: by the hectare-bruise.
The unborn: not yet.

Lie on the floor at pericenter, in the bronze perpetual dusk, and take the census the only way it can be taken: the hum of the tide coming up through your ribs, the tick of messages handed down the plates, a seam sighing open somewhere in slow motion, the three-beat drumming of a herd a month away, the Cathedral finishing its slow sentence in the dark. The floor registers everything that touches it. It has registered you. Stand on the rim — no one does — but if you did, you would be the rarest creature the basin has ever counted: a visitor, a guest-note struck once on the instrument.

Stand still. The count has changed.
