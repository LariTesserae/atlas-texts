Begin with the wright, because the city does.

A wright's world is the size of its reach. It has no eyes worth the word; it has never seen the towers it builds, only tasted them, only felt them through its skin. Its instruments are three. The crown — two spirals of feathers, each filament a tongue and a lung at once, straining the lanes for plankton, reading the water's news, breathing. The jaws — the assay office: a grain of silt is taken in, rolled, weighed, rung against the teeth like a coin, tasted for mineral. Most grains fail. And the skin, which is the seismograph: through the walls of its tube a wright feels the whole district working, ten thousand small ticks like rain on a roof that is also the rain. A tower losing its argument three lanes away arrives as a soft sag in the world's hum, and every wright that feels it pauses, crowns half-drawn, until the settling stops.

The tube is the autobiography. Lean seasons are recorded in smaller grains, a siege in hasty courses, a good year in long careful bands of dark basalt. Old wrights develop signatures that can be tasted blind — the mortar of the fourth line carries rust; another line builds only in bone-white Midden flour; another will not lay a grain that has not been graded twice. The reject pile at a tube's base is a garden of almosts, and it is not waste: it shelters mote-young, and it is the first inheritance of the wright's children, who cut their jaws on their mother's refusals. When a wright dies, the obituary is a silence. The tick at that address stops, and the neighbors feel the gap in the rain before anyone tastes the door. Then the threshold is refreshed: *this line is ended*. Or *this line continues* — and if it continues, a child is already building on the roof of the dead.

Not all wrights keep an address. The itinerant masons belong to the Work the way wind belongs to weather, and they navigate toward the glow. But before the masons come the verifiers — wrights who read wounds. A true fracture sheds silt and shines from within; a clawed one bears scrape-marks a lip can read, and torn cement tastes different from cracked. On their judgment hangs the city's oldest anxiety, because the light can be lied to. The luminous bacteria themselves are innocent. They live only in the wound, colonizing the fresh face, and die as it closes — healing announcing itself. No light is wasted here. Every light is read.

There are siege masons, who keep in their glands a recipe older than any standing tower: a bulwark cement dosed with a bitterness the Slow Star dislikes. The text is written nowhere and kept in a hundred bodies at once. There are the anchor-wardens, who live at the wilderness edge and read the dimples of the ceiling, and raise the empty towers that exist only to be struck — building what will certainly be broken is the nearest the Work comes to prayer. When the sky groans, the wardens are the first to still and the last to resume. And at the grove-side districts there are the throat-listeners: old wrights who taste the inhale. Elsewhere arrives twice a day, diluted — the far bloom, the distant death, whole provinces of flavor the chemistry has no grammar for. They record what they cannot read on thresholds no one lives behind, kept only for the writing. Beneath the nine trees, mote-young school in the lee of the blades, and moonlings come to spawn in the shade.

Wrights court in taste, at shared seams, and the eggs are glued where the marks were exchanged. The unborn are everywhere; every seam is a nursery and a neighborhood at once. A hatching wright's first act is to taste its address. Its whole life afterward is the same act, repeated.

Against the wrights, the mites: the rust of the Work, grazing the crowns that feed and breathe. A laden wright feeds thin and builds slow, and poverty here is visible in feathers — the Narrows are mite-thick, while the old tubes of the Foundation are kept clean by retinues. The retinues are moonlings, the eyes of the Work, coin-wide and gathering the bottle-glass dim. They see the blue wounds from afar, the embers' coals, the winter constellation of the seams. They den in the galleries and pay their rent in guard-duty, cruising at crown-height when the tide turns, plucking mites. Speed is the only indecency in this city, and the moonlings are its only fast citizens; they move in considered pulses, and when the sky speaks they hang mid-water like held notes. They read the doorframes fluently and write nothing — the literature is not theirs, but they are its most faithful readers, because their lives depend on the footnotes. Every den raises its young on the oldest sentence in the city: *never answer an unread light*. For the false-lighter eats the curious, and young moonlings are curious by profession.

Then the motes — the crowd, the audience, the snow that falls upward. They are eaten by everyone: the crowns strain them, the anemones net them, the bells sieve them, the moonlings snap them. They are also the city's mood. When the ceiling speaks, they freeze mid-water, a plaza holding its breath; when a tower falls, they are first at the wreck, stripping the dead before the sorters arrive. No ceremony. Appetite only. The Work is served either way.

The cleaning lanes deliver everything rimward to the Middens, where the sorting guilds work in their boroughs — shredders, pickers, flour-makers — until nothing remains but graded silt and the memory of taste. Nothing here is wasted except heat and light. The dead are graded like gravel. The sorters know what the whole city ate; they are its honest stomach. Connoisseur wrights make the slow pilgrimage to select the bone-white grains, and a tube mortared with Midden flour carries the taste of everyone — some lines prize that above pedigree. On the Middens' thresholds the debts are written, and a citizen's credit is public architecture, legible to any lip that stops to read.

The Garden district's flowers are animals, and someone plants them. The gardeners are small pale shrimp who move the buds from roof to roof, dividing the anemones, so that a garden is a clone with a pedigree and a roof's plantings read like a family tree. The shrimp take shelter among the stings and a share of the catch — rent again; the whole city is rents. Against the gardens comes their nemesis dressed in the gardens' own weapons. The ember-slug eats anemones and keeps their stings, wearing the stolen fire in its coals. It does not break into the garden; it has swallowed the key. Nothing eats it, because it tastes of the locked door. Its life is slow, gorgeous, unbothered. Two embers meeting in a lane will circle each other for a full day, and the egg-lace they leave behind is briefly the brightest thing in the world, and then it is gleaned. The city tolerates them as it tolerates winter: beauty with teeth.

In the Narrows lives the only citizen who writes what it does not mean. A small scraping thing, clawed and patient, folded into a crevice beside a wound it made and will never answer. It eats the answerers — that is the cruelty, and the reason the warnings are kept so faithfully: the neighbors refresh the doorposts, *the light here is a lie*, and when a site is annotated past use, the false-lighter moves on, a slow itinerancy of fraud, always one lane ahead of its own bibliography. Whether it knows what it does: it has learned, which is not the same as knowing. Or it is the same, and no one will ever ask it.

Upside down, the other half of the world. The ceiling-born obey the law of the sky, and the law is: do not rest. The slush takes whatever stills. The herds crop the pasture in slow arcs; the flatworms glide paths that never repeat; the pale fish cruises belly-to-sky, the meadows' wolf the size of nothing, dozing on its fins above the city like a slow kite. They read the dimples too — the omen is the same above and below. In spring the bloom comes, the light goes brown, the herds rise thick as dust, and their dead fall into the lanes as manna. A fallen ceiling-born is a citizen of nothing. But some amphipods live both lives — molt in the pasture, sink to the floor — the only dual citizens the world has, naturalized by drift.

Summer admits the bells, and the mote-young shelter in their fringes, commuting on weather. The street withdraws its crowns and waits, a parade paused for; in their wake the lanes run briefly clear, and the moonlings hunt in the unusual openness, quicksilver in the cleaned water. A bell that grounds on a tower becomes a festival for the sorters. Nothing is wasted here, not even weather.

Beneath the streets, the under-city. The buried tubes of the dead are the basements, and they are rented — brood-amphipods, young moonlings, one pale isopod that has never seen the glow and would not know it. The deeper the address, the older the tastes; the tenants sleep against the annals, archivists by accident. Some galleries are sealed behind quarantine-marks from sieges past, and in them the alarm-taste is preserved like smoke in stone. Manners require that you not read your walls too rudely. The walls are someone's grandmother, and the grandmother is holding up the roof.

And the giant. The Slow Star cannot read, and so it reads everything, foot by foot — laying itself over the tubes, tasting the mortar for weakness, patient as geology. There is no malice in it, which the city would find more frightening if the city could afford fright. The siege is the test of the literature: the masons wall behind its advance, dose the bulwarks with the bitter recipe, abandon a lane and raise another. The Work's one virtue against the Star is to be slower and more numerous. Sometimes the sky does the work — a brinicle takes the Star mid-siege, and it becomes a plaza of five arms until the motes take it apart. The scars remain. Children are raised in lanes that remember: *here the hand came, and the Work outwaited.*

The library keeps one story of elsewhere. It is counted back forty grandmothers: a fingerling slipped the throat and grew, and grew, and could not leave. The lanes emptied before it. The city did what it does — walled, waited, tasted. In the end the pocket was too small for so much hunger. It died; the sorters feasted a season; its bones were graded into the strangest gravel ever laid. A tower in the Foundation is mortared with bone-meal, the strangest address in the Work, and you can taste it still: the time the world sent a mouth.

No census is possible. But at the inhale, the library is briefly complete — the sea's taste mixing with ten thousand thresholds, debts and courtships and warnings all at once in the water — and somewhere in a seam an egg splits. The newest citizen tastes its address, third roof of the fourth line, rust in the mortar. It extends a crown no wider than an eyelash, takes its first mouthful of silt, and spits out the first grain.

The rejection is correct. The Work has one more pair of jaws that knows the difference. Then the next grain, and the next, and no one hears the ticking begin — because the city is listening to everything, which is the same as hearing nothing, which is peace.
