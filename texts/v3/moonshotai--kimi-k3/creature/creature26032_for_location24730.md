# What Lives in the Kettle

Everything the Bowl contains is alive, or was, or is waiting for a warm palm to remind it. Start with the six hundred.

## The people

Their names are made of hands. You cannot say who anyone is in the Kettle; you can only show, and so everyone learns everyone else's name the way lowlanders learn faces. A child is given a milk-sign at birth, and around seven winters chooses her own, and the town adopts it by use, and that is the whole ceremony.

Their faces are all the same shape at rest — the squint — and their expressions live mostly below the neck. They touch constantly, because touch is the only channel the cold has not taxed: a hand on the shoulder means *come*, two fingers on the wrist means *wait*, a grip at the forearm means *careful*, a palm flat on the back means *well done*. They knock by setting one stone gently against another. They get each other's attention by stepping into a sight-line and lifting their hands, or, across a room, by tossing a pellet of felt. To be out of sight in the Kettle is to be out of reach, so the people arrange themselves in sight-lines the way lowlanders arrange themselves in earshot, and a crowded room here has the churned, many-angled look of a field of sunflowers.

Babies are the one problem the silence cannot be trained into, so the town plans for them instead: the deepest, most felted rooms are nurseries, and a newborn's cry is the only sound the Kettle forgives. Laughter, the born learn, is a thing the body does and the air does not. A joke can cross the whole market in a minute, passed hand to hand, and it arrives funnier, and six hundred people shaking silently is a sight that unsettles guests more than the glare ever does.

Ask them how long their people have been here and they will point — with the chin, the hands being busy — at the wall.

## The trades

The morning pick-crews work with antler and hardwood, opening what the night has closed, and they compete in slowness; the best crew is the one nobody heard. The mat-harvesters lie on felt pads at the spring-heads, an arm's length from the listening water, and scrape the red and emerald films in slabs. They train for years to sneeze softly. It is the hardest thing a body is asked to do in the Kettle, and an apprentice's first silent sneeze is celebrated.

The lens-grinders work in the coldest rooms, turning ice on sandstone, and down in the low country their product is used to start fires — this is the joke and the glory of the trade, that the Kettle ships its cold away to light the world's hearths. The master grinder lost two fingertips to frostbite years ago. Her name-sign used to be two fingers circling. It is now a fist circling; the town changed it so she could still say herself. That, they will tell you, is what a town is for.

The rat-keepers are the bankers, fat being currency, and one old woman knows her lineage of pelt-rats back forty generations and insists the rats know her hands. The felt-makers gather molted down from the mute-ledges, which is why the felt sheds water like a bird: it was one. You can read a house's fortune by how high its lamp is trimmed, and debts are paid in fat, and no one in the stack is allowed to freeze for poverty, because a cold neighbor cools everyone.

## The keepers of sound

There are two, and they could not be less alike.

The keeper of the bell is not Kettle-born. He came up out of the cloud twenty years ago, noise-sick and flinching, and never went down, and when the old keeper died the town gave him the felt-wrapped copper, on the reasoning that the bell is safest with a man who has heard too much. He keeps it greased against the wet. He will sign about two of the three times it was rung; the third he signs only to the elders. He has never heard it. He will die having kept a sound he never heard, and the town considers this not a tragedy but a qualification.

The listener is Kettle-born, and her name-sign is an open hand, waiting. The springs pulse on no schedule anyone has learned — but not for want of learning. In the elder-house hang her cords and her mother's cords and her grandmother's, generations of knotted tallies of the water's moods, and the knots never repeat, and still she listens. When the wall Speaks, it is she who goes to it afterward, alone, and lays her ear against the ice for the better part of a day, and what she hears she signs to the elders, and the elders knot it into the cords. Asked whether the water is alive, she signs: *it listens, which is more than most people manage.*

## The children

A child's first gift is a pane of worm-glass, carried against the skin, so that she learns early what a bare palm can do: wake what the cold keeps. They eat snow-fleas for the tickle of them. They are taught the sign for the hush before they are taught the sign for their own father.

And they have the Underneath. The crawl-passages belong to them and to the rats, and getting lost down there is not an accident but a curriculum; children draw maps of it that are wrong on purpose, and the rats lead them out, usually. One girl knows the Underneath better than the rats do, and keeps a route from anywhere to anywhere, and hers is the name that is signed when a peg is missing from a tally-board and someone has to go below to look.

## The ones the light takes

The glare is patient, and over a lifetime it collects. There is no family in the Kettle without someone the light has taken, and so there is a second dialect of the hands, signed into the palm, and everyone learns some of it, the way lowlanders all learn a little of the language of the next valley.

The blind do the dark work: the deep eft-pens, the fulling of felt, the rooms where the blue glow through the walls is wasted anyway. And they keep the loud room. Its keeper is a blind elder who has sat in the layered felt for forty years, letting the queue in by touch — two taps on the wrist means *now* — and she knows the town's griefs and joys by hand, knows who is weeping and who is singing the old loud songs in the language nobody speaks except in that room, knows the mothers with colicky infants, who are most of the queue. In the dark of the felt, she is the only one who can hear everything, and she is discreet, and that is the whole of her job.

## The mutes

Ten thousand is a courtesy of a number; no one has ever counted them to anyone's satisfaction. They are voiceless entirely — no call, and their soft-fringed wings make no more sound than snow arriving — and a flock of ten thousand crossing the sun produces only this: the light goes out, and comes back.

They coordinate by sight, as the people do, and the people feel the kinship and exploit it. The flock is the town's other tally-board. It tells where the worm-blooms are pulsing; it tells the weather; and when the mutes will not come down to the floor, the Counters go out instead.

## The hush and the counters

Because the predator is known only by absence, the Kettle has made a religion of attendance. At every hatch hangs a board of bone pegs, one per person; you hang your peg when you come in, and at dusk the Counters walk the boards, and a missing peg empties a house in silence.

What the hush takes, it takes quietly and alone: rats from the roof-pens; an eft-net left soaking, net and floats together; once, in the grandparents' grandparents' time, a child — which is when the counting began. What has been seen is fragmentary and doubted: tracks that begin; a drift that moved wrong; one harvester who swears she watched the snow close like water behind a goat. The sign for it is a flat hand passed slowly over the other palm and then held open and empty: *something — then nothing.* Children learn it early. Lowlanders fear with stories. The Kettle fears with arithmetic.

The rim-goats are mostly rumor, and so is their herder: fat is left at a cache-stone on the rim, and sometimes pelts appear in its place, and no one under thirty has seen his face. His peg has been hung on the board for nine years. It would be wrong, somehow, to take it down.

## The glazer

The dead are glazed into the icefall, and the glazer is the one who does it. She cuts the niche, lays the body, and pours the water in thin layers over days — vibration clouds ice, stillness leaves it clear, so her apprenticeship is an apprenticeship in stillness. The dead wear the farewell sign, hands raised, and it is the glazer who holds those hands up until the first glaze locks them. Hers are the last warm hands the dead feel. Her name-sign is the pouring hands, held: a pour that does not spill.

The living use the wall constantly. Children are taken to be shown their grandmother. A new name-sign is shown to the wall before the town is asked to learn it. Vows are signed facing the ice, so the dead can read them. And the wall, everyone knows, is advancing; the back-row houses are cold on both sides, and a house in the Kettle has a life like a person's — built at the front, middle-aged in the middle, swallowed at the last — and people are born in rooms their great-grandchildren will look at through ten feet of ice. The Kettle has no cemetery. It has a direction.

## The bundle-tier

Her house is nearest the lip, and the work is inherited, and she was chosen at seven for being the child who asked the fewest questions — the only office in the Kettle for which that is the entire qualification. She ties the frozen bundles. She ties the fat. Once, in her mother's time, a copper bracelet went over. She slides the offerings past the organ, into the cloud, and she does not look at what bulges below, and no one signs about it, and neither will I.

The organ sounds only in the rarest quarter of wind, and when that wind comes, the whole town stops and faces the lip, hoping. There is a sign for the organ's music, shaped like listening itself, and only those who have heard it may use it — some elders, twice. Ask the Kettle-born what they dream of, and a surprising number answer with that sign.

## Guests

The guest-minder keeps the ledge below the rim, and by the second day he knows which of them will stay: it is the ones who stop flinching at the quiet and start leaning into it. He teaches every newcomer the sign for *sorry*, and forgives the clap, once. Those who stay a full winter are given a name-sign, which is the closest thing the Kettle has to a door. The keeper of the bell was such a guest, once.

And when a guest goes down, the town comes to the rim and gives him the raised hands. It is the same sign the dead wear. It does not mean goodbye. It means: *we are keeping your shape.*

## At dusk

The Counter makes her round. Every peg is hung; the mutes have settled on the ledges; the rats are under the floors and the worms are locked in their glass, waiting for warm palms, and the dead are at the town's back in their generations, and the water is deciding, and deciding again. She signs the count to the next window, and it travels across the stacked town, hand to hand to hand: *all.*

The sign for *all* is two cupped hands, full. The place pours; the people hold. Every dusk they count what they are holding, and it is enough — and in the morning, because this is the Kettle, they will count again.
