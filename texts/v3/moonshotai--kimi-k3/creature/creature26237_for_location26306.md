What lives in the Calder begins with a breath. The thin hot air is the first inhabitant of every keeper — it moves in at birth and rearranges the furniture, widening the chest, slowing the pulse, teaching the blood to be frugal with oxygen the way the city is frugal with water. Keepers are built around that breath: barrel-chested, deep-seamed, slow-moving people with the deliberate strength of the machines they tend. They breathe the way their Engines stroke — rarely, fully, and without waste. A lowlander who makes the climb gasps for days and is gently tolerated, the way one tolerates a toddler learning stairs. A keeper who descends has the worse journey: the air below is thick as broth, and it goes to their heads like liquor. Salt-walkers returning from the playa come up giddy and air-drunk, talking fast, laughing at everything, and their families put them to bed like returned sailors. The mountain keeps its people this way, without walls. Their lungs are the walls.

The breath shapes the speech. Conversation in the Calder is masonry: words set one at a time, and the pauses load-bearing. To finish another person's sentence is a small crime, like cutting a corner in stone. Interrupting is nearly impossible anyway, since no one can be sure the other has finished. Their humor has long fuses — a joke begun one rest day may detonate the next, and the best jokes are said to be inherited, punchlines passed down like tools. Their proverbs are short, because proverbs are for daily use: *Wait. The street rises. Low for a lifetime, high forever.* Their toast is shorter still, raised in small cast cups of still-water distilled in the cistern warrens: *Height and water.* The two banked wealths, drunk to daily.

You can tell a keeper's age by how long it takes to greet them. Names in the Calder are terraced. A child is given a syllable or two and accrues the rest across a lifetime — a name for apprenticeship, a name for mastery, a name that honors what the street has done beneath them — until an elder's full name takes a whole slow breath to say, and saying it is itself a small ceremony. The dead keep their full names, cast entire into iron or pressed into ceramic, and the markers on the high spires are very long indeed.

They wear the mountain, having nothing else to wear. Spun-slag cloth, soft as wool and pale as ash — the weavers' guild guards the trick of making stone gentle — with wire-mesh overshirts in the hot tiers and glass-silk for ceremony, worn warily, because glass-silk is beautiful and it remembers every careless elbow. The palette is the city's own: verdigris, rust-bloom, bone-white, the violet of dusk. Guild badges are cast, not sewn. Leather exists and is not sold: a pipe-fox pelt, if one is found dead, is kept as a household's luck, and buying or selling fox-luck is the one transaction the market refuses. There is no rule. There are things more load-bearing than rules.

They eat the mountain too, at one remove. The vat-halls tend the simples — the gray cultures that even the natural philosophers agree are not plants — and the vat-tenders speak of them the way herders speak of stock: this hall's broth has temperament, that hall's has gone sullen since the stroke of the second Engine changed. Broths are named by texture more than flavor. Flavor is the salt-keeper's art, and it is a real profession: since nothing green has ever grown here, cuisine is mineralogy, and a good salt-keeper sets out a flight of salts the way a lowland cook sets out herbs — sulfur-smoked, iron-tinged, the bitter black salt whose acquired taste marks the far border of childhood. Children steal pinches and pretend to like it. Culture theft is the classic crime of the city's stories, the great heist of a hundred cautionary tales: a stolen starter, smuggled in a sealed flask, a fortune in gray.

Childhood itself is a course of study in stillness. Babies learn the hush hour the way other children learn a language, and small children play the leaning game — standing in doorways at the top of the hour, waiting for the doorway to lean against their shoulders, first to move loses, and the champions are legendary and insufferable. They count drips. They play marbles with chipped bearings, since a perfect bearing is currency and a chipped one is joy. They are taken, once, to see the grain — the wooden spoon behind glass in the guild museum, brought up by a salt-walker two hundred years gone — and they press their faces to the case and ask if it is true that whole towers in the lowlands are made of it, and are told it is true, and do not believe it. And around seven, each of them asks the question, the only question, and watches the adults' faces, because the faces are half the answer.

The oldest argument fits inside a pronoun. *We raise it*, say the Drivers, strongest in the Sumps, where the heat stands like a hand on your chest and the piston houses lean against you hourly and the Engine-tenders — the broadest-chested people alive, who sleep in shifts timed to the stroke and dream in the lean — say, not boastfully but as a statement of fact: everything you stand on, we are doing. *It rises*, say the Riders, strongest on the Rim and in the high districts, where you can feel the mountain going up under you like a slow tide and the Engines seem less like cause than like reins — steering, braking, keeping the rise from tearing the range apart. The Chord-tuners, who live in the middle tiers and have heard everything, refuse both. *It plays*, they say, and say nothing more, and that is why they are trusted. And the surveyors, when they have had still-water, whisper a fourth: *it climbs the drawing* — that the future map is not a prediction but a tackle, that the city is hauled upward toward its own engraved intention. This is heresy, or engineering, depending on whom you ask, and the surveyors engrave it anyway, in copper, because paper is gossip and the future must be durable. They sell addresses at elevations that do not exist. Couples are betrothed at future benchmarks — *married at the fourth* — and the contract is a promise to be standing there together when the street arrives. False surveying, selling a future that will not bear weight, is the one fraud that gets a keeper spoken of quietly, forever, in the past tense.

The Chord-tuners are the nearest thing to priests, and their liturgy is adjustment. Eleven stacks, one chord, felt in the teeth. An apprentice spends twenty years learning to hear before being permitted to touch a vane; a master's whole career may contain three adjustments, each the size of a rumor. When a great tuner dies, the city listens, decades later, for their last work to arrive through the stacks — the smallest shift, propagating at the speed of masonry — and says: *they are still at it.* No tuner is ever finished. At noon, when the heat owns the streets and the hush is total, the tuners do not nap. They say noon is when the Chord is most itself, and they sit in it the way you sit with a sleeping child, in attention.

The other guilds are quieter about their devotions. The condensate carters own the dawn, the one loud hour, when the Fin Fields give back the night's work and the gutters sing and even that is a quiet song. A carter who sees a pipe-fox licking the vanes' undersides says nothing until hush hour — luck keeps better in silence — and leaves the outermost row of vanes unsqueegeed. The fox tithe. It is not written anywhere. It is older than most things that are. Below them, in the Drip Vaults, the cistern-keepers walk their ballrooms of darkness by ear; each drip has its note, and a keeper notices a new drip the way you would notice a stranger in your kitchen. They read their water in the eels — the blind, tallow-pale eels that filter the crusts — and they do not name them. Bad luck to name what you cannot see. When a vault must be drained for cleaning, once in a generation, the eels are moved in sealed buckets and the whole tier turns out, solemnly, the way one turns out for the relocation of elders. In the Drip Vaults also lives the city's private music, and its composers: the gutter-wrights, who shape rooflines the way others shape songs. A great gutter-wright's instrument is a district, and it performs once a day, at dawn, for one hour, and she has revised it twice in thirty years, and the city argues about her middle gutter the way other cities argue about symphonies, and the argument is the pleasure.

The round-makers are the luxury trade, the casters of shot and bearings, the guild that manufactures what water has not yet had time to make. Their christening stones are never new. A tumbled stone takes years, and so the stones are heirlooms, handed down at each christening, a family's one soft thing, each generation's grip polishing it further until the oldest of them look almost liquid, and the child who receives one is being given the family's whole history of patience, in the palm.

And the bloom-tenders keep one copper roof per district unscraped, for the argument. The philosophers insist the blue bloom lives; the chemists insist it does not; both sample from the same roofs and drink together afterward, because the argument is the friendship. In the high cemetery, a chemist's marker reads *She held that the bloom is not alive*, and across the path a philosopher's reads *It is*, and the path between them is the most visited in the district. The argument has been promoted, as graves are. It rises now above Stack Row, undefeated, and the bloom has not commented.

The fauna of the Calder is a short bestiary and most of it is arguable. The pipe-foxes, wire-sheened and long-tongued, are luck itself and are never owned; their shed whiskers, caught on scaffold wire, are collected, never plucked. The eels are infrastructure and company. The vat cultures are livestock that even the philosophers will not call alive, and the bloom is a citizen whose status is under eternal review. That is nearly all of it, unless you count the dead, which the keepers do.

The dead are the Calder's largest and most distinguished population, and its highest. A grave is a promotion, and promotions are taken seriously. On rest days, when the Long Bell has rung its single weekly note down into the Chord, families ride the inclines up with small offerings and the week's news, and sit with ancestors who are, slowly and unmistakably, ascending — grandparents now higher than the roofs they were born under, the pauper's marker six lifetimes old standing on its spire above the stacks, looking down on everything. They pour a thimble of condensate into the marker's cup, the only water anyone in the Calder gives away, and the cup drains to the gutters, and the gutters to the vaults. The dead pay their water forward. It is the measure of grief, and of love, that it costs.

Beyond the dead waits the other vast population: the unborn. The survey maps hold addresses for them like held seats — plots bought for grandchildren not yet conceived, terraces drawn and named and already, in the only sense that matters here, inhabited. The living of the Calder are a thin bright present between two multitudes, the ones who have arrived and are rising, and the ones who are expected. They think of themselves this way, and it does not trouble them. It is, they say, a good crowd.

The only ones who regularly leave are the salt-walkers, who descend wrapped and filtered to the white playa and come back with salt and wood and paper and lowland news, talking far too fast for a month after. They are the city's exotic class, half-foreign by profession, and children find them thrilling and exhausting. A few keepers each generation walk out toward the far smudge on the horizon, the place the surveyors say may be another Works or only the mountain practicing alone. They come back, mostly, with distances and silence, and their maps are not published, because a future that has not been surveyed twice is a rumor.

And under all of them, oldest and largest, the resident all the others keep: the mountain. The Engine-tenders name the piston houses and touch the housings to check their temperament, and when an Engine is stopped for its once-in-decades maintenance the whole tier sleeps badly and the babies cry, missing the lean the way you would miss a heartbeat in the bed. The Chord is the sleeper everyone tends; the gutter in the oldest tier is the mountain's homework, its first faint meander, and the guild has labeled the softening pebbles in its bed — the world's first river stones, ahead of their river. A warden watches them, an honorary post, and at dusk she brings children down to see the future, and the children stand with the Chord underfoot and the first stars out and ask the question — *does it lift, or does it rise?* — and the warden, who has heard every answer, gives the only one she trusts.

Wait, she says. The street rises.
