Everything that lives here lives at the wrong speed to be seen. That is the first thing to surrender. The midge's whole life is an afternoon; the film's afternoon is a grain of stone; the lichen's century is a thumbnail; the rim's repair of a breach, which takes years, is to the rim a flinch. No two inhabitants of the plateau can perceive each other as alive. So the census cannot be watched. It can only be inferred, the way everything else about this place is inferred — from deposits.

Begin with the citizens, because everything else here is a guest — though some guests have been coming for ten thousand years, and some citizens have never once been seen.

The films are not a population. They are a condition. Anywhere the water touches, they are the touching: a skin microns thick, present as texture and tint rather than as anything you could point to. They are zoned by chemistry the way a city is zoned by trade. At the seep-mouths, where the rising water still carries the plateau's deep body in solution, the iron-eaters and sulfur-eaters strip the minerals out and lay them down, and this is the whole origin of the ochre and the rust — those are not stains, they are citizenry, dense as a port district. Near a few of the mouths there is the faintest ghost of struck match on the air: the plateau's only smell besides wet stone. In the clear sunlit shallows live the light-feeders, tinting the pool floors olive, tobacco, dark wine at the deeper margins. In the shadowed slots between terraces, the gray-green films work the slow water. Each basin, looked at closely, has its own slight cast of life. The districts really are districts.

And beneath every living skin are meters of the dead. That is the ratio here: a rim is a fraction of a percent alive, and the rest of it is the same citizens, gone to stone, holding the wall up by being it. The city is a cemetery that has never stopped holding its funerals and builds exclusively with the buried — and this is not morbidity, it is masonry. Burial is the local form of success. The films do not know the plan and there is no plan; there is only the feeding, and the feeding has an architecture the way a flame has a shape. They have worked in unbroken succession longer than anything below has had a name. The colony on a given lip today is the same colony, in the only sense that matters, that laid that lip's first grain when the district was new.

The moss fringe is the one citizen visible from across a plaza: the green hem at every waterline, the workforce's public face. It lives by a strange contract. It grows outward, toward light and air, and is buried from behind, grain by grain, by the films it feeds and shads. A moss bed is therefore a colony in lifelong retreat from its own remains, walking away from its ancestors at the pace of growth. Where a breach shows you the inside of a rim, you can read it: page after page of moss-textured stone, the bed's whole ancestry shelved in order. Nothing here calls this tragedy. To be sealed in is to have one's work accepted.

The city has plumbing, and the plumbing is not empty. In the conduits where the seeps rise — cool, dark, heavy with dissolved stone, water that has never yet been weather — there are things thin as thread and pale as the stone itself, eyeless, because for the whole length of their lineage there has been nothing to see them with. They feed on what the water carries up. They are, in a sense, the first to taste the city. They are known only because of breaches: when a rim fails and a basin empties its hour of local roar, some are flushed out onto the daylight stone. They do not struggle. Nothing in their world has ever required struggle. By evening they are still, and within the year the mended rim has sealed them — everything here goes from life to archive, but nothing else makes the whole journey in a single season. No one has seen one alive twice. No one has named them. They may be the oldest residents on the plateau: citizens of the water from before the water had walls.

In the open pools, life thins to events. The least-cold margins hold midge larvae, red threads in the silt, and on the brief afternoons the hatch comes — the plateau's one abundance, a gauze over the warm pools, wings catching the doubled light. Each adult has hours. They mate, they lay, they die on the water, and the water closes over them and the films begin; the floors of the warm pools are dusted with the winged dead, and a dipper working the weir below a hatching pool stands in a slow rain of food. In the milky settling basins drift small comma-shaped things, rust-red specks riding the mineral milk, grazing whatever is loosest in the films' work — these are what the heron is doing here, when there is a heron. The small clear pools hold nothing visible at all. A pool can be too pure to be lived in; clarity is a kind of poverty; those are the city's quiet rooms, where even the films work slowly and the bottom is lensed pebbles and nothing else.

Then there is the one resident with a face. A dipper holds a whole flight of terraces — a hundred pools, a dozen weirs, one slate-gray bird the size of a fist. It feeds at the films' growing edge, taking the larvae, and it works underwater, gripping the smooth weir-stone against the pour in the green light beneath the sheet-flow, as though air were a habit it had heard about. Its voice is a single metallic note, and in a country where the loudest ordinary event happens once in a lifetime, that note is the closest thing to a schedule the plateau keeps. It nests behind a spill-curtain, in a dry slot, mortared with moss — the only dry bedroom in the wet city, rented from falling water. And here is the thing: the nests outlive the birds. The films find the abandoned cups and case them. There are nests in the rims now, stone pockets with the cup-shape still inside, eggshell sealed like an inclusion. The city keeps the dippers' tenancy on file, a thousand generations deep. To the dippers, the films are the ground. To the films, the dippers are a recent and charming weather.

The heron comes once in a season and stands in a settling basin, gray on milk-glass, like furniture left by a previous tenant of the world. It takes the comma-shaped drifters, leaves one footprint the size of a spread hand in the silt, and goes. Occasionally a film seals such a print: an afternoon, archived. At night in the crossing seasons, migrants pass high over the cloud cap, calling faintly from the dark, using the plateau's own weather as a landmark the way the lowlanders do; none of them land. Sometimes at first light a raptor quarters the dry tiers, after the herd's young, and is gone by full sun. These are events. A year here can be summarized in sightings, and a summary of sightings is a biography.

The dry quarters have their own citizens, and they are the only ones who work at the films' own speed. The lichens — rust and ash-green crusts on the retired stone, a thumbnail a decade — may be the oldest continuous lives on the plateau; some began when their district first went off duty and have persisted through the whole of its retirement. But they work in reverse. Their acids take the stone back, grain by grain. The wet city is written; the dry city is read; both at the pace of stone. The cushion plants sit bolstered against the wind, a century in the making of a footstool. Cotton grass nods its white heads in the silted pool floors, the dry city's only harvest, cropped occasionally — and this is what brings the herd: the gray, goat-like shapes far off on the white tiers, gone before you finish counting them. They come up for the mineral bloom as much as for the grass, licking the ochre crusts where iron weeps. Their prints go around the basins, always around: animals of detour, patient as water and with none of water's rights.

And the other tracks. Broad, round, pressed into the silt of basin floors, crossing rather than going around — entering the milky water on one side with no exit recorded on the other, as though the far rims refused to witness. The spacing says weight, and no hurry. There is never more than one. The tracks belong to the cold months, which is to say to the season without witnesses, and nothing else has ever been found: no scat, no hair, no feeding-sign, no body — and this in a place that archives every fallen bird, so that the absence itself is a kind of presence, a negative space in an otherwise complete record. Once, a traveler says, a breach-face showed a sealed trackway, the same print, laid down when that district was still wet. The films have since closed the file. If the account is true, then it has walked here longer than that district has been dry; the films have been covering its footprints since before the low country had words for what they were covering. What it eats, where it sleeps, whether it is one animal or a succession wearing a single shape — nothing is known. The plateau, which hides nothing, keeps this one thing the way its pools keep the sky: in plain sight, and without any intention of giving it back.

As for the dead — nothing here is wasted, nothing is buried, and everything is entombed. The films find a fallen bird within days. A decade makes it a smooth white shape with a guess inside it; a century makes it wall. Every rim is a crowded cemetery holding down a job, and walking the terraces you walk over the unmarked guests of ten thousand years. The city does not distinguish, in its masonry, between citizen and visitor.

People have tried, in their way, to be visitors at least. No one lives here — there is nothing to eat, nothing to burn, and the water reads every structure as a deposit in waiting. A herder's shelter left for a decade is a white hump with a door-shaped rumor in it. A forgotten cache becomes a nodule. Exactly one human thing persists: the cairn at the eastern rim, above the escarpment, and it persists only because it is maintained. Every traveler adds a stone; the lower courses are already sealed; it stands the way everything here stands, by moving — in this case upward, a stone a visit, the slowest construction in a country built by patience. It is the only lease the plateau grants. The rent is a stone.

At night the census continues, unwitnessed, which is its ordinary condition. The films do not sleep; the light-feeders idle and the mineral-layers work straight through the dark, because there has only ever been one shift. The drip ticks somewhere below. Ice skins form on the still basins and knock as they thicken. The dipper sleeps behind its curtain of falling water. The herd is a gray shape among white shapes on a dark tier, visible from nowhere. The pale things ride their water up through the body of the stone. Somewhere the tracks are being made, or not being made, and no one will know which until the melt shows the silt. The dead lie in the walls in the company of everyone, which is the plateau's whole idea of company.

All night the citizens lay down their grain, and the grain becomes the wall, and the wall becomes the record. The census is never taken and it is never wrong. Above the work, ten thousand small skies, one to a pool; beneath each sky, the workers; and the stars stand doubled over the city no one lives in — which turns out to be the most thoroughly inhabited place of all.
