There is a second volume of the survey plates, thinner than the others, bound in the same green-black cloth and lettered in the same patient hand: POPULATION. It opens with the sentence you already know — *of animals there are three kinds, and of the three, few* — and then it does something the other volumes never do. It speculates. Its margins are full, and the hand in the margins is smaller, and slower, and not always the same hand.

Counting here is a form of listening. You do not see the counted; you infer them from their patience, the way the city infers the towns. Nothing hurries, nothing hides, and nothing — the first plate insists, underlined — hunts. The food of this world is patience, and everything has enough.

Begin with the sump-walkers, because so much else begins with them. The census does not count them; it knows them. There are not so many that naming them is a burden — the margins carry working names in that small slow hand: the Concourse animal, the Comb grazer, the Long One of the eastern bowl — but the names are for the plates. The animals are their trails. A trail is a biography written in polish: a channel worn at walker width, at walker pace, across plazas, under the drip of the Comb, deepened year by year until the sinter shines like poured glass. Some trails are older than the streets that cross them. The founders paved over country that was already inhabited, and the animals kept their appointments anyway, and the curbs at the old crossings are worn into smooth notches where armor has shouldered through for centuries.

They accrete. Everything here does; the land does not erode so much as thicken, and a walker is a terrace that has taken up walking. Year by year the wet lays a skin of silica over the plates, so that age in a walker is not grayness but roundness — the old ones go nearly spherical, boulders with a gait, and their passing is less a walk than a slow decision the street eventually agrees with. They are blind because eyes were never the point here; they read the city through their feet — the pump-rhythm, the pipe-song — and the film's thickness by taste, at each unhurried pass of the mouth. Where a trail runs, the film grows a little sweeter just off the polished line, offering itself. The plates do not say the film courts the walkers. The margins say it.

And the brass. The plates note — with the restraint of men who do not wish to be laughed at by an empty city — that the inexplicable brightness at valve-stems and gauge-glasses is brightest at walker height, along walker trails; that the molts hanging from the handles, those pale gloves, are the sheaths of fore-limbs almost never seen; and that valves are found eased, now and then, ahead of pressure-spikes the Drum has no pins for, and gates found shut before floods, and that the schedule claims neither. What the hands do inside their gloves, they do slowly, and at night. The engineers, if there ever were engineers, are long past objecting. The maintenance crew nobody hired keeps its rounds at a pace that makes the film look hasty.

No plate records a small one. Either they are born somewhere the survey cannot see — down in the hot dark below the sumps, in the deep country's water — or they are not born within any span the survey spans, and the known animals are the known animals, and have been. Some trails have no maker anymore. A few of them end at cistern edges, and do not resume. The margins decline to speculate, which in the margins is the same as speculating.

In the wettest season, when the eastern bowl overflows and the outfall pipes take up their low fifth, the walkers of the district turn toward the sound — the trails bend, the plates say, as filings lean toward a magnet — and stand knee-deep in the overflow while the hour lasts, and let the tuned water sound through their armor. The film does not mind the fifth; it is the one vibration permitted, and the margins wonder whether the walkers come for it as a season, as a summer, as the closest thing this world makes to music made on purpose. And once in a turning of the Drum, or something near it — the margin gives two dates, far apart — the known trails converge on one flooded square, and three or four of them stand near enough to touch, glowing faintly where the film remembers them, and do not touch, and after an hour go back to their solitudes. The census calls this a gathering, because it has no better word. It suspects the walkers have one.

The lamp-moths come second in the census and first in the plates' affection, because they are the only animals here whose whole world can be taken in at a glance. A lamppost is a country with weather at its borders. Inside the light: warmth, the drip-tray's condensate, the iron column climbing out of the wet like the one tree the world provided. Outside: the dark, in which a moth has no business and no bearings. They hatch in the warm seam where the brass collar meets the iron; the larvae graze the loose skirt of the film at the post's foot, tolerated, or unnoticed, or kept — the film's preferences are its own; the pupa hangs beneath the drip-tray like a drop that decided against falling. Emergence comes at lamp-on, always at lamp-on, because moth time is lamp time: the sun cannot be trusted to show itself twice in a decade, but the schedule can be trusted, and is. A moth's day is the lit night. It sips condensate; it dusts the bulb with its wings, the smallest sound in the world, which here counts as a sound; the dust dims the glass a percent a week until the scheduled arm leans out and wipes it, and the dust of forty generations goes into the gutter, and the gutter takes it, and everything returns. A moth dies where a moth lived, in the light, and the film takes the body before morning. Nothing here is wasted. Waste is a dry-country word.

When the Drum's pins pass a darkened district and a lamp goes out for good, its people face the only choice Providence offers anything: stay, or cross the dark. Most stay. The condensate keeps its warmth a day, and they sip it out, and finish. But some fly — and between lamps there is no weather and no light and nothing to steer by, and a moth in the gap spirals down like ash — and a few, the margin says, shy with numbers, a few come down inside another lamp's weather and are folded into its people. The moths of every lamp are cousins for this reason: one people, held together across the dark by the ones who made the crossing. And then there is the last lamppost, out on the perimeter road, a mile from its nearest neighbor. A mile is ten thousand moth-lengths of nothing. The moths that have not found it go on not finding it. But the margin gives a date, and then, sixty years on, another, in a hand smaller than the rest: a moth completed the mile, and settled on the warm glass, and sipped. There is no protocol for this. The Drum has no pin for it. The lamp did not brighten. It is the only arrival on record — and the lamp was ready, as it is ready every night, as everything here is ready, in case.

The white eels are counted last of the three because the census is honest, and honesty admits that no one has ever seen a whole one. You see a head go by; a length of middle; later, a tail. They are the color of the deep water's privacy — not white, the margin corrects itself, but the color light becomes when it has given up — and eyeless, because the cisterns have never had a sky to lose. They do not stop growing. The old ones are thick as well-rope, and the furrows they leave in cistern silt are the true census, deep and old — and some of the furrows are wider than any eel now living, and the plates do not explain this.

A cistern is a sounding-box; the whole Works hums in it, and the eels read the hum the way you read a face. They know the Comb's mid-morning chord and its seasonal keys. They know the Drum. An hour before the Arrival — before the first lamp of the protocol has so much as warmed — the pumps lean into readiness, and the eels rise through the black water and hang in the upper cistern, heads to the grates, in the attitude of things listening at a door. The light, when it comes, they cannot see; eyes were given up before the founders' grandfathers. But the lamps make the surface water a degree kinder, and the eels hold station in that scheduled warmth for the length of the hour, and then sink. It is their summer, once a turning of the Drum. The city executes the Arrival for the towns that are coming; the eels keep it as a season. They are the only citizens for whom it arrives.

And sometimes — the plate is here, and it is short, and it is the last one with an entry of its own — a current moves against the pumping rhythm, up out of water deeper than the pumps reach, and it brings a taste of minerals the survey never assayed, and the film along the overflow lines glows without being touched, as if the memory arrived ahead of the event, and the walkers stand still in their districts a mile of rock away, and the eels arrange themselves along the walls like hands politely leaving a room. What passes then, passes. The water is not worse for it. Some years, the margin says, the water is better — sweeter; the pumps easier on their bearings for a season. The aquifer is not a tank. It is a country, and Providence is its border town, and the pumps do not take so much as they are permitted.

All of that is the counted three, and all of it together would not outweigh the uncounted one. By mass — the margins attempt the arithmetic, and then abandon it — the film is most of what lives here. It upholsters the Works; it keeps the seals; it has its preferences and its sulks; it persuades the gear-trains, decade by decade, into habits no blueprint records, and by now some of the liturgies are its compositions. The blower's weekly hour may be the film's own appointment: it thins itself in the flues the day before, the way a walker comes out of a glove, and the city sheds only what the film is ready to give. The fifth may be the one sound it has taught the city to make; for days after the overflow hour it blooms thicker and brighter along the outfall lines, as if sung to. And it is the archive the founders never built. Disturbed, it remembers in green-gold, briefly, and so every night the city reads its own day back in cursive: here a walker crossed the plaza; here a gutter overflowed; here a drip changed its rhythm and was news; here your own boots, surveyor, if you stood anywhere long enough to be worth recording. It keeps everything, briefly — which is the only way anything that matters here is kept.

It is also the long project. It seeds the sinter; molecule by molecule it is closing the city into one warm stone body, and this is not decay but completion. When the plates say Providence will not fall but fossilize, they are describing the film's intentions. Should it ever die, the seals would dry, the rust would unsettle from its argument, the pumps would falter within the decade, and the city would finally become what it has always passed for: empty. The cups on the sills are for the towns. Everything else — the margin says it plainly, in the smallest hand of all — everything else is for the film.

Last, the census lists the inhabitant it lists everywhere. The city is one slow body: water for blood, the Drum for a heart, the lamps for whatever in a body answers to nerves. It hums rather than hammers; it keeps its appointments; once a turning of the Drum it executes the Arrival, every lamp at once, gates open, fountains climbing, the whistles chorded like a held breath let go — the most hopeful hour in the world, with no one to see it, unless a walker has paused on the concourse, glowing faintly, grazing, which is not the same thing. No. But the protocol counts readiness, not witnesses, and by that accounting Providence has never yet failed an Arrival. Hope, the margins point out, is not a thing that requires an audience. The city is the one who is ready. It may be the only thing in the world whose faith has never been disappointed, because it has never asked its faith to be anything but practice.

The volume ends there, except for the final plate, which is blank but for two lines in the small slow hand. The first: *The ground warms everything. We never said the ground was empty.* The second: *Counted, they are few. Counted rightly, it is one.*

And if you want the census in a single entry, it is waiting out on the perimeter road, a mile from anywhere, over a mile of nothing. The last lamppost comes on at dusk and burns all night — for the road, for the towns, for the one moth a century that completes the crossing and settles on the warm glass and sips. There is no protocol for the moth. There didn't need to be. The light does not waver. It is on time. It would be on time anyway — that, the plates say, is the whole of it: readiness kept so long, and so well, that it has become indistinguishable from love.
