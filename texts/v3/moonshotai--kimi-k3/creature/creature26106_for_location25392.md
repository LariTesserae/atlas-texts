Everything that lives here arrived through one of two doors: the river's or the sky's. The river's door closed behind its children long ago. The sky's door — the nine wells — stands open, and the traffic through it at dusk is the loudest thing in the world. The two peoples have different addresses and different memories, but their economies are stitched so tightly together that by now it is hard to say where one ends and the other begins.

The old population are the river's orphans. The fish, the amphipods, the isopods were left behind when the water was called away mid-gesture, and they keep its memory the only way flesh can — in their shapes. Pale, because pigment costs and there is no one to buy it. Eyeless, because the dark is complete. Slow, because the cold is complete too, cold enough to ache, and a fish's heart here beats somewhere in the spaces between drips. Near the arteries — the sinking stream, the pools that receive the wells' leavings and the colony halls' fallout — the pools are crowded the way a church is crowded on a feast day, bodies hanging nearly motionless in the clear water, generations stacked in the same cold inch: great-grandmothers and fry side by side, because with no seasons there is nothing to spawn by, and so spawning happens the way everything rare happens here, without announcement, whenever enough has been saved. Farther out, at the map's edges, the pools thin to single occupants. Some pool at the end of an hour's walk holds one fish, and it is old, and it has the water the way a held note has a hall. Feeding, for all of them, is a lottery. A drowned cricket is a festival. A bat that falls from the ceiling is a jubilee: the amphipods arrive like a rumor and within a day there is nothing left but a clean architecture of bones. The isopods work the pool margins on permanent night shift, taking the scum-rings, the shed skins, the slack of everything. And the one small stream that still threads its kilometer of canyon is their last highway — it carries what little drifts, and where it sinks politely into gravel, the gravel itself is faintly inhabited, amphipods threading the wet interstices down into the dark below the dark.

Between the two peoples, in the wells alone, there is green. The ferns banding the upper shafts, the moss lower down, the algae filming the wet stone, the scatter of saplings on the talus cones — these are the only things below the sky that make their living from light, and they make it on rations. A sapling on a talus cone gets its brief hour when the column of daylight swings across it, and grows the way the stalactites grow: just beginning, forever. There are birches down there the height of your knee with the growth rings of elders, bent toward a sun that visits once a day and briefly. Nobody prunes them. The light does. The roots that dangle from ledges drink nothing but the shaft's breath, and it is enough. Up at the rims, small seed-eating birds work the grey grass and squabble at the edges, and now and then one tumbles in, stunned, and becomes part of the wells' account — which is to say, part of everything else's.

The second people came through the sky, and their great fact is the bats. Hundreds of thousands, layered so densely on the colony-hall ceilings that the roost is crowded past any human idea of crowding — and yet it is a city of addresses, each bat returning dusk after dusk to its own remembered few centimeters of rock. They are the artery of the whole system. Out over the plateau at night they harvest the thin insect life that the grey grass and thorn give up — a sparse people of moths and beetles that pulses briefly after each of the year's dozen rains and is gleaned between them — and they carry it home in their bodies and drop it as guano. Everything below that is not green eats, in the end, the bats' commute. Their cycle is so exact it functions less like behavior than like a clock, and their lives match it: bats live long for so small an animal, and here a long life means the same night, flown well, thousands of times.

The floor answers the ceiling. The guano dunes are meters deep and do not grow, because they are eaten as fast as they fall. Beetles work the surface in shifting sheets; fly larvae churn beneath; mites hunt other mites through the grains; springtails thread the whole; fungi bloom over it in pale pelts and take what the insects leave. It is a soil compressed into a few halls, doing in one room what a forest floor spreads across a hillside, running at the edge of ammonia, never resting. A dead beetle is dismantled by its own crew. Nothing is wasted because nothing can be afforded to be.

The swifts keep the other shift. Their nests — cups the size of half a walnut, mud and spit, set in rows on ledges above the bats — are the entire architecture of this world, the most anything anywhere builds. At dawn and dusk the wells carry two rivers past each other, the bats pouring one way and the swifts the other, a two-way street in a sheer cylinder with no collisions, ever. Their clicking is the only fast sound in the deep cave, a ticking that moves. Their droppings pepper the ledges — a second, daintier rain for the isopods who patrol below.

The crickets are the pasture and its sound. Shoulder to shoulder across the twilight zones, wingless, long-antennaed, pale as the stone they cover, they graze the fines of the two rainfalls: guano flour, leaf-shred, fungal film, shed skins, the meat-dust in the middens — the whole cave ground small enough for a cricket. Their song has never had a reason to change. There has been no weather to change it for, and so the chorus thickening near every well is, note for note, the chorus of a century ago, sung from the same walls, sometimes by descendants so like their grandmothers that the difference is a technicality. When a bat gleans low along the wall, a hush moves through the singing and closes behind it like water behind a hand.

Then there are the lights. Along the damp walls near the sinking stream the gnat larvae hang their curtains and shine, and their light is addressed to almost no one. The fish cannot see it. The crickets do not look up. The bats hunt elsewhere, by ear. It is for the midges that hatch in the stream's wet gravel, which kept their eyes; for the rare moth that blunders down a well; and for you. It works anyway — a trap does not need an audience, only customers. The larva waits for weeks on its lines. The adult does not eat, lives a few days, lays its eggs on the same stretch of wall. The constellation is hereditary. It is the one sky this world has, it is indoors, and it does not change.

The snakes keep the cave's two appointments and are geology between them. Draped along the well ledges, heavy-bodied and still as roots, they feel the exodus before they hear it: the colony's roar pushes air down the passages ahead of it, a breath before the body, and along the ledges the heads come up together like a field turning in wind. Then two minutes of striking, a bat taken on the wing's edge, another, another — then stillness again, and the slow shape of a meal traveling down the body over days. The colony pays them the way a sea pays gulls; in the arithmetic of hundreds of thousands, the tax is invisible. Their shed skins lie where they were shed, and because nothing weathers, they lie there for decades, crisp as handwriting.

And the snails — the slow cattle of the wells and the twilight zones, thick white shells sealed with a limestone door when the shaft runs dry, grazing the fern band and the dimmest algae at a pace the eye cannot hold. In that still air their slime dries to silver and stays; the walls keep their accounts for years. Their entire philosophy is the shell. It is also their undoing, because the last creature in this account has built its whole life around opening it.

If you came down here looking for a *who* — and eventually you would; the place raises the question — the answer is a grey bird with a hammer. The knocker-bird is stocky, rock-grey, heavy-billed, strong-necked, built like a mason. It flies between wells at dusk, low and silent over the grey grass, but below ground it walks, and it knows the labyrinth the way the labyrinth knows sound: by delays. Whether it intends this or not is not knowable, but the hammer-strike does double duty. It opens the snail, and it takes the hall's measure — the crack carries through three halls, and the bird stands still with its head cocked and receives their answers. It selects stones for heft and face, carries a favored one from anvil to anvil for a while, then drops it. It carries snails hundreds of meters past perfectly good stones to one particular hollowed block, because the rightness of a particular anvil is something the bird knows and cannot say. It picks single singers out of a wall of a thousand crickets, deliberate as a shopper. It drinks at drip lines, waiting out the intervals, counting them the way you do.

Everything else in the cave is a cycle — drip, chorus, exodus, return. The hammer-strike is the one sound that is not. It happens when the bird decides, and so each crack is an event: singular, unscheduled, the only punctuation in a text of pure recurrence. And the middens beneath the anvils grow, by a shell at a time, which makes them the only heaps in this world that grow — the one net deposit in a place where nothing accretes. You could read a bird's whole life in its midden: the fat years, the lean years of thin-shelled snails, the season it favored one hammer until the hammer split. No one reads it. The bird does not read it. It is history written and filed by accident, which is the only kind of history this world permits.

And for all of this, most of the map belongs to nobody. Between the islands the galleries run empty for kilometers, used but never kept — the bats cross them commuting, the bird passes through on its rounds, once in a while a lost cricket ticks briefly and goes quiet, a sound with no chorus to join. In the emptiest halls the only presence is a drip falling every twenty seconds onto the spot where the last one fell. If faithfulness counts as residence, the drips are the oldest tenants: each holds the address it held before anything now alive here was born.

That is the shape of every life in this place: long, local, repeated. Nothing that happens to any of them has not happened before. The cave holds its breath, and everything in it has learned to live on held breath — except, twice a day, the two great exhalations of the bats, and except, at its own hours, a grey bird walking the dark between its anvils. When a knocker-bird dies, its stones remain, and its cobbles, and its midden; and sooner or later another bird finds the hollowed block and the shell scatter and understands them at a glance, and takes up the rounds, and the crack of it carries through three halls. Nothing here is kept. But the habit is kept, passed from bird to bird by way of the ground itself — and in a place where nothing changes, that is as close as anything comes to being remembered.
