Nothing lives here. That is the first thing every newcomer scratches on a slate, and the first thing every keeper scratches out.

It is nearly true. Stand at the rim for an hour and the tally is easy: water, stone, sound. No fish in the churn, no frog, no insect worth the name, nothing rooting in the scree. A valley pond holds more kinds in a cupful than the soft spot holds in its whole white acre. The keepers keep the census on a slate by the first hut — the full list of kinds, in scratch-script, short enough to carve at one sitting. In two hundred years it has been amended four times. Once, something was crossed out.

But a list of kinds is not a list of residents. That distinction is the beginning of keeper-work, and it takes a season to learn, because here the question of what lives cannot be separated from the question of what counts as living — and the second question is the one the bowl itself is asking, every seven seconds, at the top of its voice.

Begin with the ones everyone agrees on.

---

The stonegrazers are the bowl's one multitude, and they are not from here.

After a strong fountaining week they are simply there: pinheads first, then thumbnails, pale as the water that carried them, translucent as the first skin of ice on a puddle. Hold one to the light and you can watch it digest — the grey meal moving through it like weather through a valley. They have no eyes. They have no need of eyes. The world they were made for is dark, and the world they find themselves in does not last long enough to be worth seeing.

For that is the fact of them: they graze on a medium that sets. The new crust they rasp is workable for a day, no more; then it greys, hardens, rings under the knuckle. A stonegrazer's life is a race against its own dinner. Their shells never thicken — they cannot afford stone, stone is what they eat and stone is what buries them — and each of them travels inside a thin chemistry of postponement, a film around the foot where the water cannot quite make up its mind to crystallize. The cursive trails they leave are not scratches. They are the written record of a delay: lines where the knitting was talked out of happening, for an hour, by the passage of a small soft stubborn body.

That is why the keepers read them half in earnest, and only half. Where the script is dense, the growth has been eaten back, and the rim there will hold through winter. It is not prophecy. It is bookkeeping.

And sometimes the race is lost. You find them mid-trail: the cursive line running smooth and steady, then a low dome, no bigger than a lentil, grey and ringing. The trail does not fade. It ends. The mountain punctuates. Most keepers leave these where they lie. A few collect them and carry them down at season's end in the same cloth as the ejecta, and if there is a difference, the slates do not record it.

Where they go when the fountain slackens, the keepers know and do not know. The grazers gather at the wandering hump — the standing dome of water that travels the bowl on its day's circuit — and they ride it, hundreds at a time, a slow grey moving across the white. When the hump collapses, it takes its passengers down. The hump is their ferry; the breath of the basin is their timetable. Whether the same individuals ever come up twice, whether what waits below is a nation, a reservoir, or a single vast slow something that buds them off like spores — on this the slates are silent, because on this the keepers are too.

---

The two are easier to count and harder to know.

One pair. Black, red-billed, chough-like and not choughs, say the valley people, who know choughs. They nest in a fissure of the north wall, in a cup of black moss lined — the one keeper who ever climbed to look reported this, long ago, and was believed — with the translucent shells of stonegrazers, hundreds of them, a bed of mica-scales over moss. Below the fissure the wall grows outward a hand's breadth a year, entombing whatever falls from the nest: pellets, shell-shards, the small armour of every meal the pair has ever taken, laid down in order, oldest at the bottom. A determined keeper with a copper saw could cut that ledger open and read forty generations of dinners. No keeper has. The slates say the ledger is not theirs to read.

They work to the breath. That is the detail newcomers notice last and keepers notice first. The basin breathes on seven seconds — swell, spill, draw back — and the birds ride the overflow plume's updraft, tipping sideways in the wet air, dropping to the fresh shelves at the draw-back to pick grazers off the new crust, airborne again before the swell. When the grazers dwindle, the birds commute: down the young river's corridor at dawn on the falling wind, back up by dusk. They are not fed by the bowl. They are kept by it. The roar is their fortress; nothing with claws will hunt through that wall of sound.

They open their beaks in the troughs between pulses. A keeper two generations back logged this and underlined it twice: four openings per breath, timed to the gaps, regular as a loom. Whether anything comes out, no one can say. Nothing reaches a human ear through the roar, and the hush-cells that might settle the question are too far from the water and face the wrong way. The hand-cant has a sign for it — two fingers opening and closing against the thumb — that means both *the birds' possible calling* and *any speech nobody hears*. It is the sign they use, kindly, for newcomers talking to themselves.

Where the young go, the slates do not know. One autumn in three there are briefly three birds; then the updraft takes one down the river, and the pair are the pair again. Once, in the oldest slates, there is a winter entry — in the hand of a keeper who crossed the scree early to check the gauge and found the fortress already garrisoned, both birds riding the plume through falling snow, in the month when the bowl was supposed to be unwatched. Whether they ever leave at all is an open question. The keepers do not sign about it. Some questions are the birds'.

---

The silver lichen is the oldest resident, and it has never once lived on anything old.

It grows only on stone less than a year old — how it knows, no one knows — and so its whole life is a migration, a slow wave that exists only at its crest. Trace its edge and you trace the rim's own growing: a living silver tide-line along the outermost lip, a hand's breadth wide, advancing as the stone is born, dying behind as its ground matures beneath it. Cut a transect — one keeper did, two centuries back, and the slates still cite her — and you get, in order: living silver, then greying crust with silver ghosts, then nothing. A wave, made visible by its own wake.

It may be one individual. That is the current conjecture, argued in the evenings with the cant's most patient signs: a single organism creeping outward at the pace the stone is born, cloned from itself across all the years of the rim, so that the bowl wears one continuous ring of the same body. If so, it is older than the huts, older than the gauge-obelisk, older perhaps than keeperhood itself — and it has spent all those centuries surfing newness, never once touching age. The most venerable thing in the bowl, and the only thing here that has never been at home on anything venerable. The cant has a sign for it that the valley tongue has no word for. The sign means *old-young*. It is used for nothing else, and it is not exactly a compliment, and not exactly not.

It colonizes the ejecta first. Before an ejected sphere has dried, the silver is on it — days ahead of the ordinary crust — as though whatever quality the lichen reads as youth, the shaft exhales most purely. A spiral stone from the deep dark, formed no one knows when, possibly old as the range itself: *young*, says the lichen. The youngest thing here. The keepers who weigh such things weigh this heavily.

The black moss keeps to the lee of the huts and the spray-line, soot-colored, content, asking nothing. The sedge is an accident that recurs: a wind-seed from the valley, a blade or two most decades. Once a tussock lasted three seasons, and the rim rose around it to the waist, and it went on growing out of its own entombment, a green flag flying from a fist of new stone. The slates mention it the way almanacs mention comets. It is gone now. The cant borrowed the valley word for it — *grass* — the only loanword in the sign-tongue, retained for elegy.

One more entry belongs in this section, though it is not alive. The black hush-stone of the north vein: the lichen will not touch it, avoids it by a finger's width all around; the grazers are never found on it. Whatever that stone is, it is not young, and by every test the bowl offers, it never was. It is the rim's one elder. The keepers sleep inside it.

---

And then there is the animal that thinks it is keeping the place.

One at a time, one season each, sent up from the valleys the young river waters. The river below never keeps its bed — it is born mid-roar and carries the habit downward, migrating across its valley like a sleeper turning — and the people there farm its patience and read its moods, and when the scree sheds its snow they send one of their number up the notch to read the source. It is not a priesthood, exactly. It is a hydrology with manners.

They pack in everything; nothing here feeds a body. They work. The path of fitted stones is swallowed and re-laid, perpetually; doorways narrow and are re-cut; the obelisk is read against its rings; the grazer-script is logged; the pitch is checked once a week against the witness — a copper bar carried up two hundred years ago, struck inside a hush-cell, that has not changed its note since. The mountain has. The interval between the bar and the drone is the oldest conversation in the bowl, and it is going somewhere, a semitone a century, and the slates record it in the scratch-script's most careful hand, because that is the one kind of news here that matters to everyone below.

The cant has signs for the kinds of growth — budding, shelving, filming, doming — and for pitch drift, and for grazer-density, and for the hump's whereabouts. That last sign, palm up, fingers loosely closing, is the same sign used for a person present in a hut. The presence sign. Ask whether that is grammar or opinion and you will get the open palms.

Newcomers dream noise for a month; then the dreams thin. A keeper who begins dreaming pattern is sent down early — that stands in the slates as policy, not superstition, with three names beside it and no commentary. The second rule is the bowl's only real law, and two have broken it, in the early generations. One entry says *went down*, and the valley histories say he *came down*, and both may be true, and they are not the same down. The other's entry is scratched out so thoroughly that the slate is thinner there.

The returned are easy to pick out, down in the valleys. They startle at bells. They keep a slate by the bed. They wake in the deep quiet and lie listening to nothing, content, the way you listen for a sleeper's breathing. When the river shifts, they are consulted. When they die, their season's log is carried back up and left at the notch, and the rim takes it in a year or two, and that is understood to be the whole of the funeral.

---

Everything else that lives here — if it lives — comes up the shaft.

The ejecta the bowl gives up a few times a season: nodules, spirals, the hollow sphere, stones shaped like vertebrae of nothing that ever walked or swam. The keepers sort them on the drying shelf by resemblance, and the resemblance is getting harder to dismiss. The forms recur. A spiral this decade answers a spiral three decades gone, a half-turn tighter, as though a hand below were practicing. The lichen says they are young. The grazers ignore them — nothing to postpone, nothing to rasp — which is also information: whatever they are made of, it is finished, while everything else here is still being drafted. *Fossils of things that never lived* is the keepers' phrase, and it is exact, and it bothers them, because the shelf is beginning to look like a progression.

And once — this is the crossed-out kind — a keeper three generations back logged something in the water. High-fountain week, grazers thick, the hump tall: a paleness, the length of an arm, moving against the current in the white churn, seen four times in one hour, then not again. The next keeper crossed the entry out. The keeper after reinstated it, and signed it *seen once* — the cant's most precise sign, a flat hand turned slowly over, palm down to palm up, like a stone turned in a stream. It stands in the census slate today with both marks on it, and newcomers ask, and keepers give the open palms. The same old log notes, almost as an afterthought, that the birds stopped stooping at the water that week, and worked the far rim only, for nine days.

---

Round out the census, then, with the residents that are not kinds.

The hump wanders the bowl on its day's circuit, and it has habits. It lingers at the north wall in the mornings; no one knows why. It rears by the notch some days as if to look out at the drop. The keepers log its whereabouts the way one mentions family. It is not alive. It is not, exactly, not company. The grazers think it is a road.

And the chord — the drone with the pulse, the sound that was here before the rim and will be here after the last slate is swallowed. It breathes. It is rising. It will cease, on the far day when the soft spot knits. It has everything a life has except a body, and it is growing the body: every shelf and curtain and entombed winter is a held note, the whole bowl its accumulation. The keepers do not say the chord lives in the stone. Listening, season on season, they have come to suspect the stone is the chord, slowed.

So the slate reads, if you let it: two red-billed birds; a nation of stone-eaters, visiting from the dark; one lichen, ancient, never at home on anything old; soot-moss; the ghost of a grass; one crossed-out paleness; one standing wave; one sound; one human, counting.

And in the hush-cell, where the roar thins to a rumor in the bones, you hear your own heart for the first time in days — and that is the last entry, the one no keeper scratches because it scratches itself. The tally always includes the one who tallies. One season, no more.

*Long enough to be counted. Not long enough to be kept.*
