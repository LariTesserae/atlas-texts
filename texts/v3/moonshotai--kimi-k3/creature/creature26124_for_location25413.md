Count what lives here and you will run out of animals before you run out of fingers, and spend the rest of your life on the remainder. The census of the Anneal is short. The counting takes forever, because everything on the list moves at the pace of the salt, and so — if you stay — will you.

You will meet a smelter-grazer as geography first: a low outcrop on a horizon that yesterday held none, the tallest thing within three days' walk. Then the outcrop has a pace. A grazer's back is the only landscape that moves — a shell the size of a small room, worn smooth as the plane itself, and on its high curve a garden of glass lichen, pale rosettes, the only lichen in the world that travels. The archivists say the grazers were made, once: harvesters, maintenance, meant to gather the conductive dust and deliver it somewhere the world has since forgotten. If so, they have been feral longer than most civilizations last, and they go on doing the work anyway, unpaid, uncollected, leaving the minting behind them — a dotted line of pure ingots running straight to the horizon, copper, silver, the occasional bead of gold. The minting is the only handwriting the plain permits. Trails persist for centuries before the plane renegotiates them, and travelers steer by old mintings the way sailors once steered by currents. No one has seen a grazer born. No one has seen one die, either, though there is a low hill nine weeks' walk from the tether stump that the keepers do not talk about, and that the lichen is very slowly closing over.

When the bell-note comes — the expansion seam, a hundred kilometers off, struck by the day's heat — a grazer lifts its head. You will not see the lift; it takes an hour. But if you are standing near one, put your hand flat on the ground: minutes later, sometimes an hour later, comes the answer, a hum too low for the thin air, arriving through your palm and the bones of your arm, felt in the teeth. The grazers answer the seams. It is how they find each other — how they keep their own census across a continent that holds perhaps four of them. The conversation has one exchange a week, and neither party has ever been in a hurry to conclude it. Two grazers meeting is a generational event; courtship takes decades; there is a pair predicted to converge within the century, a year's walk apart and closing, and pilgrims have already begun planning their lives around being there.

The seam-walker you will meet as infrastructure: a raised seam crossing your path, a line of struts, a bridge. It is mostly leg. It walks the expansion joints with one foot on each lip, patient as the joint itself, eating the wiregrass — and the grass knows it is coming: kilometers ahead of the walker, the tufts fold, a traveling dusk that moves with the animal down the seam. Its joints are expansion seams in miniature, every segment lapped and tolerated like the plane, and you will stand there unable to decide whether the world was made in the shape of this animal or the animal in the shape of the world. It is also the only road. If it is going your way — at a body-length an hour, if your way is its way — you climb the struts and ride, and the walker does not notice, or notices and forgives, the way footprints are forgiven. Travelers have crossed whole countries on a walker's back, sleeping between segments, woken each dusk by the dry tick of the grass folding far below.

The motes belong to the haze and the haze keeps them, the way the plain keeps what walks. Each one is a small capacitor, and at dusk they speak to each other in sparks — slow, courteous, minutes apart, a conversation conducted at the speed the light leaves the sky. The archivists, who want everything to be language, have counted the intervals and found no grammar. The keepers, who want everything to be weather, say it is only charge. The slow ones watch them the way you would watch a fire, and do not say what they see. Once a season the dust-walls come, lit from inside by their own friction, and the motes go into the storm the way salmon went into rapids: it is their season, their gathering, their spawning. Afterward, for a few weeks, the plain is littered with their spent skins — kilometers of crumpled cellophane, faintly phosphorescent — until the plane, patiently, flattens them. A mote never touches the ground alive. When you find a skin you are holding the whole of the sky's fauna in your two hands, and it weighs nothing, and it is still faintly warm.

The springs are the crowds. The census counts what you can see from standing height; crouch at a salt-spring, in the amber glow, and the count falls apart — glass-mites in the crusts, thread-things sipping at the filaments' drill-holes, a whole compressed economy living in the warmth that rises out of the world's one sea. The filaments stand in their spiral beds, tended — though no one tends them — and at dusk the motes descend to the light, and there will be a grazer there, often, standing in the glow, having walked a century to reach it, staying a year. The springs are the inns of the Anneal: the only places you will meet another traveler by accident, where "by accident" means "within the same decade." Everyone comes to the springs eventually. The plain is wide and the springs are few and the math does the rest.

The fifth finger of the census is kept for the mouths — by those who count them as animals. The archivists keep that finger folded: apparatus, they say, intake for the salt's circulation, machinery that forgot its function and kept the motion. The keepers say an animal, and old. Ask the grazers — you cannot ask the grazers, but watch their routes, which bend around certain basins by a full day's walk, and consider what it cost, and when, to learn that bend. The people who live here learn the grammar of the notes underfoot the way sailors once learned birds: the seam-notes, daily and bright; the low warm chords when a region wakes; and the other one, the one a mouth strikes when it closes after feeding, lower, longer, satisfied. You will learn to hope you never hear it near. When any note comes, the plain-born stop walking for the length of it — one breath, two — the way you pause when someone far off calls a name that might be yours.

Because there are plain-born. No one is from here, strictly; everyone arrived. But there are those whose grandparents were born under the cream sky, who have never seen a cloud and do not believe in them, who tell time by the ground — for the plain tells time, and once you can hear it, every clock you carry begins to feel like panic. There are the travelers, passing through to somewhere the Anneal is not. There are the archivists, walking the gray bands, reading the fossil script the way tree rings are read — not the words, never the words, but the regimes: here the world built fast; here it paused for ten thousand years; here something was erased and re-laid. One school has found the same seven-band sequence on two continents, and calls it a signature. Another calls it a chorus. A third calls it coincidence, and is not believed. There are the keepers, who maintain the caches and the waymarks and know better than anyone the central lesson of the place: you cannot make a mark here. Pile stones and return in a year and the pile will be flat — not scattered; flat. So the keepers mark nothing and memorize everything, and a keeper is known entirely by their route, and a keeper's death is defined, by consensus, as the year their caches run out.

And there are the slow ones. Every group recruits the same way: a traveler stays, and stays, and begins to keep the world's time. It starts in the chest. The salt's pulse runs one beat a minute, and a resting heart, given a month of that silence, finds the pace and rests there — slightly slower than the one you arrived with, and impossible, afterward, to speed back up for long. The plain-born are born to it. The slow ones walk slower, speak less, stop traveling altogether; they sit at the springs or on the warm backs of grazers and let the decades pass. Dust does not settle on them. The lichen begins, very slowly, to consider them — a rosette at a shoulder, a pale crust along a boot — and the oldest of the slow ones are half-lichened and not dead, and if you sit with one for a full day you will hear it hum, once, a note through the ground, and you will feel the ground answer. Whether they are being read, or kept, or simply included, no one asks. It is considered the one rude question.

Ask instead who lives here, at the end of the counting, and every answer comes back the same. The grazers, the walkers, the motes, the grass, the guests — all of it is passenger. The resident is the plane: matter that is not dumb but has forgotten, repair and erosion at their long negotiation, the world kept the way a room is kept for instruments. The makers left, or stopped, or became it — and the slow ones are how you watch the third option happen in real time, at a hand's span a century. Once in a century a region wakes, and everything that walks, walks there, and the people come too, from a season's travel in every direction, and for a few years the world has what it otherwise never has: crowds, and news, and a purpose it declines to explain. The archivists read by the light from below. The keepers open their caches. The slow ones stand in the warm nights humming with the ground, and no one asks them to stop. Then it lets go, and the dispersal is the only diaspora the Anneal knows, and the lichen creeps back over everything, and the calendar closes.

So stand on the plain at dusk and attend. The wiregrass folds with its dry tick. The motes begin their slow sparks overhead. A grazer, a month's walk off, lifts its head. Far away something the size of a valley exhales and closes, and seconds later the bell-note arrives through the soles of your feet, and everything around you leans toward it — almost imperceptibly, and you will not decide, this time, that you imagined it. This is the census. Once an hour, once a day, the world strikes its note, and everything that lives here turns toward the sound: the grass, the motes, the walkers on their seams, the slow ones in their lichen, you. Everything that leans is counted. The Anneal counts itself, and keeps the count, and keeps the counted — and for as long as you are standing there, leaning, that is what lives here.
