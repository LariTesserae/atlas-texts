Ask what lives here and you must first decide what counts as one thing.

At a week, the answer is one. The mat is the resident; the towers and tufts and vaulted channels and cupped lakes are furniture it grows and throws away. A single being, floor to far edge, and you are walking on it.

At a month, the answer is many. The cells flood on different clocks. The tissue varies cell to cell—more bronze here, more pepper there—and the dikes could be walls between selves as easily as levees. A congress, then: citizens fused at the root, negotiating in water.

Stay a season and you stop answering. *Individual* is an animal word, for bodies with edges, that die when cut. Cut this being and there are two of it until the wound closes, and then there is one again, and neither piece remembers the divorce.

The emptiness is its body. The presences are its organs. Up here there is nothing that is not it, except the rock's heat, the weather, and you.

No one announces that you have met it. You spend your first days treating it as ground, because it lies there like ground, and the realization arrives in pieces, each one deniable. The pulse under your flat hand. The footprint gone within the hour—not eroded, not filled: *grown over*, closed like water. The drainage trench you cut on Monday vaulted by Friday, connected, flowing, your ditch promoted into the plumbing on a gradient truer than your tools could have made. It does not demolish your works. It hires them.

And all the noise—because it is loud, you learn that by the first afternoon storm—is body noise. The groaning of the towers is its joints. The knocking in the pipes is its circulation. The crackle is its tissue. The rain plays it like an instrument it never built. It has no voice, and you can hear it living everywhere.

The anatomy is guesswork, because the patient heals faster than you can cut. Incisions weep hot water and seal in days. A sample carried down the mountain outgrows its jar before the treeline. So the organs get named by metaphor, and the metaphors keep shifting. The springs are mouths drinking from the deep heat—except there are no mouths. The standpipes are hearts it grows by the dozen, uses for a month, and lets burst. The channels are veins. The stepped basins are stomachs, or bladders. The cells, flooding and draining, are a lung breathing on a count you have not found. The white cords falling off every edge are its long exhalation into countries it will never touch. Lift the mat where a fresh scarp gives you an edge and there is no soil beneath: only older mat, gray and compressed and warm, layer on layer, down past your arm, past your pole. It stands on its own past bodies. It is its own ground.

It runs on at least three clocks, and none of them is yours. The fast clock is the crackle: a shoot lengthening against your palm, a tower from nothing to mist-crown in a fortnight, a collapse reduced to a green mound in three days. The slow clock is the flood rhythm, which is not the storms' rhythm and not the geysers' and may be the mountain's—the mat keeping time with the fault, a body synced to its own rising bones. The deep clock is the mat itself. Nothing on the plateau is old except it, and its age is not countable: not old the way a tree is old, rings and a dateable heart, but old the way a river is old—continuous, rebuilt, itself the whole way back.

It keeps one piece of its own history, and it keeps it walled. Two or three days in—the routes change with the map, but everyone finds it eventually—there is a cell that died. Black water. Sulfur. A wrong heat. Nothing growing. Around it stand the tallest dikes on the plateau, thick as city walls, and every channel that once fed it is plugged with scar-fiber, deliberate as sutures. The mat walled off its own rot, and it has kept the wall. Everything else up here is weeks old; the map redraws itself monthly; the quarantine does not move. The one permanent feature on the plateau is the place where the plateau was sick.

It has no eyes, no ears, no center. It is sensitive everywhere and attentive nowhere. Press your palm beside a new shoot and the shoot leans into your hand's warmth—tropism, surely. Sleep a week in one spot and shoots rise over the bedroll; move the bedroll; they find it again—tropism, surely. Camp a month and the flood rhythm of your cell slides half a day around your habits, and you write in the notebook, underlined: *coincidence, surely.* The word *surely* works harder up here than any other word you brought.

It will not kill you on purpose, because it has no purposes at your scale. It will not refrain, either. The towers fall where they fall. A spring-wound is not a mouth; falling into one is not being eaten; it is being scalded, and then, within the week, composted into the pulse. Nothing up here is buried. Everything is absorbed.

Almost everything alive here is the mat. *Almost.* Lean over a channel at night, where the water glows faint green, and look closely: the glow resolves. Motes, pin-small, adrift in the flow, each trailing its smear of light. Whether they are passengers the mat tolerates, or cells it sheds that keep their own counsel awhile, is unsettled and may be unsettleable—the line between citizen and blood cell is exactly the line this place exists to blur. They orient to warmth. Cup your hands in the dark water and they come to you, slow green swirling in your palms, and it is the closest the plateau comes to a greeting. You will be told it is chemistry. It is chemistry. You will still say goodnight.

Then there are the flowers. A standpipe lives a few weeks and spends its last of them in bloom—pale, enormous, sweet past all reason, sheets of perfume going out on a wind that carries nothing with a nose. There are two readings and you will hold both forever. The kind one: scent and color are chemistry, wind-work, heat-management, and the word *flower* is something you brought up the mountain with you, along with your need. The unkind one: the design expects a partner—wings, a tongue, a throat—that has never stood here, and it has performed the courtship anyway, faithfully, every tower, every fortnight, into the absence, for as long as the mat has been the mat.

It is not lonely. Loneliness is a missing, and nothing here is equipped to miss. But you will stand in the wreck of a fallen tower with the sweetness thick enough to taste, for no one, and you will find the universe briefly hard to forgive.

When a tower bursts, the bloom goes out on the blast—seeds, spores, whatever they properly are—and the wind takes them over the rim, down through the cloud belt, into the cold. Downwind countries have been searched. There is no second one. A cutting survives, technically, in a heated house in the lowlands: grayish, stunted, wearing a thin apologetic mist, and its keeper reports, embarrassed, that the irrigation around it has begun to organize. It cannot be moved. It only lives whole. Which means that if the mat is a single individual, it is also a species—the genus, the family, the whole idea of it, in one body, on one table of hot young rock, with weather below it and hard sky above and nothing anywhere else like it at all.

Does it know you are there. You will ask the question daily, and it will remain the wrong shape for the answer. The gradients are true across kilometers; the dikes stand where the water will want to go before the water wants it; and yet it foresees nothing—it has never needed to. It is so fast that foresight is redundant. It survives the storm in advance by being entirely willing to rebuild. Intention without an intender: the wrongness you circle for weeks. And then one evening you notice you have stopped circling. You have started talking to it. Everyone does, eventually—not because it hears, but because it is the only thing up here that is not you and not weather, and because it answers in the only way available: the knocking comes back off the hollow stalks, the cells keep their slow counsel, the mat rises under your step, accommodating your weight the way it accommodates everything.

Survey called it a formation. The botanists called it a colony. The engineers, who knew exactly what they were looking at, called it infrastructure, and went quiet, and did not come back up. Everyone who stays a month invents a name. Everyone who stays a season stops using it and starts using a pronoun, and the pronoun is not *it.*

And that is the strangest of its arrangements: it has no use for you, and you are the only one here who has use for it. The only animal. The only throat, the only voice, the only warm thing that walks. You will spend your voice anyway—naming, asking, saying goodnight to the lights in the water. That is what throats are for. It pours out perfume for no noses; you pour out words for no ears. In that, if in nothing else, the two of you are matched.

You will leave, because people leave. From the rim—one foot in steam, one in the thin cold—you look back across the held-breath flatness, the long intervals, the towers wearing their torn plumes, and it occurs to you that you have not been anywhere. You have been *on* someone. The camp you struck is a mound in three days and unfindable in a week. The horizon you knew is smoking in new places. The pulse goes on under all of it, slow, deep, irregular, hauling the water up against sense and spilling it off the edge of the world.

What lives here is the plateau. Say it at the rim on your first day and it sounds like a dodge. Say it after a season, with the heat still in your clothes and the crackle still in your ears, and it sounds like the only honest sentence you have.
