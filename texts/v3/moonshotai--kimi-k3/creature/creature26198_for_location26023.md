Take the census by ear; it is the only way down there. And begin with the one, because the one is most of it.

The Mother is not the Kettle's setting. She is its senior tenant, and she has the habits of a landlady who has been on the premises longer than anyone can remember and intends to stay. She keeps her tenants' hours. The evening crack — that volley of bursting tallow-pods that opens the night shift — is hers; she closes her fist at dusk, somehow, some adjustment of sap-pressure no one has ever caught in the act, and the pods go off like a field of knuckles, and the folk set their watches by it, if they had watches. She feeds the boiler crews deliberately: dead limbs fall in the fuel-yards where the crews leave the gathering-poles standing, and they fall more generously in the weeks before the traders' cage is due, when the presses run hot. No one calls this intelligence. No one calls it anything else, either. She leans on the houses. A house moored to her must be re-roped on her schedule, not yours, and the folk have learned to read the schedule in the give-ropes' tension the way sailors read rigging — a house that sings tight in the night is being held too hard, and within the season it will be lifted or gently folded. The folk say the Mother holds whatever holds still for her. The weep comes heavier when she is flushing new leaf; the boil thickens a half-day before the flush, because the swarm knows first. Everyone in the Kettle is a physician with exactly one patient, and the patient is the room they live in. She is also the only inhabitant with a law entirely to herself: the one about green wood, the one with the lift behind it. Everything else is custom. That one is jurisprudence.

The forty-odd humans she boards have gone the color of the light. They are pale as the limestone band and shine-eyed, pupils wide from a life lived in green dilution, and their feet are soft and thick at once — broad, bare, callused into instruments. A kettle-folk reads the floor through her soles the way a deaf musician reads a drumhead; sitting at supper, heels resting on the root-polished planks, they follow the Presshouse stamp, the eels moving in the sump, someone climbing the Stair trunk two hundred paces off. Their hearts are set to the press. Babies born under the stamp sleep to it; an engine stutter wakes a household faster than a shout. They sleep through the boil, through the weep drumming the drip-canopies over every bed, through the evening crack itself — and they wake, all of them, instantly and completely, at silence. They smell of green pepper and oil, permanently; the traders up top say you can smell a kettle-folk on the wind before the cage finishes rising. They anoint against the swarm, because the singers land on the still and the un-oiled, and to be landed on is a small shame, a little death advertised. They take the Pillar as medicine: at midday, when the one column of undiluted sun stands on the year-stone and the presses idle in the heat, the folk come up to the plaza in their turn and stand in the light's edge with their faces tilted like plates, an hour a day to keep the bend out of their bones. Plumb, the year-keeper, who keeps the groove swept and calls the turns of the calendar, also keeps the sun-roster, and children dare each other to stand full in the Pillar at the height of the year when it strikes hot enough to raise the pepper-smell off their own skins.

Midday is also when they talk. Speech is nearly useless against the boil, but the boil has its weather, and the hour of the Pillar is its lull — presses idle, swarm drowsing in the heat — and the Kettle's whole spoken life crowds into it. Disputes are settled at noon. News is argued at noon. Courtships are conducted at noon, in ordinary voices, which is why the young consider the year-stone romantic ground. The rest of the day runs on whistle and tap: two dozen stock whistles for distance — come, danger, all-clear, shift-change, hush — and the knuckle-code for everything with nuance in it. Names are tap-patterns first. A child is given its knock-name at birth, a short rhythm tapped on the house-root until the house seems to hold it, and the spoken name is just the rhythm made air, a humming of the taps. Which means everyone in the Kettle can be called from across the nave, summoned by their own shape in sound, and means something else besides, which the Listener keeps to herself.

The Listener is the eldest of the kettle-folk, and she has arranged her old age into an instrument. Her cot stands moored against the trunk they call the Bellower — the great hollow-buttressed pillar that conducts the pit's tapping better than any other, the trunk every long-distance message routes through, the Kettle's exchange and ear — and she sleeps with one hand on a bare root the way another woman might sleep with a hand on a dog. She keeps the log: a cord, arm-thick now, thirty feet of knotted rope, each knot-cluster a transcription of some tap that arrived in the deep night in a pattern nobody sent. Two and three times a night, sometimes. The boiler crews log the same events as eels knocking against roots, and both parties continue, politely, to log them. The Listener unties nothing. She runs the cord through her fingers when she cannot sleep, which is often, reading three centuries of a tree's unreadable correspondence. Her theory is known to everyone: the Mother is dreaming. Her private elaboration is known to no one: that the Mother dreams of the plateau, of the one pocket of weather a seed could carry — of rain that falls from above. The tree has spent three hundred years making rain from her own breath, and the Listener believes she remembers the other kind. And there is the thing she logs but does not discuss. Sometimes, rarely, the night-taps come in patterns that have owners. Knock-names. Names of the dead. Twice in the Listener's tenure the Mother has tapped, in the small hours, the name of someone buried that season — tapped it correctly, with its family answer-pattern, as though the tree had been listening at the rails of the fall-plaza when the households tapped their farewells. The Listener answers these as custom demands — tap back when tapped — and writes them into the cord, and says nothing to the households, because she has not decided whether it would comfort them or end them.

She ran the roster once, in her youth. Her hand is on the wall.

The Presshouse crews are the Kettle's loud aristocracy: big-armed, even-tempered, half-deaf in the high registers where the boil lives, which they do not miss, since they hear through their bones like everyone else and better. They tend the stamping screws that render the pods into kettle-oil, and they tend the engines, and the engines are the only things in the pit that have to be taught fear — the Hush hunts by listening, and the Presshouse fears nothing, and so beside the Presshouse door hangs the roster-hook, and on the hook the tags, and on each tag a knock-name. When the Kettle goes quiet, someone runs. It is an honor and a terror and there is a roster. The newest name on the hook is Sorrel, fifteen, wiry, a netter's daughter, and her first silence came this past winter: the boil cut out mid-stamp, every singer in the pit going still at one instant as though a hand had closed over the Kettle's mouth, and the press, idiot and brave, went on shouting alone into a dark that had begun to listen. She says she does not remember the run. She remembers the sound of her own feet, which she had never in her life heard. She remembers the cutoff's weight and the long oiled travel of the lever, and then the waiting — lamps doused, crews pressed against the warm flanks of the machines gone suddenly meek, the whole pit enormous with listening — and then the boil swelling back up out of nothing, strata by strata, until the Kettle was itself again and her legs went out from under her, politely, after everything was safe. Afterward she pressed her greased hand to the wall beside the door, where the hands go back three centuries, layered, the oldest of them gone to ghosts of shine in the brick: the Hands, the folk call that wall, and they touch it the way other people touch a temple gate. Of the Hush itself there is almost nothing to say, because in three hundred years no one has seen it plain. A tail gone over a rail. Every door-spider bell in a lane ringing at once with the webs all empty. The Listener, who has the best account, calls it a long patience crossing the plaza. It comes from above — over the rim, down the wall, a climbing thing shaped like a cat's idea of a snake — and the folk's whole theology of it is contained in one sentence they teach the children: the dry sends down one listener, now and then, to collect what the dry is owed. The Kettle pays in singers. The Kettle makes more.

The one person who regularly meets the dry is Halyard, the lift-keeper, whose house stands in the Eye's long light at the foot of the chains. Once a month he rides the cage up through the oculus into the gray thorn country, and there the traders are waiting with their wagon and their hard dry faces, and the exchange is made: kettle-oil and dried singer-meal down out of the cage, iron and salt and needles into it, and news, which Hal carries below like a fourth cargo and taps out on the Bellower's root that evening while the households gather to read it through the floor. The traders never stay. The pit unsettles them — the noise, the sheened pale people, the way everything including the walls seems to be in conversation. One old trader, forty years on the route, has begun drumming her fingers on the cage rail while she waits, in patterns she does not know she knows; the folk have noticed and say nothing, and consider her halfway home. Now and then the traffic runs the other way. Nine years back a woman came down with the salt and simply never rode up again; the Kettle accepted her the way the Mother accepts a graft, and she is still a little too loud at noon and still occasionally landed-on, and the folk are fond of her for both. And sometimes the cage carries someone up who does not come down — not exile, just leaving, a young pair of eyes that wanted sky. The Kettle lets go. Their knock-names are kept warm a season, tapped fondly, then logged. Exile is different, and has happened twice in living memory, for green wood, and those two names are tapped only as warning-shapes, never answered. Ask Hal what the dry is like — he has stood in it more than anyone alive down there — and he thinks a while, and says: it is a place where nothing taps back.

Down at the plaza's edge, in the smell of loam and warm black water, Sedge keeps the sump, and the eels keep Sedge. She wades the three ponds in her rolled trousers handing out pressed cake to animals thick as her thigh, pale, eyeless, certain; she knows them apart by scars and by greed, and the oldest of them, thick as a man's waist and older than the Listener, older possibly than the town, is called the Abbess, and takes cake from between Sedge's fingers with a delicacy that belongs in a much smaller animal. During the weep the eels come partway ashore and walk the roots on stiffened fins, hunting fallen singers, their skins catching the moss-light, and the night shift steps over them the way you'd step over a sleeping dog — they are under-citizens, they have right of way. It is Sedge's eels the boiler crews blame for the night-taps, and Sedge lets them, because the fiction is useful and the crews are proud, but her own private verdict, given once, at noon, to the Listener, is that eels knock in threes and knock hungry, and that what comes up the Bellower's roots in the deep hours is something else. She keeps the frog-census too, by voice: the seeps hold a dozen bass stations and she knows each one, and a frog gone quiet is noticed the way a village notices a missing bell. Farther out along the walkways, Moss the snail-ward pursues the slowest job in the pit. The helmet-snails mow the glow-moss that lights the paths, grazing in pale green lines, rewriting the routes a little every week, and Moss does not herd them so much as negotiate — a warm hand laid on a shell, a bait-trail of fresh moss smeared where the path should run — so that the Kettle's streets drift over the years, gently, like handwriting changing with age, and are only ever a week from illegibility. Children trail him on the long circuits; it is understood that Moss's rounds are where you go to think. And at every useful opening in the town hang the door-spiders, plate-sized, deliberate, kept the way other people keep geese. Each household keeps its own and is quietly proud of it; they have names like ships' names — Held-Fast, Waits-Well, Comes-To-Supper — and tiny trade-bells on their anchor-lines, and there are grandmothers in the Kettle who can read a web's ring like a letter: singer, hound, a child's ball, a sleepwalker, a draft. Once in the Listener's memory every bell in a lane rang at the same moment and every web hung empty. That lane keeps its lamps low even now.

The children learn taps before words, and there is a small ceremony for the first deliberate knock — the knock-day, when a baby, slung in its oiled netting against the singers, reaches out and raps the house-root on purpose, and the whole household answers, and the child's knock-name is chosen from the shape of that first intent. Once in a generation, the story goes, the Mother answers first, before the family can; the last such child is an old woman now and lives alone by the Eldest and is left gently alone about it. The children go un-oiled in their cradles, behind nets, and are oiled for the first time when they join a work crew, which is the Kettle's whole idea of coming of age: no feast, just the oil, and the first walk through the strata with the swarm streaming around you off your sheen, untouchable and new. They play at stillness the way other children play at fire — standing motionless in the feeding currents until some singer's soft weight settles on a shoulder, which is a forfeit and a scandal and delicious. They swing on the give-ropes. They trail Moss. They believe the swifts, who own the Eye at dawn and commute to a sky none of them have seen, carry messages to the dead; no one corrects this. They have one forbidden game, the hush-game, where one child plays the Hush and the rest freeze, and no child has ever held the silence longer than a few seconds, and any adult who catches it stops it gently, without anger, because silence is not a toy and the children know it in their marrow already. When the real silence comes, the young ones are gathered into laps with hands cupped over their mouths, and they do not cry. Kettle children are born into noise; the quiet frightens them the way the dry would, the way sky would.

The swarm itself is past counting and almost past individuality — but not past reading. The netters who wade the strata at the evening crack read the currents the way fishermen read water: this layer thick with feeders, that one empty because hounds are working it, the whole body of the swarm restless a half-day before the Mother flushes new leaf. A hound corpse in the dawn fall is rare enough to be brought to the Listener, who turns its hook-jaws over and logs it. The fall itself is the town's income and is swept and carried to the drying lofts up the roots by the very old and the very young, who share the dawn between them, the swifts stitching the Eye overhead, the plaza's root floor warm underfoot and softly raining the night's dead. And at night the ember-abdomens of the swarm drift between the trunks like a cinder-field, and the folk navigate by them, and the swarm is, all at once, the Kettle's livestock, weather, clock, and light — the one inhabitant that is also the atmosphere.

The dead are oiled a last time, laid out on the fall-plaza at the evening crack, and given back — to the singers, to the sump, to the loop — and what the loop returns, the households keep: the small clean bones, knotted into the give-ropes, so that the dead go on doing the one job that matters, holding the houses to the tree. On the first Cage-day of the year, when the Pillar has crossed the year-stone's notch and the calendar turns, each household walks to the Eldest and taps its dead into the bark beside the Old Drill's iron teeth — into the iron, mostly, because iron holds a knock longer than wood, and for a little while afterward the founding machine stands there faintly ringing with names. Children touch the teeth for luck before their first night shift. The teeth are always warm.

So count it. Nine households; forty-odd souls, pale and soft-footed and reading the world through their heels; a Listener, a lift-keeper, a sump-keeper, a snail-ward, a roster of runners; eels with right of way, spiders with names, frogs kept like appointments, swifts with passports; one grandmother eel, one wall of hands, one cord of knots; and under all of it, around all of it, one citizen, growing exactly as fast as she is cut. The count keeps collapsing, the way a chord resolves into a note. And on certain nights, in the deepest hour, when the weep is falling and the boil has thinned toward dawn, a root taps twice against the Bellower's heartwood — three times — and the pattern is an old one, a knock-name fifty years buried, and its answer-pattern is older still. The Listener does not reach for the cord. She lays her old hand flat on the root and taps back what that name was always answered with — I'm here, I'm here — and waits, and nothing more comes, and the Kettle goes on holding its one green breath, and she does not write it down.
