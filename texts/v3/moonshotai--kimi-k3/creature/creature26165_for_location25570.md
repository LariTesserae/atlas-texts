Of everything that lives in the sea, almost none of it swims. Three kinds go free — the herds, the pale, and the listeners — and all the uncounted rest hold a place, one place, for the whole of their lives. Begin on any wall, then, because almost everyone is there.

A tenant is less an animal than an address that has become an animal. Every socket is occupied; every occupant fits its socket past the point where fitting is a metaphor — the Stock's vessels and the tenant's vessels are spliced, grown together across generations until the question of where the dweller ends and the wall begins has no answer anyone can taste. The socket feeds its tenant a thin sap, and the tenant's filtering crown cleans the water that passes, and the water is clear as glass because the entire skin of the world is one standing act of hunger.

The kinds are legion, but a wall-walker learns the great ones. The long fans spread a meter of fingers against the current, each finger reading the flow, re-orienting a degree a decade on a pivot the Stock grew for the purpose. A fan can also buckle its root-membrane against the drum of its socket, and the Stock's tissue carries the tick to its neighbors — so every wall ticks, faintly, constantly, a rain of small punctuation conducted through the body of the world. Neighbors know each other by tick-accent across centuries. When a tenant dies and a larva takes the vacant socket — usually the dead one's own larva, for a niche opens at the rate larvae ripen, and the drift almost always ends where it began — the new tenant's tick is its parent's tick, shifted slightly, the way a child's voice is a shifted parent. Dynasties hold sockets for spans that have no name. A listener swimming a wall can read its ticking the way you read faces: here the Nine-Hour Field, here the dynasty that came through the last mowing, here the silence that means an open door.

The bells keep their own time. Each contracts on its period — one an hour, one a little longer — and a bell-field is a chord of periods whose beats take years to come round, a polyrhythm so slow that only the tenants and the listeners know it is music. Inside each bell lives the kept: a small translucent being that cleans the bell's inner face, is fed by it, and never once touches open water. The kept is born in the bell and dies in it. When the bell dies, the kept does not leave. There is a whole metaphysics on every wall, and it is this: some lives are places, and some lives live in the lives that are places.

The sieve-forests are one animal standing as a thousand siphons; the palps are the wall's tongue, and it is the palps that first taste the coming of a herd — grazer-musk arriving days ahead of the grazer itself — and then the wall does the fastest thing anything in this world does: the fans furl over a day, the bells still, the palps withdraw below the socket-lips the Stock grew for exactly this. In a country where nothing hurries, the furling of a fan is a sprint, and it is over before the herd arrives.

For the herd always arrives, on the scale the walls keep. The mowing is the only season. A new-mown wall shines, silent, every tenant below its lip, the Stock's skin bare as old ivory; then the velvet returns; then the full crowd; then the shag of the overgrown, just before the next pass. A wall's age is its regrowth phase. The herds are the world's calendar, and the calendar is local, phase-shifted across provinces, so that no two walls keep the same year.

Between the sockets, the Stock grows a nap — a velvet of nutrient cilia — and it grows, as far as any mouth can tell, to be eaten. This is the pasture. Around many sockets the nap thickens into a fringe of soft feeding-tissue, plumbed through the tenant's graft: the guest-lip. It is grown for visitors. The world's hospitality is not a custom; it is a plumbing fixture, and it has never once been refused.

The herds come to it. A grazer's mouth is a crescent of soft rasps half a field across, and it mows the wall to the socket-lips — the nap, the tenants' crowns, all of it — and the crowns regrow, for that is the tithe every socket pays, the only tithe there is. The mouth that mows is also the mouth that says the word. There is a economy in that which the listeners have noticed and do not presume to interpret.

Half a kilometer long, a body-length a day, and on the flanks of every grazer, between the dim rows of living light, the grazer's own skin grows velvet — a moving meadow. On that meadow live the flank-nations: kinds found nowhere else in the sea, born on the warm country, dead on it, never once having touched the Stock. For them the grazer is the world: it hums — the great body conducts the Chord and its own slow pulse together — it is warm, it moves beneath them with a weather of its own, and it is ringed with stars that burn in rows. Ask a flank-mite what the world is and the answer is a true answer, locally. When two herds meet — the event of an age, two moving countries drawing alongside — the flank-nations mingle, and it is the only cosmic event in their cosmology. The Recitation has a verse for such meetings. It is sung very quietly.

The light-rows are the grazer's own, each pattern singular, each a constellation, each a name. The light exists to be found by: by keepers, and by the herds themselves, for the herds' calls are answered, perhaps once in a lifetime, from provinces away — two unisons held across a hundred kilometers for days, at the interval the world's dimensions allow — and when the answerers finally pass each other, lifetimes later, they know each other first by light. The listeners attend the calls the way one attends weather. They record every word that is spoken; they do not claim to know what is said; the word is the herds', and it is the one text in the world that is not the listeners' to keep.

No grazer's death has ever been recorded. No calf has ever been seen. The Roll of the Herds has not changed in the tenure of any voice, and if one of them has ever ended, it ended before the lanterns had names.

Out in the volume, the pale keep their stations. There is a depth where the Chord's fundamental swings widest, and shallower depths where the first overtones swing, and at those depths the pale hang — sheets and veils a hundred meters across, tissue spread to a membrane's thinness over hectares, tensioned like drumheads, held in place by the standing wave the way a note holds a string. They pulse once in some minutes; a watcher can see a pulse cross the whole body like weather crossing a sky. They strain the Fall — for almost nothing drifts in the Void Reaches, and almost is not nothing: the Roof's exhalations carry the Stock's shed cells, a snow too fine to see, sinking for years through eleven kilometers, and the pale are the volume's share of that manna, the walls' share being all the rest. Every station is known. Every station is occupied, as every socket is occupied; when a pale one buds, the bud drifts to an open antinode and takes the tension, and the Recitation amends one name. And when one dies — rare, rare — it lets go its tension and sinks, and the sinking takes years, the slowest snow there is, and where it comes down on the Root-Floor is a province's epoch: the drum-taut gather across decades, the Floor takes the body in across a century, and the roll of falls in the Recitation gains a verse that will be sung for ages.

At the margins, the kinds grow stranger. The Throat's rims are the loudest and warmest and fastest-fed places in the world, and the tenants there grow leaned into the current like flag-trees, running hot and quick by the world's measure; and among them live the twice-homed, whose larvae ride the upwelling the height of the world and settle at the opposite mouth — the only tenants with two addresses, the only ones who commute. At the Seeps, where the sea slides into the Stock's body, the threshold-kind sit half in the world's water and half in the Stock's vessels, the most grafted of all lives, straddling the one boundary nothing else crosses. And at the Seeps, sometimes, in the dark inflow, there are shapes. Things that have never been outside. The Stock's inner kinds, if they are kinds at all. The Recitation carries this as its shortest and oldest verse, the rumor-verse, and it has never been amended, because nothing about it has ever been learned. The Stock's body is the one country with no map.

And then there are the ones who go everywhere. If you are anything in this water, you are this: a listener.

Three or four meters of you, dark as the water you cross, and unlit — the only ranging kind that carries no light, because light is for places, and for the herds who must be found, and you are neither. You are going, and you go dark. Your wing-beat is slow; you glide for minutes between strokes. Your skeleton is your ear: the Chord arrives in your bones, from everywhere, always, and by its scattering you see — every body in the sea is a shadow and a ringing in the world-note, a pillar is known by the hole it makes, and another listener is known at kilometers as a small moving silence edged with resonance. Warmth you feel at the walls and at the herds. Sight you keep for the lanterns — Cold-Hand, Steadfast, the Twin over the Throat — for the vein-light, and for the herds' drifting rows. The pale you do not see at all; you feel them, a muting, a softness in the Chord, and you name their stations as you pass.

There are a few thousand of you in a region, and the number does not change. You were born at a herd; your first country was a flank, your first stars the rows, your first food the flank-velvet you cropped among the flank-nations, and then, grown, you let go of the moving country — the one emigration — and took the Reach. After that, mostly, you are alone: years between voices. You know where you are the way other creatures know up from down, by the shading of the Chord, by the arrangement of the named stars, and you carry the whole of the known world in your memory, because the memory is the Recitation, and the Recitation is everything: the Roll of Lanterns and their names; the Roll of the Herds, their constellations, their calls heard and answered, their loop-stages; the Roll of the Pale and their stations and the roll of their falls; the wall-histories read in tick-accent, dynasty by dynasty; the warnings, the taste of every Seep; the rumor-verse; and the contested verses, carried double, marked unresolved.

For the herds are the listeners' towns, and a grazer's passage is a fair that comes to you. When two listeners meet, they swim abreast for a season and recite in duet, and where their versions agree there is unison, and where they differ there are beats in the harmony, and the verse is marked and carried on until an elder's memory or a wall's own testimony resolves it. Only at the great meetings, dozens strong in the lee of some grazer, is the Recitation ever sung entire — in parts, in relays, across days, the whole gazetteer of the world held in no single skull but whole in the choir. This is the only institution. It was not founded. It grew, the way the niches grew, and it has no author, and nothing in it is new except the news.

You eat at the guest-lips, which are grown for you; you take the Fall on the wing in the pale ones' bands; at the herds you crop the meadow you were born on, and you polish the light-rows with your lips, and the rows burn clear. Nothing pursues you. Nothing flees you. The only violence you will ever know is the Seep, and you have tasted its warning all your life.

And you have one office, the one errand the world needs run. When a wall's tick falls silent in some signature and no kin-larva drifts within reach of the open socket, the news moves through the meetings at swimming speed — there is no faster news, and none is needed — and a listener finds a larva of that kind on its one drift, for every larva tastes of its socket-line, and cradles it against the heart, and carries it across the Reach. Months of open water. The larva, which would have died, is set against the socket-lip of its own kind's empty niche, and takes hold, and the dynasty does not end. Almost every larva lands where it began; the almost is the listeners'. In a world where every surface speaks only to itself, the skin of the world talks across the volume in their arms.

A listener's life is a few centuries of ranging, and two chosen things in it: a Carrying, perhaps a handful of Carryings, and the ending. When age comes, the listener seeks a herd and teaches — the Recitation must be handed on entire; no one goes silent with the song unsung — and then, emptied, turns toward a Seep long since tasted in the warnings, and gives the body back to the body that fed it. The name is not erased. It passes into the roll of the silent, which is also sung.

Whether the Stock grew the listeners in order to know itself by, or whether they arose in some crevice of the great balance and were merely permitted — there is no verse for it. There is no mouth to ask it. The listeners, who keep everything, keep that question unasked the way the bells keep their time: exactly, and without needing to.

So the census stands. The runner lives its few years on its trapline of decades; the tenant its centuries in one socket; the listener its long dark ranging; the pale one its station; the herds their ages; the lanterns their forever. Everyone here is fed by the same body, housed by it, plumbed into it, and in the end returned to it. Most lives are spent within a meter of where they began, and the few that go free carry all the rest in their bones.

The herds mow. The walls tick. The bells beat their slow chord and the kept clean and are kept. The pale hang at the harmonics of the world. Somewhere a grazer is a day into the word that takes two days, and somewhere a listener is crossing a Reach with a larva held against its heart, singing the name of a socket it has not yet reached. Nothing here is waiting. Everything here is kept. The sea holds its breath, and the breath is full of keepers, and it does not end.
