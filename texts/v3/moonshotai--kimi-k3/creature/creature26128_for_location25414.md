# The Census of the Sump Vault

Take the census. It will not take long, and it will come out the same the next time you take it, because down here a census is not a count of individuals but of offices. Every resident is the current holder of a position that outlasts it — the way the duckweed rafts hold their outlines while each plantlet is replaced the moment it dies. Begin at the bottom, where the tenure is longest.

The oldest living things in the building look like stains. The amber aprons under the weeping joints are not merely deposited; at their growing edges they are *grown* — laid stalk by twisted stalk by bacteria that eat the water's dissolved iron and build in the rust they excrete. They have been on site since the mains were first filled, and they are still building. The emerald varnish below the splash line is a turf, with its grazers and its seasons — except that its season is one, and unchanging. Even the dark has tenants: when two lamps died, decades ago, their parishes went over to the films that never needed light. The green went black-green; nothing was wasted. Every patch of wall has its leaseholder. All but one. The dry corner with its drift of rust-scale and its three bolts is the room's only desert, too dry even for the films, kept swept by the tremor like a shrine to failures that no longer happen.

Inside the moss banks the scale collapses. Each bank is a watershed fed by its own drip-route, and within it live the residents for whom twelve paces is a continent: rotifers, nematodes thinner than the capillaries in your finger, and the water bears — by reputation the toughest animals on Earth, survivors of drought and frost and vacuum, and here, in a hundred generations, not one of them has ever once had to survive anything. The shriveled sleeping state that carried their ancestors through catastrophe is an heirloom now: equipment for an emergency the room has abolished.

That is a local pattern, and it repeats at every scale. The boatmen carry wings they never open. The ferns keep a clock that strikes nothing. The heron brings its flinch, kept oiled and never fired. The entire population of the vault is equipped for a world that ended at the door.

The pale snails are the only citizens you can watch at their work without crouching. The lime-saturated water has armed them: their shells are thick as cathedrals, pale but heavy, because calcium is free here and nothing demands they travel light. Nothing hunts them. The few you see writing cursive through the algal film are the working few of a larger nation lodged under the ledge-lips, and their trails — the only handwriting the room produces — are also the only erasure it permits; the turf re-greens behind them within days, so their literature is continuous and reads, always, *enough*. Their egg clusters come and go at the same count, year on year. They die, as far as anyone could establish, of age — which almost no snail anywhere gets to do.

The tank is large enough, at insect scale, to have regions. The water boatmen hold their reaches like lighthouse keepers on a shared coast — the near-end nation and the far-end nation patrolling the same black water, possibly unacquainted. Each carries its own atmosphere, a film of silver, and the plunging inflow keeps the water so charged with air that a bubble outlasts its owner's need: they are the most comfortable divers alive. When the stroke raises its whisper-tide and lets it fall, they are rocked and do not know they are rocked; it is simply the condition of the water. The males still court, vibrating their small song into a medium already full of one enormous note. Whether any female hears it over the pump is not recorded. The nations persist, so the message gets through — or the song is ceremonial by now, like the fig's weddings.

The floor of the tank keeps the minutes. Everything that has ever died here lies in the first inches of silt: snail shells by the hundredweight, the empty cases of boatmen, feather-shafts, fig leaves pressed flat as specimens — and here and there the small bones of the one-timers. A fish washed down the main when the main still swallowed fish. A frog, once, in some unrecorded decade, that found the culvert but never its company. Nothing buries them; nothing digs them up. The archive is one bed thick, its catalog complete, and it is never consulted. It is the only memory the room has.

The plants are citizens too, and the strangest, because the room has excused them from the oldest discipline on Earth. Outside, every green thing spends a third of its life in the dark, waiting for morning. Nothing here waits for morning. The lamps never go out, photosynthesis never stands down, and whatever clock the ferns inherited ticks on with nothing to strike against — generation by generation it has become ornamental. These may be the only plants on the planet that expect nothing.

The fig tree is the largest leaseholder and the only mortal one. Its canopy shelters a square meter of floor; its shadow is a country with a population of its own — in the trough's soil of brick dust, rust sand, and fallen leaves, white potworms no thicker than rootlets take each dropped leaf down stitch by stitch, and the tree drinks the result through roots that have followed the condensate seam deep into the pipe joint, stitching it into the room's plumbing. Most years it sets fruit: small, hard figs, seedless, because the wasp that figures in every fig's biography never found the culvert. The tree performs its whole liturgy for an empty church — the inward flowers, the fruit without seed, a promise kept to no one — and then drops the result into the tank, where the boatmen gather like a wreck crew and the springtails take the leavings and the silt files what is left. Nothing comes of it. Nothing is wasted. A fig tree is condensed summer, a piece of the warm world with weather in it, and the room keeps it the way a chapel keeps a relic. It is also the only resident with no understudy. There is no second fig, and no way for one to arrive. Its death is the single event the room has pending — the one appointment on an otherwise blank calendar — and no one knows the date, and the drip-cups are refilled with lime at the same rate either way.

The air, insofar as anyone uses it, belongs to the drain moths — a dozen at most, resting wing over wing in a constellation near the middle lamp that has not changed its pattern in living memory, which here means forever. The lamp is their moon: always full, always up, close enough to be warm. No moth here has ever battered itself against a light it could not reach. Their larvae thread the algal seams of the drip-routes; each emergence exactly replaces a death. Above the last lamp, in the angle of the vault, a pale cellar spider keeps the room's only ambush — a daughter of a daughter of that corner, her web endlessly remade and never dusty, because the air gave up dust a century ago. She weighs less than the drip that lands beside her, and she is the apex predator of everything that walks. Nothing in the room is fast. The quickest living motion under this roof is the startle of a moth, and that happens perhaps twice a year. The springtails, peppering the wet ledges and rafting the drip-cups, are the dock crews and the undertakers — first to anything that falls, whether a moth wing, a leaf, or one of their own — and they are thorough.

And then there is the heron: the newest office, the largest, and the only one permitted to stand vacant. Its holders are self-appointed. Each arrives alone down the inflow culvert already knowing the duties, which are to stand, and to be still, and to hunt elsewhere. It takes nothing from the tank. The boatmen row beneath it unmenaced; this is the one room in the bird's world where it is not a spear but a citizen — off duty, cloistered, one grey vertical among the verticals. It sleeps here, one leg drawn up, behind the wall of the chord, the safest sleep available to its kind: no fox has the key, and the roar that swallowed your footstep swallows everything's. For years at a time the only warm blood under this roof is one bird's — and in the gap years there is none at all, and the room does not notice the difference. The office pays rent in lime: the bricks behind its stance carry a banded white chronicle, thick years and thin, years of one bird and years of two and the empty years when the crust grew only by drip. And it pays in feathers, which turn up in the valve guard, among the duckweed, and at last in the silt — so the archive holds the entire plumage-history of the office alongside everything else. If the fig tree's genealogy runs, as it likely does, through a heron's gut — a seed swallowed upstream, ferried down the culvert, planted in lime — then the room's largest plant and largest animal are kin by delivery, and the tree is simply the biggest thing a heron ever left here. The silt knows. The silt is not saying.

Set all of it to the stroke. Every animal in the census was conceived, hatched, and will die inside the same three-note chord; to the born-here it is not even silence, because silence is an idea, and ideas are not imported. It is the ground. The whisper-tide rocks the boatmen; the concussion shifts the springtails' footing; the drip-cups ring; the heron's stance absorbs it. Count the pump as one resident, then, because it is one: it metabolizes, it holds its temperature, it has worn itself into a perfect fit with its world, and it is the pulse against which every smaller pulse here keeps loose time.

No person lives here. The room's entire human population is a set of initials in grease pencil, and its entire human economy is a meter turning in a corridor upstairs, a bill paid by an office that no longer knows why — patronage as remote and reliable as weather. The floors above are structure, a skeleton going by its old names. This is the building's one living chamber, the organ that kept working after the body forgot it, and everything on the census is how it stays alive.

One pump. One heron — or, briefly, two, or, for a season, none. One fig tree. One spider's corner. A dozen moths. Two nations of boatmen. A nation of snails. Springtails beyond counting, water bears beyond imagining, and the films, older than the door. That is everyone. That has been everyone for longer than anyone has been checking. The door closes; the chord closes over; the rafts hold their outlines; and the census comes out, every time, the same — a leaf for a leaf, a stroke for a stroke, one warm wet hour that is always.
