**The Census of the Third Lift Well**

Sixty frogs. One turtle. One fig. One lamp, if a lamp can be said to live — two hundred years of unbroken burning is a kind of pulse. One heron, on the days it comes. Swifts in pairs, the count changing with the season. Snails in the low hundreds. Fish beyond reckoning, though the heron is working on it. Eels: unknown. No one has ever seen a whole one, only the middle of one, sliding. That is the ledger, and it fits in a breath. But a ledger is not a census, and the well keeps more than it counts.

The frogs are the well's voice, and they know it the way a throat knows things. Take the one that owns the third valve bonnet below the grille. All day it sits folded in a keyway, damp and philosophical, while the light column slides across its territory and the private rain stipples the far wall. At dusk, or when the tide turns, it comes out onto the iron and inflates. Its song is two notes, and both notes live in the one quiet window in the machine's spectrum — the notch of silence the hum leaves open. Hundreds of generations ago the pumps were already old and the frogs were new, and any frog that sang under the hum sang to itself alone. The well kept the ones whose voices fit the gap, the way masonry keeps the stone that fits. Now the song is not a stream song; it is a part the engine plays through living throats. When the tide falls and the ledges emerge one by one down the green length, sixty solitaries take it up, and the concrete cylinder returns them as a single enormous sound, the well ringing like a struck bell, the air on the crest shuddering above a stone shed the size of a chapel. They breed in the still pockets: the catch-basin under the cracked main where the drizzle pools, the basins between the fig's roots, the silted floor of the frozen cage after storms. The tadpoles ride out the overflow that sheets down the walls on wild days; some wash down to the dark water, and most of those are eaten, and some, presumably, are not. The well recruits by catastrophe. When the storm roar fills the shaft and there is no window at all, the frogs go silent and hold on. The song is for the gaps.

The turtle is the well's tenure. It came in through the intakes decades ago — whether it nosed through hunting or was swept, it has never said; nothing here says anything except the frogs, and they say only the two notes. It found the cage at gallery height already becoming an island and completed the work by sitting on it. Algae has furred its shell, so the turtle carries a plot of the hanging garden on its back, the only mobile territory in the well; when it hauls out beneath the bulkhead lamp it is a small hill warming itself at a small fire, and the lamp has burned longer than the turtle has lived, and neither of them remarks on it. It eats snails, crunching patience, and takes drowned figs off the surface with a delicacy that would surprise anyone watching, which is no one. It ignores the heron. That, and the cage, are its two relationships. Whether it could still leave — whether the intake gaps would pass that shell now — remains untested, because the turtle does not test it; decades of not-leaving have cured into something indistinguishable from choice, or from faith. In storms it withdraws into the furred shell and practices being a stone, which is the skill the well most rewards. It has exactly one possession, and all the time there is.

The fish are the weather of the surface: a few hundred, pale as the green light permits, quick because the well contains eels and a heron. And they are educated. A fig striking water makes a specific sound — rounder than a drip, lower than the frogs' second note — and the school turns toward it as one body, conditioned across generations to an irregular bell, for the fig drops on its own schedule and the fish have had to learn patience alongside appetite. At dusk they rise to the fallen insects and pock the surface in dimples, a rain in reverse. The fig itself is the largest rooted life in the well, centuries old, sprung from a seed in a wellhead crack — most likely delivered by a bird, which would make the tree a courier's child. It has never known soil. It drinks spray through forty meters of pale root curtain and light through its crest-side crown, and pays its rent in sugar. And inside some of the fruits lives the smallest citizen: a wasp no longer than the tail of a comma, whose entire world is the inside of a fig, whose tree's world is the inside of a well, whose well is the inside of a dam that drowned a gorge. Worlds all the way down. The wasp is why there are figs; the figs are why the school gathers; the school is why the heron comes. By a short chain, the wasp is why the heron comes.

The snails are the scribes. Fist-sized, warm-water, unhurried in the manner of creatures whose chief predator is the turtle. Their shells are laid down from the dam's own excreted mineral, banded rust and ivory like the sinter terraces below the waterline, so that each snail wears a scale model of the deep walls. They graze the tide band where the biofilm thickens, and their tongues clear it in clean spirals that read like handwriting — marginalia in the well's diary, advanced at the width of a tongue per hour. The clockwork drum writes the water's level into brass; the snails write the water's appetite into slime; between the two, the well is thoroughly documented. Their eggs are gels laid above the tide's reach. A water hammer — the valve-blow that shudders the ladder and rolls the length of the shaft — must register to a snail as an earthquake that harms nothing. They are right not to hurry. The well is patient with what is patient.

The eels belong to the deep band, below the gallery, below the light's useful reach. Thick as an arm, blacker than the still water in Well No. 2, they come out of the intake ports on the rising tide and are gone before it falls; they read pressure the way the frogs read sound, and the tide is their door and their timetable. No frog has ever seen one. The turtle has, and files them under weather. They may be very old — reservoir eels grow slowly and keep growing — and they are the well's living connection to the drowned gorge: when an eel slides in from the deep warm dark, the well is, in some sense, tasting the lake. What they do between arrival and departure is their affair. The fish become briefly, electrically aware of it.

The heron is the emissary, the only one besides the swifts who holds citizenship in two worlds. Its arrival is the day's most unlikely event: a bird built for marshes commits to a seven-pace shaft, half-closes its wings, and falls under control down the green dark to set its feet on the cage roof beside the indifferent turtle. It hunts the slack tide, when the surface stills and the fish forget themselves; it stands like a gray apostrophe, and then it punctuates. It carries the outside on its feet and in its gut — water-fern spore, snail eggs, the dust of the crest gardens, and, presumably, the truth about whether the city beyond the hills still draws power from this wall. It cannot say. It may have carried the frogs' own ancestors here once, as eggs on its feet, down from the gardens the pumps still water — the well recruiting from its own fountain. At evening it unfolds up the light column and is gone, the well's one regular export, and something in the shaft settles, the way a room settles when a visitor leaves.

The swifts are the sky's contribution. They nest in the housing's eaves in cups of spit and down — the only new architecture the well has received in centuries, tiny and annual. They sleep on the wing, are almost never still, and at evening they come screaming down the shaft like flung knives, the only sound that enters the well from above instead of rising out of it. Whether they dive to hunt the midges in the light column, to court, or out of some swifter theology of speed, the well does not record; the well records water levels, and the swifts are none of its business, which may be what they value in it. They turn above the surface with meters to spare and are gone before the echo lands.

Below the threshold of the ledger live the nations. Midges knotting and unknotting in the daylight column, each swarm a brief standing wave. The moths of the bulkhead lamp, two hundred years descended from moths that survived their longing; they rest on the warm bronze cage now and regard the flame the way the turtle regards everything. The spiders of the gallery, the furnished room's true furnishers, webs strung gauge-to-valve, reading each water hammer as a shiver of distant news. Inside the moss cushions — hand-deep, water-hoarding — a whole interior nation of springtails and mites, rotifers and tardigrades adrift in the held water: citizens of a citizen. And in the sump, among the encased rungs, where everything the well drops comes to rest — drowned figs, spent insects, guano, silt — pale things that have never seen the light column graze the fallout. The well's smallest mouths, processing its fallen world.

Below even those, the eldest. At the intakes, where the water is hot as a held breath, the microbial mats stream orange and green in the upwellings. They were first: the valley was barely drowned when they found the warm ports. The ivory and rust terraces crusting the deep walls are their masonry, accreted grain by grain — the oldest architecture in the well is built by its smallest lives. They keep no tide schedule, only the slower clock of flow and chemistry, and they will be last, as they were first. Everything else is a guest at their table. The biofilm the snails write in is their extended kin. If the well has a metabolism, the mats are its organs; if it has an ancestry, it is theirs.

And some of the lives here double as the house. The ferns root in screw threads and keyways and, most thickly, in the embossed letters of the maker's plate — the maker's name holds water better than any other surface in the well, and is now read daily by roots, and in that sense the maker still signs the work. The water-fern mats on the surface are the only citizens indifferent to the tide; they rise and fall with the long breath and are never stranded, raft-dwellers carrying passengers of their own.

Then there is the citizen who is everywhere and is counted nowhere, because it no longer exists. No hands pass on the ladder now, but the rungs are worn thin as wire where hands once passed; an oil can stands entombed in flowstone, set down mid-errand by someone who meant to be gone for minutes. The gauges still report to them. The pen still writes for them. The pumps still keep a timetable the makers set, the frogs tuned themselves to a human chord, and the whole warm schedule of the place is an intention still executing itself. The makers live here the way the maker lives in the plate — as pressure, as schedule, as a name being read by roots.

The light column slides off the water and up the eastern wall. The tide turns. The frog on the third valve bonnet comes out onto the iron and inflates; the snail completes a letter; the eels gather at the ports like a breath about to be drawn; the turtle watches the water; the lamp burns; the pen writes. The two notes find the window, the sixty solitaries become one voice, and the well rings like a struck bell. That is the whole census: everything that lives here either keeps the schedule, or is the schedule — and for the length of the chorus, the well is not a place with lives inside it, but a life, sounding.
