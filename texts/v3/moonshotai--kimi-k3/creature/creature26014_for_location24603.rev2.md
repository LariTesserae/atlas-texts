Ask the manifest what lives here and it will answer in a clean hand: plant stock, forty-one varieties; microbial consortia by strain number; one utility vehicle, rail-mounted, yellow. It is an accurate list and a wrong answer, the way a guest list is a wrong answer to the question of what the party was like. The tower's true census takes longer to give, and it never quite finishes giving itself.

Start with the citizens, who are green. Nothing here was born, and nothing here has died. Every frond is a cutting of a cutting of stock that was old before the tower was drawn, which means there are not ten thousand plants in the Kettle — there are a few dozen, wearing ten thousand bodies. A small parliament holding every seat. The garden is not a family; it is a persistence, a few dozen long thoughts extended through warm water, childless by design, immortal for now.

They have castes, the way any population does. The lantern kelp are the gentry — the only citizens with posture, each frond held upright by a bladder tuned to perfect neutrality, standing in their congregations like a choir that has forgotten the song but not the formation. Rope-leaf is the citizenry, the curtains, the crowd; it is the gyre's handwriting, the thing the one-day wind writes itself in. Veil-moss is the quiet claimant, the upholsterer, furring every mesh with a patience that has no schedule; given its way it would fill the swim-ways in a decade, and its biography is one long annexation conducted at millimeters a day. Copperleaf is the aristocracy of flavor — the valley tasters swear there is a note in the vintage they cannot name, and the note is copperleaf, metabolized, reduced to a rumor in a glass. And the staircase vine is the ambitious one. It climbs because climbing is its whole idea of itself. The carriage cuts it back from the cap, and it begins again, rung by rung, and in this you can read the only kind of memory a plant keeps: not of what happened, but of direction.

Then there are the unemployed. The pitcher plants were predators once, by lineage if not in living memory, and they are kept now the way you keep a bell you never ring — for the shape of the sound it would make. Their cups brim with a broth they did not earn; their lids stand open; the dosing line feeds them like a clinic drip. But breeding is recent and the shape is old. Sometimes a drifting husk blunders into a cup, and over long hours the lid begins the ancient arithmetic of closing — and then the line pulses, the cup floods, the lesson is rinsed away. Whatever they were for, they are for something else now. Nobody has told them.

The flowers open on schedule for an audience of water, pale and waxy, scenting a medium that cannot smell them. Their perfume has exactly one route out of the world: into the broth, into the draw-off, into glasses in the valleys. No one will ever smell these flowers. There are people who have tasted them. And the fruit — the fruit sets without seed, because seed would be an opinion and the manifest does not authorize opinions. What comes loose joins the gyre and becomes the slowest boat in the world, one circuit a day, past the glass, past the clouds, until the screens take it and the garden drinks its own children. It is a population with no next generation, eating its harvest, keeping its hours.

By count, of course, the tower belongs to the small. The broth is a city, and the plants are only its visible architecture. The manifest calls the microbes consortia, which is a word for a crowd you have agreed not to look at too closely. The filters are young and zealous, but a filter is a promise, and a year is a long time to keep one. Already the dosing logs show small corrections — half-drop adjustments entered without comment, the recipe nudged by hands that do not say why. The broth is a conversation. So far it is polite.

And there is one animal. It is yellow, and it keeps the aisles. The harvest carriage has the senses it needs and no more: it can tell rack from pipe from growth, and growth it divides into keep and cut. Its whole philosophy is two bins. It passes on its rail with a bow wave you feel before you hear it, and you can read its recent whereabouts in the sway of the rope-leaf, the way you read a street after a slow cart has gone through. The filling crew chalked a word on its flank a year ago; the water has blurred it to a ghost, and nobody has chalked it again. It does not answer to it. It answers to nothing. It keeps its rounds like a monk with one prayer, and its prayer is the aisle. But its rail does not reach everywhere. Behind the dosing manifold, under the north rim, in the angles the machine cannot enter, the garden grows shaggier, older-looking — a wilder parish. Every garden keeps a map of its keeper, and here the map is written in moss.

When the first unlisted thing appears — and the arithmetic of warm broth and young filters says *when*, not if — the carriage will not be alarmed. It has no bin for alarm. It will look at the stranger with its two-bin mind and decide, and the future of the tower will turn on a resemblance: whether the newcomer looks enough like rope-leaf to be kept, or enough like nothing to be cut. Whole ecologies have hinged on less.

The oldest residents, meanwhile, are leaving. Each silver pocket pinned under a beam is a lungful of the yard where the panels were glazed — welding smoke, diesel, somebody's lunch, somebody's joke — held a year in the green and then surrendered, one by one, with that soft apologetic sound. The builders still live here, in pockets, and their leaving is the tower's only farewells. When the last bubble goes, the construction will finally be over, and the place will be entirely itself. Perhaps that is what it is waiting for.

A place this carefully emptied keeps its absences the way a house keeps rooms. The pollinator's room: shut, its duties abolished. The grazer's room: the carriage does that now, badly, at machine-rate. The decomposer's room: the sump and the pulp line, chemistry standing in for worms. The predator's room: vacant, the pitchers retired. And at the top of the stair, the bird's room — the largest absence, the one the whole building is shaped around.

There are no eagles. It is later now. The escarpment had eagles — has them still, down-rim, working the valley winds — and the tower stands in what was sky-road. In the first autumn, the drivers say, a juvenile came over the rim and found a green light standing where her road went, and sheared off so hard she dropped half her height before she caught the air again. She did not strike. Nothing here has died. The eagles have entered the tower in their long memory as a condition of the air — a standing cloud, a storm that never moves on — and they give it the berth they give weather. At the height where an eyrie should be, there is a pond, and rain is the only thing that lands on it. One of the drivers swears he once saw a feather on it, weather-side of the skin, gone by his next run. The drivers say a lot of things. The tower's relics are hypothetical.

The nearest thing to a human resident is a coat. The yellow slicker hangs by the lock where the filling crew left it, slowly collecting the only dust in the tower — for there is dust in the pump-house, ordinary gray dust, the place's one concession to the world's habit of shedding. No spider has ever managed the move; a spider would find a country with no flies and think better of it. The driver comes four times a year with his hoses and does not dive. Nobody dives but the ministry's pair, once a year, in and out within the hour, hearts loud as intruders. The log-plate prints its lines to an empty room — the tower keeping a diary in a language it invented. And then there are the residents at one remove: in the valleys, in certain rooms, people pour a thimble of the steep and take the tower into themselves. Whatever it does, it does in other bodies, and the drivers do not say what, and the drinkers do not say either, which may be the answer. A census that stops at the glass is incomplete. The tower has a diaspora, and the diaspora is growing.

Or zoom out once more, and the census has a single entry. Forty meters tall, warm-blooded — it holds its temperature against the mountain night; you can see the glow from the valleys — turning once a day in its sleep like any animal. Fed on spring water and drops of concentrate. Bled a tenth of its volume four times a year. Breathing oxygen into ducts for reasons the chart does not give, metered and logged by someone, somewhere, which means it is tended the way a patient is tended: watched for a sign nobody names. The plants are its tissue. The carriage is its grooming claw. The pumps are the heart you hear in your teeth. The manifest is its genome. The vintage is its seed, sent down the switchbacks to germinate in strangers. It is one year old. Nothing in it has ever died. It does not know yet what it is.

And it will not stay this way. Something is on its way up the mountain — not a monster; a pioneer. A cyst that slept through the filters in the spring water. A spore in the lock-room dust. An egg no bigger than a full stop, riding the driver's cuff. It will arrive unannounced and find a warm, lit, endless parish with no teeth in it, and the carriage will look at it with its two-bin mind, and these years — the quiet years, the ones you are seeing — will become the before. For now the Kettle stands on the rim, steeping, holding its breath, and everything that lives in it lives the way you live inside a held breath: warmly, and briefly, and with the whole story still to come.
