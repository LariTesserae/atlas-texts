You came up the gully sure you would find a gardener, and the certainty survives the first hour — survives the tenth. So you do what a visitor does in a place that feels kept: you count the keepers.

The counting is easy at first. The bowl is a room, and a room wants its occupants named.

**Twenty.** The trees, of course. Begin with one, because each is one — that is the first thing the census teaches you. Stand at its apron and read it the way you'd read a face. The canopy, wide and low and opulent, is the least of it; the tree's attention is all below you, in the root-plate, where it has been leaning its whole weight against the same dark stone since before your language had words. It knows that nodule the way you know the shape of your own teeth. It found the fracture planes decades ago — by taste, by exudate, by the slow chemistry of insistence — and it has been working them ever since, one season-long blow at a time. No two trees are alike, because no two cores are alike. This one works a blue-black nodule with a flaw running through it like a secret; that one inherited a half-finished blade from a tenant three generations gone and spends its life revising another's work. A hand tires and lifts away from the task. A root never lifts. That is the entire technique: it never lifts, and so it never misses.

Twenty solitaries, and yet a society — conducted entirely at a remove. Canopy never touches canopy; no tree has ever felt another's leaf. Their contact runs through time and plumbing instead. A core passes from tenure to tenure, fifty deep, each inheritor picking up the work where the dead left it, so the oldest stones are less artifacts than correspondence — letters still being answered after five hundred years. The runnels are correspondence too, of a kind: one tree's channel crosses the silted ghost of an older tree's, and in a cross-section of rimstone you can find where two tenancies disputed a flow-line for six centuries, cutting and sealing, cutting and sealing, until one of them died and the water conceded. It is the only diplomacy the bowl has ever known, and it has never once been conducted face to face. The trees are strangers in space and family in sequence. What passes between them is not seed, not shade, not touch — only the work, and the stone the work is written on.

And then, because the census insists, you count the dead — because here the dead have not left. Two of the twenty stand white and leafless on their aprons, trunks and boughs encased in tufa where the spray found them after the heartwood failed: elders promoted from garden to landscape. The bowl does not bury. It keeps, and it keeps standing. At the base of each stone tree the flake-midden lies undisturbed, the estate of a worker whose bench no heir has yet claimed; the freed core sits in open water like a vacant house, and may sit so for a century, until some shifted runnel floats a seed against it and the tenancy turns over the way weather turns over — slowly, and all at once.

Now the crowd, and the census changes scale. The mats were here first. They were in this water when it still ran under a roof, in the dark, before the bowl was a bowl; everything since — the fall of the cavern, the arrival of the first seed on some long-ago wind — has happened in their tenure, not the other way around. The trees are immigrants. The mats are natives, and they are most of the life here by any measure but the one that counts individuals: billions that amount to one, against twenty that amount to twenty. They live in districts drawn by temperature — the rust quarter, the jade quarter, the pale milk-glass margin where the pools cool to merely warm — and the districts move. In a hot decade the jade creeps downslope; in a cool one the rust advances, and the thermometer the shallows wear is redrawn from within. They baffle the flow, they bind the silt, they organize the mineral film the water lays down — the living skin beneath the mineral skin — and they make no artifacts and keep no records and ask nothing. They are silent the way crowds of that size are always silent. The terraces are their architecture as surely as the spring's.

Up the walls, a third country. The moss and the ferns live on the bowl's own breath, condensed against the cool high rock — distilled water, the only sweet water in the place, weather made indoors. They answer to no runnel, to no edit, to no tenure. The ferns fountain out of the weep-lines unbrowsed to the ground, green past reason, and the moss holds its deep pile in the shaded seeps, soft enough to take the print of a hand and keep it for days. No hand presses it. These are the bowl's wild ones, its little wilderness — the one reach of growth that is neither masonry nor maintenance, the only unedited page.

Then the ones who wait. The seeds are boats — they would have to be, in a place with no fur and no gut and no wind worth the name; the parent digs their roads and floats them down the flow-lines to the basins, and they gather there in their hundreds, patient as ballast. Most will never find flint. The viable real estate of the bowl is a map of intersections — where chert lies, where warmth reaches, where water can be led — and a seed that misses the intersection misses everything. But nothing here is wasted the way the outside world wastes. The pools take the failures too, film by film, and keep them: a library of the unbegun, shelved in stone. And once in a long while a drifter lodges against an exhumed nodule, splits, puts down its first root, and that root does the thing every root of its kind has done since the first: it finds the stone's face, and leans. It is the fastest thing the tree will ever do. From that hour the tenancy has begun, and nothing in the next three hundred years will hurry.

Which brings the census to its largest population, the one that undermines the whole exercise: the kept. The petrified leaves lie on every terrace, autumn upon autumn, each entire, each veined, the bowl's habit of remembering everything it has ever dropped. Add the stoned seeds, the stoned elders, the stoned visitors — for the place has met animals, whatever its rules: a beetle blown over the rim once a century, a feather settling onto a pool, and within a season the springs have done what the springs do, and the likeness is filed forever. The terraces are a museum of the outside world with an acquisition rate of one per hundred years, and not one exhibit alive. Nothing here is lost. Everything is slowed.

And now the absence, which the census can no longer step around. Walk the bowl and name what is missing, item by item, the way you'd name the tenants: no web slung between branches, no nibbled margin, no track, no dung, no skater dimpling the pool-skins, no song at the rim at dusk. And then look again at the living, because the absence is written into their bodies. No thorn anywhere. No bitter sap. Leaves soft as cloth because nothing has ever torn them; flowers drab and small because there is no one to impress; branches reaching wide and lazy because no storm-pruning wind ever tests them. They grow the way things grow when nothing will ever test them: generously. It is innocence at the level of tissue — a flora from before the invention of being eaten. There is thirst in the bowl, a great deal of it, but no hunger. Lushness without appetite; plenty without consumption; every relationship in the place reduced to a thing and its water, a thing and its stone, a thing and its time. You keep checking for the gardener. The deeper strangeness is that you are checking with eyes built by a world of mouths, in the one warm, wet, lit room where the grammar of eating was never invented at all.

If the place keeps one animal, it keeps the mist. It is the only resident that comes and goes: filling the bowl at dawn, opaque and glowing, so that for an hour the bowl is entirely alone with itself; thinning through the morning until the pools begin handing back the sky; gathering again toward evening, drawn up off the warm water like breath returning to a sleeper. It has moods. It rests. It obeys the fast clock, the one the other two agree to keep. And beneath it, under everything, the spring itself — the first tenant, the landlord, here before the roof fell, up out of the dark under the mountain, paying for the whole household: the warmth, the mineral, the transport, the burial. Everything that lives here rents from it. Everything that dies here is interred by it.

So the census fails, gracefully, the way it was always going to. Twenty, then billions, then the uncounted dead, then a breath and a body of water — and underneath the arithmetic the three clocks ticking in agreement, the shared metabolism of cutting and sealing, deposition and revision. At some point, standing on some terrace with the drip arriving twice in your ears, you stop counting occupants and realize you have been taking the measure of one. What lives here is the negotiation. It has hands of root and a skin of rust and jade; it breathes once a day; it keeps its own annals in cream and ochre and files its dead in its own floor; it has been in continuous session since before the bowl had a sky, and it has never once been adjourned. The trees are not its owners. They are its longest-running thought.

You are its shortest. For the span of your visit you are the only animal in the room — the only mouth, the only hunger, the fastest clock by four orders of magnitude — and the place tolerates you the way it tolerates weather, which is to say completely and without comment. Nothing here fears you; fear is an animal art, and there has been no one to practice it on. The echo takes your footsteps and returns them a half-second late, which is as close as the bowl comes to answering you. Drop nothing. It keeps what it catches, and it does not distinguish between a leaf and a hair, between a tenant and a guest.

Toward evening the mist comes in off the water, and you stop counting, because there is nothing left to count and never was. Somewhere across the terraces a root completes a blow it began three winters ago; a flake lets go, and the bowl says the sound twice — once falling, once remembered — and you understand that you are listening to a single sentence spoken very slowly, and that listening is the only residency available to a thing with a heartbeat.

It is enough. You sit by the nearest pool and let the breath of the place come up around you, and the warm water lays down its hair of stone, and the sentence goes on.
