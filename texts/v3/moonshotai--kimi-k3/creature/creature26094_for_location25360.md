Ask who lives here and you have asked the Plateau's one young question — not because the answer is hidden, but because the answer is still arriving, like everything else spoken in this place. The census of the Plateau is short and very old, but it is not simple. Counting here is not a young art. You do not count the citizens; you *regard* them, in categories, at their several speeds.

Begin with the ones still moving.

—

The Slow Ones are boneless — there are no bones in this world, there never were — and so a Slow One is held up by nothing but the habit of itself, the way the city is. Peat and lime and moss-fiber and finished water. Grey-green. Faintly warm, as they have always been, for warmth is the temperature of the unfinished, and the Slow Ones are the warmest things on the Plateau — which is another way of saying they are the youngest, which, at the age of the city itself, tells you everything about the census.

Each one is shaped by the gesture it has been making longest, the way a tree is shaped by wind. The one in the comb-court has been raising an arm toward the comb-vane for what you would call a decade, and it is *reach-shaped* now — the whole body become the gesture, the way the cushion-domes are growth-shaped. You read a Slow One the way you read a road. This one has been turning left for six hundred years. This one has been almost sitting down since before your language existed.

Their names are their gestures. The one at the vane is the Vane-Turner. The one keeping count is the Census-Taker. When the gesture completes, the name retires, and the Slow One — being now someone slightly else — takes the next one. Their true names, like the city's, are long, and still in transit.

They drink the way the city drinks: they go out before the dawn into the fog and stand, and the mist combs itself on them and runs down the grooves of their long gestures, and this is enough. Nothing here eats. Nothing here ever has. The city drinks the air; its people do likewise; it is the oldest shared habit, the first syllable of citizenship.

The eyes are the last fast thing. In all that mineral stillness, a Slow One's gaze arrives *promptly*, and it will startle you every time. The eyes are dark and still as tarns, and like tarns they remember forward. This is the mechanism of the season on the bench: you sit with one for a full season, and it begins, perhaps, to turn toward you, and by the time it faces you, you are looking into water that shows things as they will have settled — and what it shows you is yourself, settled. That is why, by the time it faces you, you will have decided whether you are staying. Do not look too long.

—

Mostly they do not speak. Sound is conserved here — a thing said is a thing in transit forever — and so speech is a commitment measured in centuries, and courtesy is silence. There are courts the Slow Ones do not enter, where a quarrel from the founders' time is still passing through, syllable by syllable, and to stand in one is to be caught in ancient crossfire. And there are courts everyone loves, where a blessing is still arriving. The ninth court, where the bench is, has been occupied for six thousand years by a slow kindness. Sit there an hour and you will understand what is meant by *in transit*.

When one does speak to you, it makes an effort at speed: a whole word in a season. For the Slow One this is gabbling, nearly song, and afterward it will rest from the exertion for some years. You will not notice the rest. It looks exactly like everything else.

During the Audible they are at their most animated. Heads turn within a single fortnight. A syllable lands in the right court and they incline — this is the whole of the service, and it is sufficient. They are connoisseurs of arriving sound; a Slow One can tell a bell's age by the softness of its landing, and knows every bell by name, including the ones that settled long ago. The thaw releases their voices each year like letters forwarded from a dead— from a *settled* address. Visitors are advised that this is the carnival season, and not to be overwhelmed.

—

Then there are the ones who settled into the city, and this is most of the census. Vess is not inhabited. Vess *is* the inhabitants.

You are never alone in Vess; loneliness is a young world's problem, a city with no people in its stones. Greet a wall by leaning. Do not knock — knocking is fast. The walls that loved their straightness are still loving it; the gates swing on their pebble bearings at the speed of welcome, which is about a day, and you will learn to wait for doors, and the waiting will stop feeling like waiting, and that will be the first syllable of your settling.

The bronze is sparing here not because the ore is rare but because the metal is spoken for. The chisel, the gate-pivot, the bell — each is someone. The decisive ones, who chose final forms of use. You do not mint your neighbors. The bell is someone whose final form was a voice, which is why the bell-notes that land each thaw are not nostalgia. They are correspondence. And the chisel found more patina than bronze and still sharp — that is the first citizen to refuse its rest, because its final form is use. In the Unsettling, the bronze leads. The tools are warm. This is not said loudly, because sound is conserved, and a thing said goes on being said.

—

And the Sleepers are the census at its widest: the domes on the horizon and sometimes much closer, in herds and congregations, each one someone, each one dreaming. The Slow Ones do not speculate about the dreams. Speculation is a fast emotion. They *regard* — which is wonder that has settled — and they read the evidence the dreams leave behind. When a shoulder presses down in its sleep and a new tarn is born, the Slow Ones come, over decades, to read it. Water remembers forward. A Sleeper's fresh tarn shows the Sleeper's future.

Some of the fresh tarns show the domes *standing up*.

The uphill roads that have begun to loop — that is the Sleepers turning toward home in their sleep. A loop is a hill that is homesick. When the ground turns over once an age, the Slow Ones note it in the ledger and adjust the paths and call it a dream, and they are right. It is also, perhaps, the way a sleeper stretches, just before —

Do not say it. Sound is conserved.

—

The ledger, by the way, is written in lichen. The only colors that flare on the Plateau are chemistry, and chemistry is allowed, and so the Slow Ones farm chemistry into meaning: sulfur and rust and verdigris, sown in patterns on a field of boulders, growing at a lichen's pace. The Written Field. A text here is never finished and therefore never wrong, which is the Slow Ones' historiography in a single stroke. Half the splashed boulders you pass are annotated; the landscape is full of marginalia; telling wild chemistry from authorship is their paleography, and it takes as long as it takes.

The groves are citizens — the census says so plainly. One citizen, repeated forty times. A grove attends a court the way water attends a glass: by being shaped around it, completely, for ages. The cushion-domes are listed provisionally. The provisional list is ten thousand years old. On the Plateau, *provisional* is the most permanent word there is.

And the strangest entry: the words in transit are counted too. A spoken thing here is a citizen between addresses. The full name of Vess is a citizen who has been walking home since the founding. Somewhere between two courts there is a Slow One the others call the Answer, who set out some thousands of years ago to meet a question that has not finished arriving. Both parties are on time. This is conversation, Plateau-style.

—

And the last entry is the blank line: the unfinished. You.

Climbers come up out of the fog the way weather comes to a mountain — eventually, and without explanation. No one returns down the loops, so the fog gives up only two things: people, and what people dream. Both are recorded. A newcomer's first-season dreams are the only letters from below, and there is a court for telling them, and a Slow One whose gesture is listening, who has been turning toward the telling for some years now and will arrive in time.

Some of the Slow Ones were climbers once. The lichen on one elbow keeps the pattern of a traveler's knot. A shoulder in the ninth court wears, mineralized into it, the frayed end of a climbing rope gone to moss-fiber. Arrival and creation are the same event at this timescale; perhaps the builders were the world's only animals, and perhaps they were only its first climbers, and the Plateau, which has no opinion, receives both readings without surprise. The bench is not particular because someone's final form was welcome. The blank line in the ledger is the same shape.

—

And now the census must add a line, or un-draw one.

The tarns show people in the streets. The city is setting places: pots carried from the storehouses to doorsteps, rope uncoiled, and the doorway that remembered itself after six thousand years is being swept — one pass of the broom a season — by the Slow One the others now call the Broom. New work. That is the true heresy, deeper than warm stone: a Slow One *beginning* something. The world had not seen it since the building.

There are Finishers, who hold that the sentence should be allowed to end, and Continuers, who hold that it has noticed something, and the schism proceeds at one doctrinal position per century and both sides are winning. And the Vane-Turner raised its arm further this year than in the five years before, and lately — the scandal of the age, regarded at length and in silence — it *lowered* it, in nine years, which is faster, and if you understand what faster costs here, you are already half a citizen.

—

So: who lives here? The warm and the settled, the green and the bronze, the spoken-and-still-arriving, the sleepers and the sweepers and the not-yet — and, provisionally, you.

The Census-Taker is mid-round. It has been mid-round for six thousand years. It will reach you. In the Written Field, beside the ninth court, there is a patch of bare south-facing stone, good light, prepared — no one will say when, or by whom — where a lichen-name can grow. You do not choose your name here. It grows.

Sit. Be counted. The census is short and very old, and it is not closed. It has never once been closed. And lately — this is the part the Slow Ones regard with their dark, forward-remembering eyes — it has begun to grow.
