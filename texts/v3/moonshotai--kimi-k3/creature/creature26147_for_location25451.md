At first you would say no one lives here. The hush reads as emptiness — a place this quiet must be abandoned, or never inhabited, or inhabited only by water. Then the mist gives up a figure on a deck, and another below it, and you begin to understand that the valley is full. Full by its own measure. No one has ever taken a census; the census is the boot rack at the cookhouse door, and it says some hundreds. They are arranged to be hard to notice. Quiet is their camouflage, worn the way the valley wears its moss, and it has been worn so long it has become skin.

They live inside the fabric, the way the water does. Rooms are tucked under the great decks, built into the backs of terraces, leaned against the warm flank of the turbine hall where the thrum comes through the wall like a pulse. Bunks of black timber, wool blankets that never entirely dry, a window if the level allows one, glazed with a pane that matches nothing. Through every wall runs water — in a pipe, in a channel, in the masonry's own weep — and the sound of it is the sound of being home. A valley person sent down to the lowlands lies awake half the night in the terrible dryness, in a silence with no thread sewn through it, and comes back up the incline saying the cities are too loud and too quiet at once.

The work shapes them the way the water shapes the gravel. Hands go blanched and thick-nailed, fingertips split and re-split, until the oldest waders have hands that look like what they handle — knuckled, pale, all their heat retired inward. Everyone walks the deck walk, flat-footed, testing, because the algae gloss is slick as grease and a fall from a high level is the way the valley takes its due. Voices are kept the way tools are kept — close, and not lent out. Sound travels three levels over open water; a shout is public property. So quiet here is not piety. It is handrail.

The roles turn over like everything else, on a rota nobody wrote down. There are waders, who spend the working day waist-deep in the troughs, lifting and washing and grading the root with that card-trick quickness. There are gate-keepers, who know each sluice by its clack the way you know a neighbor's knock. The turbine crew live inside the thrum and speak least of all. The packhouse weighs and wraps and rings the bell that sections the day. The grating room is the weeping room — the air in it bites, and new workers are sent in with the culls and cry their first week through, which is counted as christening, not weakness; old hands go in to sit a while when the damp gets into their chests, and swear the pepper air draws it out. The cookhouse is the warm heart, the one room where voices rise to something like ordinary, where the day's store of words gets spent — still low, still few, but spent. Even here, heat is rationed like everything else, and eaten: the culls are grated into the food, so the valley's sharpness lives in the people too.

Children are tied to the decks by a line, like sailors, until their feet learn the algae's grammar. They wear cut-down oilskins and inherit duties the way lowland children inherit furniture. The whistle code is not taught for the same reason breathing is not taught; it is in the air over the troughs, and they come up knowing it — a dozen signals, stop the flow, send the basket, gate coming down, need hands, water wrong. They learn water before letters: how to read turbidity at a glance, how to hear a flume going wrong from two levels off, how to tell time by the pale blade of sun that crosses the far slope in clear weeks. Meet me when the blade touches the third weir is a complete appointment. A child is said to be born under flume nine, or above the second weir, and that is a whole biography in one line.

The elders migrate uphill through the hierarchy of warmth. When the hands finally stiffen against the cold, a wader moves to the gates, then to the lamps, then to the bench by the cookhouse stove, and none of it is retirement — it is reassignment upward. They are the archive. Ask where a channel ran before the last rerouting and the answer comes from a bench, not a book: where flume nine runs there was once flume seven, and before that a deck that burned in a winter nobody else remembers. They are consulted the way the heron is consulted — by watching where they sit.

There is no soil, so the dead cannot stay. They are wrapped in wool and sent down the incline to the lowland ground, and something of theirs stays up: a gate they hung true, a stair they rerouted, a weir notch cut by their grandfather that still meters water exactly as his hand intended. On certain gate posts there are small brass tags, stamped with initials, gone smooth at the edges. New workers learn the tags along with the levels. The fabric is full of the dead in the same way it is full of retired sheds — cannibalized, continuing.

The law is the water, and the water's law is one sentence long. Everything drains below the last intake; nothing warms or fouls what runs above it. Violations are rare enough to be generational, and the valley's answer is not a tribunal but a change in the weather of the place — a silence that settles on the guilty like the cloud settling on the rim — and if that is not enough, the incline is there, and it goes down. Beyond that, nobody much governs. The rotation governs. The bell governs. The work is older than any worker and it does not hurry, and so neither do they.

Time runs differently under the cloud. Days are sectioned by the bell and by the rumor of light. Years are counted in rotations, and since the rotation runs decades deep, a working life spans perhaps two full turnings of the valley's mosaic — two turnings of the people, too. Nobody alive remembers the valley wearing a different face, and yet it has never once been the same, and neither have they. The fabric and the population turn over on the same schedule, each replaced part made in the manner of the thing it replaces, so that both stay ancient-looking while never being the same twice.

Night belongs to the lamp-tenders and the moths and the night-ear — the one who sleeps in a hut above the intake no bigger than a cupboard and wakes for a wrong note in the murmur the way a mother wakes. The moths work the caged bulbs and pay for it, and their wings drift on the troughs at dawn. The slugs come out on the moss, big as a thumb, and the night walkers step around them by lantern-light; it is considered clumsy, nearly ill-mannered, to tread on one. You announce yourself after dark by footfall and a knuckle knocked soft on wet timber, because to startle someone on a narrow deck above running water is not a small sin. And under it all the basin gives off its combined murmur, the sound of a sleeping crowd — which is exactly what it is, the valley talking in its sleep through a thousand small throats of water.

Then the high cloud bursts, and for one night the valley speaks in its outdoor voice. Every sluice manned, lanterns moving in the rain, the spillways white, and actual shouting — gate to gate, level to level, voices that have been kept all year finally let out like water from a penstock. Flood nights are when the news moves, when the decisions get made, when the young manage to say things to each other across a trough that could never be said in the hush. By morning the quiet rule is back, the valley is wearing a slightly different face, and the flood stories begin their long slow circulation, told low over the cookhouse tables for years, the epics of the place, murmured.

The animals hold their standings. The heron is a consultant — when it leaves the settling basins, the gate-keepers look up and check the high cloud. The cat is the packhouse's landlord and is sighted the way weather is sighted at the rim: rarely, and with interest; its appearances are reported and discussed. The cistern fish are nameless on principle, because a named thing is a pet and the rotation feeds no pets. The swifts are citizens of the rim who never come down — the valley's idea of elsewhere, racing along the edge of the world at the hour the light goes. There are no songbirds in the deep levels. The valley has no use for music it cannot make itself.

People arrive the way everything arrives: up the incline, counterweighted with water, among the timber and glass and salt and wire. A lowlander comes up loud, dropping things, startling at the hush, and is sanded down by it the way stair nosings are worn into smiles — you can gauge a newcomer's tenure in decibels. Some cannot go quiet, and go back down. Some go down on their own, to see the cities where the root is eaten, and come back with almost nothing to say, and stand a long while at the intake, just listening. A few stay below, and the valley says of them that they dried out.

But if you ask who truly lives here, the honest answer grows older the longer you look. The people tend the green, and the green was the reason for all of it, and it wants nothing but cold and shade and running water and never hurries — it is the eldest resident, the one the whole basin was arranged around, and its harvest goes down the incline crated in leaves like children sent to the cities, and no news comes back, and none is expected. Older still is the second green, the moss and the fern and the liverwort, the old family that was here before any of it and signs nothing and is argued with daily, scraper and brush, and never defeated, only maintained.

And the water passes through all of them — guest, not resident; lent, and returned, and let out at the foot with no memory of its use. The people carry the memory instead. That is the trade. They are the part of the valley that moves: its hands, its ears, its repair. The valley keeps them the way it keeps everything — in use, in wool, in the manner of what they replace. When a wader's hands give out, a granddaughter steps into the oilskins, and the oilskins are patched, and the patch is in the manner of the old cloth, and the troughs cannot tell the difference, and that is the entire point. The bell rings at the packhouse. The lamps come on level by level. The hush takes them all back, and they go into it the way the water goes into the intake — willingly, and with a sound so low it could be silence, and isn't.
