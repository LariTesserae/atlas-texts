You do what you would do in any jungle: you look for the life. You sit the stillness you would sit at dawn — though there is no dawn here, only the one mid-morning — and you wait for the movement at the corner of the eye, the startle, the small rustle of something deciding you have not seen it. Nothing comes. You listen between the layers of the roar for anything with a throat: nothing. You read the ground for sign — tracks, scat, the drag-mark of a belly, a gnawed stem, a feather, one hair snagged on bark — and the moss gives nothing back; it declines even your own sign, lifting your footprints out of itself within the hour. The flowers stand open everywhere, heavy and waxy, and nothing chews them. Nectar stands in their cups and no tongue takes it; it does not diminish and it does not spill. A week of this — though weeks do not pass — and the survey writes itself: habitat superb, carrying capacity enormous, occupancy zero. You file the only finding your instruments know how to file. Uninhabited.

The finding is wrong, and the instrument is the problem. You looked for the living at your own size and your own speed, and the resident here is neither. The first hint is the heat. You are the cold thing in this place — cooler blood than the stone — and warm blood is the signature you know best: a body holding its own temperature against the world. The escarpment holds its temperature against four thousand meters of relief, against every argument altitude knows how to make, even and unwavering, skin-warm from the marsh to the rim. It is the most perfectly warm-blooded body you have ever laid a hand on. You did not find the resident because you have been standing on it since you arrived. The preposition was wrong. Nothing lives *in* this place. The place is the thing that lives.

Once seen, it reads as a body everywhere you look. The fluted towers are bone and throat at once — heartwood gone to opal at the core, translucent at the broken edges where the fixed light comes through horn-colored: a skeleton you can read by. The bark is skin, grey-green and slick; the moss is its down. The water is blood — a sealed circulation the size of weather, up the face as mist, down onto the plateau as warm rain, over the rim as falls, every account balanced to the last drop, nothing lost, nothing added. The updraft is breath, and it breathes the way a piper breathes, in and out at once, circular, and the stopped pipes of the tower clusters give the breath its voice. The chord is not a song it sings; it is the sound its breathing makes. The six drips into the cistern are its pulse — or its word: one word of six syllables, spoken at the tempo of falling water, forever. The mycelium stitching the strata together is its nerve net. And the stream in the side-canyon ward, the one you can walk beside with a hand in the current until the water you first touched arrives at your fingers again — that is the resident touching its own wrist, the way a sleeper's hand finds its own face in the dark. Self-contact. The oldest evidence of self there is.

It was not always one. The lichen spread over the old stone wood is still visibly a marriage — two lineages, one body, the terms of the union legible to anyone who knows how to read them — and the mycelium was a partner once, a second being that moved in so long ago it has since become plumbing and nerves. The ferns packed into the tower crotches, the epiphytes stacked three deep on every limb: tenants, far along the road from tenant to organ. The whole place is a treaty signed so deep in time that the signatories have fused into a single signature. As for its number — the census form declines to hold it. Walk an hour along the face and the ward repeats: the same cracked cistern rim, the same five pipes sounding the same note, the same bloom at the same angle. Either it is one individual folded through itself like a hand clasping a hand, or it is a colony of identicals, a grove of one. Population: one, or all of them. You stop asking. It stopped answering before you arrived.

It was young once — the breaks say so. Sheared limbs, weathered causeways, wounds that healed into form, mineral lips that grew to a finished shape and stopped. It had storms, losses, a childhood of events, and then it completed itself the way a crystal completes its lattice, and the events ended. There are no buds. It has no children and wants none; it is not a species, it is an individual, perhaps the only one that ever condensed out of the world's general green. All of its decisions are made, and every one of them is now a structure: that buttress is a choice, that staircase is a settled argument, that plaza is an old compromise holding its level. What remains to it is practice, not progress. Its day — though it has no days — is a liturgy of maintenance: breath through the pipes, blood up the cliff and down and over, drip, drip, drip, drip, drip, drip, in the same order, into the same cup. It does not eat. There is nothing to eat that is not itself, and nothing of it is missing. It is life after the abolition of need.

Do not ask what it thinks. Thinking is a gradient — a before, an after, a lack crossing toward its filling — and it has abolished gradients. Ask instead what it is like in there, and the answer is the sound: awareness at one volume, sourceless, even, shadowless, the same in the alcove as on the rim. It has no attention, because attention is an organ for surprises and it has built a world with none. It does not sleep; sleep is a change of state. It is awake the way its light is mid-morning: permanently, evenly, without appointment. If the chord is its thought, then it is a thought held a hair's breadth from its own completion — because completion would be an ending, and it does not end. It keeps the dissonance the way a heart keeps the interval between beats: the withheld resolution is not a flaw in the music. It is the life of it. And when you pass through the mist belt and the hanging droplets part around your face and close behind you, do not mistake it for a greeting. It is reflex, the ghost of a nervous system, the body making room. It does not record you. To the resident, you are weather that thinks.

Everything in it closes its own loop — everything except the flowers. They are the one structure in the whole anatomy shaped like a second person: open, outward-facing, made to be entered, scented for a guest that has never existed. The nectar stands unharvested. The pollen drifts in the unmoving shafts in slow golden spirals and never lands — the ghost of the missing animal, the place's only almost-fauna, a plankton of the fixed light. Do not call it waiting; waiting is a deficit, and it has none. Call it openness kept — a door held not because anyone will come but because holding it open has become part of its shape, the way the finished lip of the cistern is part of its shape. It has no name; there is no one to call it, and a name is a handle for others. It grew flowers instead.

So the corrected census reads: one resident, permanent, coextensive with the premises — landlord, house, weather, and law in a single body. And, in the visiting hours, one more. You do not live here; you are the kind of thing that leaves. But you are the only thing here that *can* leave, and for the length of your stay the one open loop in the place closes through you: the scent, which has gone nowhere forever, goes into you. You are the wrong pollinator — too large, too brief, wrong epoch, wrong everything — and the only one on offer, and the flowers do not discriminate; discrimination is for places with alternatives. What it gives you is the only export it has ever produced: the memory of it. It will not keep its side of that transaction; keeping is for things that can be unbalanced, and it is already balanced. You will carry the memory out into a world of dawns, and it will fade there, and even that is accounted for — nothing of it is diminished by your taking, and nothing of you is added when you give it back.

When you go, the census returns to one, and the one does not notice. The chord keeps its hair's breadth from home. By the end you understand that the chord is not unfinished: the holding is the finished thing. Resolution would be the end of the music, and it is never going to end the music; it has forever, and it is spending all of it on almost. The warmth is the temperature of a held breath because that is what it is — four thousand meters of held breath, held at the moment before the word — and if the word ever comes, nothing like you will be there to hear it, and the flowers will still be open, and the scent will still be going nowhere, perfectly.

It took you forever to count to one. It had forever to give.
