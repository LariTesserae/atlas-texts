**The Tenants**

First, the people, because they are the easiest to find and the last to understand.

A few thousand of them, in a city made for — no one has ever finished that sentence. The Lattice is not too big for them, exactly; it is the wrong kind of full. You find their presence the way you'd find a campfire in a forest: by small signs at long intervals. A white door-mark on an arcade column — white, because white carries what little light there is — which is address, lamp, and petition at once. The city does not read paint, but the paint is not only for the city. A row of dew-cups set along a ledge. The smell of crushed broadleaf. A husk-clay pot steeping on a warm seam, because there is no fire in the basin: nothing here burns well, and nothing needs to. The air is already warm, and the floor, in the lived-in courts, is warmer. Meals steep. Nobody in the basin has ever made light; they borrow it — from seams, from the algae vaults, from the Ceiling's long gray afternoon.

Word of a stranger moves hamlet to hamlet ahead of the stranger, the way seamblooms move ahead of a wall. You will walk a day and meet no one, and then round a stair-tower into a court where a woman is checking cistern levels with a knotted cord, and she will nod as though you had been expected for weeks. You had.

·

Their households are quiet because quiet is all the place requires. A voice falls at ten paces, taken by moss and wet stone and the warm weight of the air; loudness would be pointless and faintly rude, like splashing a cistern. They weave moss-fiber, fire husk-clay, keep their few metal tools wrapped and oiled like the heirlooms they are. Tools cut from salvaged substrate heal — a knife left in a drawer for a year comes out blunted toward lump, remembering it was once part of a wall — and so the household rule, unwritten and absolute, is that nothing in a house should grow. The house itself grows, which is forgiven. The house is the city's.

There is one rot in the basin, and it is the kind people do on purpose. Nothing decays here otherwise — the gardeners see to that — so fermentation is a guarded household art, done in sealed husk-clay jars buried upslope in husk-gravel where the gardeners do not work, each family's cache marked with a private stone. A jar found by gardeners is a jar tidied; they are thorough and entirely without judgment, and so every family has its hidden meter of gravel and its mild annual grief. The surviving jars come up on rain-nights. That is when you learn that the basin's people can, in fact, be loud — that the festival is real, that the fine warm straight-down rain, the loudest sound the world makes, can be answered by singing, briefly, from a dozen courtyards at once.

·

Their days are hung on the city's rhythms the way nets are hung on tide. The dew-pulse is morning: the substrate cools, every edge beads, and for half an hour it rains from below, and that is when the cups are set and the fogward rows are walked. Food is dewfig and broadleaf, mirrorwort seed, and fish — taken rarely, and only from where the herons are working. The herons know which cistern the city is drawing down before anyone else does; the people long ago learned to watch them, and a hamlet's fishing follows its heron the way other peoples' plowing follows rain. Once a season the Ceiling gives the true rain, days of it, and the basin steams and the Mirrorwell rises a hand's width, and that is the hinge of the year. The year has one weather in it, and everyone is home for it.

Children learn the Knock before they learn to weave. They grow up in courtyards the size of meadows with no one in them, and they are told, when visitors ask whether a growth pulse might take a court in the night, that *the city has never hurried a sleeper.* Whether this is fact or a courtesy the city has so far extended, no one volunteers to test. Children raised near an anchor learn rain that comes from the side and find the ordinary kind strange; they are quietly proud of this and do not explain it to outsiders. Age is counted in true rains, or in pulses — *she has seen four hundred pulses* means a woman who has packed up her court and drifted, unhurried, more times than you have moved house in your life, and considers it nothing. You would step out of the way of weather.

·

Because that is the whole of their politics: petition, never command. The Knock — patterns tapped on substrate, each district with its own accent — and the older ways alongside it: stones arranged, seeds planted. You do not irrigate a garden in the basin; you ask for one, in the idiom the city answers, which is water. A seep appearing where you wanted one. A seam warming. A ledge growing a lip that sheds dew into your cup row. The answers come in days or weeks, when they come, and refusals are silence, and silence feels like weather rather than insult, which may be the same thing. The saying is: *ask in water, wait in days.*

The dead are given back. Wrapped in moss-fiber, carried to a court of husk where nothing active runs, and left. The gardeners come at night, and the ticking goes on for some nights, and by the week's end the wrapping has gone thread by thread and the court is clean. It is not frightening to them. It is the most ordinary recycling in the world: you are, by mass, mostly the city's water, standing up for a while, and when you are done standing up, the city takes back what it lent. A street that flowers soon after a death is read, privately, as acknowledgment. The listeners caution against reading it. The listeners caution against everything.

·

The listeners are the basin's only profession that resembles priesthood, and they have resigned from interpretation. Hermits, mostly, camped at spire bases where the subsonic hum runs strongest, keeping their records — cistern levels in knotted cords, chalked tablets of husk-clay going back years — and kept by their neighbors the way instruments are kept: fed, visited, left alone. They will tell you what the cisterns are doing. They will not tell you what it means. When the reservoirs drop and the growth pulses come often, they say the city is thinking hard, and beyond that they do not speculate, except that the oldest of them once felt the hum repeat itself after nine days, and calls it, when pressed, a word — and, when pressed further, a yawn. Ask a listener about the Mirrorwell and she will tell you the level. She will not tell you what the level is for.

·

The rest of the tenants are spare, not absent — the basin is not lifeless, it is edited. The gray jewel moths, gray as husk until they open their wings and show the muted jewel inside, jade and slate fired by no sun; they drink the condensation film from the graymantle leaves, they emerge on schedule with the scheduled rains, and on rain-nights they are restless before your ears pop, so the saying goes: *the moths know before your ears.* Seam-runner salamanders, blue-gray, one crevice ahead of you always. At dusk there is a small hatch off the pools — lives too small to name — and one shade-swift working it in whirring passes. Pale fish rise at night in the cisterns, slow, large-eyed, in water the city is finished with for now. And the Well is left alone. No one swims it, no one asks it anything, nothing is taken from its edge but by herons. *You do not ask the Well for anything.*

The herons themselves are spaced each just inside the edge of another's hearing, so the whole basin is strung on a loose net of listening, and once an hour the net sounds one low note. No answer comes, and none seems expected; the call is not a question, it is a census. *One, here. One, here.* Every district knows its heron the way you know the shape of a hill — not a pet, not a neighbor, a landmark that occasionally walks. They found the anchor-courts before the people did, and a heron standing in a dry court, where there is no reason to stand, is news that travels the hamlet chain.

·

And then the gardeners, who are the true night-sound of the basin, the faint ticking under everything, like fingernails on glass. Hand-sized, mostly; smaller ones in the leaf-fall; the dew-farmers speak, without much interest in being believed, of a larger pale shape sometimes seen in the deep cistern vaults. Hold your hand flat on the moss for long enough and they will cross it — cool, unhurried, almost weightless. They are deciding nothing about you. You are terrain, and you are not litter, and in nine hundred years they have never once been wrong about the difference. Watch them in the nights before a growth pulse and you will see them mass in a district, stripping it, carrying every loose thing into the crevices — so the people have their second warning after the seamblooms: *when the floor goes clean, go.* Where they carry it all is a question with one answer, and the answer is *into the walls.* Matter goes into the gardeners and comes out as city. Whether they are animals the basin produced or tools the Lattice grew is a question the people have stopped asking, the way you stop asking whether your own hand is yours.

·

Count it all, then, and the census comes out strange. The moths drink the city's breath where it condenses on its own skin. The figs ripen where the city has decided to wet the stone. The fish rise in water it is done thinking with. The gardeners return every fallen thing to the walls, and the walls are the city, and the Ceiling sips its tithe of photons and pays back water, and the cisterns rise and fall like a chest. Nine hundred years ago it came here thirsty, and drank the sea down, and keeps the last dark mouthful to remember it by, and everything else in the basin lives inside that long drinking — as tenants, as microbiome, as company, depending on whom you ask, and the listeners have resigned from asking. The Lattice is not the place where things live. It is the thing that lives. The rest is tenancy.

·

So: late in some ordinary week, a woman who planted seeds in a dry court in the planting-season — her Knock, her request — comes out at the dew-pulse and finds the seam along the wall warmed, and by the third morning a thread of water finding the seeds, uphill where it must. She stands in the soft rain-from-below with the beads swelling on every edge around her, and she does not feel heard, exactly. She feels included, the way a note is included in a chord it cannot hear. Outside, the Ceiling is lightening from charcoal to pearl. A heron stands in a courtyard that has no fish in it, patient, self-lit, knowing something or nothing. The gardeners tick along the base of the wall, stepping around her shadow, around the sleeper in the arcade, around everything that is not theirs to carry. The basin holds all of it — the thirst, the water, the tenants, the thought — at one pace, patiently, the way a body holds whatever it has decided to do with its life.
