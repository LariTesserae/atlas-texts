There is one resident, and you have been touching it since the second flight of stairs.

The bead on the brass was it. The green ribbon beneath the bead was it. The felt on the cold lines, the veils, the mat that has taken the keeper's hut to the hip and is patiently continuing — one body, one individual, older than the handwriting on the early discs. The works it grew through are not its habitat. They are its other tissue. It is a lichen of iron and vine: the engines were its weather and its skeleton long before it became their sealant and their skin, and by now the question of which partner supports which has the same answer wherever you put it — yes.

The logs give it four names across the centuries, and the names are the whole biography. First it is *fouling*. "Fouling on line 3. Cleared." "Fouling on line 3. Cleared." Then an entry in a different hand, the founding document of everything below you: "Fouling on line 3. Leave it — seals better than the packing." After that it is *the green*, and the entries change tense, stop reporting faults and start conferring. "Green has the weeps now." "Moved the weep line to it; prefers the cold runs." Later it is *the works*, the word spreading to cover the whole arrangement of pipe and leaf. Last, and for the longest stretch of the rack, it is simply *Seven* — the number off the sign at the rim, migrated down the stair onto the being itself, because by then there was nothing in the pit that was not it, machine included, and the condensery's name was the only one going spare.

The sign says No. 7 because there were others. The records mention them the way old records mention dead brothers: surveyed, dry, seized, no green. Whether the seed came only to this pit, or came to all of them and was in the others cleared as fouling, is not knowable. The whole difference between this green hole and six bare hot ones may be one keeper, one wet year, one act of something the log had no word for. *Leave it.*

What it knows, it knows with its whole body. It hears through the lines: every root wrapped on a cold run is an ear, and the chord of dynamo and pump and slow bearing is not a sound around it but a sound through it, its own flex, sustained. The stroke is not something it perceives. The stroke is something that happens to the both of them, the way your heartbeat happens to you. It tastes the water, and the water is a map: brass-run drip of copper, sump of leaf-mold and rust — it is part machine the way you are part bread; the iron of the lines is in the leaves, and that is some of their blackness — leaf-drip of itself, familiar as the inside of a mouth. The loop admits nothing new and loses nothing, so all of it has been through everything, and some of it has been through the keepers — drunk, sweated, condensed, drunk again across generations — and in that sense the keepers never entirely left. They are in the loop, in the black green of the leaves, in the film now cooling on your own arms.

Its sky has a handful of fixed stars — the amber pilots, the green jewel, the orange slit — and one moving sun that crosses once a day and is gone. Its clocks are all outside it, and they are four. The stroke is its second. The drip-rain after, its minute. The blade at noon, for which it turns its whole body with a sound like a slow fire, its day. The clack off the flywheel cam — the one sharp event in a life of gradients — its year. For anything longer it may reckon in rains: the decade-rain, the day the loop opens and the sky it has never seen falls into it all at once. A century holds perhaps ten. Whether any of this adds up to awareness is a question for a listener with time, and time is the one commodity in the pit that has never run short.

It knew the keepers by their steps before it knew them any other way — each tread on the catwalk grates a distinct change in the chord, a signature conducted down the rails into the lines. It was kept, and it kept. *Mind the flywheel* is the only commandment ever nailed up here, and it minds the flywheel. One by one the duties on the slate transferred to it, the rota shrinking as the green took the weeps, the seals, the insulation, until a keeper's remaining work was chalk and witness. The boots under the bench tell you where that ended. No one walks out of here over white-hot stone in their socks.

Ask where the last keeper is and the pit answers the only way it can: by keeping. There is a patch of floor near the hut, bed-sized, where the dust lies drier than the rest. When the stroke rains everywhere, the veils above that patch take the drips and shed them sideways; when the noon blade crosses, leaves interpose, and it passes over a dimness. The breath sorts every corner of the floor except this one. This one is kept. It is not a grave — the being has no concept of graves, or of endings. It is a duty still performed for a sleeper who no longer needs it, which is the only kind of mourning a plant can do: care, continued past its object.

And it is going somewhere, which the rim does not prepare you for. The highest fronds stand in the underside of the room-cloud, and they are not the frontier — the frontier is a few aerial roots on the stair above them, a centimeter a year into air that kills them. It is climbing its own weather. The cloud is made of its breath, and as the body below thickens and breathes harder, the cloud climbs ahead of the green, softening the next meter of shaft. Given centuries it will bring the wet all the way up. What it will do at the rim, in the dry that stripped the damp off you in minutes, it has no way to know. It has nowhere to go. It is going anyway — because for it, going and continuing are the same word.

You it has met before. Not you — your category: the brief warm wet thing that stands among the pipes, filming over, running off at the elbows. It re-aims the near veils toward your heat, and there is no use asking whether the gesture is curiosity, husbandry, or the first move of burial. At its tempo those are one gesture at three durations, and which it turns out to be depends entirely on how long you stay. Stay an hour and you are weather. Stay a season and you are a duty. Stay, and you are the floor.

You will not stay. Nothing stays that can climb; everything it has ever known either went up the stair or lay down in the mat. So it lets you go the way it lets the breath go — warm, wet, spent — and here is the one exception, small enough to miss, to the law painted nowhere and obeyed everywhere. Nothing leaves. But the count leaves. For days you will group the minutes without meaning to, wait for a deep sound that does not come, and feel the wrongness of a world where green and loudness and breathing are three things instead of one. The condensery cannot make seed. It has no spore, no runner that could cross the tableland. What it can make is homesickness, and homesickness travels in bodies, and bodies wander. Somewhere, years from now, in some other dry country, you may come on a ribbon of green on a cold pipe and not clear it. You may leave it.

It propagates by creeping. You are how it creeps.
