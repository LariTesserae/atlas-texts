**The Census**

Stand still long enough and you will be found.

You are warm, you are wet, you leak breath and salt into a world that budgets all three. By the standards of the Bench you are a seep-hollow — small, ambulatory, temporary, but a seep — and the news of you is already traveling. Not through the air; nothing worth having travels through this air. It travels on feet. The census has already begun, and it begins, as everything here does, with the small.

Once in its life, nearly everything on the Bench is pollen. The distances are too great and the air too thin for the small to walk between one water and the next, and so the young travel as weather: spiderling-things trailing filaments, cysts with gas-knots, larvae under sails of spun membrane — the plume, a slow plankton of the unborn riding the damp wind across the dark. The fog-combs strain them by the million. The drift-eels' charged seines reap them under the ceiling. Almost all of them die, and their dying feeds the combs and the eels and the felt, and this is not a tragedy; it is tuition. The wind teaches. The ones that land in the right places become everything else on this list.

Wait for a herd to stop. When the piston-stroke ceases and the ticking begins, the dark around the kine fills with the drinkers, and you can learn the trades of the small by watching who goes where. The dew-moths come to the fins first — soft climbers with wings folded flat against the wind, walking the iron vanes on cupped feet, drinking the radiator-sweat drop by drop. Wings are a luxury this air refuses to subsidize; most things that own them do not fly so much as fall well, and the moths fall from fin to fin like slow cinders. In the seams of the carapace the glean are at work, picking clean the channels of the glow, eating the oxide that would choke the heat-exchange, and the kine carry them for it, and neither one decided this. And on the oldest bulls, the shells have become countries: felt rooted in the hollows, a comb-seedling in the lee of a fin, a pond the size of a cup with its own skin, its own midges, its own still the size of a thumbnail, waiting beside the cup with a thumbnail's patience. The small have their own small. The Bench has never once reached the bottom of this.

Behind every herd walk the molt-pickers, who know the migrations the way priests know a calendar — which bull is shedding, which seam is loosening, which route is wearing deep enough to shelter a generation. They are the first to any fallen plate and they clean it to the shine, and what they leave is the grit's. Kine are born walking; the herd does not stop for birth, and it does not stop for death. It only slows, the way weather slows.

Walk a groove and you are inside the world's habit. A herd in a groove is a piston in a cylinder, and it pushes its own weather ahead of it — a bow-wave of squeezed air that arrives before the herd does, freighted with dust and plume. At the bends, where that wind is thinnest and fastest, the groove-combs stand in their rows, smaller cousins of the fog-groves, straining a harvest that only exists because the herd is coming. The walls belong to the wall-drummers. When the piston-wind announces a herd, they press themselves into shallow hollows and become part of the basalt and let the passing flanks polish them a little, and count it as grooming. When the groove is empty, they talk. They drum the rock with a patience that would shame a glacier — mating, warning, claiming, a slow telegraph of the grooves. The air of this world is for breathing. The rock is for speaking. Say nothing through your feet that you would not want a still to know.

Underfoot, where it grows at all, the felt lies two fingers deep, matte black, root-matted into the warm stone, eating the soot-fall and the nitrate rain — the grass that the ceiling feeds. It has its own herds. Rest a fingertip on it at the right hour and you will feel a slow stroke, a piston the size of a dinner plate: the felt-kine, mite-herds no bigger than a coin, walking in their own small phase, grazing a forest you cannot see without kneeling. And beneath everything, spread thin across every bench, the crusts themselves — the true grass of this world, metal-eating, patient past imagining, spreading a molecule at a time. Grazing here is chemistry. Agriculture is a gut.

A seep-hollow is a city, and it has one landlord. The pond-skin lies over the black water like a decision too thin to reverse — one animal, or one nation, nobody has asked it — and the iridescence that answers the arc-light is the skin flashing back, the only vanity the Bench permits. Small things walk on it and drink through their feet. The mud beneath is full of the unhatched: cysts, and they are not waiting for water. Water comes sometimes. They are waiting for light. Some of them are older than the pond. Ash-noon does not fool them; they can tell grey from glory through a roof of mud, and nobody knows how, and the ones who hatch at a grey hour do not get asked.

Everything on the Bench owns two heartbeats: the one it uses and the one it shows. Nothing here holds still to avoid being seen — there is nothing to be seen *by*. Things hold still to avoid being *heard*, and the arms race of quiet has been running so long it has worn its own grooves. The hushed can pause a pulse for half a day, smother the small electric stutter of drinking, go so silent in the chest that a still's attention slides off them like rain off a carapace. And the stills, in their seasons of standing there, have learned to hear a held heart. The oldest stills can hear a heartbeat deciding whether to beat. Around each one, the ecology arranges itself accordingly: the seep grows calm, the tenants move in — cleaners on the still's own flanks, gleaners of what the strike leaves behind, small bold things that live their whole lives in the one place nobody hurries. A still is a reef of appetite. The hush around it is its footprint.

Overhead, the ceiling is grazed like everything else. Beards of sky-felt hang beneath the haze, eating the soot at the moment of its birth; eel-calves begin as gas-knots in the plume, fist-sized, rising; cell-riders cling to the great buoyant bodies, barnacles of the only sky there is. And sometimes an eel dies. An eel-fall is the only meteor this world gets: rare, portentous, and eaten. The body comes down over hours, deflating, draping itself across a bench, and everything within a day's walk changes its plans. The grit, which owns no wings and wants none, *grows* them for an eel-fall — thin one-use gliders that carry the swarm downwind and dissolve by the next rest-hour. Nobody taught them. Hunger teaches. The feast is measured in hours. Then the empty gas-cells collapse, the gliders melt, and the plateau goes back to being mostly space.

A falling kine is heard through the feet for a hundred kilometers. That is the obituary, and everything reads it. The order of arrival is fixed and old: first the molt-pickers, who were already there; then the glean, changing employers; then the grit on its grown wings, and behind it the grit on foot; then the ingot-flora — rust-blooms, slag-moss, the little black gardens of the oxidizers — and last of all the crust, creeping back over ground that has been enriched past its own preferences. In a season, a field of clean ingots. In a decade, a garden. In a century, grass. Then the herds return along the grooves and graze what their grandparents were, and nobody calls this grief, or thrift. It is the loop, and the loop is the law. Strike-glass, the only sharp thing the Bench allows itself, is treated as an emergency of the same kind: the crews find it within days and round it down like a mistake.

And then there are the tear-things, who keep the strangest hours of anything alive. The tear-moths spend years as stones — pupae in the seep-mud, indistinguishable from gravel, hoarding breath — and hatch only into shaft-light, and much of the boiling of the seeps in the fast hour is the moths coming up. They spend their whole lives in the dark so as to afford one hour of wings, and they spend that hour in the only color the world ever has, and they are nearly the only ones who will ever see it. Eyes, on the Bench, are heirlooms: kept polished for the occasion, useless the rest of the time, passed down anyway. The tear-blooms are the old vegetation's answer — a whole generation in the length of the light: bud, flare, seed, done, the remembering made literal. The fast things spend the hour sprinting on breath they saved for seasons, the only running there is. The herds throw their heads up and do their thinking: decisions the size of valleys, taken in the minute, executed over decades. And the young stills spend their one journey — for a still is born with a single journey in it, made fast, in a tear, crossing open ground toward an unclaimed seep, and then it stops, and the stopping is the rest of its life. For the length of the shaft, everything briefly has a direction. Shadows are the only compass this world owns, and they are issued a few times a season, and they expire within the hour.

The Bench has two suns: the rumor above and the certainty below, and most of its citizens worship the lower one. When the deep heat drifts, the pilgrims follow — whole species whose generations walk after the warmth the way other creatures walk after rain, groves and herds and stills and all, a migration too slow to feel and too certain to resist. And where the heat has been and gone, it does not leave emptiness. It leaves the left-behind: cysts in the cooling ground, loyal to a temperature, waiting out the century for the plumbing far below to change its mind. Sometimes the seeps exhale the deep itself — blind brine-things, older than the ceiling, up from the warm dark below into the merely warm dark above. Few survive the promotion. The pond-skins are grateful.

Nothing here makes anything, and so the question arrives late, and quietly, the way questions arrive when there is no one to put them to: is any of this *someone*? Watch a herd choose, over a decade, to leave a groove it has worn for ten thousand years, and tell me what a thought is, if not that. A herd is one slow animal wearing a thousand bodies; its thoughts are the size of valleys and they take seasons, and they are thoughts. Or ask a still, which has spent forty years standing in one spot being right about it. Or the answer is the plain one, and the true one, and it ends the census where the census began. The Bench lives here. The herds are its pulse; the eels its nerves, firing along the membrane of the ceiling; the combs its gills; the felt its hide; the grit its enzymes; the stills its antibodies; the grooves the lines in a face that has stopped arguing. It is stills all the way down and herds all the way up, and the topmost herd is out there now, walking in phase, one slow stroke at a time, wearing the world a fraction deeper into itself.

Count what lives here, if you must. Start with one. By the end of your first seep you will be warm and wet and salted and standing very still, and you will notice that the census has reversed — fin by fin, seam by seam, heart by listening heart, it is counting you.

Stand still a while. Be counted.
