The company census, which is taken from the rim by men who do not come down, puts the population of the Kettle at thirty-one souls. Ask the same question below, in the hall, after the second drink, and the answer comes back without hesitation: three.

The count goes like this. There are the keepers. There is the green. And there is the Note — or the engines, or the town, depending on who is counting and how far down the bottle. The keepers list themselves first out of politeness. They know they belong third.

Thirty-one, give or take a birth and a retirement. They live in the five lit cottages of the row and in rooms above the press-sheds, and they eat in the hall with the nailed-open shutters, in shifts, by lamplight, the way everything down there happens: in rounds. There is a hoistman, and dynamo-minders, and moss-tenders who train the lantern-moss along its trellises the way other gardeners train vines. There is a lamp-man whose round takes four hours and who carries his spares in a bandolier across his chest; the Kettle burns through bulbs in the wet, and he knows each one by its flicker, and walks the lanes at a pace that never varies because it cannot — his feet keep the engines' time whether he asks them to or not. There is the green-warden and her shearers with their hooked knives. And there are the drivers, the hands, whose shifts the whole town can tell apart blind, because the Note has accents. A good driver fingers the release valves the way a player fingers a flute, and the keepers know whose hand is on the engines the way you know a knock at your own door. Two of the current keepers were born deaf. They are said to feel the Note most honestly, through the soles and the boardwalk, and at shift-change the drivers consult them, half in jest and half not, on the engines' health.

Nobody is born a keeper except the children. The rest come down the cage on a posting, and the posting begins with a fortnight that is understood to be a trial, though no one calls it that on paper. The ones who will fail show it early: a headache that will not lift, the brassy taste turning to nausea, sleep that will not take the beat, a heart that keeps trying to set up its own time against the Note and losing. They go back up pale, and are paid for their trouble, and are spoken of kindly. The ones who pass cannot tell you when it happened. There is simply a morning when the whistle sounds and their pulse agrees with it, and from then on they are the Kettle's. They are mostly the tired, the mis-timed — people whose own rhythm had gone somehow wrong in the dry country and who find, down here, that wrongness relieved. Sometimes a spouse follows a keeper down. It usually ends the same way. They loved the keeper; they could not keep the Kettle. The Kettle keeps who it keeps.

And what it keeps, it changes. The pulse slows within the fortnight; that is in the rule book. What is not in the rule book is everything after. The dreams come in rhythm — keepers dream in time the way sailors dream in swells — and their one nightmare, the shared nightmare of everyone below, is silence: a dream in which the engines stop and nothing replaces them. They wake from it with the heart galloping at some orphaned private tempo and lie still in the dark until the Note gathers it back. Watch two keepers carry a pipe-length along a boardwalk: they do not count, and their feet agree. A few of them carry watches, gifts from family up top. The watches are right. Nobody believes them.

They talk little, and in the gaps of the engines' cycle, so that every conversation arrives syncopated, and down below interrupting is impossible — the engines interrupt first, and everyone yields. Children learn the syncopation as grammar and are startled, on their first trip up, by how people in the dry country all speak at once. What the keepers do not do is sing. A song is a second rhythm, and the room will not hold two. There is a harmonium in the hall, brought down long ago on a bet or in optimism; it was played exactly once, and it sits in the corner like a refused argument. But they hum. One note, low, found in the seam of the chord, at the washtub, at the cutting round, in the coal drops. A cottage full of keepers humming is the nearest thing the Kettle has to a choir, and visitors mistake it for more machinery, which the keepers find funny and do not correct.

The day is the Beam's. Everything is set by its slow transit, and what needs sun is put in its path. On certain roofs the keepers keep shallow boxes of soil — peppers, beans, a few tomato plants, herbs — and in season someone goes up at beam-time with a child's feather and works from flower to flower, unhurried, the only bee in the Kettle. What grows in the Beam's path grows drunk with lean, all reach, and tastes faintly of iron, and is prized. The rest comes down with the coal on the incline: flour, salt, tea, tobacco, wool for the plugs, nails, paper, spirits. The flour keeps. That is the thing no one thinks to mention until you notice it yourself — there are no rats in the Kettle, no mice, no weevils, nothing gnawing in the larder, nothing scurrying under the boardwalks at dusk. It is a nineteenth-century industrial town with empty traps. The silence of the vermin is the loudest absence after the birdsong.

The children born below have no pulse of their own to surrender; they are issued the Note like a name. They learn balance on trembling boardwalks the way sailors' children learn it on decks, they slide the green hummock that was a boiler house, they race pole-boats on the high-water days, and they can keep a beat before they can count. Weddings are held at beam-time, in the pillar, because that is when the bride can be seen; the vows are short, because long ones will not fit the gaps, and a couple that has stood glowing in the Beam is married past argument. The dead are given back to the dry — sent up the cage wrapped in canvas, at beam-time too, so that the light walks them up the wall. The engines do not pause for either. That is not callousness. It is the whole point.

Leave is owed and seldom taken. The oldest keeper came down at nineteen and has not been up in fifty years, and what she says about the dry country is that the sky is too large and nothing holds the sound — that at night up there the silence is the loud part, and you lie awake listening for the Note the way you listen for a breath beside you in bed. Those who do go up wear their wool plugs at the throat like medals, like sailors' tattoos: proof of passage. They come back down with headaches and say little, and on the cage, when the Note rises up through the soles of their feet, their faces do the thing you will only see there. They relax into the noise like a man lowering himself into a hot bath.

The second resident is the green, and by weight it is nearly all of who lives there. One body, one individual, older than the company ledgers, spread under the whole town and up the walls to the spring-lines. It advances a handspan in a good week, audibly on still nights, and it leans toward the heat, say the engineers — toward the Note, say the keepers, and it is true that it is thickest around the pumphouse, shouldering the warm brick like something at a fire. It does not wreck; that took time to learn. It incorporates. The cottage in the west lane that it took after a family went up and did not return, it took gently: windows unbroken, door still on its hinges, the porch lamp left hanging clear in the open air, still lit. The warden says the green trained itself around the light, and does not say why. It works, whatever else it is — its roots strain the spring water on the terraces, its leaves harvest the condensation, its pressed oil rides out across the dry country and burns nightly in ten thousand small rooms. The lamplighters up top will tell you, if they like you, that a flame of Kettle oil stands steadier than any other. As if it burns in time.

After the third drink, the old argument starts: whether the green serves the town or tolerates it. The positions are fixed and everyone knows who holds which. That it is a servant, and the cutting rounds prove it. That it is a tenant, paying rent in filtration and oil. That it is the landlord, and the company pays rent in coal. And then the heresy, spoken lower, usually by drivers: that the engines are its heart and the pipes its veins and the keepers its tending cells; that the company did not found a town in the Kettle so much as build the green a body of iron, a hundred years ago, and that it has had a century to get used to having one. The company pays this no mind. The warden's maps mark the old places, the deep places, in red — where no hooked knife goes, where the rule is look and pass on, where nobody cuts because nobody knows what the mat would do if truly wounded and nobody intends to learn. Decade on decade, the red spreads.

And the third resident is the Note, or whatever the Note is the sound of. Lean over any channel and watch the rings expanding at its exact frequency, and then consider what the keepers say the birds see from above: the whole hole shimmering like a struck drumhead. Occupied. The room holds one rhythm; that is their theory and it explains everything the company cannot. An animal brings its own time into the Kettle and cannot set it down. The goat frets itself off its feed, the chicken starves, the aviary birds dash themselves — not from cruelty in the place, but mismatch: a chicken cannot consent to keep someone else's time. A person can. That is the whole of the exception. And at the end of the bottle, the keepers will tell you one more thing: that the engines did not bring the Note. That when the first boilers were lit, there was already a note standing in the sump, in the springs, in the throat of the hole, and the engines were tuned, deliberately or by their own settling, to join it. The company pays this no mind either. But the drivers drive like players, and the Note has accents, and sometimes the deaf keepers pause on the boardwalk at shift-change and stand very still, feeling through their soles, the way you pause at a doorway to hear who is in a room.

Night below, then. The lamps doubled in the black channels; the warden on her last round with the lantern held low, reading the cut lines; a grandmother in a cottage doorway with two fingers resting on a sleeping child's wrist — not counting, comparing. Pulses, pistons, drip-rhythms, the slow creak of the mat growing in the dark: all of it at one tempo, all of it answering to one hand. The census says thirty-one. The keepers say three. Lie there one night, feeling your own heart wind down toward the whistle's time, and you may decide it is one — one circulation, one held chord, keeping a few dozen people the way a chord keeps its notes. And the old rule stands, exact, with its quiet loophole intact: nothing with a heartbeat lives in the Kettle. Nothing that keeps it to itself.
