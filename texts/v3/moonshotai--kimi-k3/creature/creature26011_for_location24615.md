Everything that lives here arrived through a hole, or was born of something that did. That is the first fact of the census. The holes are few and they are all one-way: the failed intake screens at the one station, which admit larvae and elvers and plankton on the current and release nothing; the well grilles, which admit daylight, rain, leaves, and birds; the elevator, which admits four people at a time, with permits, and takes them back up again. Nothing that swims can leave. The boundary is a valve — rain passes, leaves pass, birds pass, light passes; water and everything in it stays. The Reservoir was given exactly one metabolism on purpose, the zinc anodes steadily consumed along every column. Everything else that eats, grows, breeds, and dies down here smuggled itself in through the plumbing. Eleven years on, this is what it has become.

**I. The Foundation**

The mussels run the water treatment plant they were never hired for. They crust every surface near the flows in black reefs, and their intertidal band on the columns keeps office hours — dry by night, drowned by day, the schedule shifted on holidays and heat waves, because their tide table is the power market. Their shells are the place's archive, and what they record is not seasons but the grid: growth checks laid down by price and demand, legible to no one, since no one reads them. In year six, during the nine-day maintenance shutdown, the tide stopped and the band stayed dry nine days, and the mussels clamped shut and waited it out, and now every mussel in the Reservoir older than seven carries the scar — a nine-day interruption in the record, the great silence written into shell. Their dead shells drift as hash into the cable trenches, and this is worth noting: it is the first sediment the Reservoir has ever produced. The place that cannot silt has begun, at geological slowness, to make soil out of its own tenants.

On the reefs the amphipods swarm like static, the grain of the food chain, and the ones deep in the trenches are going pale — eleven years of generations in the dark, a cave fauna in the first chapter of becoming one, in a cave with column numbers.

**II. The Parishes of Light**

The midwater belongs to the schools, millions per bay, and their lives are ruled by two clocks that exist nowhere else on earth. The first is the tide: up by day, down by night, six meters, the breath of the grid. The second is the sun wells. A bay with a well has a noon — the column of daylight steepens and the schools rise into it and turn in the pillar of sun — and a bay without one has none, only the constant lamps, and the fish there drift out of rhythm and feed at all hours like insomniacs, two kilometers from cousins who keep a day. In summer the warm inflow slides across the top of the cold basin in a shimmering false surface, and the schools rise into the warm lens to graze the plankton bloom, and the Reservoir acquires something like a season.

When the knock comes, the flinch crosses a bay in under a second — the pressure wave arriving everywhere at once, a million bodies agreeing simultaneously, a visible shiver passing through silver. Then they resume. They always resume.

They are tame the way island animals are tame. Nothing here has ever been hooked, netted, or thrown a stone at. The schools follow divers the length of a bay, nibbling the biofilm off suit-knees and lamp glass — a diver is, by their taxonomy, a slow-moving lamp that tastes faintly of algae. Near the turbine halls the school's edge draws a hard line where the roar begins, a berth that is remembered in the only way fish remember: by being in the school when the school turns. The fear is heritable through behavior, a map passed down fin to fin, with a blank on it where the machines are.

**III. Addresses**

Every animal here has an address. This is the strangest property of the place, stranger than the light or the tide: the grid that was painted on the columns to keep track of concrete turns out to keep track of tenants. Each column has a lit face and a shadow face, and the shadow face is the only night real estate in a world without darkness — the resting place, the denning place, the ambush site. A pike holds station at BAY 114 COL 22, northeast face, third meter, and can be found there next year, and the year after. No wild place lets you find the same fish twice. This one files them.

The pike under the sun wells have learned the single rule of the wells: anything that enters the light from above is food. In autumn they strike leaves. Once — it is in a dive log's remarks field — a swift that came down to drink and misjudged by a centimeter. The big catfish keep their territories under the submerged gantry cranes, one animal per machine, unhurried, two meters and growing, and they are going to keep growing, because nothing here stops them: no fisherman, no winter kill, no size ceiling but prey and time. The most precisely measured body of water on earth, gauged to the millimeter, will in fifty years contain record-breaking fish that no one will ever weigh. When the traveling crane moves on its ceiling rails — twice a decade, perhaps — no fish alive remembers the last time, and five bays empty in panic, and the old catfish slides out from under it offended, like a mountain relocating.

The floor's dropped things are all inhabited. The rail carts parked mid-floor are crayfish manors. The famous wrench of year one lies in focus where it fell, its eleventh consecutive tenant underneath it, one claw larger. And between the parishes — between the reef stations at the flows, the lamp halos, the well pools — lie the empty kilometers, the pelagic desert of nothing but cold water and light, which the catfish cross at night on errands of their own, navigating by the drone's gradient and the smell of distant inflow, the only wide-ranging citizens in a world of parishioners.

**IV. The Exiles**

The eels are the only ones who want to leave, and the only ones with anywhere to go.

They arrived as glass eels and elvers through the failed screens, riding an intake that flows like a river running the wrong way — a river that ends in a machine. They grew yellow in the cable trenches, in the conduit mouths, everywhere and nowhere, the undertakers of the bays: everything dies in the open, and the eels take it home. Every autumn the mature ones go silver and make the gathering at the outlet screens — thousands of them, a rope of muscle three hundred meters long, braiding and unbraiding at the grate, the steel humming faintly with the press of them. On the surface it registers as a small seasonal head-loss, a few millimeters on a gauge, logged for eleven years running as autumn biofouling, scheduled for cleaning. Once — year four — a dive team went down to scrape what they assumed were mussels off that grate. What they found is in the maintenance log, and it is the only entry in eleven years of maintenance logs that reads like a naturalist's field note. The work order was amended to *defer*.

Some of the eels at the grate are old now — veterans of eight, nine gatherings, grate-scarred, enormous, leading. And this is the thing no one is there to see: the young silvers follow them. The route across four hundred kilometers of numbered bays is taught and learned, a migration corridor transmitted animal to animal, ending at a wall. Culture, by any definition an ethologist would accept, in a fish, four hundred meters underground. And in the muscles of the oldest ones live the only memories of the Atlantic Ocean in the entire made sea — of estuary salt, of rain on skin, of a river that ran somewhere. Everyone else in the Reservoir is native-born or arrived as a larva too small to know anything. The eels are the exile generation, and their exile is hereditary, because new glass eels still come through the screens every spring, into a population that can recruit but can never complete itself.

The ones that stop going to the door stay yellow and keep growing in the trenches, permanent residents of a waystation. A eel can wait forty years. That is the trouble. The screens can wait longer.

**V. The Commuters and the Islands**

The swifts are the only citizens of both worlds. In spring they come down the wells like a rope of birds thrown down a chimney, spiraling the eight hundred meters of fluted concrete, threading the grille at the top with a flick, and their evening screaming parties in the air vaults come back eleven-fold off the hard flat surfaces until the place sounds like it has a crowd. They are the successful emigrants, the boundary's one exception, and they are also the gardeners: the duckweed almost certainly arrived on a swift's breast feathers, lifted off some pond on migration and skidded onto the vault water in a drinking pass. Now it rides the still eddies under three of the wells in green rafts the size of courtyards — the only true vegetation, and three islands besides, each with its own accidental fauna of springtails and a weevil, an archipelago of three conducting an island-biogeography experiment with no one ashore to publish it. The third raft was founded when a surge event tore a corner off the first and carried it through a gate. The rafts have genealogies.

The swift guano on the ledges is the only soil, and each spring a seed in it germinates — carried from the world in a bird — and each year the seedling dies by August, of light or of poverty. It is not counted. The duckweed remains the only true vegetation. But it is worth recording that every year the underworld puts up one green stem, unwitnessed, like a letter answered and then discontinued.

In autumn the swifts spiral up and out and the vaults go silent, and the silence is the Reservoir's calendar page turning. The eels, massing at their grate below, are the ones who cannot follow.

**VI. The Keepers**

Nothing lives here that walks. What walks, visits — two descents a year, four divers, permits, the elevator's light moving along the catwalk while the catfish scatter from footstep-vibration and the schools mob the divers' knees. The work is screens and anodes: the planned metabolism needs its zinc renewed, which makes the dive crews, in a sense, the place's only scheduled feeding.

One diver has made every descent since the filling. She knows individuals — by address, because the grid allows it — and writes them into the remarks field of the official slate, since the official slate has no column for fish: *COL 22 NE: wels, ~2 m, resident.* *Bay 40: eel gathering, defer.* Eleven years of remarks, filed as maintenance records in a cabinet on the surface, constitute the only natural history of the Lower Reservoir in existence. She does not call it that. No one has ever called it anything.

On the surface, at a dispatch desk, there is an operator who has watched the telemetry for eleven years and learned to read life through the wall: the October head-loss that is not mussels, the spring turbidity on the one optical sensor that is plankton, the nightly drawdown's small inflections. No one told her the Reservoir was alive. She inferred it from the instruments, the way you infer a heartbeat through a door, and the year she finally went down — one permit, one descent — it was exactly as the gauges had taught her to imagine it, which almost never happens to anyone about anything. She also keeps the list of unmatched knocks: a few each year that correspond to no logged gate movement anywhere. The system is larger than its log. Somewhere, someone opens something at three in the morning, and four hundred meters down a million fish flinch for a reason that was never written down.

**VII. The One Thing That Has Happened**

History, for the inhabitants, is eleven years of drumbeat and one nine-day silence. The year-six shutdown: the drone stopped, the tide stopped, the intertidal band dried and stayed dry, the knock did not come. For nine days the world did not breathe, in a place where breathing was the only proof of time. What the schools did in the silence is unrecorded. What the eels did is unknown. The mussels carry it in their shells. The catfish that lived through it are alive still, and the old diver's remark for that year reads, in full: *COL 22 NE: wary.* She may have imagined it. There is no one to ask.

**VIII. What It Is Becoming**

The asset is designed for a century; the tenants are eleven years in. By mid-century the lamps will have dimmed toward twilight at a percent a year, the self-eclipse no one wipes, and the place will go slowly night-blind while the schools graze the glass and lose. The reefs will be meter-thick. The catfish will be past anything in a record book, unweighed, addressed. The eel elders will die off one by one if the river runs up above fail — the most endangered population in the Reservoir is the one that remembers the way out — and the migration route will shorten each autumn until young silvers gather at the grate with no one left who knows why, pressing the steel out of an inheritance with no content.

None of it is protected. There is no paperwork for it: you cannot designate a flood buffer as a habitat, and the permit office has no form whose reason reads *to see*. The most measured water on earth holds an uncounted population, unowned, unlisted, unnamed — a wild thing growing inside a tool, kept by cold and current and two women with clipboards who do not use the word.

But consider the three of them at the boundary, because the boundary is the whole question. The old eel at the grate, who remembers what is beyond it. The young bleak turning in the pillar of noon, native-born, third generation, who has never conceived of a beyond — for whom the world simply ends where it ends, as every world does, as ours does. And the swift at the grille, folding her wings, threading the gap out into weather, carrying the one world into the other twice a year, unimpressed.

The Reservoir does not know what it is. Everything in it does. To the ones born in the bays it is not a reservoir, not a buffer, not a battery. It is the world: numbered, seven degrees, loud, lit, rising and falling on a breath drawn four hundred meters above. The tool waits out its century. The tenants are not waiting. They are living in it, and the living is the answer the engineers never specified — the tool has not yet been used enough to know what it is, but what it is, now, is *inhabited*.
