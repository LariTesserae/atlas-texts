Take a census of the Kolk and the trouble begins with the categories. Souls: forty-one at the last counting, nine of them children. Willow: one, though it roofs six lanes and will not hold still to be counted. Engines: three, by the enginemen's count, and they will hear no argument — Maud, and the twins, and if you suggest that a pump is not a living thing you will be asked, reasonably, what you call something that breathes, eats, has a pulse, and dies if neglected. Moss: uncountable, and the Clerk refuses the category. And then there is a dash in the margin where everything else should be — the webs, the nests, the tracks, the small warm multiplyings that any other census of any other place would spend its ink on. Nothing with four legs. Nothing with wings that stays. Everything that lives below the rim is a person, a plant, or an engine, and the longer it lives there the harder it is to say which. The wardens put it in six words: everything that keeps, and nothing that strays.

The town calls itself the Forty, and has done at every number — the Roll in the Clerk's office shows thirty-six once, fifty once, and the name held through all of it. The Roll is one of two books on the Clerk's desk; the other is the Level Book, and between them they divide the world: the one is the water's autobiography, dictated daily, a single digit at a time, and the other is the town's. Births, deaths, arrivals, leavings. Everyone in the Forty is two things, because forty pairs of hands is what the keeping wants and the keeping wants more than forty tasks: the engineman's mate cuts hair, the senior mosser lays out the dead, the Clerk pulls teeth on the last barge-day of the month with a pair of bright pliers and no ceremony. When the Roll runs low, word goes up the Float and a lad or a widow or a runaway comes down the stairs to try the life, and the town watches them through their first winter the way it watches a new gasket. Some stay. Some go back up. Both leaving and dying are called *gone up*, and the Forty enjoy the ambiguity — you can come back from one of them.

You can read the place in the bodies. The Forty walk with a gait that visitors mistake for drink until they feel the raft move under them — a slight sea-roll, knees soft, weight carried a little aft, the walk of people whose floor has never once in their lives been entirely still. Children learn to walk on a flexing world and are preternaturally steady; they go barefoot half the year, since frost has never been recorded, and their feet widen and toughen and carry the biofilm sheen like everyone else's boots. Hands are soft from wet work, nails soft, skin never quite dry; a Kolk man in an up-country market can be picked out by smell alone, wet wool and peat-reek and the green, though he cannot smell it himself. There are mercies. No fleas, no lice, no flies on the meat — the old Book notes a fever year that came down the Float with the flour, because the town's plagues arrive by barge or not at all. Wounds are dressed in wound-moss from the mire, changed daily, and heal clean if they heal at all. What the pit takes instead is the lungs and the joints: the Kolk cough comes with age, and the old people carry their hands like tools that have been left out in the wet. They would not trade. Ask them and they will tell you what the midge costs up there, and what the frost costs, and that they have seen neither below the rim.

The day is three watches long, and the whistle is its bell. Everything in the Forty is done between whistles — bargains are struck between whistles, meals happen between whistles, a child who asks *when* is answered in strokes, and learns without teaching to shape a sentence to the engine's four-second beat: the words in the gap, the rest while Maud speaks, the thought resumed. It makes a distinctive music, and up-country folk find the Forty slow-spoken and loud, with strange holes in their talk — talking, one bargee said, like men waiting for an engine. The Forty, for their part, feel talked over everywhere else; outside speech has no rests in it, and they come home from the market hoarse and bruised from conversation. At shift change the whistle swallows everything, and under it the town signs — a hand-language grown from work-signals, now used for jokes, for courtship, for scolding children across a lane, for the things said during the one sound too big to speak under. Newcomers cannot sleep at first for the stroke, and then, in their second season, cannot sleep without it, and the town has a saying for the day a person truly belongs: the first night a wrong sound wakes you. Not the noise — the change in the noise. The whole Forty sleep lightly inside their roar, tuned like instrument strings, and a valve taking up a new note will bring people out of cottages at the third watch in their shirts, asking each other what it was.

The Clerk writes the level each morning when the warden brings the brass gauge up from the leats, and children learn their letters from the Book's margins, where a century of clerks has noted what the digits wouldn't hold: storms, births, the birch that came down on the catenaries, *heron over, did not stop*. Noon is known by the silvering overhead. The year's turns are known above, not below — the Forty feel winter as the Float gone hard and the coal pile sinking, summer as berry weeks on the mire, when the children go up and come back stained and astonished, itching from midges they had only heard described, full of the size of the sky. The sky up there has no sides, the children report, and some like it and some cry, and the first ascent — the day a child is taken high enough up the stairs to hear the birdsong of the mire, which does not follow you down — is remembered by everyone the way other people remember first snow.

The Kolk keeps no graves. The pit is below the water; a grave is a sump; so the dead go up the same stairs everything else has come down, carried feetfirst on a duckboard — the town's own walking-surface, the only bier it knows how to make — to a walled plot on the dry ground beyond the rim. Maud does not stop for funerals. She cannot, and the Forty have made a theology of the cannot: the engine keeps her beat through the procession, and that is the town's mourning, the world refusing to pause because it must refuse. What happens instead is this. The First Hand — Cobb, these twenty years — opens the crank-pit door, and the name of the dead is spoken into the motion, and a name once given to the wheel goes round with her as long as she turns. So the dead stay below after all, in the only way the Kolk permits: in motion, or in wood. Because each child of the Forty is given a willow cutting at seven to plant and tend — their own rod of the one great clone — and when a man dies his willow is harvested into the fabric, post into strut, strut into wall, and the lanes are full of people. There are willows in the wattle whose planters went up fifty years ago; the wardens call them the absent, and prune them first.

There is always a girl named Maud; which came first, the engine or the naming custom, is an argument the Forty keep the way they keep everything else, carefully and forever. Cobb reads Maud through the soles of his boots and the palm laid flat on her column, and will tell you she runs proud on the good coal and sulks on the small, and that she has a cough coming in the big end that he will re-metal in the spring. The twins quarrel — everyone says it — when their quick strokes fall out of alternation, and their keeper goes down and settles them like children. The Knocker in the rising main is blamed, affectionately, for misplaced tools and broken sleep, and the wardens read its knock for news of air in the main the way a doctor reads a chest: it is the town's oracle, and it is almost always right, which is more than can be said for oracles elsewhere.

And then there is the Quiet, which the old people talk about the way other people talk about eclipses, and which one woman alive actually remembers. Mally was eight at the great re-babbitting, and she is the town's pilgrim-shrine now; children are brought to her to hear about the nine hours, and she demonstrates the silence the only way it can be demonstrated, by stopping talking, and letting the room hear the drip-rain come close, and the sumps breathe. She tells how the water leaned closer — everyone swore it, the Book shows a rise across those hours, the only ascent in a hundred and eleven years of entries not answered stroke for stroke — and she tells the other thing, the thing the town has never decided about. In the ninth hour, she says, a bird came down. A dipper, on the leat by the kiln-cisterns, walking in the water the way dippers do on the mire brooks, like a little man at work. She was eight; some say she dreamed it. The Clerk of that day wrote levels only, and was much blamed afterward, and a later hand has penciled in the margin of that page a single question mark — the only one in the Book. But the wardens who hold the fence theory, the ones who say Maud's infrabeat runs through the ground like a wall that nothing wild will cross, are Mally's strongest backers, because in the nine hours the fence was down. And this is why every child of the Forty is taught the strangest rule of a strange childhood: if ever you see wings below the rim, run and wake the Clerk. It will mean the engine has stopped.

The plants are citizens. The Willow — one tree, one sex, alone, a century old, never seeded and never able — roofs the lanes and lags the walls and is renewed entirely by hand, and the Forty are its bees: they carry its cuttings, strike its rods, weave its wattle, and it shelters them in return, in the only pollination contract the pit contains. The moss is a crop on a decades-long rota; the alder is a coppice that goes down into the dark — under every house a wood stands head-down in the black water, holding the floors up, and the wardens speak of it as the other town. And everywhere the Forty do the work of the missing animals, because the work must be done by someone. No worm turns the litter, so the wardens fork it into the raft's edge. No bird moves seed, so boots and hands move cuttings. No grazer crops the green weight, so the mossers climb the catenaries before the storms and strip it. The Forty are the Kolk's fauna — its one animal, forty-bodied, loud, warm-blooded, endlessly busy at the tasks a living place generates and cannot do itself.

The absence is the thing visitors feel first and the Forty feel last. Nothing below the rim has ever crept up on anyone. There are no footfalls but boots, and the duckboards drum them hollow, and the town knows one another by step the way up-country folk know faces. In the clerk's loft there is a box of carved animals made a lifetime ago by a man who had been up and seen the originals — a dog wrong in seven ways, something meant for a sparrow — and they are brought out when a child asks, and the words survive with their referents gone: a small soft thing of moss is called a cat, for a while, at a certain age. The margins of the Book record the crossings — *heron over*, *two geese, high*, always *did not stop* — like comets. And the Forty have their theories, the fence and the acid and the deep, and none is proved, and the old people hold a fourth that they do not press: that the pit is already inhabited, that a place can be spoken for, that there is room in a bowl for one life and the Kolk's is taken.

Because that is the honest census, in the end. The water writes its one-word letter every morning and the Clerk copies it down; the pumps grow a cylinder at a time to answer the raft's slow sinking; the rota outlives its makers and is kept anyway. We don't beat it, the wardens say of the water; we keep up with it — and the saying is the whole character of the people below, a people of keeping, for whom maintenance is not a chore but a covenant, whose law is the rota and whose scripture is the Book and whose heart must not stop. Ask what lives in the Kolk and the truest answer is the largest one. The Kolk lives in the Kolk — one thing, engine-hearted, willow-skinned, moss-furred, breathing mist through its rim of leaning alders, remembering itself in a hundred and eleven years of careful hands. The Forty are not its owners. They are its attention: the part that watches, the part that worries, the part that writes the number down.

Inhabitants: one. Hands: forty-odd. Heart: one, and it must not stop. And once in some years, high over the rim, something with wings crosses the shadow and does not stop, and the Forty look up from their keeping — and the Kolk, the one thing that lives there, looks up with them, out of forty pairs of eyes.
