Count the inhabitants. It can be done, and it never has to be done twice. There are three, or there is one, or there are millions, depending on how you count — one alga, some forty-odd grasses that may be a single grass, one tree mostly elsewhere. A population that fits in a sentence, in a province that would fit inside a village green.

Begin in the grey, as everything here does. The grass numbers forty-seven clumps — say forty-seven; no one has counted, and the figure changes at the rate of roughly one per lifetime. Each clump stands a hand from the next, and the spacing is not a habit but a record. A seed that falls in windless air falls straight; a clump's radius is the distance a seed can drop from its parent; and so every tuft stands exactly one seed's-fall from its mother, and the whole garden is a genealogy written in placement, a family spreading across the shelf at the pace of one hand per generation. In all likelihood they are one plant. Once, a single seed came across the plain, over the dimple's lip, through the slot — the purest accident the place contains — and everything green that has ever grown here is that one fall, repeated. One self, fractured into forty-seven bodies, each alone in its allotment of dust. The loneliest population in the world.

It lives on the average. The grey that feeds it is noon and midnight stirred together and poured thin, and the grass has organized itself around this: it is a plant that has never known morning. Somewhere in its cells the old clock still runs, the one that tells leaves when to open, but it has nothing to grip — no dawn to anticipate, no dusk to close against. The grass has forgotten the day the way the rubble has forgotten the collapse. It spends nothing on color, because color is wages and it earns barely enough light to live. A blade may stand for decades. Growth is measured in grains.

And the dead stay. Nothing grazes, and almost nothing rots, so the dead blades remain among the living, dried to the same pale thread, indistinguishable — every clump a congregation of ancestors, half of every tuft biography. The living roots thread down through the dust of their own dead, and this is the only eating the place permits: a thing consuming itself, at the pace of stone. The grass is its own fossil record, still growing.

Once in a generation, a clump flowers. It sends up a stalk — the tallest event in the grey zone — and opens a flower with no color in it, because color costs, and because color has never once been witnessed here. Nothing comes. No insect, no wind, no eye. The flower is pollinated by itself or not at all; it is not for anyone; it is the grass's own idea, repeated. Then a seed or two falls straight down, one hand away, and the species takes its single step. Add a germination once in a lifetime, and somewhere in the crust a crystal letting go once in a decade, and you have the place's entire calendar: three clocks, all ticking at rates indistinguishable from rest. The walk of the grass ends at the second bend, where the grey fails. The innermost clump is the frontier of the sun. Beyond it, in all the room, nothing green has ever stood.

On the one wall the grey can reach lives the alga — the film, the dullness, the least living thing. It is almost a habit of the rock, a thought the wall keeps having. Its cells divide at intervals geology would find reasonable; it makes the minimum of green; and its far edge is drawn with precision, because the film ends exactly where the last photon ends. The wall carries, in life, an exact map of the light's reach. Whether it arrived before the grass or after it — a cell on a grain of blown dust — the room does not say; it keeps no order of arrivals, only the arrivals. Between the alga and the grass there is nothing: no exchange, no dependence, no competition. Competition would require a shared want, and the only want here is light, and each stands where its light is, and that is the whole of their relationship. Adjacency without ecology.

The third citizen is the largest and the least present. Only its fingertips live here. Above, on the plain, the tree lives its bright life — the hard light, the wind that never once reaches its roots, rain when rain comes, whatever small traffic of birds and beetles the scrub affords — the only resident with weather, with neighbors, with days. It does not know. Generations ago one root found a hairline crack and followed it down, and now a dozen pale roots hang in air that has never moved enough to sway them, and a root's whole education is soil — pressure, moisture, the chemistry of others — and these have none of it. They are unsoiled. They grow downward on the old instruction, into a nothing that never answers: a question asked for a hundred years. One hangs a finger's width above the salt pan. It grew toward the rumor of water, and the rumor stopped there, and so did the root, and the place has thereby measured, precisely, the distance between the living and everything else. It is the narrowest gap in the room, and it is held. Others reached the floor and spread in slow stars, palms flat on the dust, and found dust, salt, stone, and no one — and persist anyway, working their mild acids line by line into the rock, the tree signing a document it cannot read, in a room it does not know it is touching. The one thing that travels between its two worlds is scent: the ghost of resin, the tree's breath conducted downward, the room's only perfume, a message with no reader. The room, for its part, borrows a little moisture from the roots and gives nothing back; it is the only transaction the place conducts, and it is one-way, and it is nearly nothing. The grass knows nothing else. The tree knows everything else. Between them, the whole of the place is inhabited, and none of it is known.

If the tree dies — drought, fire, the plain's long chances — the roots will remain, dried in their poses, indistinguishable from the living, death here being a way of remaining. The room will keep them, as it keeps everything. Its most recent resident will become part of its furniture, and nothing will change, and that will be the change.

And in the dark room itself, in the flour-dust, lie the seeds. A few each generation drift past the second bend — the species' only diaspora — and come to rest where nothing germinates, because nothing permits it, because no water has stood in this room for ten thousand years. Viable or dead, it makes no difference; both conditions are remaining. The room keeps the garden's entire future, embalmed, a rumor of green filed beside nothing else.

Three arrivals in all of time — by wind, by dust, by persistence — and no fourth is coming, and none is needed. This is not an ecosystem; an ecosystem is made of hungers, and nothing here has ever been hungry. It is not a refuge, because nothing here is fleeing; not a frontier, because nothing is following. It is three lives that happen to share an address and nothing else, spaced like the scrub above, like the columns, each one alone — the place's one rule, which the living obey exactly as the stones do. If the salt keeps sleepers, if the roots carry tenants in their flesh, they have made no sign, and here sign and no-sign are the same fact. Life is not the exception to this place. It is one of its properties, like the dark, like the temperature of a held hand: something the room has, at rates indistinguishable from rest.

They are not waiting. The grass stands in its hourless grey; the alga holds the light's last line; the root keeps its measured finger's width above the salt; the seeds lie in the dust beside the footprints no one has left. The room is finished, and these are its finishing touches — alive, unwitnessed, continuing.
