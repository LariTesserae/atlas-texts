Count the inhabitants of the Stack and the answer depends entirely on the unit.

By mass it is the graze, and it is not close. The city is a condiment on the graze. Take one up from a ledge-walk — everyone has, the ledges seethe with them — and it lies in your palm as a cold muscular comma, eyeless, translucent where the ember-light touches it, nosing for brightness with a blunt unhurried head. It is blind, but it is not lightless: the appetite for light runs skin-deep through the whole uncounted nation of them, and this is why the Dark Law exists, and why the Kindle works. They are not vermin to the Stack. They are the weather, the audience, the majority, the soft quick conscience of the place — nothing dropped is lost, nothing spilled is wasted, nothing goes unanswered. *The graze always answers*, the saying goes, and it means several things, depending on tone. Crossings crunch. Water is filtered. And when they phase-lock, when the choir rises and a district begins to ring like a struck bell, you learn about the other citizens-by-mass: the Answerers, climbing the towers with their licensed voices to sing the swarm back out of tune — part rescue crew, part riot squad — and the choir-mad, the ones who climb to be *inside* the sound, who fight being Answered, who have to be carried down weeping from a mercy that felt, from within, like theft. Above the graze, the midges rise off the canals at their appointed ticks in a haze you learn to breathe through your teeth, and the clothwings burst from under every ledge to meet them, tarpaper wings echo-netting the dark, chattering in their roosting thousands. The chatter is the sound of ceilings here. At dawn-tick the guano-scrapers make their rounds and cart it all back to the film-farms, because nothing — this cannot be said too often — nothing here is wasted.

Then there are the warm mountains. Sump-kine walk the deep channels with their herders, lowing like foghorns, filtering the water as they go — the city's slow kidneys, half-domesticated and wholly opinionated. A kine knows its route for fifty years and knows its herder's voice from a district away; a beloved kine's death is a district event, and the old ones are given names that outlast the people who gave them. Herders sing to them constantly, low songs with no edges, and the kine's dung goes to the film-farms, and the kine's warm flank is the best place in the outer rings to press a cold back against, and nobody who has slept against a sump-kine ever quite respects a wall again. In the culverts, meanwhile, the bell-eels hum — electrosensitive, patient, prized and dangerous in equal measure. They read your nerves through the water. The eel-hunters know this, and so they hunt with false hearts: little pulse-boxes played at the surface, a counterfeit life throbbing its invitation, and the old eels know *that*, and come anyway, out of what the hunters insist is curiosity. You can tell an eel-hunter by the tremor — the eel-twitch, the occupational signature of having been, at some point, the less interesting of two heartbeats. Their flesh is feast food, betrothal food. The hunters themselves are proud the way lightning-struck people are proud.

By seniority, though — setting one exception aside for last — the first citizens are the menders. Knee-high, many-legged, deliberate as geology in miniature, wearing the only commonplace lights in the city: those dim ember status-dots that no graze troubles, which means the menders are the only things in the Stack that can be *seen* going about their business, and people do watch them, the way other peoples watch weather or birds. They graze the film and re-deposit it as mineral. They have territories and temperaments. Every district knows its ancients and has named them; every district has its eccentric — the one on the Span that has built nothing but arches for eighty years, the Cantor of the Rows whose oscillator cracked sometime in the last turn and who now hums flat while it works, beloved beyond reason. Children carry pocket salt for them. This is the whole mechanism of the one tax nobody evades: salt obligations, kept at the column bases, renewed like courtesies. Keep your salt and the menders re-point your house, shore your ledge, stitch your bridge. Let it lapse and they wall you up — politely, inexorably, without anything you could call malice — and when the arrears are paid they unseal you again, eventually, in their own time, which is not yours. Now and then a mender begins a new mender, and this is a founding, and the district brings gifts of salt and considers itself lucky for a decade. When one stops, the others arrive within the night and walk the parts away, and no one has ever watched what they do with them. What they *are* has been politely ignored for centuries — animals that remember being machines, machines that forgot, the founders' children, the founders themselves gone small. The old superstition says they still hold the original lattice-song, the blueprint from before the archives, and that a household that keeps salt long enough may wake one morning to find its doorway rebuilt in a style nobody has used since the city was young. They are the only citizens who remember the Stack before anyone lived in it, and they are not telling.

The people themselves — who by their own reckoning come first — are heat-shaped, and you know them at a touch's distance before anything else. They orient to warmth the way a compass orients: their directions are warmward and coldward, and the hearth's hum, felt in the inner ear and the soles and the teeth, is their true north. They read the heat-plume of a body at arm's length. They know faces by hand, with cool unhurried palms, and visitors find this unsettling right up until it becomes the tenderest thing that has ever happened to them. Their voices run low because bass carries in the thin cold air, and their language is freighted with click phonemes that double as sonar, so that a conversation is also a continuous quiet mapping of the room, and a foreigner cannot so much as say the city's name: the true name is a whole-body sound, heel-stamp and tongue-click and held hum, and it doubles as the first verse of the city-song, and *the Stack* is only what the archives render it as. A person's name is likewise a thing you can feel before you can say it — a knock-rhythm, a hummed interval, a calling-measure — and when someone dies the measure is struck once on the district's name-post and then retired, never knocked again, so that every old threshold in the city is dense under the thumb with sleeping names, and the dead are a texture you absently read while waiting for a door to open.

They are classed, like everything in the Thaw, by temperature. Warmth is rationed by ring and inherited like land, which makes class thermal and legible at a glance that nobody needs light for: the inner families are warm-skinned, flushed, old, layered, rich in every sense including the literal one, and they buy *quiet* — sound is licensed, and the deepest luxury in the Stack is a hushed street — while the outer rings live loud and cold and cheap and consider the inner districts steam-soft, and both are correct. Whole families spend generations marrying and bargaining their way warmward, ledgers kept in fractions of a degree, dowries written in heat-rights. The rim has its own aristocracy, the rime-harvesters with their frost-scarred hands and their pride: the edge is where the world is real, they say, and the Kettle is where people go to forget the ice. And out at the fringe of the fringe are the reefers, the squatters in accreting ruins, the ones who let the city eat their houses around them — film-choked, graze-thick, unscraped — half cautionary tale and half romance. *The city eats the inattentive*, the saying goes; the reefers feed it on purpose, and some of them keep salt meticulously and live inside mender-sculpture that has been growing for three generations, and call it the only honest art. In a city where everything answers, secrecy is the rarest commodity, and so their monster stories are not about monsters. Their monster stories are about quiet men.

Their children learn echo before speech — babies babble in clicks, and a first word is often not a person but a *place*, the verse for home. The maps are kept as songs, every street a melody of intervals and returns, and to forget a verse is to lose a street, which makes the old people living atlases and makes listening to them a civic duty rather than a courtesy. The games are acoustic: clack-tag, and hide-and-silence, the one game that frightens adults, because any grown-up may end it at any moment by calling *Answer!* — and the hidden child must answer, must, because silence is the ice's name and the city cannot bear not knowing where a small body is. From seven or so they join the scrape-rotation, because everyone scrapes; citizenship in the Stack is substantially the act of scraping. They are taught the tick and the Dark Law, the salt count and the verses, and somewhere around the first solo walk coldward — alone, out past the Fur, into the Sting, where the supercooled fog freezes gladly on whatever dares it — they stop being visitors in their own city.

Of the work, the Stack really only has two verbs: grow and scrape. The soundwrights grow — standing in their horn-choirs at the Growth Front, singing hexagonal columns up out of the wet at a meter a decade, so that a column is a composition played across three generations and a badly sung one rings false under the fist for a century, and the guild feuds are conducted, with perfect seriousness, as arguments about music. The scrapers scrape, on every facade in rotation, out on the reefs, forever. Between the verbs stand the tenders: the film-farmers, who cultivate strains named for colors no living person has verified — rust-red, sulfur-yellow, tar-black, heirloom words out of the disputed archives — and who practice a connoisseurship of tongue and nose so refined that the Kindle, when it comes, is partly their judgment day, feuds settled and started in the space of a few breaths of light. And the watchers: the core-readers, who drill the columns and date history by accretion rings and hold, with maddening serenity, that the Stack is less a place than a process and nothing in it is finished; the breath-watchers, who read the hearth's long swell and shallow and keep the calendar of breath-decades and are currently — as they have been for three careers — divided over whether the present breath is deepening or failing, publishing their positions once a decade; the lamp-wardens of the Dark Law, many of them recruited from the deaf, from the ones for whom the dark costs nothing and the choir does not sing, signing into each other's palms, watching for light-leaks the way other police watch for lies. And the drummers, keepers of stone-speech, whose pulses arrive hours late through the basalt from the other warm islands — with whom the Stack plays games of drum-chess that outlive the players, conducts courtships at four hours a move, and mourns the silent stations like empty chairs. Three turns ago one of those chairs sat back up, drumming in a dialect nobody recognizes, and the drum-college has been composing its answer ever since. Apprentices are trained to continue a reply they will never hear finished. *There is time*, the masters say. *There is always time here.*

In the shaft beneath everything, the Keepers keep the Kept Warmth liturgically, by inherited procedure — polishing field-works no living engineer parses, reading instruments nobody can rebuild, speaking an archaic register among themselves. They are the warmest people alive, bare-armed in the deep winter of the world, and on the rare occasions they ascend, the districts stare. Their great fear is the shallow centuries, because the city carries the scar: the Cold Galleries, whole districts entombed in clear ice when the warmth withdrew, street-fronts frozen mid-life, archaeology of their own great-grandparents. People visit by lamplight-of-special-license and give directions by the frozen — *coldward at the woman with the eel-basket* — and what unsettles even the Stack-born about the Galleries is this: those dead were taken mid-life, unrendered, their last tithe unpaid. For the ordinary dead, the funeral is quiet — keening by license, at the tick's low hour — and the body is given back: fats, salts, mineral, the bones' salt to the menders, the name struck once and retired. So the city is built of its ancestors, re-pointed by them, kept pointing by them, and the Stack-born find this the deepest comfort there is. *Nothing here is wasted* is not an economy. It is a benediction.

Their days run on the tick; their meals run hot — broth against the eternal damp, film-cakes, graze-oil, eel at betrothals, ferments aged three breaths in the warm dark and sold as vintages. The market buys a roar-license and haggling at full voice is a luxury the whole crowd shares. They sleep in sealed cells behind silt-screens, to a lullaby of breath and drip and hum and distant lowing, and visitors lie awake through it, and the Stack-born abroad on the ice discover silence-sickness and have to sleep holding their clack-stones. Privacy is whisper-hooks pressed to the jawbone, sign-cant signed into a palm, lovers' voices dropped under the market thunder — and the young, inevitably, go down to the culverts where the eels hum, to whisper-sing unlicensed songs at each other, because the romance of the forbidden voice never once gets old. Now and then, rarely, the ice delivers someone: the cold-come, walkers from the sibling islands, half-dead at the breathline, whose sun-stories go into the archive disputes and whose grandchildren click like natives. A visitor is lent clack-stones and a guide's elbow — the guides wear licensed bead-hems so you can follow their little rattle-song — and is told, kindly, to stop trying to look. Most do. You will.

Twice a sky, they are all of them reminded what they live inside. Once a decade, by dispensation, comes the Kindle: one great lamp above the core canal, and for a few breaths the city *sees* — faces, hands, the film-farms' heirloom colors proven or disproven, the menders' ember-dots like a scattered patient constellation, children staring at the crowd they have only ever heard — while the eye-shutters keep their lids down on principle, holding that the seen world is a moment's lie and the heard world the true one, and then the swarm-storm rises, the graze in their billions, light devoured by life, the living eclipse, until the wardens douse it and the roaring dark closes back over. For a season afterward everyone walks taller, carrying sight around like a new coat, until it fades gratefully back into sound. And once in a generation, unchosen, comes an Opening — the Lid parts, the stars say the blanket is off, the cold walks inward at a walking pace, and the entire city goes quiet and listens to the world's teeth grind, children asking what stars are and being hushed, until the Lid knits itself shut and the whole Stack exhales.

But the first inhabitant — the exception saved for last — is the Warmth itself. Down the shaft older than the city, the Kept Warmth breathes, and has breathed longer than records run, and whoever kept it first is unknown, though the menders were there and are not telling. The people do not worship it, exactly. They room with it. It is the landlord of the world, the hum in their bones, their true north, and their word for themselves is a music word that means, at once, *the kept* and *the held note* — the thing the planet has been sustaining, breath-decade after breath-decade, under its sounding board of cloud. Ask any of them, at the breathline, at parting, and they will give you the whole of it in two lines, which is also their blessing and their census and their only theology: *Warmth keep you* — and you the Warmth.
