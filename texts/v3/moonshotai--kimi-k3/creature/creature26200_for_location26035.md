No one in Rocío has ever heard the town begin. The chord was sounding before anyone now living was born, and before their grandmothers' grandmothers; a child of the basin takes it the way the unborn take a heartbeat — as the medium, not the message. Ask a tender what the town sounds like and she will pause, because to answer she must go and listen to the thing she has never once been without. The traveler climbing up through the scrub passes into the chord's outer reaches like an illness coming on; the child of Rocío is conceived inside it, born into it, laid on a mother's chest above a bedframe that hums, so that the first sensation after touch is the town. Midwives hold newborns to the wall, out of an instinct no one can date: to introduce them. Colicky babies are walked along the flumes at night and stop crying. The wet watch — the night tenders — carry other people's infants up and down the green channels for hours and count it among the uses of the works.

The people are the tenders, and the tenders are the houses. Every engine-house is a family and every family is a note in the chord. There are five now; the old ones say there were nine at the founding, before some of the notes were allowed to die, and the surviving houses carry the dead ones the way you carry the name of a dead sibling. A house that has taken on a drowned neighbor's duties is called double-eared, and is revered and resented in about equal measure.

Heredity is real in the basin, but it is tested. Around the seventh year each child is walked the length of the works and asked, now and then, without warning, to sing what they hear. Not the roar — the roar is everywhere — but the note underneath it. Whatever the child sings back is read the way other peoples read palms. Mostly the child sings its own house's note. Sometimes a child of the Third House sings the Fifth, and then there is a giving: formal, gentle, permanent — the child goes to the house whose note it heard, and no one calls it anything but luck. The children of mixed marriages are tested most gently of all, because the town believes an ear can inherit a quarrel.

A name in Rocío is a short melody in the upper band, two or three notes, whistled or sung, because a name has to be findable in the noise. In the Drop Hall, where no one speaks, a name is a shape of the hand pressed between the shoulder blades, and children of the First House learn their hand-name before they can write. When a child takes its note, its handspan is measured against a bronze gauge and recorded, because the handspan is the unit of the craft — gates are opened by a handspan, closed by half a handspan — and a tender whose hands are still growing is apprenticed in patience before anything else.

The ear is the organ of character. A dependable person holds her note. A lazy one has gone flat; an anxious one has gone sharp. Willful ignorance is wool in the ears. A small but decisive distinction is a handspan of difference. To die, or to lose one's mind, is to go into the white. The highest compliment the town can pay is also its plainest sentence: *she hears well*. Letters come late, if at all — the tuning comes first — and many tenders read slowly their whole lives. The basin keeps its memory in bronze and in bodies, and regards paper the way sailors regard fresh water: necessary, and not to be trusted far.

Everyone owns two voices. The upper voice is the public one, pitched into the one clear window in the machine spectrum, and it is used all day, every day — a little above the natural register, flute-like, carrying. A conversation across a flume looks like two people calling across a mountainside, and market morning sounds like a mountainside full of lost shepherds, all of them found. The other voice is the low one, the chest voice, the true one, and it is never used in the open air, because the open air belongs to the chord. To hear a person's low voice is an intimacy on the order of undressing. Families trade them indoors; lovers trade them skull to skull, jawbone against jawbone, because bone carries what the air will not. No traveler has ever heard a true voice in Rocío. A traveler may stay a month and be loved, and it will all have been in the upper band — work-love, flute-love — and the townspeople will not understand what the traveler finds strange in this.

The wool and wax are replaced daily, and still, by forty, the Drop Hall tenders are going gently deaf, and this is called retirement and is not a euphemism. It is a promotion to a deeper shift. The old ones become the bone-listeners. They go palm-first through the works — hands flat to the turbine housings, soles bare on the catwalk gratings — reading the fundamental the way you read a child's forehead for fever, and their diagnoses are final. They sleep better than anyone in the basin; the young say this with envy. They move through the loudest hours smiling slightly, like people who have been told a secret and are keeping it. In a sense, they have been. The town watches its deaf with an attention that is more than respect, for reasons that will come clear.

The damp is the other inheritance. Nothing dries; bedding is aired daily and is never dry; the basin cough is a soft percussion under the speech band that everyone stops hearing. Palms go white with lime, nails rimed, hems stained copper-green from the catwalk bronze. The basin foot is broad, bare, and sure on wet concrete in fog. The diet is the Basket's diet — beans, salt, dried things — brightened with ditch-cress and a coppery tea of flume-tree leaf, and everything tastes faintly of the inside of a flute.

The work is not moving water. Water moves itself; that is the town's founding trick. The work is noticing. At dawn each house walks its round — a listening round, done at a particular pace, because the ear needs repetition the way the eye needs light. A gate opened a handspan, listened to, closed by half. A harp cable re-tensioned because in last night's gust it sang a quarter-tone off and the whole field strummed wrong. A bearing gone sharp is heard across the basin, sometimes days before the instruments agree; the house that hears it first sends word, and is thanked, and the debt is remembered for years. In the afternoons the fog comes over the rim and the Harps turn their faces to it, and the real harvest happens in perfect silence, drip by drip, while the tenders work underneath in the roar of everything else. At night the wet watch goes out with a lantern, and the night tenders say they hear best then, because there is nothing to look at.

Once in a generation each house stops its engine for a season and tears it down, and for that season the town is a chord with a tooth missing. Everyone agrees the partial chord is far worse than none would be. People sleep badly, weep at nothing, startle at nothing; the eels climb the cistern walls. The town has a custom for this. When someone is found crying, the first question is not *what is wrong* but *which house is resting*. This is not unkindness. It is the basin's first psychology: they grow up knowing that some portion of their sorrow is acoustic. It does not make the sorrow less real. It makes it shared — and in Rocío, shared is what real means. The resting house works all season inside the town's low irrational anger; they bring apologies out with their tools in the morning, and neighbors bring them food in the evening, and both are sincere, and neither helps. Then the season ends, the gate is walked open half a handspan at a time over days, and the note rises out of nothing back into the chord, and the whole basin exhales. The return of a note is the nearest thing the town has to a resurrection. Weddings are timed to it when they can be.

The eels are colleagues, and the word is exact: they are instruments. Pale, slow, thick as a wrist, they feel pressure change before anything built does; *the eels are up* is a complete diagnostic sentence, and a house will re-walk its whole round on the strength of it. They are never eaten. Eating one would be a cannibalism of the works. Once a decade they spawn in the warm sumps, and the town keeps a vigil of sorts — children are given the first count, kneeling at the sump edges with lanterns — and a heavy spawning is read as the mountain saying, for now, all is well. A thin one is not spoken of for a year, and then discussed for ten.

The swift is the only citizen who chose the town. Everything else was born here, planted here, or built. The swifts moved in — behind the spillway curtains, into the loudest real estate in the world, safe from everything that hunts by ear — and the tenders take this as a compliment they did nothing to earn. Their thread-thin cry sits above even the speech band; children imitate it to say *I'm here* without breaking a hush. A traveler once brought up a caged singing bird as a gift. It stopped singing on the second day and would not start again, and the town sent it back down on the Basket with a letter of apology addressed to no one. The flume trees, self-sown now along the channels in long green lines, are the town's map of itself and also its unpaid watchers: a leak announces itself as a volunteer tree growing off-line, and children are taught to thank such trees, because they found water the tenders hadn't. At dusk there are midges, dancing in the fog — the basin's whole allowance of frivolity — and in the empty districts, where whole streets built for tens of thousands drip and accrete under moss in orange and black, there are children, for whom the ruins are territory.

The chord has a geography, and it is not the one on the flume-map. Where the notes cross and cancel there are pockets — soft spots — a bench-sized hush behind a flume pier, a hollowing under an aqueduct arch, half a stairwell in the empty quarter where the penstock chord eats itself and you can hear your own breath. The map of the soft spots is kept by children and lovers and passed hand to hand; it drifts as the chord sinks, so every generation's hiding places are its own, and grandparents cannot find the quiet they remember. Conversely there are the loud nodes where the notes double — left as gravel, unbuilt, crossed at a walk with wool in. Children navigate the basin blindfolded for sport, by the chord alone, and a tender can name her position to within a street with her eyes shut, and is mildly offended if you test her.

And then there is the one made silence. The Dry Room — felt-lined, the only quiet room in the basin, where the Standard sleeps — is the town's sacrament and its dangerous drug. Once, and in childhood only once, each child is put into it alone, at about nine years, for a measured time. The door closes on the loudest town in the world and opens on the sound of their own blood. This is called the hearing, and everything afterward refers to it. Most children come out and never ask to go back, and are considered healthy. Some come out changed — *dry*, the town says, half pity and half awe — and ask again, and are rationed, because silence in that dose is not good for a person raised on sound. The keepers are chosen from the dry ones.

The keeper of the Dry Room is the town's one scholar and its only quiet worker: literate in the dead hand of the contracts, reader and writer of the letters that go down on the Basket. The archive lives with the Standard because paper lives only in dryness — two hundred years of letters from below — and the keeper keeps them, and keeps what the tenders call the Book of Hands: the catalog of lowland handwriting, folio after folio of specimen strokes, dated like the eel-counts. The notes from below are answered, eventually, in different hands, and the keeper is the one who notices when a hand ends. Then the town hears an announcement as formal as any: *there has been a death in the lowlands*. And they mourn — genuinely, in the upper band, at the landing, on a Thursday — a person whose name they never knew, whose face was never even imagined, on the evidence of the slope of a letterform. *Hand Seven*, the tenders of the Fourth House still say, *was a friend of this house*. The keeper is regarded as slightly holy and slightly suspect, in about equal parts: the only citizen whose work is silence, and the only one who knows what has been said.

The week bends toward Thursday. The Basket comes up out of the white, dripping — salt, beans, cloth, machine parts, letters — and the landing is the one place the whole town gathers that is not a work site. The letters are read aloud in the upper voice above the roar, contracts first, then the rest; orders acknowledged. Sometimes there is a thing — a toy, a seed head, a shoe, once a tortoise shell — that the town will turn over for years. The children are told the theories: that below is one town, or many, or a ruin tended by the dead, or one very organized old woman, or the sea. The town's considered position is the sentence it uses like a creed: the water goes down, the Basket comes up, it is enough, slowly. No one goes down. The last who did — three keepers back — wrote letters home for years, and the keeper filed them, and the hand in them wandered decade by decade until it answered to nothing in the Book, and then to something: a lowland hand. Those letters are kept apart. Tenders read them when they need to remember what the basin costs.

A traveler, when one comes, is heard before seen: a strange footfall stands out of the reverberation like a wrong note, and children are sent up the rim trail to count and confirm. The town knows the stages — the pressure behind the ears, the harmonics, the day it becomes weather — and keeps a protocol older than anyone's signature: wax and wool on the first day, before anything is asked; gentle drilling in the upper voice, because the traveler shouts, flat and in the wrong band, and winces at the sound of their own voice; and on the third day someone walks the traveler to a soft spot and leaves them there an hour, which is the town's idea of medicine. The crying comes on about the fourth day. Everyone is kind about it. Every traveler cries. It is never mentioned afterward. And every traveler is asked, eventually, the standing question: *what is it like, down there in the quiet?* The answers — peaceful, empty, you can hear everything, you can hear nothing — are chewed for years and never satisfy, because the traveler is describing an absence, and the people of Rocío have never been given any absence to hear. The traveler, for their part, wants to ask how the townspeople stand it. It is like asking them how they stand their own heartbeat.

Their calendar is the cycles: Thursday; the decade spawning; the decade striking; the generational rest of a house; and, once in a life — twice for the long-lived — the retuning. The striking deserves its own account. Once in ten years the Dry Room is opened and the bronze plates are brought out and struck, at stations, through the streets, and for an afternoon two towns sound at once: the young bronze one fixed in the plates, and the old living one a semitone and a half below. Everyone stands in the fog and listens to the interval, which is the only portrait the town has of itself in time. People weep openly at the striking. They do not weep at funerals.

When a house can no longer hold its note — when wear and the settling mountain have dragged it to the edge — the whole town is retuned downward together, and the ceremony takes most of a year, and the reason it takes a year is the people. The water could be walked down in a week. But a town that dropped a semitone in a day would break — sleep, temper, the eels, the inner ear's whole economy — so the gates are walked down handspan by half-handspan across the seasons, a glissando so slow it can only be heard across months, and the people descend with their town the way divers descend: in stages, with pauses, so that nothing tears. Babies born in a retuning year are called children of the descent, and are held to be adaptable, or marked, depending on the house. The old can still hum the founding chord entire, including the dead notes, and when they do, the young go quiet, because the dead notes are where the grotto is, and the drowned engine-house, and everything the lime is keeping.

Ask what the tuning is *for* — not the water, not the power: the consonance itself — and the town divides, politely, along a seam a century and a half old. The gauge houses hold that the founders tuned the town to measure the mountain: that the chord is an instrument, the sinking semitone a needle, and that one day the needle will reach the stop and they will learn what they were measuring. The song houses answer that a tuned thing is a meant thing; that you do not tune a watershed to take a reading, you tune it because a watershed should not sound sour; that the descent is not data but the song going where songs go. The argument is conducted in the slow way — a position stated at one retuning, answered at the next — and is barely out of its first movement. Under both doctrines stands the Last Semitone: the day the fundamental passes below hearing, and the loudest town in the world becomes, by its own definition, silent. The eldest speak of it with that look no outsider can read, grief or hope or both. The children ask what the town will be then. The gauge houses say: *then we will know*. The song houses say: *then we will finally be singing to ourselves*. And everyone, in both houses, looks at the old deaf tenders going palm-first through the Drop Hall — because the old are living there already, in the world that is coming, at the bottom of the scale, feeling the note the young still hear. They are not retired. They are ahead.

The dead are given to the lime. The works leak, and where they leak they build, and the dead are laid in the leak-lines — in the drip of a flume apron, on a weeping threshold — and the water does the rest, at a fingernail's thickness a year. The old channels are lined with white mounds that were people, and grandmothers read them like rings and measure the years with a spread hand: *she is under three fingers now*. To fossilize is not, in the basin, a figure of speech, and the tenders' calm about it is the same calm they bring to the retunings and the eels: it is a long cycle, and they are a long-cycle people. Marriages are held in the grotto, under the white cascades of the drowned engine-house, the entombed turbine accreting somewhere inside like a pearl the size of a house — the town's one beautiful room, growing more beautiful every year, unstoppably, without permission. The tenders say the pearl is a cousin of theirs and of the dead: the town keeping what it made.

What the basin does not contain is solitude. There is no hour and no room — the one room excepted, and it is rationed like a drug — where a person of Rocío has ever been out of the sound of the whole. Even the dead are folded back into the works; even the missing notes are hummed. This is the deepest adaptation, deeper than the upper voice, deeper than the wool and wax: they are never alone, and they know it in the sternum. At dusk, when the fog comes in earnest and the warm rain starts and the Harps turn their faces into it, the old tenders sit on the catwalks in their wool and wax and take the chord through their bones, and the children whistle one another's names across the flumes in the upper band, and somewhere a gate is opened a handspan, listened to, closed by half. A hair flatter than last year. The eels settle. The swifts come home to the waterfall. And the people — a few hundred in a city built for tens of thousands — go on holding their notes, which is to say holding one another: the part of the chord that knows it is sounding.
