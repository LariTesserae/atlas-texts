The census of the vesicle is short, but it takes centuries to read.

Almost nothing lives here, and what does lives carefully — so carefully that living, in this room, has come to resemble something else: custody, apprenticeship, a long rehearsal for a lake that does not yet exist. Three citizens, if the count is kept strictly — a writer, a keeper, a parliament of drifters. A populace, if the thicket is counted as many instead of one. A mystery, coiled in the silt. A relic, held at the ceiling. And the rain, which is not a resident but the only caller. That is the whole of it. It has been enough for longer than any mind here can hold, and the room is in no hurry for more.

The scribe first, because the scribe is writing everything down. A hand's length of translucent ribbon, thicker than the filaments, thinner than the fronds, moving the way decisions move through something slow: by increments too small to witness, and then all at once, a body-length gained. The map is not in its head. Nothing so heavy could fit in so light a body. The map is in the water — in the taste of the rain, which has seen the world above and reports it the only way water knows how, by flavor — and the scribe reads the instruction with its skin and writes it with its passage, the way a needle reads a groove and in reading, sings. Nor is the work only drawing. Silt is a poor archivist. A bay inked a thousand years ago is shallower now by the width of nothing, and so the scribe's labor is re-tracing as much as tracing, refreshing the shore the floor keeps trying to forget. Where its path crosses an older line, the older line is always fainter, and the scribe lingers there a season, the way you might repeat a name you were in danger of losing. Its going makes almost no sound — a faint hiss, a grain's complaint — but the room keeps even that, holds it in the fronds and lets it go late and out of order, so the scribe is sometimes heard where it is not, drawing a bay it finished decades ago. And sometimes, the laws bending as they do, it is heard drawing a line it has not drawn yet. It will arrive at that reach of floor years later and find the work half-familiar, the way a word is familiar before it is spoken. During the blue hour it stops. Everything stops; the room is thinking about something else then. The scribe rests inside the one hour when seeing is possible, and does not see, and does not mind. It has never looked at the map and never will. It is not drawing the lake's shoreline. It is being the lake's memory, early.

The gardener is easier to watch, being slower. Its body is glass; there is nothing to hide it behind, and a visitor with light enough could read its whole history in it — the last meal it took, decades gone, still faintly visible as an amber thread along its gut; the old injuries, healed transparent. It stacks. The cairn near the center stands six stones high, and a seventh is in transit, and has been in transit for nine years, and the gardener waits beside it with the serenity of a thing that has never once been on time and never once been late. Falling here is a negotiation, and the gardener is the room's chief negotiator; it knows which pebbles will decide to rest — each sealing its choice with a small click the felt will hold for a generation before releasing — and which will choose the ceiling instead. Those it does not correct. It walks the walls as easily as the floor, many-limbed, unhurried, and tends the inverse floor in the felt above, the hovering stones, which are not mistakes. Nothing here is corrected. Everything is catalogued by patience. The notches it has worried into the wall are depth marks for water the room has not yet risen to — but among them, spaced by no interval a visitor would catch, runs a second record: the count of the blue hours. The gardener is the room's calendar. It has never missed a lighting. When the lanterns come on it sets down whatever it is holding, or stops holding whatever it has not yet set down, and turns its glass face to the nearest lamp, and its whole transparent body takes the blue and holds it, and for that hour it is the most visible thing in the chamber — a lamp answering the lamps. And once in a decade it makes a slow circuit of the coiled thing in the silt. It does not touch it. It does not quite avoid it. It keeps the path the way a path is kept around something that must not be woken, or must not be lost. Perhaps the gardener knows what the coil is. Or perhaps it makes the circuit the way the scribe draws: by being drawn.

The undecided are the last of a cohort, and this is the room's one history: everything fixed here was once adrift. The thicket — all that pale profusion, the felt, the ribbon-fronds, the pearl-chains — is made of motes that chose to be plants, long ago, before the silt was deep. The scribe and the gardener are motes that chose otherwise. What still drifts is the remainder: a handful of flecks of almost, gathering near the center where the fronds are oldest and time is thickest, because there is more time there in which not to decide. They are waiting for more information, and information arrives at the rate of rain. During the blue hour they come closest to choosing — light is an argument, and some of them rise toward the lanterns as if light were a kingdom recruiting. Then the hour ends. The dark is also an argument. They relapse into deliberation, and the room, which is mostly made of their decided kin, is patient with them. Once, long ago, one of them half-resolved: it grew the ghost of something — a pallor of pigment, the bud of a limb — and thought better of it, and let it go. It drifts a little apart from the others now. They cite it to each other, in whatever way motes cite things, as precedent. When the last of them decides, the room will be finished being young. None of them knows this. Or all of them do; it would explain the deliberation.

Whether the thicket counts as one citizen or a populace, the room has never said. It is everyone else's address. It is the metabolism — drinking the thin warmth up through the young crack, drinking the gradient, drinking duration itself, so that the room slows as it feeds and feeds as it slows — and it is the clock. The lanterns are its one recurring thought: a thought about light, had by things that have never seen any. Where it found the blue is a fair question. Perhaps from the rain, which tastes of sky and insists, in its flavorless way, that the sky is a color. The blue hour is the thicket rehearsing the sky the way the scribe rehearses the shore — for one weak hour the fronds hold up their small hooded lamps, the shadows lean in toward them, curious, starved, everything that can stop stops, and the thicket thinks its thought, and then puts it away for years. It is the only appointment kept here. No one keeps it. It is always kept.

And there is the coil. Half-buried in the silt, thick as an arm, pale beyond even the room's pallor. The map makes no room for it: the scribe's lines bend around the place where it lies, leaving a blank in the drawing, a reach of shore uncharted. Or not uncharted — reserved. It lies near the center, where time is thickest, and that matters more than it seems to. From inside such a density of duration, stillness may be a rumor the rest of the room tells about speed. The coil may be moving quickly. It may be thrashing. It may be turning in its sleep like anything young, and the room, slower than its slowest citizen, cannot see it. The silt above it is finer than elsewhere — sieved, perhaps, by some long breathing, or by the room's own caution. What it is, the room declines to clarify. If it is a root, then the thicket has a heart, and the whole pale forest is one tree growing down into the dark as into soil. If it is a seed, then the vesicle is not the egg; the coil is, and everything else is albumen. If it is the first fish — rehearsing being a fish the way everything here rehearses being what it will be — then when the wall gives way it will uncoil into the young tarn, and the new lake will open its eyes already inhabited. Or it is the lake itself, curled small, the way a cat sleeps in the box it will outgrow. The gardener keeps its circuit. The scribe keeps its blank. What lives in the room has learned the room's manners: do not ask, do not hurry, tend.

If the bubble is counted — and it should be; it has outlasted everything that ever tried to leave — it is the only resident with a destination. One held breath of air, kept in a cup of stone near the ceiling, and it does not rise. Rising, like falling, is negotiable here, and the bubble has not finished negotiating. It is the room's sample of the world above: the rain speaks of air the way the airless speak of anywhere, and the bubble is the proof, kept like a relic, that the rain is not lying. During the blue hour it does nothing. It has seen the blue hour many times and is unimpressed. It is waiting for a different light. It is the room's one word, and the word is *up*.

The rain is not a resident, but it is the only caller, and it is always already here — there is always a drop in transit, shouldering in at the fissure, beginning the long fall through the crowded dark, threading the pearl-chains, tasting of sky. The drop now nearing the floor entered decades ago; the storm that sent it is long over; the moor it fell on has changed its weather a thousand times since. By the time rain arrives it is history, and the room prefers history. The scribe tastes it as it passes — iron, cold, a green bitterness, and under that the rumor of open light — and adjusts a bay by a hair. Everything the room knows of the world above it knows from this one witness, and the witness knows only falling, so the room's idea of the sky is of something one moves through downward, at length, in the dark. Which is not entirely wrong.

What lives here, then, is a lake's habits, forming in advance. And when the far age comes — the hill slumping, the fissure widening, the wall giving way to weather — each of them will find that the rehearsal was the thing itself. The scribe's grooves will be the shoreline the water settles against. The cairn will be the island's first stones; the hovering ones will fall or stay, and either way be right. The notches will become true soundings, one by one, as the water rises to meet them. The thicket, meeting actual light, may take up pigment like a vow and green within a season, having practiced in the dark for all the years the gardener has tallied; and the blue hour will end, because noon will come every day, and the thicket's one thought will be answered so often it stops being a thought and becomes a life. The undecided will decide — the spill is the one deadline the room has ever had — and whatever they choose, the tarn will accept, having room at last for both kingdoms and everything between. The coil will surface, or wake, or stand revealed as a root all along, and the room will finally know what it was keeping. The bubble will lift, and cross the new surface, and be for one instant the only thing that has lived in both rooms — and then be ordinary, and be free. And the scribe and the gardener will go on, in the open, under the sky, older than the lake they live in, practiced, patient, already home. The tarn will not need to stock itself. It will have been lived in longer than it has existed.
