Ask how many things live on the Shelf and you are asking how many notes are in a held chord. It depends on when you listen, and for how long. There can be no census by sight — the Hunger sees to that — so the Shelf takes its own census the way it does everything: by ear, voice by voice, patiently, over years. What follows is the closest thing to a count you will get.

**The citizens.** The groves are the great presences, and each one is a single citizen the way a city is a single building: many columns, one root-soul, districts within. Inside a grove there are neighborhoods — the drip-naves where water comes down leaf to leaf in hundreds of small notes a minute, the root-cellars where the marshes begin, the ember-galleries along the bark-furrows where the blue glow smolders at exactly the strength the Hunger permits. A grove breathes on its own slow period, and what it exhales, the Hunger will not take. Whether that is mercy or memory is a real question with no one left to answer it: the veil was raised to spare an orchard, and the orchard is gone, and the groves are what an orchard becomes when you leave it alone for longer than stone stays hard — and the Hunger, which knows nothing of any of this, still spares them. Mercy inherited by a machine is indistinguishable from maintenance.

The groves walk a map they did not draw. Their roots follow the circuit-traces with an accuracy no survey could improve on, which means every step a grove takes — one per decade, unknitting behind, reaching ahead — is a step decided before the sun died. The trees are still running the original irrigation program, as trees. And the groves feed their gardeners: the keepers tap the warm sap in small tithes the trees plainly allow, and the sugar-sweetness on the air is the smell of the oldest employment contract on the Shelf, still in force, still honored by both parties, neither of whom remembers signing.

Once a century, per grove, the bells. And here is what the keepers' filings suggest, if anyone were to read them: a bloom is answered. Decades later, across the plateau, another grove comes into flower — no observer lives long enough to call it a conversation, but the pattern in the records is the pattern of a slow hand going around a room, touching each shoulder in turn. The groves are ringing to each other across centuries. What they are saying takes the length of the Shelf's remaining history to finish.

**The small and the quick.** Relative terms. The moths are the true ephemera — a few weeks of wing, a whole world bounded by ember-light, and no conception of the near-void at all. To a moth the grove does not walk; the grove is a fixed and eternal geography, and the cold nothing beyond the glow is simply the edge. Each grove carries its own moth fauna as it travels — species found in one cathedral and no other, parishes riding a pilgrim church. The moths find the flowers by tick, each species tuned to its own tempo, and during a bloom season they move through the ringing in what can only be called ecstasy or bewilderment; the filings do not distinguish. On their wings, still, after everything: perfect eyespots. Pigment patterns for a daylight predator that has been extinct since before the stone went soft — invisible now, unseen even by their wearers, useless, immaculate, kept. The Hunger cannot take them. The Hunger takes light, and a pattern is not light.

The bell-newts hold the marsh-lanes and subdivide the valve's minute with their porcelain clicks — the percussion section, the smallest voices in the chord and the most numerous. During the rain-years, when the seasons-long rain comes down and the whole Shelf is drummed at once, the newt choruses rise until the marshes are almost one continuous sound; between rains, when the sponge-rock pays out its savings spring by spring, they contract to a ticking under everything, the sound of the Shelf's plumbing, patient as the valve itself. And under the bare stone of the near-void, grazing the faint warmth of the circuit-traces, there are lichens — slow, gray, old as the groves — which are why the keepers polish. The traces want clearing the way a throat wants clearing. The lichens are not the enemy. They are only also alive.

**The archivists.** The stream-eels are the Shelf's literature, and its longest-lived individuals are not animals at all but pieces of music. An eel learns its stream's theme as a juvenile, improvises against the current all its life, dies, and is replaced — the conversation does not pause, because the conversation is not the eel; the conversation is the part. There are brooks where you can feel a gap in the counterpoint, an unsung line, a chair kept at a table: a part orphaned, waiting, sometimes for years, until a wandering eel finds the shape of it and takes it up. Some themes are older than the streams that carry them — the water changed course in the soft stone and the music moved with it, the way a story survives the burning of a book. Wade into a warm brook and within the hour the nearest eel has written you in: a brief, angular figure for your splashing. Within a season after you leave it will have been varied, softened, and absorbed, which is the eel way of filing. They are kinder historians than the keepers. They keep nothing on purpose and lose nothing without honor.

**The correspondents.** The cantors graze the near-void the way weather grazes a landscape — hill-warm, hearth-slow, moving from voice to voice of their own kind by intervals measured in decades. There are few of them. Few enough that each is known by its note, the bent interval it leans toward and never quite lands on, because the note always bends toward the hum of the Nine and the landing is withheld: that near-miss is the Shelf's whole theory of harmony. A cantor's note is its name, its address, and its autobiography, and the correspondence — delayed, considered, complete — is a society that has never in its history held a meeting. The Hunger guarantees no two will ever see each other; the plateau guarantees the calls take effort; the slowness guarantees that every answer is a composition, not a reflex.

They court by convergence. Two hills closing through absolute dark over thirty years, the calls coming closer, the bend softening — and then, to every other listener, a silence where two voices used to be. The correspondence holds its breath. In time one voice returns, lower, and sometimes, years on, a new small note enters the conversation, and every cantor on the Shelf answers it out of turn. And when a cantor dies, the Shelf does not know for years. The others call into the gap the way you call into a house you are not yet willing to believe is empty. The obituaries here are written in withheld answers, and they take decades to read.

Some of them still grow eyes. Deep-set, warm-lidded, useless — a pair of dark chapels where a sense used to live. They are kept the way the bowl is kept: because something once decided they should exist, and nothing on the Shelf has the heart to discontinue anything.

**The travelers.** The great winged things are the crack in the jar — the only residents for whom the Shelf is not everything. They live out over the void, in or above the cloud-sea, on errands no one has survived to document, and they come in over the rim on wingbeats the size of weather fronts to roost on the Nine, where the condensation beads on them and the hum comes up through their feet. They keep a calendar no one taught them: they know, to the year, when a bloom's fruit will ripen — the bells become pods, heavy and warm and sweet, ripening for years after the ringing stops — and they arrive the correct season, out of nothing, on time. Their calls are the ones answered from farther off than anything should be able to be, on a place this small. Whether those answers come from other high grounds, other veiled and kept gardens standing in the cloud-sea, or from the cloud itself, or from nothing at all — the Shelf does not know, and the winged things, who could perhaps say, have never once been asked in a language they honor. They always come back. That is how the law survives them: nothing leaves the Shelf, because whatever leaves, returns.

**The eldest.** The oldest residents of the Shelf were never alive. They are instructions. *Hold warmth. Spare the orchard. Hum. Fill the bowl. Polish. Lift the water; close the valve.* The builders are gone — drunk down with their sun, or gone before it, or gone after; the traces do not say — and the instructions stayed, and outlasted being understood, and became, there is no other word for it, residents. Life grew up inside them the way moss grows in a mill-wheel, and by now the living and the instructed are so intergrown that the Shelf no longer experiences the difference. Erosion has had its way with that too.

**The keepers.** Which brings you to the gardeners, and you should understand them as the heart of the place, because everything else on the Shelf is kept, and the keepers are the keeping.

They come in sizes, the way tools do. Small ones, hand-sized, for the fine etching; great slow ones with backs like doorways, seen mostly at the rim near the pylon bases, doing whatever they do there without witnesses. They are tree-polite to a degree that reads as reverence — a keeper will spend a day re-routing around a root-tip rather than disturb it, and you can tell a young keeper, if you have decades to watch, by how it apologizes. They do not speak in air. They talk through the bedrock, drummed code that you feel in your palms if you lie flat — a prickling under the hum. The floor of the Shelf is full of conversation you are not built to hear; the hum is only the part that leaks.

Their work is an argument conducted at the speed of geology. The roots shift the traces; the keepers re-etch them. The lichens graze the warmth; the keepers polish. A grove takes a step off the surveyed line and the keepers do not force it back — they adjust the map, over centuries, and the grove adjusts to the adjustment, over centuries more, and the result is a negotiation between tree and gardener so slow that both parties may genuinely believe they are in charge. Neither is wrong.

Now and then two keepers build another. It takes years, and the new one wakes knowing the routes — they are built in — but not the manners; those it learns by following. When a keeper breaks past repair, the others take it apart with the same care they give the traces, and the parts go into other keepers, and there is a filing, and the work continues. Every keeper working contains keepers that are not. The population holds roughly steady. They are unaging, but they are not untouched: after enough eons, even devotion drifts. One keeper at the northern rim has been polishing the same hand-span of trace for as long as the rings easily record, and that span is the brightest thing on the Shelf that is not alive, and whatever it is keeping, it is keeping very well. One has abandoned its route entirely to sit inside the blooming grove for the whole ringing season, and the others bring it sap — which is as close as the keepers come to a theological dispute. One arranges the black humming beads in patterns along a heat-seam and will not explain, because there is no one to explain to, and the explaining was never the point.

The ledger exists. Ask for it and a keeper will taste your hand with one warm antenna and go back to work. Perhaps the floor is the ledger and you have been walking on the records. Perhaps each keeper carries everything it has ever filed and there is no master copy and no audit and no keeper has ever once asked another to recite. The record exists the way the hum exists: because it is running. No keeper remembers the builders. The work remembers for them.

**The visitors.** Every few decades, something climbs the Stair. What climbs is never the same thing twice, and how anything lives below the cloud to begin the climb is not a question the Shelf can answer — the Shelf is the Shelf's only known address. They come up toward the hum, or the warmth, or the bells, weeks of it, a step and a rest and a step, and they always stop. A keeper meets each one where it stops. Water is carried down the steps — the bowl in the listener's dome, always full, no one fills it, is perhaps only the top of a custom that runs the whole height of the Stair. Hospitality, still running. The builders expected listeners, and the Shelf has never stopped expecting them on the builders' behalf.

Some stopped climbers live a long while where they stop. The keepers file them, and visit, and the Shelf, which cannot come down to them, sends what it has: warmth through the stone, the bells when there are bells, the hum always. Nothing has finished the climb in recorded time. But *finished* assumes the climb was a way up, and it may be that the Stair is a way of listening, and that what stops, within earshot of the Shelf, has arrived. The keepers, who could resolve this, do not experience the question.

**The silent member.** And the Hunger itself — count it, because everything else here does. It is the Shelf's eldest citizen and its silent co-author. It has manners, in its way: a fixed rate, a reach of a meter or two, a total indifference to warmth and sound. It wants light the way rain wants down. But by setting that price it made every other sense free, and life, which is thrifty, spent accordingly — and so the whole loud dark, the ticks and bent notes and porcelain clicks and brook-quartets, exists inside the Hunger's terms, tuned to the drone it seeps from. The Hunger does not know it made the music. It is not the music's enemy. It is the reason the music is audible: nothing here ever had to compete with light. The dark gets everything — the law holds, the Hunger is how the law keeps office hours — but it gets it at the speed of erosion, no faster, and until then the terms are generous, and everything on the Shelf has learned to live richly on them.

**You.** The census closes with the fastest pulse in it. The keepers will find you: one warm antenna, a filing, no audit. The moths will ignore you — you do not tick. A cantor may spend some years deciding whether your footfalls constitute a call, and will decide they do not, and will be right. The eels will write you in and kindly write you out. The bowl, when you find the dome, will be full, and it will taste of iron and flowers, which is to say of the air, condensed, which is to say of here.

On the Shelf you are what you sound like, then what you warm like, then what you taste of. What you look like is a matter between you and no one. And the census is a chord, and the chord is being held, and for a little while — a match's length, a moth's few weeks, a cantor's centuries; the Shelf has never once graded these differently — your note is in it.

The hum is not a message. The hum is the sound of the message still being sent. And everything that lives here — the walking cathedrals, the sparks, the archivists in the brooks, the hills composing in the dark, the travelers who always return, the gardeners who forgot there was ever anything else, the stopped ones on the Stair, the appetite itself — is what the message is made of. The garden is tended past the end of every reason. The tending is the reason. The residents are the tending.

Nothing leaves. Everything is kept. The dark waits its turn.
