The oldest continuous resident is a bird, though not the same bird. A heron lives twenty years if everything favors it, and the Weep has run untended longer than that — so the grey figure at the weir's edge is best understood as an office rather than an individual. Somewhere in the decades since the logbook closed, a young heron followed an old one down into the bowl, shadowed it for a season or two, learned which cells to stand beside and when, and one spring the old one simply did not come back from wherever old herons go. The post has been filled ever since. The works has never once been unattended. Only the species of the attendance has changed.

The current heron is grey as the fog it fishes in, motionless in a way that is not patience — patience implies waiting against some inner hurry, and there is no hurry in this bird at all. It reads the works the way the tide table on the door pretends to: float-balls, stain-lines, the small behaviors of water at weir edges. It feels a siphon priming through the stone under its feet a half-second before the glug reaches the air, and lifts its head then, already knowing. It stands where the mud will be, not where the mud is, and when the cell has drained it walks out onto the fresh plates and works them with one foot, stirring, taking what rises — boatmen, a newt it accepts without enthusiasm, whatever the water has handed it. One standpipe near the gauge hut is whitened at the base from years of its standing. On the rare occasion something startles it — a shelf letting go at dusk, the fox — it gives a single raw syllable, the ugliest sound in the Weep, and the fog takes even that. Otherwise it is silent, and otherwise it is right, and being right is the whole of its job description.

The dipper at the outflow is the only other voice, and the only resident with a route out. It bobs on the valve wheels, blinks its white eyelid, flies low and fast down the outflow channel and vanishes under the rim's weather toward whatever the water eventually joins. It is the one proof, delivered daily and never believed, that the world beyond the rim is more than rumor. For its nest it has chosen a bracket under a catwalk above a weir that no longer sheets — it can tell the dead weirs from the sleeping ones, which is more than the tide table can do — and in winter, when everything else has gone quietest, it sings. The Weep permits one song. The fog keeps most of it.

Below the birds, citizenship gets narrower. The pale newts hold the clear cell and only the clear cell; the drowned pumps are their wreck and their reef, and they drift over the rivets with the unhurried entitlement of owners. When their cell fills or sheets they adjust their depth by a body-length and consider the matter handled — at newt scale, the cycle is weather with excellent manners. The water boatmen row the still cells under beads of carried air, bright as droplets of mercury, the only residents who bring their own atmosphere; when their cell sheets over its weir they simply go over with it, rowing the fall, and take up residence in the next cell like a family moving house with the house. Nothing smaller than the heron holds more than one cell. The Weep, for most of its population, is not a place but an archipelago — dozens of small countries with different laws, and the heron its only cosmopolitan.

The midges keep the fastest clock. Over the warm cell, where the steam makes its private weather, they stitch a column of rising air that is June all year, hatching and dancing and dying on a period of days while the works breathes on hours and the heron measures in years and the embankments in centuries. At dusk they find the lamp over the gauge-hut door, and the spiders have found the midges. The spiders live along the pipe racks at head height, and the fog does them the courtesy of making every web visible — each one permanently dewed, hung with beads that travel and let go, the whole apparatus on display in broad grey daylight. The lamp is fed by the works; the midges come to the lamp; the spider takes the midges. Everything here is fed by the works eventually. The only variable is the number of steps.

The fox is the exception, and the tracks say as much: from nowhere to nowhere, days old, crossing on the weir crests as though the bowl were a bridge rather than a place. It does not drink — it reads the sheen the way the heron reads the float-balls — and it does not stay, and it is the only thing in the census the Weep does not feed. The heron watches it pass without interest. The fox is not on the schedule, which makes it the only surprise the heron allows.

All of this, though, is the daylight census — the census of things with backs. The population underneath it is larger by almost any measure you choose. The algae film that takes anything that holds still for a day is the Weep's naturalization ceremony: hold still long enough and you are counted. The iron cell's floc, the color of dried blood, is not deposit alone — there are bacteria here whose entire metabolism is rust, who breathe iron the way other things breathe air, and the floc is their bodies and their buildings both. Half the transient architecture of the place is grown, not precipitated: the rust-ice curtains under the weirs are part tissue; the geode-mouth of the burst pipe is lined, in its dark, with a felt that has never seen light; the warm cell runs films of pink and sienna, different citizens for a warmer country. Inside the pipes is water that has been dark since the hills, and it is not empty either — the whole body of the works is lined with life the way a throat is lined. The drained cell's cracking plates are a tenement between shifts; everything in the mud is waiting out the dry hours, and when the water returns it simply resumes. And the shelves that let go at dusk with their small applause are not losses. They are a harvest falling to whoever lives underneath.

By mass, of course, the Weep is mostly works — and here the question of what lives becomes a question of what living is. The works drinks everything the hills send down, a stream of mineral it can neither refuse nor finish, and it breathes: every siphon is a lungful drawn and swallowed, and the fog itself is its exhalation. It deliberates by filling and concludes by tripping — it thinks in levels, considers slowly, acts all at once. Its heart runs behind brick and you feel it in the sternum rather than hear it. It has memory, written in wear: polished valve seats, greased ways, stain-lines on the gauge posts, a diary it keeps in its own substance. And it has drifted. The hand-inked table on the door almost matches its behavior, and the almost is the autobiography — decades of slow wandering off the printed schedule, the way a heartbeat settles with age, the way a song changes in the keeping. The schedule the works keeps now is the one it has settled into. Not the one it was given. A thing with habits of its own has a claim on the word alive, and the works has had decades to accrete them.

Which explains the thing every visitor notices without being able to name it: the specific quiet of a large interior where something is being paid attention to. The attention is real. It is hydraulic. Every float-ball is a fingertip resting on the water's pulse, and the basin is one connected body, and when you step anywhere on it the works feels your weight and answers — a puddle swelling at a duckboard's edge, a bubble rising ten feet away. It cannot see you. It weighs you, continuously, from below, and replies from somewhere you are not standing. That is the presence in the hush. You are not being watched. You are being carried, and the thing carrying you knows exactly where you are.

As for the keeper — the chair, the enamel mug, the pencil on its string, the last entry decades dry — it is worth being precise about what was lost. The works did not lose a keeper. It lost a scribe. The table on the door was never the works' own account of itself; it was one man's transcription, and when he stopped coming the works went on keeping the real schedule, the one in the pipes, the one that has since wandered into its own handwriting. The heron keeps it too, the older way, by heart and hunger, and writes nothing down, and is never wrong.

So the census at dusk, when the fog has taken the light down by degrees and the small warm lamps come forward doubled in still water: one heron, walking unhurried across the weirs toward the cell that will empty next. One dipper, silent now, above a weir it knows is dead. Newts over their wreck. Boatmen under their beads of air. Midges at the lamp, a spider at the lamp's edge, films and felts and flocs beyond counting, one rumour with feet somewhere on the rim — and, for as long as you stand there, you, continuously weighed. Stand still long enough and the Weep will naturalize you too, a film first and then a resident. No one ever has. The attendance has always been handled.

The heron arrives before the water leaves. It has never once been late — but then, neither has the water. They keep each other's appointments, beak and siphon, the oldest arrangement in the bowl, older than the logbook, renewed across generations of grey birds and it asks nothing of anyone. Only attendance. And attendance is the one thing the Weep has never lacked.
