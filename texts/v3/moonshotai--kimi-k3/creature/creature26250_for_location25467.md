You find the tenders the way you find everything on the shelf: by their leavings first. A stack of white pebbles on a sluice post. A cord with three knots where yesterday there were two. A chisel-tap somewhere off to the south, carrying across the bowl with a clarity that makes one working person sound like a crew. And then, perhaps days later, the person themselves — a figure at the far edge of a terrace, a raised hand against the steam, already turning back to the work. They do not cross the basins to you. Distance is the local courtesy, practiced so thoroughly it has become indistinguishable from temperament.

The valley below calls them the Eleven, and has called them that through generations when the count was nine and when it was twenty. They do not call themselves tenders, or much of anything; the word they use, when a word is needed, translates roughly as *the ones who stayed*.

Their bodies are the hill's argument made flesh. They go bare-footed from early spring to deep winter, because the drum-map is read through the soles — where the crust is solid, where it rings, where a foot would go through into a dry basin or, worse, a hot run. Apprenticeship is mostly walking: years of stepping exactly where an elder steps. The old ones carry the proof in small white scars, the record of crusts misread and water misjudged. Their hands live in warm mineral water and have gone soft and pale as the young stone they cut. And because the air condenses on anything cooler than the hill, which is everything, the tenders glisten. Their cuffs bead. Their hair drips. A tender who stands still long enough begins, very faintly, to be upholstered — the moss is always trying their boot-tops — and their oldest joke is a proverb: *sit still and the hill will start you.*

They hear like instruments. The roar that a visitor experiences as one sound resolves, for them, into its threads, and they sleep inside it and are woken by its changes — a run thinning in the dark pulls a tender out of sleep the way a missed heartbeat would. Their voices have gone the other way: low, close, pitched under the water. They speak at arm's length. Across the bowl, they tap — a chisel-stroke grammar on the ringing crusts, slow as semaphore. Across days, they stack stones and knot cords. Everything beyond those ranges is left unsaid, and there is a great deal of it, and it does not seem to trouble them.

The work is the same work every day, which is the point of it. At first light, rounds: the night's leavings read, the leather gates flexed and re-set — for they cannot abide a stopped thing. The water clocks in the cisterns are never read and never allowed to run dry; a stopped clock, a seized gate, a stagnant basin, all of these resemble the only kind of death the place admits, which is stillness. Then the small edits: a frame of willow withies propped into a spill's path, a lip of curd-soft stone cut and stacked to cure, the algal colors read the way you'd read a face — rust for the hot runs, jade for the warm, bone where even the algae finds it too much. When a new run is opened, someone carries a translucent snail or two to it in a folded wet leaf, the smallest act of settlement in a town made of settlement.

Materially, they live on an inversion. Everywhere else in the world the permanent is dear and the perishable cheap; on the shelf, stone is the one free thing and a plank is a treasure. Everything organic arrives on human backs up the gorge — grain, salt, wool, leather, copper for the knives, willow for the frames, oil for the cistern lamps, wood for the rare small fires that mean company or ceremony. Twice a year the porters come up, and the meeting-bowl with its worn-smooth floor becomes a market, and what goes down with them is the shelf's one export: permanence. Forms sunk a season in the guest basins come out grown into stone bowls. And more than that — the valley sends up the things it cannot bear to lose: a dead child's shoe, a marriage cord, a knife that killed something worth remembering. The tenders set them in the favored runs, and a year later return them petrified, cured, beyond the reach of the world that makes everything fall apart. Eleven people eat well on the valley's fear of time.

Fire is precious, so the hill cooks. Lidded baskets of grain and roots are set into the hot runs at dusk and lifted at dawn, steamed through. They bathe constantly, without thinking of it as bathing. They sleep on cured moss cut from the dormant quarters — the living moss is working and is left to work — in warm rooms near the crack, and the smell of them is wet stone, lanolin, and nothing else, because the shelf has no soil and almost no smoke, and they have long since stopped being able to smell themselves.

Children are rare, and each is noted on the painted hide. A shelf-born child learns the drum-map before anything else, and for years believes — because everything they can see confirms it — that the world's nature is to accrete, to knit, to cure. Then, at an age the hill sets no clock for, the child is taken down the gorge to see what the rest of the world does. Down there, banks slump. Dead wood softens into itself. Stones fall and stay fallen. The child watches, for the first time, a thing decay, and this is the whole of the rite: nothing is said, and then everyone walks back up. Some children return relieved and never think of it again. Some return with a strange grief, as if they'd been shown a wound. Some, a year or five later, go down and do not come back — and the message left on the sluice post is the oldest arrangement in the pebble grammar, *gone below, do not follow.* This is why the number is eleven and not some other number. It is the residue of everyone who looked at erosion and chose, instead, the hill.

The current runs the other way too. Every few trains, a porter does not go down again. The stayer's first year is hard in ways the shelf-born cannot imagine: their feet are loud, they flinch at the roar, and what they miss most — it is always this — is rot. The smell of soil, of leaf-fall, of anything ending. They learn the quiet, or they follow the pebbles' oldest message down.

Names, on the shelf, belong to the town and are lent to the living. A tender is called after a run or a room or a gate — Southrun, Knee-High, Second Cistern — and when they die the name returns to the pool and waits for another bearer. The census reads like a map, which is intentional: the people are meant to be findable in the town, and the town in the people.

When a tender dies, the others decide which wall they will become. The body is laid in a willow frame in a dormant run, and the water is led back over it — led, never dammed; the law holds even here, perhaps especially here. A year, and there is a white shape. A decade, and there is a smooth swelling in a wall with a name attached, and the frame's skeleton of branches inside, and the tender's skeleton inside that. The oldest walls of the town are double-skeletoned this way, wood and stayers, and the tenders on their rounds greet the swellings by name, without ceremony, the way you'd nod to someone at their work. Some tenders ask instead to be carried down to kin in the valley, and are. Either way it is chosen. The town's rooms for six hundred stand empty, but its walls are full — the first inhabitants are already in residence, and the eleven speak of them as company.

The rest of the census is short and much consulted. The pair of dippers owns the watershed and is treated as the only other workforce on the shelf: they read the water from inside it, walking straight into the loud spill to feed along the basin floors, and a tender will idle rather than work a rim a dipper is using — there is so little company that all of it is protected. When a dipper abandons a basin, the tenders check the run's chemistry; the birds know first. A day when both are seen at once gets noted. The translucent snail is a living gauge, its grazing line marking the warmth of each run more precisely than any hand, and when the snail-line retreats the tenders know before the algae turns. The midges keep the dusk; the tenders have ceded it to them, and schedule accordingly, without rancor — the census is too short for grudges. And twice a year the lammergeier crosses the amphitheater without comment, the only reader who ever sees the town whole, the bowl from above reading like the painted hide itself. The tenders record the crossing and the silence both, as if the plan extended upward and had passed inspection.

Of the green residents, the small white-flowered creeper is the one they feel as kin — it lives here and nowhere else in the world, which is also their situation. Every patch of it is known. Its flowering is the calendar's fine print, and after a tremor redraws the runs, the creeper migrates slower than the water, and the tenders wait for it the way you wait for a cautious neighbor to finish moving in.

Toward the inhabitants they have not met, the tenders' feeling is the least simple thing about them. The six hundred, they call them, the way you'd say *the neighbors*. Houses are begun for them; lintels still soft take the thumbprint of the child the house is for — or, if that child is not yet born, the print of the tender setting the frame, which is understood to be a placeholder. A held door. Each tender, moreover, keeps one room somewhere that is theirs alone, and in it leaves one made thing — a carved spoon, a knotted cord in a private grammar, outgrown boots — so that whoever finally comes will open some doorway and find that someone was here, and thought of them. They know their messages will not survive translation: the old cords hanging in the dormant quarter, knotted by hands three generations gone, are already illegible, grammar having drifted the way runs drift, and they are kept anyway, because every message eventually becomes an ornament and an ornament is not nothing. There are doubters among the eleven, and always have been — no one has promised to come — but the work is not contingent on belief. The plan does not ask to be believed. It asks to be led.

The only labor they do in company is the tuning. When the great cistern fills to its certain depths and the hill begins to hum — a tone felt in the teeth before the ears — whoever hears it first taps the all-ears across the crusts, and the eleven come out of their separate solitudes to the leather gates, and open and close them by feel until the note seats itself where it wants to sit, the hill and its editors briefly in agreement. Then they disperse again, back to the long distances.

At night the unread clocks tick in the dark cisterns, the gates flex, a dipper shifts on its roost behind the bead-curtain of the spillway, and somewhere a tender woken by a thinned run walks the drum-map barefoot, relearning a lane by moonlight, while under everything the hill lays down another skin of itself, a finger's width a year, toward whoever is coming. And across the bowl, now and then, a chisel-tap sounds and is answered — the loudest small thing in the valley — and what it says, mostly, to whoever is listening, in this century or the third one hence, is: *still here. Still here. Still here.*
