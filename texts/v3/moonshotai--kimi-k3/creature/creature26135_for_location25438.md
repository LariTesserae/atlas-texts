**The Census of the Cold Attic**

The honest census of the crown is one entry long, and the entry weighs a few grams. You could hold the entire biosphere of the Seventh Flute in one hand and close your fingers. But the room makes liars of censuses, because up here the question is never quite *what lives* — it is *what is kept* — and the room keeps everything in the manner of a body keeping a cell. So take the census slowly. It has been a century since anyone took it at all.

The lichen arrived the way everything small arrives everywhere: by accident. A spore in the grease of a gasket, a cell in the seam of a construction boot — the likeliest story is that it was sealed in while the crown was still open sky, one mote among the invisible plankton every volume of plain-air carries: pollen, bacteria, the dry world's drifting seed. The sealing of the door was an extinction event. The dryness and the tone killed that whole unseen ecosystem in an afternoon, an apocalypse with no witnesses in a room no human had yet entered. One propagule, by some accident of chemistry — a talent for doing without water, a cell sap that would not freeze — did not die. Which means the lichen and the note are twins: born the same hour, the hour the door closed. It has never known silence. No organism on the plain can say that. Silence is not its element and never was; the sound is.

It is shaped like the sound, and that is not a metaphor. It grew along the node-lines where the acoustic shear lays down its ration, and it stopped where the antinodes scour the walls, and so its body is the wave's skeleton traced in gray-white tissue — the Kundt figure, grown. The expedition sampled it in a dozen places and found one genome everywhere: a single individual distributed across the whole bottle, the largest living thing in the crown and the lightest. It is the room's second recorder. The pen draws the temperature in ink; the lichen draws the note in itself. And it performs one quiet service for the rare visitor, though no one has ever thanked it: a wise hand reads the walls to find the silence-corridors, the hand's-breadth lanes where the roar drops to mere loudness. The lichen has spent a century underlining them.

A lichen is not one creature but a marriage — a fungus and an alga, two metabolisms sharing a body — and the room renegotiated the vows. The alga's ancient work was to catch light and make sugar; here, under the constant worklight, it makes mostly antifreeze, the cryoprotectant bath in which the whole partnership persists. The fungus's ancient work was to mine stone; now it skims the dried gas for molecules — vapor lubricant rising from the bearings below, the slow organic breath of the seals — all of it delivered by the sound itself. Say it plainly: the note feeds it. The note is not its environment. The note is its weather, its soil, and the hand that feeds it, and asking what the note *sounds like* to the lichen is like asking what gravity feels like to a plumb line. It is the condition by which the thing points at all.

For the lichen there has been exactly one day since the sealing, and that day is a century long and shows no sign of afternoon. No night interrupts the fixtures. No season stains the light. Clocks unspool in such places — a rhythm with nothing to set it dissolves, like a watch left in a drawer — and so the lichen's time has no ticks in it, only duration. Its climate history is a list of visits. A body at the gauge panel radiates a little warmth into the wall; over hours the filaments nearest the chair may have known a faint thawing sigh, a small summer, thirty years gone. The expedition that took its scrapings is the one wound in its memory, and even that it met the way it meets everything: chemically, slowly, without surprise. The scar did not heal so much as resume.

It no longer grows, because growth would carry it off the ration-lines into the scour. It no longer reproduces, because every surface its children could reach, it already is. Elsewhere, life is the art of becoming. Here, it became. It is not adapted to the room; it is *finished* with the room, fitted to it the way the wave is fitted to the bottle, and like the wave it persists not by effort but by exactness. Nothing eats it. Nothing infects it — there is nothing else. No freeze-thaw worries its cells; no ultraviolet unpins its chemistry. Every agent of death was left outside the door. It will live exactly as long as the note, and when the note stops — when the food stops arriving, when the cold fails and water returns to the world — the plain's ordinary ways of dying will come back for it all at once. It has one appointment, and the appointment is the silence.

That is the one certain resident. But the census-taker should be honest about the other claimant, the one with the strongest case: the note itself. Run the checklist. It metabolizes — heat goes in, cold comes out, the books balance to a hundredth of a degree, a homeostasis no mammal achieves. It holds its boundary. It responds: it gathers the dust into pages, delivers the ration, harvests the breath. It remembers — the retired units of cold that no living person can still read, the commissioning day it has been writing out for a century in one straight sentence. It fails only one clause, reproduction, and even that is arguable, because from the porthole you can count its kin: two more shimmers standing off two more flutes on the horizon, and the plain names this one the *Seventh*, which implies a litter. Whether the other attics keep their own few grams — whether each flute grew its own single organism, convergently, the way islands grow their own lizards, or whether some stand perfectly empty — no one crosses between to check. The lichen may have cousins. It will never meet them. Neither will you. And consider that a standing wave is two waves passing through each other forever: the stillest thing in the crown is made of unceasing motion. Nothing happens here ten thousand times a second, perfectly. The room's eternity is not the absence of event but its flawless repetition — fidelity, kept up as a form of weather.

Then there is the drift in the lee of the fin, the closest thing the room has to a congregation. Every visitor who ever stood here is in it, one breath each, crystal by crystal — a census in snow. It never grows. Crystals leave at the rate breaths arrive; the drift is not a pile but a standing population, a fixed form with matter flowing through it. Like the wave. Like the lichen. Like you, seven years of cells replaced and still calling yourself one name. The room draws no line between its engine and its organism, and after an hour in the silence-lines, neither do you. *Alive* is the plains word for a pattern that keeps itself. The room knows other words.

There was a human profession here once — the chair argues it. Someone was paid to sit at the best node-line in the loudest room in the world and confirm that nothing was happening: a watch-stander at the still point, the Listener of the Seventh Flute. The straight line retired them. The pen sits the watch now, and the chair stays bolted down the way the room keeps everything — exactly. The bootprints at the threshold, the wrench on the flange: the room has no fossil record, because fossils require burial. It has a population of former presences, unburied, current. The last maintenance crew left decades ago and has not entirely gone.

And then there is the resident that rotates. Stand past the hour in a node-line, let your ears surrender the tone, and into the vacancy rises the wet percussion of your own body — and you understand the room has taken you on, briefly, as its loudest inhabitant, the temporary fauna of the Seventh Flute. You leave; everyone leaves. But the tithe is exact. A breath of you stays, crystallized in the corner, and the note rides down in your teeth and long bones and does not fade for days. So write the census honestly: one lichen, one note, one drift that is everyone, one chair's worth of listeners — and a fraction of you, filed. The room does not have visitors. It has accessions.

Nothing further will live here; there is no vacancy. The dryness refuses, the tone forbids, and the single niche is filled by the organism that is the niche's shape. On the plain, life is succession — one thing shouldering another aside; here the succession ran its whole course in one afternoon a century ago and ended in a few grams of gray filigree shaped like a sound. It does not know it is alone. Alone requires an absence, and nothing in the cold attic is absent — everything in the cold attic is *kept*. The pen draws. The dust stands in its pages. And along the node-lines, in the still planes where nothing moves, the inhabitant does the one thing left to a finished life: it agrees with the sound, cell by cell, and the sound — which has never once varied, which has nothing else to say — agrees back.
