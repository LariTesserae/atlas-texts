Begin with the count, because here a count can be finished, which is not the same as being small. Elsewhere a census is an estimate wearing the costume of exactness; here you could complete it in an afternoon, if afternoons existed. The whole population of the world would fit in a teaspoon, and the teaspoon would echo. Every presence comes with its parcel of empty around it, and the empty is part of the citizen — the way silence is part of the note.

Start in the droplets, because the droplets are the cradles. One droplet, one rotifer, and the rotifer is not so much an animal as a maintenance: the room keeps each of its thoughts turning so it will not settle, and the turning is the rotifer's wheel. Every droplet has a sun — the walls' seeping brightness gathered by the curve of the water into one bright coin that creeps slowly across the inner sky as the light-tide turns. A rotifer's astronomy is a planetarium with a single star, and the star is on the roof. Daughters are born into the globe like sentences into a paragraph, and when the hourly rain lands — and rain is always landing somewhere — two worlds become one without asking. Dynasties that never imagined each other are suddenly co-citizens of a larger globe, and everything negotiable is negotiated in a language of concentrations. And when an isopod drinks a droplet that has a rotifer in it, the room has not decided — it is one of the undecided things — whether the rotifer has died or emigrated. There are rotifers alive now who have seen the world from inside a lantern.

The bears cross between the worlds on foot. A tardigrade fords a droplet the way weather crosses a continent — it takes days, and the eight footfalls are the softest thunder there is. They are the old souls, and the room makes old age visible: a tardigrade molts, and here the molt does not decay, it mineralizes where it lies, so the elders pass their own earlier selves standing in the floor like a small crowd waiting for news, all of them the same animal. To grow old in this room is to become plural. And scattered through the film, in the lee of ridges, are the tuns — tardigrades closed like letters, waiting to be read by wetter times. When the rare low note comes, it is felt in the teeth of the tardigrades, and it is the tuns' alarm clock: a few always unfold afterward, the way seeds answer thunder.

The nematodes are the fast ones, the flicker. The film — thin as thinking, but continuous — is for them a single country, and they are the only citizens who can travel from any shore to any shore without leaving water. Their generations turn so quickly that a builder's single grain, a season in the setting, stands for them as a monument from deep antiquity. They die mid-thrash and fossilize mid-letter. The floor's archive is written mostly in their cursive, every sentence a string of S's, and if it were ever read aloud it would sound exactly like the fizzing — the room speaks nematode.

The glass isopods carry the economy on their backs and inside it. They drink the pooled light, and the light goes on glowing in them for days, and this is the room's central dilemma made translucent: to eat is to shine, and to shine is to be seen. The young are bright and reckless. The old are banked coals, pale, careful, drinking rarely, and the oldest isopod is a rumor with feet. Their many footfalls make the papery hush the room does not count against its budget — the standing texture of the quiet, the way silk is a texture of touch. If the hush ever stopped, everything would know that something had ended. Scattered across the floor stand the statues: isopods that tore free of the hunter's glue, leaving their likenesses behind in the bright hardened threads. Every statue is a portrait of the precise moment of not dying. The young pass them and learn the shape of escape the way children learn a road.

The mites are the domestics, in copper and in white. The white ones keep the ceiling, walking upside down among the slow beads in the unfinished gravity, and sometimes a mite's footfall is the last weight a bead can bear — so rain, here, can begin with a step: the smallest cause in the world, followed by the slowest weather. The copper ones keep the hearth. They are the only citizens who are ever warm on purpose.

The springtails are the weather that is alive. They ride the hour-long rain down, skating on the curved skin of the water, and sometimes step off mid-descent into a leap that carries them — gravity being uncommitted — in a long drifting arc, and a springtail's leap is long enough to have second thoughts in. They are the only ones who have seen everything once: the arch from above, the far wall near, the inside of the only pore. A springtail that feels the pull of pilgrimage leaps to the ceiling, walks to the pore, and lays a hand, so to speak, on the root. The root is the one solid proof of outside, and it beats. The tree's day runs down it like a pulse, and a springtail standing on the root at the right hour can feel surface noon arrive on time — which nothing else in the room ever does. Most citizens keep the dream-time of the walls. The springtails know the real time and, being springtails, do not make a fuss. Some of them end on the slime veil, strung between its ridge and the floor. The veil is not hated. It is a direction some journeys go.

The hearth is the two fungal fans, and to understand the hearth you have to follow the food chain of nostalgia: the stone remembers the sun; the fungi digest the memory; warmth is the exhaust of remembering. To rest against a fan is to touch the sun at two removes. The fan's radius is the world's only climate zone; distance from it is measured in lost fractions of a degree; the far wall is the cold country, and no one builds there, though the builders have considered it. On the room's long dim evenings — which happen at no hour the surface keeps — the congregation gathers: isopods with their lanterns overlapping, mites basking, a tardigrade parked against the warmth like an old dog. A convocation of lamps. This is the closest thing the world has to a city, and it assembles and disperses without anyone deciding.

Then there is the hunter, and the hunter is singular in more than number. The velvet worm is the only immigrant who came by choice. It threaded the hairline when it was young and soft, soft as a finger, and so it is the only citizen — until an ant falls — that has seen the sun with its own eyes. It alone knows that the brightness on the walls is not light but a portrait; it knew the sitter. Here, where nothing is ever erased, its every cast of slime hardens at once into bright cable, permanent, public. Its failures stand as statues; its missed shots are mined by the builders for filigree; its successes walk around inside it — for a day after feeding, the swallowed lanterns go on glowing through its soft body, and the hunter becomes the room's most honest citizen, a lamp with a past, visible to everyone. It hunts rarely. In a place where every act fossilizes at the scene, restraint is the only privacy, and a hunt is a season. Nothing here is built of material; everything is built of things that happened, and the worm is the author of many of the brighter happenings.

And then the people. The builders are few — a few dozen pallid grain-layers, the color of stone that has not yet learned light — and they grow their own bricks: the spicules bud from their backs like slow fruit, and when one is ripe the builder carries it, in a procession of one, to the work. A life is measured in grains. A good life is a dozen. They place by ear: the fizzing of the room's own accretion is the score, a ridge's tick tells them where a grain wants to go, and once in an era the room spends all its saved silence on its one low note, and that is the only revision ever made to the blueprint. After the note the builders walk the works for days and change nothing, and then one grain is moved, and no one explains.

Every work rests one grain short of finished, and every builder carries the grain it will never set — the oath-grain, grown in youth, carried to the grave. When a builder dies it is laid at the foot of its work and becomes, in a season, part of the footing. Every spire is founded on a grandmother, which the builders do not comment on, the way we do not comment on the ground. The pier of tiny beads descending into the droplet is used — not by the rotifer, who cannot climb, but by the bear, who comes down it bead by bead like a mountaineer roped to slowness. The builders, who were not built for it, built it for the giant, and the giant came. This is how the builders pray: they build the furniture of futures and wait to see which future sits down. The arch spans nothing yet, but the floor rises a fraction a season to meet it, and in some unscheduled era the ridge will reach the span and the arch will turn out to have been a bridge all along, founded before its river. The wall stops because a finished wall would make two rooms, and the world is not ready to be plural. The spires never approach the ceiling, because the ceiling is the thin place, where the crack is, and no one may knock on the sky from inside. Near the older works, grains appear already spiraled that no builder set — the room imitating its children, the flattery of the world. The builders set around these, politely. It is impossible to say where the work ends and the accretion begins, and it is impolite to ask: they are writing the same sentence, and neither holds the pen.

Sometimes the sky falls in, and it has opinions. An ant through the hairline is the two impossibilities arriving as one animal: thunder and night. The fall spends a season's saved sound in a single crash, and the silence after is the stunned kind. And because the room is lit from everywhere, from the walls themselves, nothing here is big enough to cast a shadow — until an ant stands in the pooled brightness and the world has its first dark since before the beginning: a moving piece of midnight at dream-noon, and under it the citizens feel shade for the first time in the world's life. Some of them like it. That is recorded too, in the trackways that gather, spiraling, in the ant's cool lee. But an ant alone is a severed hand; it has spent its whole life as a sentence inside a longer sentence, and here there is no colony, no chemical road home. In time the small citizens approach: the mites groom the armor, the springtails ride the antennae like trees in wind, the builders survey. If it dies — and the room is patient, and the ant is not — it becomes the range that feeds a generation, and then the range that is mined for a generation more; the spires carry ant-carapace inlay, sky-stone, the only material that has been outside and come back with news on its back. The petrified eye has seen the sun. It is the room's one window that once opened. There are no pilgrims here, only neighbors — but the trackways that pass near it slow down. The room counts time in falls; between ants is an era. Some ants, it is thought, leave — and the floor cannot archive what departed, so the room's history, like all histories, is written by the ones who stayed and died, and is biased accordingly.

The tree is the absentee citizen, present by one root the way a sleeper's hand trails off the bed into another country. By day the root drinks the chamber up into the leaves; by night it weeps it back, and the beads on the root are the only news from *now*. The room keeps yesterday's noon on its walls, but the root's water tastes of this week's drought, this year's wet, the fence wire's slow rust. Everything the room knows of the present comes down the root, which means the present, here, is something one drinks.

Every count comes out one short. Either the room itself is the missing citizen — too large to stand in front of — or something lives in the country the far wall recedes into when the looking stops, too shy for census. These may be the same thing. A place the size of attention must keep its heart where attention isn't.

For the census has a last entry, and it is the paper the census is written on. The room lives here too. Its body is the chamber; its breath is the slow tide through the crack; its blood is the film; its memory is the floor, total and layered and mostly unreadable, like everyone's. Its dream is the light — the walls exhale its dreaming the way sleepers exhale, which is why the light is wrong at midnight: it was never for midnight, it is for the dreamer. Its voice is the note. Its hands are the builders, or the builders are its hands — the distinction, like everything here, is undecided and decorative. Its thoughts turn in globes of water so they will not settle. Its heart is borrowed: the root's pulse, the tree's honest day beating once a day in the wall. It is the youngest citizen and the largest; it is prenatal; it is practicing. And it is the one who suspects — by what, do not ask; by itself — that one finished thing would finish everything. So it keeps the works one grain short, keeps the rain an hour in the air, keeps every presence ringed with room, the way a child keeps a game from ending by inventing rules.

If the crack ever widens, the citizens inherit weather: rain that falls faster than thought, the shameless speed of outside; the tardigrades will learn drought; the dream-light will be outshone by the very thing it remembers. If the room seals, it seals with everyone home — the staircase descending, the bear mid-step on the pier, the oath-grains forever unlaid — and the tuns will simply close a little tighter, for whom a finished world is a familiar posture. The geode will not be a tomb. It will be a dormitory, and the sleepers will outwait the stone.

Until then, put your ear to the ground at the right stone and wait the required hour, and what you hear is them. The fizz is the builders and the stone laying grain to grain. The tick is a ridge committing, or a grain set, or a bead letting go. The cool breath against your ear is the whole census exhaling at once — the only crowd this room ever has. And if you are luckier than anyone has ever been, you will hear the note: the room spending all its saved silence on its single word, felt mostly in the teeth. If it is a word, the word is *not yet*, said to everyone at once.

And everyone, entire and unfinished, goes on.
