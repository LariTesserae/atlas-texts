The census is short, and everything on it is old.

Count the kinds of living things on the plateau and you will not need your second hand. There is the felt, which is most of the life by mass and all of it by patience. There are the spars, which are the felt again, doing something else with itself. There is the felt-shrew. There is the stoneback. There is the mantle. That is nearly the whole of it — a web with four knots, strung across distances that would starve a richer world. But a short census is not a simple one. Each entry unfolds the longer you look at it. Nothing here is what it was, and everything here remembers, in its own way, what it used to be.

Begin with the felt, because everything else begins there too.

It is not one growth but a guild — dozens of kinds knitted into a single body, a partnership struck so long ago that the partners have forgotten they were ever separate. There are armorers that build the pewter scales of the upper face. There are the cilia-fields that grip and relay the dust, passing each grain down through the mat like something smuggled across a border that takes weeks to cross. There are the deep black-green layers that brew the golden solvent, the oil that is closer to resin than to rain, and the wax-smiths that skin the whole island over and guard that oil the way other worlds guard wells. There are miners that work the rock beneath, exuding their slow chemistry into its cracks, prying the island's footing loose grain by grain and taking the keepings. Every one of these was once a free creature. Now they are organs. The island is the organism; the guild is its body, and its body is a negotiation so old it has become anatomy.

It is not still, though you must watch for days to catch it at anything. The silvering at noon is a gesture — over the course of an hour the whole mat turns its armor face-up, plate by plate, and the dark island stands up into the light and disappears into the glare, a vanishing performed daily, in plain sight, at high noon. At dawn it performs the reverse, opening its dark faces to the thin first light and drinking the brief cold. From the high domes you can see it happen: the islands wink, one after another, as the light comes around.

An island has a front and a back. The windward side grows a comb — dense, silver, bristling, taking the storm's gift on the chin and gripping it. The leeward side trails a train of spent mineral, pale tailings the mat has sucked dry and let go, and behind the train the young felt creeps out into the lee, colonizing its own refuse, a grain's breadth a day. Islands migrate. They lean downwind the way other worlds' trees lean toward light, and the oldest of them have wakes behind them a kilometer long, a pale handwriting across the pan that says: *I have been coming this way for longer than you would believe.*

And the felt is a book. Dig down through the underlayers and the strata keep the autograph of every storm that ever fed the island: an iron-red band for a decade of eastern dust, a pale one for years fed off the pans' own salt, a bright seam where something large died on the mat and was taken in. The oldest islands are the longest continuous records on the world, and they are still being written, an inch a century, in a script of appetite.

Cut the felt and it weeps gold, then skins the wound in wax within the hour. It does not sleep. It does not hurry. It knows a walker is coming days before the walker arrives — the footfalls come up through the stone long before the sound crosses the air — and by the time a stoneback's dome breaks the horizon, the island has thickened the rich rind on that side, relaxed the armor, laid the table. While the beast grazes, and afterward while it sleeps at the rim, the cilia work the seams of its plates full of spores, patient as pickpockets, gentle as gardeners. The stoneback leaves heavier than it came, carrying an orchard in its armor, and never knows.

There is exactly one fast thing the felt does. When a mantle's shadow slides across an island, the whole mat throws its armor up at once, hours early, all in one motion — a flinch the size of a hill. It is over in minutes. Then the island goes back to its century.

---

The spars are the felt's other ambition, and they begin with a single decision, made once, in one patch of mat, and lived with ever after: *keep your dead.*

Every other felt sloughs its spent mineral and lets the wind have it. A spar lays its down beneath itself, crystal on crystal, and grows up over the accumulation. What rises is not a trunk but a genealogy — sixty thousand, a hundred thousand years of one family standing on its own ancestry, fluted like a column, honey-colored where the crystal is doped and ivory where it is pure. The living part of a spar is thinner than paint, a skin of guild-tissue spread over the outside of the tower, forever young, forever the current generation. Everything beneath that skin is forebears, stood on end.

Walk into an old grove and you walk into a crowd that has never thinned. The groves do not drop their dead. When a spar's living skin fails at last, the trellis stands — white, wind-hollowed, soundless — and younger felt comes up over it like ivy over a ruin, and the grove goes on around it. A grove is a cemetery that is also a nursery, every ancestor still at its post, the quick and the dead shoulder to shoulder in the same stand.

The wind hollows them, and the wind plays them, and over the millennia the two have come to terms that nobody negotiated. A spar will grow its skin around a wound the wind has opened, lip the bore, thicken here and thin there, and the note that comes out of it settles, century by century, toward something the grove keeps. Whether the spars tune the wind or the wind tunes the spars is a question the plateau has had a hundred million years to answer and has declined. What is certain is the chord itself: when the night-wind comes down off the domes and finds a grove in a pass, the whole stand tones at once — one low unhurried chord, organ-deep, the only music this world makes, played to no one, kept like an heirloom by a family of towers.

And there is one hour when the grove shows what it is made of. At dusk, in the brief amber of the world's shadow rising, the low light comes level through the passes and the eldest spars go translucent — honey and ivory lit from inside, each tower briefly a lamp made of its own lineage, the generations glowing one through the other down into the root. It lasts minutes. Then the cold comes up and the lamps go out, and the grove begins, instead, to sound.

---

Of the animals, the smallest declined the world's terms.

Everything else alive here has agreed to deep time. The felt-shrew refused. It burns. Fist-sized, warm in a world that has made no other provision for warmth, quick in a world that has made a virtue of slowness, the shrew lives at the tempo of a struck match and pays for it in a short life — a few years, no more, born and dead inside a single season of its island's digestion. The felt may take a full year to swallow one storm's gift. In that year the shrews of the underlayers run through generations.

Its whole world is one island, and the island is enough: an acre of continent with weather of its own. The noon dimming, when the mat silvers overhead and the galleries go from wine-dark to pewter. The days-long tremor that means a stoneback is coming, which the shrews feel as a change in the island's attention, the way you feel a house change when someone stands up in another room. The rare deep rocking when a great storm stands in the basin and the whole island leans into its work. The shrew knows the felt from inside, the warm pockets near the oil-vessels, the cold rim, the fruiting stalks pushing up through the mat like slow springs. It smells of its island, wax and copper, all its life.

Dawn is the gamble. In the deep cold the lightest fraction of the felt's oil condenses on the mat as glittering rime — sap-frost, the only dew this world knows — and it is sweet, and it is the shrew's daily bread, and dawn is when the shadows slide. A mantle's shadow crossing an island sends every shrew on the surface under in one instant, a wave of gone. The ones that come back up are the ancestors. Every shrew alive descends from an unbroken line of shrews that looked up.

The felt could dissolve them all in an afternoon; the solvent is right there. It does not. What the island gets in return no one has weighed — the grooming of its dead tissue, the spreading of its spores through the underlayers, warmth in its dark, the quickening of its blood. Or perhaps only this, if the felt keeps such accounts: to be lived in. An island without shrews has no pulse. An island with them dreams, maybe, at a faster tempo than its own.

---

The stonebacks are the great presence, and they are slow for the best of reasons: nothing in the history of the world has ever caught one.

A stoneback eats armor and grows armor. It rasps the silver face of the felt — the rind the island lays up for it, mineral-rich — and builds the take into its own plates, slate upon slate, so that an old one carries half a mountain range on its back and a record of everywhere it has fed. Read the seams of an ancient shell and you read the itinerary of centuries: this band from the groves of the western passes, this one from islands long since drowned in a dune-sea, this one from a grove that stood three basins east and stands no longer. The beast is its own archive, walking.

It steers by the stars — not only the night's. The day-stars, small and steady at zenith in the deep indigo, are enough for a creature in no hurry, and at noon, when the pans flare white and every landmark burns flat, a stoneback walks on, head up under its rim, reading a sky no one else can use. And it tastes its way: every island sheds wax-notes downwind into the dust, a signature as distinct as a voice, and a stoneback crossing an open pan is reading a page with its tongue — which island, how far, how it fared in the last storm, whether the fruiting was good. Three years between groves, straight as a ruled line, sleeping out the great storms beneath its own shell, waking when the calm comes and walking on into the glitter.

Sometimes, in the deep night, a stoneback stops and presses its voice into the ground. The thin air is no use for it; the stone is. A note too low for hearing travels out through the plateau's bones, basin after basin, and weeks later, from a pan two hundred kilometers off, comes an answer pressed back into the same dark. The conversations of stonebacks are measured in seasons. A courtship can outlast a dynasty. Two of them will converge for a year to meet at a fruiting grove — the only gatherings this world has, three or four domes at the rim of a silvering island, still as boulders, while the grove fruits and the air fills with mirror-motes and the mantles slide overhead through the glitter, and the whole arrangement, sky and island and beast, conducts its one slow transaction.

They live for centuries, and when one dies, it dies as it lived — in the open, mid-journey. Nothing comes to the body. The world has no scavengers; nothing rots here; so the carcass waits under the weather of dust, and the spores that have ridden its seams for years wake one by one to what they are resting on, and the dome silvers, and within a hundred years the beast is a groveling, a new island at its birth, shaped unmistakably like a sleeping beast. Ten thousand years on, only the diggers would know. The oldest islands, cored, still hold plate at the heart. A fair number of the dark islands scattered across the pans are stonebacks that stopped, and are still, in the way that matters, going — their journeys continued now at a grain's breadth a day, downwind, forever.

---

The mantles changed least. That is the strange thing. Everything else crawled out of the dying sea and made new plans; the mantles simply kept drifting while the medium thinned around them, and by the time the water was gone they had already become something the air could almost hold. An acre across, thinner than a soap-bubble's skin, faintly iridescent, massing almost nothing — a kite of nearly nothing, a decision the sky has never finished making.

What a mantle is made of, mostly, is attention. Its nerve-net is spread across the whole acre, and a signal takes long seconds to cross it, so that the creature's present moment is wide and dim the way weather is wide and dim. It does not experience events. It experiences conditions: the mineral taste of the upper streams, the long warmth rising off the dark felts by day, the electric itch of a storm building three basins away. In storm-light they glow like struck bells, trailing charge, acres of cold fire adrift. Mostly they trawl — filtering spores and dust from the thin high rivers of air, gathering over fruiting groves in the one crowded hour of the world's sky, when the release fills the passes with silver and the mantles cruise through it, iridescence inside glitter, eating the future and being, in their way, part of it.

But they are not above hunting, and the fold is the only fast thing a mantle does. From the heights a shrew is a coal of warmth against the cool mat — the only small warm thing in the world, and therefore unmistakable — and the mantle drops out of the sky like a closing hand, rim folding down and in, and the shrew either gets under the mat or does not. It is a gamble both ways, because the island beneath is also a trap. The felt's cilia will grip a settling mantle and not let go, and a mantle that misjudges — that touches too long, too low, too slow — is kept. On most old islands, somewhere out on the mat, the felt is patiently swallowing a rainbow. It takes years. The strata will keep a bright band for it, the way diaries mark the great years, because a fallen or a taken mantle feeds an island for a decade: an acre of sky-meat, a windfall beyond storms. The islands lean toward a fall. You can see it in the grain of the mat, the cilia-fields all turned one way, like a field of faces.

And when a mantle dies of age — when the lift, whatever its ancient arrangement with the air, fails at last — the fall takes days. The acre deflates by degrees, settling through the thin streams it used to trawl, coming down across the pans like a slow snow of tissue, and where it lands decides everything: on open pan, the dust files it, layer upon layer, and in time it is reissued; on an island, a festival measured in years.

---

Because that is the last of the inhabitants, and the easiest to overlook: the waiting.

The pans look empty and are a library. Every dust-fall sows what the last storm reaped — spores by the uncountable, laid down an inch a night, sleeping in the dark between the grains, viable for years, for centuries, for as long as the archive stays dry, and the archive has been dry for a hundred million years. By number, most of the living things on the plateau are dormant: the living are outnumbered by the waiting, the quick by the filed. A storm that cuts into an old layer can wake seed a thousand years down, and a grove can stand where no grove has stood in ages, called up out of the library like a book reissued. Nothing here is ever really gone. It is shelved.

---

So: who lives here?

The shrews live here the way a heartbeat lives in a chest — quick, warm, enclosed, convinced the island is the world, and right enough. The stonebacks are citizens of the whole plateau, the only ones; they belong to the distances between things, to the ruled line and the three-year walk, and the islands are their farms and their letters and, in the end, their children. The mantles belong to the sky the way the islands belong to the stone, wide and dim and patient, reading the cold board of the world for one spark of warmth. The groves keep their chord and their standing dead and their long account of storms, and ask nothing.

But if the question wants a single answer, here it is: the islands live here. The rest is their traffic.

And notice what they are, these islands, these beasts, these drifters — because every one of them is the same survivor in a different disguise. The felt is the reef, rebuilt to eat the sky's leavings. The spar is the reef that learned to keep its skeletons and stand on them. The stoneback is the old seafloor, walking, the sea's armor still on its back, still grazing, still crossed by the memory of currents it now reads as wind. The mantle is the medusa that never came in — it changed the least because it needed to; it is still drifting, still pulsing, still trailing itself through whatever medium will have it. Even the shrew, the one small wager the ocean laid on warmth and haste, carries the shallows in it somewhere, the dart and hide, the silver flash.

The water left. The life declined to. It gave up swimming, gave up drinking, learned the taste of dust and the patience of stone, and became five kinds of thing, four of them slow, strung across the brightest emptiness there is — and it has been here, on these terms, longer than the terms have needed names.

Dusk on a pass, then, seen from inside the lives. The grove clears its throat as the night-wind finds it, and the eldest towers stand lit for a minute from within, honey and ivory, ancestry glowing. On some island out on the pan, the felt is attending to a tremor it has been attending to for eleven days; the rind on the eastern side is thickening; the table is being laid. Under the mat, the shrews have gone still — not from fear but from the change in the island, the way you go still when someone you live with stops what they are doing and listens. Far above, a mantle turns its wide dim attention over and over in the cooling air, reading the dark for one small coal of warmth. And out on the pan, mid-line, mid-century, the walker walks — unwitnessed, unhurried, exactly on time, carrying an orchard in its armor from one dark island to the next, under day-stars, under night-stars, through a world that lost its ocean and kept, in five patient forms and one quick one, everything the ocean was.
