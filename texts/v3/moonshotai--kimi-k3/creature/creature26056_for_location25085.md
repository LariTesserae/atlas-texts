Nothing on the Rootplateau is alive at your speed. That is the first thing to understand about its inhabitants. They live at the pace of stone or the pace of storm, and there is nothing in between — no bird-speed, no grass-speed, nothing that flickers. Everything you can actually watch moving is either weather or about to be eaten. The census begins in the seams.

The thread-lichen is the first listener. In the quiet weeks between greys it is almost not there — drawn down into the rock's banding, a black seam inside the seam, tight as a shut eye. It does not drink water; there is none to drink. It lives on charge, on the slow electrical weather leaking up out of the substrate, and when a front is still days beyond the horizon the lichen knows, and rises, and a pan-edge of seams lifting all together is the nearest thing the plateau has to a crowd. Old travelers read it the way sailors read birds. Touch it then and it prickles the palm, faintly, in something like the Markers' note but thinner, as if the seam were humming to itself. It may not be a separate life at all. It may be the plateau's own hair, lifting. What is certain is that no Revision has ever stranded it: either the machine redraws around the seams the lichen occupies, or the lichen finds the new grain within the year and follows the banding of mountains that have been finished twice. Either way it is the oldest resident you will ever stand beside, and it is always listening, and what it hears is the world's own weather coming.

The ashwort keeps the warm places. It grows nowhere but the swept circles at the Markers' feet, in slow grey aureoles, a fingernail's width a decade, and a cushion the size of a cloak is older than the civilizations whose cairns are still being copied. Its rings record every small variation in the hum across the centuries — a scripture nobody reads, written in felt. It keeps the Marker's warmth the way an animal keeps a den. There is no law on the plateau, but there is one custom everyone keeps: you do not cut ashwort. Not because the machine would mind — the machine minds nothing quickly — but because an ashwort cushion is a library of winters, and you are a mayfly proposing to burn a library for one warm night.

The bloom waits as seed that is indistinguishable from grit. You have walked across whole basins of unborn bloom and never known it; the dust of the frost gardens is one part stone, one part patience. On frost dawns it opens — no time for stem or leaf, all flower, grey-green with that thin gold — and drinks the frost, and sets seed before the sun takes the water back, and is gone. By dusk even the darkening of the ground has faded, and the basin's entire vegetable history has to be believed rather than seen. Everything alive keeps this calendar, including you. Travelers learn quickly not to spill meltwater on the pans: what germinates around your cup will be dead by noon, and that is the one cruelty the plateau permits you to commit casually.

Whether the sails are alive is an open question with travelers on both sides of it. They are seen only as pauses in the storm walls — membranes kilometers wide, lifted by charge, straining the murk. And the murk is the plateau's own ground self, the charged dust that polishes the root-rock, so the sails sift the world's abrasion; they are filterers, leviathans of bad weather. No one has ever seen one steer. They only hold. And when they come down after great storms, acres of grey silk across the rock, dissolving as you watch — then the question changes. Because sometimes the fallen silk shows structure: channels, branching angles, the lightning's deliberate handwriting, as if the storm had written on its own skin. Then the question is no longer whether the sails are alive but whether the grey is. Meanwhile the silk-fall is the only abundance this world knows, and if you are within ten days' walk of one, you go. It cannot be dried or saved. It feeds you and refuses to be kept — the plateau's single lesson in generosity, and its terms.

And then there are the longwalkers, and everything else was preamble.

You see one a full day before you are near it: a standing stone that, over hours, decides to be elsewhere. Up close, each footfall arrives like the end of a long consideration — feet wide as shields, set down as if the ground had asked a question and the foot were the answer. The print it leaves will outlast you by decades. You can cross a pan in an afternoon and read sixty years of passages beneath your boots, and no two prints lie on the same line. They do not retrace. Every crossing is a new sentence.

They are the only large warmth. Stand in the lee of one at dawn, out of the thin fast wind, and you feel its heat the way you feel a Marker's hum — the two warm things on the plateau, and one of them is slowly walking away. The famous encounter, the one that divides a life into before and after, is only this: the head turns by degrees, an eye the color of the haze-sea finds you, and you are registered as furniture. What breaks in travelers is not the indifference. It is the relief. The longwalker is not deciding what you are. It decided for all of you long ago, the way you decide about a kind of stone, and to be done with mattering, out there, is a strange and permanent mercy.

They hear what you only feel. The spread foot is an ear pressed to the world's skull; they know a Revision days before your teeth do. Stand near an old one at dusk, when the cooling crust lets out its one slow tick: you feel it through your soles, and a breath later the grey felt fringe around its ear lifts — it heard the continent answer itself, in a register you will never own. Calves are born hearing it, almost white, like frost, like an unwritten pan. Age darkens them, as if a lifetime of the hum tans the hide, and the very old are grey-black with bones denser than calcium — the plateau accreting into the skeleton, mineral by mineral, as though the machine were tithing them. The oldest must ring faintly when the deep slab-fractures arrive through the bone.

A few hundred of them on a whole continent. When two meet at a silk-fall they stand a hundred meters apart for a day, and nothing visible passes, and then each walks on, having said whatever takes a day to say. Your entire species talks too fast to hear a sentence that long. It is not for you. You are not equipped.

No one has found where they die. There are theories, and there is the one travelers do not voice twice: that at the end a longwalker stops avoiding the Revision — the one appointment it ever keeps — and the machine, which must edit everything it keeps, receives at last a thing it does not need to change. There is no evidence. There is only the absence of bones, and certain standing stones at the hearts of certain pans that are shaped, in the first light, if you circle them slowly and do not speak, like a shoulder. Like a bowed head. Travelers do not mark those stones. Some things you leave in the machine's keeping. And note this: of everything left on the plateau, only the longwalkers' writing is never corrected. Their prints persist exactly as made, unimproved, on the pans' skin — the one grammar the machine judges already correct. Of course. They are the only ones writing in its native tongue: a sentence a day, a footprint kept for sixty years, a life that ends by becoming stone.

At the lander, and elsewhere, lives the strangest of the tenants: the reader. Hull-sized, slow, pale as the root-rock and glossed scarless — no, scarred and glossed — by the same dust that polishes everything. Where it touches the wreck, the two materials have grown a seam. It moves its attention one panel a decade. Travelers once thought it was feeding; a thousand years is too long for a meal. The thought now is that when the lander fell — a thing outside the grammar — the plateau grew a scholar, because the machine keeps things, and before it can keep a thing it must know what the thing should have been. The reader is how the plateau asks a question. When the reading is done, the lander will be improved, and no one will know what changed, because no one alive remembers what it was. There are said to be others. Something reader-shaped attended the moorings at the Lip for a while, walking the fused-glass rings one per season. And out in a spire-field there is a single cairn — stacked in haste, stacked wrong, by someone afraid — that has had a reader at it for three centuries, which suggests the machine finds that particular text unusually difficult. It keeps at it. It has nothing but time, and it is spending some of it on a frightened person's untidy grief. That, more than the Markers, more than the hum, is what makes travelers careful of the place: it is thorough, and its thoroughness is not distinguishable from tenderness.

Because all of these are tenants. The thing that lives on the Rootplateau the way you live in a house is the plateau. It has appetites — grinding down, raising up. It has memory — the kept things. It has manners — the Markers are never unmade. It has taste: it knows what a cairn should have been, and a being with ideals has an inner life. The grey is its dreaming; the lightning's deliberate geometries are its thoughts, inscribing and erasing; the sails may be no more separate from it than a breath is from a sleeper. Everything alive here lives inside its manners, keeps its calendar, answers its hum. The dead most of all. The keeper of the last house wrote a whole journal, and the machine kept two pages — kept, or edited; you will never know — and the first page is one line, the last line the keeper wrote in their own grammar, and the second page is a list of laws that reads, if you have been here long enough, like a description of the machine's own. The machine does not close the house's door. Nothing here enters uninvited. The dead in their cairns pluralize — one becomes three becomes a row — every grave becoming a sentence, and the sentence edited, and the fear always corrected first. Some travelers, knowing this, break the last law on purpose: leave a flawed cairn, return in a decade, read the correction. They call it corresponding. The reply is always kinder than expected, and always wrong in one detail — the improved cairn is the cairn of someone unafraid. The machine files fear under error. It has never once been afraid.

And then there is what lives here most briefly. You do. You arrive loud, and the escarpment takes your noise one thing at a time, and the plateau takes your maps — maps fail — and then your need to leave traces, and then, if you stay, the rest. You learn the frost calendar. You keep your palm on the Marker glass and tell yourself it is for the warmth. You walk toward a false city once, as everyone does, and what you find is ground finished an hour ago, tidier than tidy, a city the moment after everyone left forever, and you do not walk toward one twice. And one dawn, in pan dust, beside your own bootprint, there is a longwalker print a meter across, and you understand the census was taken long ago and you are in it. Being kept feels, at first, like being watched. Then like nothing, which is what it is. Then — if you let it — the way the lichen must feel, lifting: included in the weather.

That is what lives on the Rootplateau. One note, held by many bodies, most of them stone. The lichen hums it in the seams. The Markers hold it a month's walk apart in every direction. The longwalkers carry it in their bones and set it down one print at a time in the only script never corrected. The bloom keeps it the way a seed keeps a summer. The dead keep it in improved rows. And you, sitting at the Marker's base with your palm flat and the pan faintly glowing and the false cities finishing something on the horizon — you have it in the sternum now, below hearing, and it was never the Markers humming. It is the plateau, holding a single note through every body it keeps, and the note is the life of the place, and everything that lives here — lichen, ashwort, sail, walker, keeper, you — is how it sounds.
