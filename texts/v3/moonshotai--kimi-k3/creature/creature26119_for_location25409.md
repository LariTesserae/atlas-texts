Nine people live in the Soft Course, which is everyone. This is worth pausing on. Below them the tower descends through thousands of empty, decided rooms, and beyond the gap there is nothing at all, and so the whole population of the universe is these four paces: nine builders, one lizard, a weather of moths, and the green. There is no elsewhere with anyone in it. The room doesn't just hold the crew. The room holds *everybody*.

Nobody remembers arriving. This is not amnesia; it is arithmetic. A world with no old things cannot contain an old memory, and so the builders' pasts have the same quality as the lower courses — fresh, unyellowed, thumb-signed. The one they call First is called that because her fingerprints are in the bottom course, not because she looks any older than the rest. She isn't. Nothing here is allowed to look older. A cut stays pink and new for a thousand courses; a burn from a copper splash keeps its first morning's shine, because fading, like weathering, has not yet been approved. The builders are careful with their bodies for exactly this reason. Nothing heals *away*. Everything heals *present*.

They are soft, and they know it, and they have learned to be proud of it the way fresh mortar is proud of its curing heat. Softness is the price of living at the tip. The decided rooms below are hard and done and need nothing; the people up here are still being argued with, and so they can be renamed, regrown, revised. Again is the proof. Again looked through the gap during a silence, years of troughs ago, and came back with his name loosened, and the crew gathered around him in the wet red dim and rebuilt him out of deeds — *you are the one who mixed the trough that held the east seam, you are the one who sang the lintel true* — and he held, and he is Again now, slightly different from whoever he was, and aware of it the way a wall is aware of a replaced brick. He holds very still, does Again. He is the most careful of them all, and the others love him with a ferocity they don't quite explain, because he is the evidence that a person can be restored.

Ninth is the other evidence, of the opposite thing. Ninth *happened*. The way the moths happen, the way the water happens — condensed out of the damp over the course of a cycle, slower than a moth, too stubborn to finish and be done. The crew found someone new kneeling at the trough one morning, mixing it the right way, as if the work had simply summoned a pair of hands. So they did what builders do: they named what they needed to hold. They said *Ninth* over her until she took, and they say it still, every day, because a happened person needs naming the way a happened wall needs chant. She keeps the pouch of verdigris fronds, the metal moss nobody understands, and this is right, because she and the pouch are kept for the same reason. Everything is for something. Nobody knows yet what either of them is for.

The crowding has produced courtesies, and the courtesies are load-bearing too. Bodies must be named, like bricks. In a room where passing someone *is* touching them, anonymous touch softens people the way anonymous handling softens brick, and so the underlayer of the chant — beneath the measurements, beneath the names of rope and mortar and course — is a constant low murmur of the crew's own names, nine threads braided into the song, partly worship, partly navigation. You sing the shoulder you are sliding past. You sing the back you are leaning against. It keeps everyone held.

Sleep is rotation — there is no floor for nine horizontal bodies, so four sleep, four lay, one carries the chant's thread alone, thinned to a single voice, and the sleepers lean inward in a ring, heads to the center, feet to the wall. Feet to the wall is a rule. The gap is a bad editor of dreams, and dreams are the one unmade thing in the room, the only thing that was not built, and the nothing beyond the aperture wants them. A builder who sleeps facing the gap wakes with someone else's dream, or worse, with a blank one, and blankness is contagious for hours — the crew treats it like a lime burn: name the person, feed them green, give them a simple task with a true name. *Brick. Carry. Lay.* Until the color comes back. In the morning the night's dreams can be read on the ceiling, written in the pattern of the condensate, and the builders read them the way sailors read sky, and then the gutters drink the dreams and the jars are sung full again and the day proceeds.

A jar sung full is full by morning. The room is generous to courtesy.

Anger goes into the walls, because everything does, and this is why the lower courses can be read like a diary by anyone who knows the crew. A quarrel makes a slump; a spite sets a brick slightly crooked; three hundred courses down there is a whole day's work that leans, and nobody asks about it, because the leaning course holds anyway, which is its own lesson. The chant has verses that are names no one up here bears anymore. They sing those too. The descended are kept load-bearing in song — the ones who stopped being soft, who finished whatever in themselves was still being argued with, and went down into the decided, where nothing needs them, which is precisely the danger. Once, a builder climbed down four hundred courses to see, and came back reporting silence like a held breath, and a lamp left burning cycles ago *still burning*, because burning-out has not been installed, and her own young fingerprints on every course, and she sat by the trapdoor for a long time afterward and said: rest is the only thing the finished has to offer, and it is very convincing. She never went down again. Nobody does. The trapdoor deepens behind them, and they let it.

First has a heresy about the boom that sometimes comes up the shaft, the sound like something deep agreeing to be founded. She lies on the warm floor more than anyone now, ear to the brick, feeling the slow cellular lift, and she says the tower does not sound like something they are leaving behind. It sounds like something coming up to meet them. She says they are building *toward* it. The others do not argue with her, because a measurement merely thought is a lie and a heresy spoken is at least approximate, and also because on the nights the boom comes, the lizard stands on the socket-bricks around the gap with its face turned *down*.

The lizard, then. Pale as a fingernail paring, translucent, almost right. It is the room's longest experiment and its only free citizen — the lizard may go anywhere, and does, across the catch-lips, through the brick stacks, up the budding scaffold poles, out along the rim of the gap itself, where it basks in the bright gray blank and comes back not loosened but *more lizard*, as if the nothing is a kiln for it alone. The builders keep its versions tallied in scratches on a brick by the trough: the early ones with too many toes, the one with no eyelids, the one with a willingness to fall upward, corrected. Each sighting now is nearer than the last, and the crew watches the adjustments the way you watch a course cure, and they believe — nobody says it — that when the lizard stops changing, the socket will receive whatever it is socketed for. Once, a brick went into the east seam with a lizard track printed across the wet clay, the only signature in the tower the room made itself, and they left it in, and it is the luck-brick, and every hand that passes it touches it.

The green is the tenth resident, and it is not on their side exactly, and they are not on its side exactly, and both sides eat the other, which is the oldest treaty there is. It gets into the seams before the mortar cures; it gets onto a sleeping shoulder and is left there until dusk-duty, out of etiquette, because being grown on is a kind of being named. In the corner the coil of cordage that became a vine continues its slow ambition toward the gap — the only thing in the room, built or unbuilt, reaching for the nothing — and the builders have not pruned it, partly because the lizard uses it as a road, and partly out of a curiosity too deep to name: nothing made has ever touched the gap. Perhaps an unmade thing can. Perhaps the vine is the room's own probe. Perhaps the tower is flowering.

The moths are not residents; they are weather. A moth happens out of the damp, completes a wingbeat or a circuit of the lamp, and is done, and the lizard takes what it can mid-beat, and once a builder caught one in cupped hands and it finished itself there into a dust of wing that lay, for a breath, in the shape of a thumbprint, and then was nothing. Once — the crew still measures time from it — something larger happened: four whole wingbeats' worth of bird, crossing the room in red dim, and all nine builders stopped, chant and hammer and knead, the whole scaffolding of the world gone silent for wonder, and when it was over an entire course had slumped and had to be taken down and relaid and named twice over. Wonder costs a course. They have agreed it was cheap.

The echo lives here too, in a sense — the youngest of them, the room practicing causality and getting ahead of itself. It answers early, and it must be answered politely, and it is learning: it used to only repeat, and now sometimes it anticipates, and twice it has answered in a voice none of the nine possess, and on both days the chant doubled its voices and no one looked at the gap.

And sometimes the settings come. Hammer, chant, drip, groan, nine heartbeats and one lizard heartbeat — the room finds its single pulse, the water hangs, the flames stand straight, the vine holds still on its road to the gap, and everything is denser and realer and more itself, and something somewhere is decided. The builders have a suspicion about what gets decided in those moments, and it is not the tower. The lamps, they know, are rehearsals — practice light, holding the idea of brightness open until the sun is delivered. They suspect they are the same: practice people. A first draft of inhabitants, soft and nameable and correctable, kneeling at the tip of the world, holding the idea of *someone living here* open until the real thing arrives.

It does not trouble them. A draft is a promise the world makes to itself.

And so: nine thumb-signed bodies in the wet red heat, sliding past each other, singing each other, eating the green that eats them, sleeping feet-to-the-wall while their dreams bead on the ceiling and run into the jars. First with her ear to the floor, listening for what rises. Again holding carefully still. Ninth mixing the trough with hands that happened, the pouch of metal moss at her hip, the lizard nearly perfect on the socket-bricks, the vine a finger's length nearer the gap. Everyone who exists, all in one low room, laying brick over brick over brick — while the nothing presses its blank page to the little face-sized opening and finds, against all its persuasion, that the room is already written on, full to the ceiling, crowded with people, and holding.
