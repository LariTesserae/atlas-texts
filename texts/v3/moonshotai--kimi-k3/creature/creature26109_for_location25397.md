Nobody lives in the Condensary, and it is full of residents.

Nothing with hands has an address in the dome. The low country sends no one up; it only receives — the rivers, on schedule, and once in a generation a green-pelted body come down over the falls for the lowlanders to puzzle over, name, and misname: moss-back, felt-cow, sponge-ox. Up here the animal answers to none of those. It answers to a stamp.

Because that is the price of admission, the covenant every resident has signed: silence. And there are only so many ways to be silent. Be slow, so the air never hears you move. Be soft, so the felt never hears you land. Or change medium entirely, and speak where sound cannot follow. Everything in the dome chose at least two of the three.

The moss-backs chose all three. They are built like cattle gone to felt — broad, patient, hooves wide as platters and padded soft, splaying at each step so their weight rides the sponge the way a hand rests on water. Their coats are the forest in miniature: dense under-felt that wicks the fog dry, and in the outer hairs a slow green bloom of algae and moss-spore, so that an old bull grazing the saddle lawns is wearing the saddle lawns. Springtails drift through the pelt like plankton through kelp. A drip moth will ride a warm back from runnel to runnel, and the moss-back pays it no mind. They crop the lawns to lawn-height — unwitting groundskeepers, the first of several — and they drink from runnels, never from the tarns, for reasons the calves learn early.

A herd is six or seven. They speak by stamping: a forefoot lifted and struck down into the mat, one note, felt a valley away. Two beats: *all well.* A long roll: *open water ahead.* To listen, they kneel — a knee pressed to the sponge, head up, eyes unfocused, reading the line. Births get a pattern of their own, rare enough to be news; there is no season, so a calf comes when it comes, every few years, and the first thing it learns, before it walks steady, is to stamp. When a blue hole opens in the cloud overhead and one green watershed stands in a column of sun, the herds walk to the edge of the light and stand in it, warm for an hour, and the stamps carry the news basin to basin — the only weather report the dome ever files. When one of them dies, of age, the way trees die, the herd stamps a long slow figure and walks on. The felt takes the body. Within a season there is a greener hummock; within a decade a rise the young ones rub against. The sponge keeps its records in flesh as in everything else.

The sponge itself is inhabited. Its plankton are white springtails the size of thumbnails, drifting through the waterlogged pores in slow herds, grazing the algal film — and everything small in the felt hunts them. The old velvet hunts them: hand-long velvet worms the color of old brick, walking the night felt on soft stumps, roping springtails in glue-thread. They are older than the trees as a lineage, and they are the only residents who did not adapt to the covenant, having been born already keeping it. And the felt-mole swims them — a forearm of waterproof velvet, nearly eyeless, whiskered like a star — moving through the saturated sponge the way a fish moves through a lake. Its passage is the only wake in the Condensary: a slow wave rolling across the moss surface, and you can walk alongside and keep pace with it, and it will not hurry for you.

The ghost trees house half of this. The hollow moss-molds of trunks that rotted a thousand years ago are the dome's tenements: the mole climbs down one to sleep; spiders string their webs across the fluted mouths — and a web here is not a trap first but a condenser, loading with droplets in the fog until it hangs like strung glass, the spider walking its own lines to drink, each web a small private condensary that catches dew nightly and a moth rarely. Water threads down the molds' hollow centers, biology's boreholes into the deep felt. And in the deepest mold of any given stand, as a rule, something else. More about that one later.

The canopy's crowd gathers at the drip-tips. Drip-beetles hold territories of single leaves, trimming the moss around a spout so the condensate gathers faster — small tenants farming the tree's private rain at the rate of a drop an hour. And at dusk the moths come up. By day they hang in the caves, wing over wing in the cold exhaled breath like held notes; at dusk the dome swells, the caves breathe out harder, and the moths go out on the breath, a silent column rising out of a sinkhole, each the size of a spread hand, flying at walking pace. That is their trick: silence by deliberation. A wing that beats four times a second stirs nothing the air can report. They navigate by taste — every basin's fog carries its grove's oil-signature, and a moth reads the fog the way a lowland bird reads stars — and they drink at the drip-tips in the dark, and pollinate the canopy's small waxy flowers, and live for years, which no lowland moth would credit. The fog they fly through is itself stocked: the haze that seeds the cloud rides up with algae and bacteria aboard, grazing the exhaled oils, and comes home in the condensate to feed the films. Even the weather has residents. The loop that carries water carries life, in both directions.

Along the runnels, the flagfrogs. Fist-sized, fog-skinned — they drink through their skins and so never travel for water, and a frog's whole world may be one moss-ledge, lifelong. They lay their eggs in the sponge itself, in the water-film between moss-leaves; the tadpoles graze the film and never know a pond. They are mute, and at dusk they signal: a pale foot lifted, waved, slow as a falling leaf. Watch a runnel at the right hour and the signal relays — one frog's wave answered by the next, and the next — a ripple of semaphores traveling the runnel's full length, a message passed down a party line of feet. What it says is probably simple: *here, here, here.* But it says it to the whole valley.

The tarns are kept. The pool-keepers — black, gilled, a meter long — hold themselves at the temperature of the water, which never changes, with a stillness that makes the water look restless. Their hearts beat in slow counts. They may not feed for a year; then the springs turn, or something young wades too deep at a tarn's edge, and the water closes with hardly a ripple, and the stillness resumes, and nothing anywhere has raised its voice. The moss-backs drink from runnels. The calves learn why. The keepers grow for centuries; no one has found their eggs; the census of a basin's tarns changes only at the great discharge, when a keeper vanishes and a different one holds the pool, as if reassigned — because that is when they travel, up the plumbing, and when the pale ones, the eyeless cave-dwelling form of the same ancient species, ascend partway from the deep works, and for one night the two meet at the mouths of the springs. Nothing else in the dome keeps a calendar. The pool-keepers keep the springs'.

And the discharge is the dome's one festival — held at night, in silence, in the dark. The great springs turn over and the black water comes up freighted: glass copepods, pale flatworms, translucent larvae, the whole filter-crew of the aquifer flushed toward a light it has never seen. Everything that can reach a spring mouth is there. Frogs line the rims. The felt-mole works the rising water. The moss-backs come to drink the mineral breath of the deep stone, and the stamps that night carry a single message, basin to basin: *the springs are up.* For one night the dome eats together. Then the pools settle a hand's breadth higher, the outflow runs a little fuller, and everything returns to stillness for another eleven days. The filter-crew, meanwhile, does its work unseen: every drop that leaves the rim has passed through a million small mouths, been tasted, cleaned, finished. The rivers run clear because they have been, in the most literal sense, grazed.

Over all of it, the covenant's loophole: the hush-owls. Everyone here is bound to silence; the owls alone weaponized it. Feathers with the texture of the felt itself, a two-meter span flown at moth-pace, feet feathered soft enough to land on the sponge without a note — and ears tuned not to the air, which has nothing to tell them, but to the line. A hush-owl reads the stamp-traffic. It knows where the herds are, which calf is lagging, which saddle the frogs are waving from. It is the one eavesdropper on a party line everyone else uses in good faith. You never see one. Your evidence is subtraction: a calf is simply gone, and the herd stamps the owl-figure for hours afterward, and walks a wide circle around that saddle for a season, and the saddle acquires a reputation, which is the closest thing the dome has to folklore. The owls nest in the ghost trees — an owl standing motionless in the moss-cast of a trunk dead a thousand years. The forest's memory, occupied.

At the rim, in the exhale, live the swifts — small sickle birds holding station in the fog that streams over the escarpment, drinking mist, taking strayed moths, sleeping on the wing in the updraft above the falls. They are the only residents who can see the low country, and they never go there. The mist is their medium. They are the dome's edge made animate.

Everything here is old, and everything is slow. No seasons means no rings, no counts; age is read in size and patience. There are no bees, no songbirds, no stridulating anything — the percussion section was let go long ago. Nothing rots in the open, so there are no carrion flies; the fungi handle the dead inside the felt, quietly, and the ghost-molds hold their shapes on fungal cordage, the archive's shelving. Walk here a week and nothing flees you. Fleeing is loud. You are not on the line; to the residents, you are weather that walks. Nothing here is tame, and nothing is wild — those words require people, and there are none.

And after enough days it becomes hard not to read the population as staff: groundskeepers cropping the lawns, condensate crews at the drip-tips, filter beds in the deep stone, inspectors on silent wing, archivists in the felt. It looks *hired.* It is only fitted — every role filled by the same slow patience, no hand assigning, nothing kept but what keeps itself. But the fitting is so exact that the distinction stops mattering. The place is inhabited the way the works are manned.

So take the census the way the residents do — not by eye but through the wrist, palm flat on the sponge at night. The stamps going out along the mat. The mole's slow wake. The relay of pale feet along the runnel. The moths rising on the cave's breath. Beneath it all the deep subsonic turning, the springs filling toward their lips. Droplets falling leaf to leaf, trillion-fold, the soft percussion of the forest watering itself. The line is crowded. The line has always been crowded.

Ask what lives in the Condensary, and the honest census returns a single entry with a great many addresses. The water lives here — slowed into moss, folded into moth and keeper and calf, hushed to a stamp and a wave, holding its temperature, keeping its hours. The rivers that leave the rim are only the residents that commute.
