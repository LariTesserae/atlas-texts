## A Census of the Still

Ask who lives in the Still, and the answer depends entirely on the scale of the asking. Count from the top down and the census is almost embarrassingly easy — there is one name, and it is the name of the place. Count from the bottom up and the names run out before the residents do, down among the sleepers and the brief ones and the things that have not hatched yet but are already, in the only accounting that matters here, on the books.

For the Still does keep a census, in its way — not of bodies but of water. Every living thing in the city is an account entry, a quantity of the endowment temporarily wearing a shape. The feltback crossing the plaza in her afternoon is a deposit. The darter stitching the canopy is a small, fast transaction. Even the patience — especially the patience — is a line in the ledger. Nothing here is off the books. That is the first thing to understand about everyone who lives in the Still: they are all, in the end, the same fortune, differently held.

---

**The largest resident is the Still itself.**

Everything else in this census is either its tenant, its tissue, or its guest, and after all this time the distinctions are mostly ceremonial. Whether it has a mind is a question with no urgency in it — the question will still be there in a century, and so will the city. What can be said from observation is this: it has preferences. It prefers to be fed. It prefers the seams mended on its own schedule. It grows cyst-chambers for its animals before they are asked for, and grows them scented to the family that will rent them — generosity as architecture, welcome as a bodily function.

Its moods are readable, if you are patient enough to read them. Warmth is contentment: the city runs warmer in good decades, and the feltbacks seek the warm walls the way anyone seeks good news. Sap-flow is attention — after a wind, the canals under the plaza flags run slow and thick for a season, as if the city were thinking hard about what it had lost. And it dreams in light. What the horn fibers drink all day, they give back all night, faintly, so that the sleeping city glows with yesterday's sun.
