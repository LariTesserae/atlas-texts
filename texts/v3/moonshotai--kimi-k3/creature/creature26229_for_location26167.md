Stand on the rim again, and count the presences — slowly this time. The count is short. The counting is not; it will take the rest of your stay, and by the end of it the numbers will have become neighbors.

Start with the seven, because everything does. Seven is not a herd. Seven is a choir that walks. Each bell-ox carries its chord in a hollow cranial crest, chambered like a fist of organ pipes, and what comes out of it is not a note but a triad argued into agreement — you hear it in your ribs from across the pan, and the dust hears it too, and redraws. At dawn the old cow lows first. The guides' family calls her the Standard, and only half as a joke: the bowl corrects itself around her the way an orchestra corrects around the oboe. Then the bull with the cracked crest joins in. His chord has a burr in it, a torn fifth, audible across five hundred meters of salt to anyone who knows the herd, and the meadow leans to him anyway. The valley does not correct its singers.

Two cows in their prime, a youngster, two calves. The calves are born with the crest still closed and soft, and for their first days they are only mammals — warm, awkward, mute. Then the cartilage opens, and one morning on the pan a new chord sounds that has never sounded before, and everything orients: the kites bank, the reed-drifts swivel, the borders of dancing dust hesitate along their fronts, and the herd stands in a ring around the calf and lows without ceasing for the better part of a day, a wall of sound with the mother at its center. They do this because they know exactly what a small new voice is, here. They know what listens for stragglers. The old and the young walk center-file, always, and the herd's defense is the braid: seven knotted voices are harder to remove than one.

At dusk they file onto the stair, and the tuned slabs play them down — that slow progression, like a cathedral thinking out loud — to a broad landing where they spend the night lying with their jaws pressed to the stone. Everything in the Kettle listens through bone; the oxen have merely made a bed of it. The stair announces whatever crosses it, and it stays silent under only one thing in the world, and the calves are taught the difference between a stair that is quiet because nothing is crossing and a stair that is quiet because something is.

Each ox carries a nation. The crest-wrens are small dun birds that nest in the sintered comb of the crests and comb the seams for lodged salt-fleas, and they go deaf by their second season — born into the chord, unbothered by it — which is another way of saying at home. When an ox dies, its wrens move entire to a younger animal, and so the wren-nations are older than their hosts, handed down like heirlooms; an ox without wrens is an ox in mourning. The ox feels their small songs through the bone of its skull as a kind of purr. And this is the beautiful part, the part the guides watch: the deaf wrens read the kites, and the ox reads its wrens. In a node's shelter the wrens forage on. In drought they bicker and fret. But when the third softness comes, they press themselves flat into the crest-seams, all of them, at once — and the ox beneath them stops walking, and the herd closes up, and the pan holds a shape it does not usually hold.

Never a flock to darken anything — nine kites this spring, eleven last year, a few, always. They are membrane-winged sailors of pressure, and a kite cannot fly in still air at all; it flies on the quarrel, tacking up the ridges of the wandering antinodes, reefing in the lulls. Their knife-cries are not song and not warning but instrument readings: a kite screams, and the bowl screams back a full report — floor geometry, the herd's position, the weather of the next hour — and so the kites know where everything is. Including where sound isn't. When the kites reef and wheel over empty pan, screaming into a place their voices don't return from, the emptiness has teeth. The herd looks up before it moves. So do the guides. So should you.

They nest in the sound-shadow behind the upright Tines, where the fundamental is strong and the floor's weather is blocked, and the eggs hatch to the hum — the chicks are quite literally shaken awake by the mountain. Some of them are the grandchildren of refugees: their grandames crossed over from the bowls whose spires fell, before the silence. On still days the flock hangs at the rim facing those bowls and screams toward them, and listens. Nothing comes back. They come back. And in the dead bowls — this is the guides' report, from the few who have looked down into them — the pans are drifted with fallen kites, because when the song fails the sky of that place is no longer flyable. The dead bowls are the fast that never broke. You can see two of them from this rim. The kites can see them better.

The three tolltrees are triplets, children of a single bad hour — one shatter-season, centuries back, that milled their seeds awake in the same long week. Glasswood, translucent at the edges, they hold the dusk-light a while after the pan goes dark, three dim lamps cooling across the floor. They cast no shade worth the name; what they cast is a lull. The pattern calms near their roots, the way water calms around a standing stone, and the herd gathers under them at noon not for shelter but for the stilling. All night the rim-wind plays their hanging bells. The unripe fruit houses small gray moths that will time their own emergence to an Agreement and ride the burst out with the seed-shrapnel, born in the same explosion that sows them.

Consider the arithmetic of a tolltree. Its fruit ripens and drops only in the Agreement, when every voice finds the chord at once. Its seed wakes only under a shatter-zone, milled open by the worst weather the bowl can make. So the trees are nearly childless and wholly patient, and the family knows of exactly one seedling — knee-high, forty years old, standing in an old antinode scar — and teaches its location mouth to ear, and leaves no mark. No tolltree has died in the family's memory, and the dread of it is plain and arithmetical: a third of the sky's bells, gone.

The meadow is not a field. Every drift is a palate — an individual with a taste. This bank tunes to the herd's chord and leans for it; that one drinks kite-shriek; the eldest banks, cathedral-thick, centuries old, are tuned to the Tines' fundamental and lean only for the deep weather. A botanist who could not hear a thing could map the floor's acoustics from the reed populations alone, which is, when you think about it, exactly what the reeds are doing. The economy is the liturgy: the herd sings, the reeds ring and feed and exude their mineral sap at the stem-nodes, the oxen lick the nodes clean, the herd sings. You can find the feeding grounds by the cold coming off them — the meadow eats the sound's heat, and shade with nothing casting it means a drift at table. In drought the meadow starves standing, and the dead drifts remain upright for years, a forest of stopped bells. Flick one and it answers. After the drought the choristers take the fallen down, and so the white flour you cross all day is the meadow's elders, ground fine. The floor is a graveyard that draws.

The hushfelt is the valley's other answer. It refuses to ring. Where the nodes wander oftenest it lays down its silvery mats and farms the stillness, and things live in the felt that have never once rung — small soft gray refusers, felt-hearted, whose whole strategy is the strategy the quiet wears as a hide. The oxen eat hushfelt only in drought, and you can hear it in them: the chords go threadbare, the triads thin toward unison. The valley wears its famine out loud.

Then below, the numberless. The pan is thin over the choir. Choristers are soft, pale, jawed things, blind, tymbaled, and every colony is a single note held for centuries — where two colonies meet, the pitches quarrel, and the dust above dances the border. The map on the floor is partly a map of nations. They are also the undertakers: nothing here is buried, everything is sung down, milled to flour that becomes dust that draws the map that keeps the living alive. They work the old conduits — the guides insist — squatters in the ear's throat, or its feral maintenance crew, depending on which guide you drink with. And the drought, the third silence, the catastrophic failure of the song: the family has another name for it. The fast. The choristers, who carry most of the music, stop to listen — all of them, at once, for a season — and everything above starves standing, and the oxen low at an empty sky, and the kites hang at the rim and scream. The valley's catastrophes are its vigils. And twice in the family's memory, after a fast, has come the Upwelling: the pan blooms pale in a single night, the emergents face the sky and sing and die, the kites gorge until they cannot fly, and the children of the dead mill their parents back down to flour. If anything here knows what the ear is aimed at, the numberless behave as if they do.

The salt-fleas are the plankton, the base of everything small, and they are drawn to moisture at a range that should not be possible across a waterless pan. You are the wettest thing in the valley; water here walks around inside the living, and the living can smell it on you. Do not weep on the pan. You will be found — by a rising whisper that sounds, of all the cruelties available, like rain on a roof. But note this mercy: the quiet has never once taken a flea. The smallest voice is the only safe voice in the bowl. And when the softness passes and the world unsoftens, the first sound back is always flea-crackle — the valley drawing breath.

In the bore of the fallen Tine there is warmth. That is what is known. The bore-mouth is warm as a byre in winter, and at dusk, under the five upright notes, a sixth sounds from under the salt — slightly flat, flattening further in cold years. The family calls it the Flat Sixth. It went silent for one whole year, a generation back, and the bowl's chord had a hole in it, and the meadow at that end leaned wrong, and the kites would not cross that quarter. Then it resumed. Theories at the family's table run to a matriarch toad grown huge on fleas, to a swarm, to a listener who went in decades ago and came to no harm the family will discuss. Once a year they leave a dish of salt at the bore-mouth. It is always gone by morning, which proves nothing; everything in the Kettle takes salt. What matters is this: when the Agreement comes, the fallen Tine sounds again, through whatever sits in its throat. The instrument is whole because something squatting in its broken part sings.

Now count down to one.

It commutes. The dust-map is its road-atlas, the nodes its highways, and when the map drifts, the quiet drifts with it, patient as weather. Nobody has seen it plainly; you know it by the softening, by small sounds not returning, by the kites wheeling over nothing and the wrens lying flat. It perceives, the family believes, only what its silence eats — it navigates by the shape of its own swallowing — and what it takes is voice. The straggler, the old, the new. The count of oxen was once higher; the tally-wall suggests as much, if anyone could read it. Whether there is one quiet or a few, the family tells only in the hut, never on the pan: the year of the Two Silences, when the weather went strange and the nodes pooled and fled, and then there was one again. If one died, it died silently, which the family points out is the only way it could.

They shed. A wedge-flake of that hide in your palm makes a small hole in a room's sound; the family's children are forbidden to play with them, and do. The guides disagree about what it is. The apex of the felt-strategy, hushfelt's masterpiece, the refuser perfected and given teeth. Or the machinery's own — the ear's pruning-knife, the editor of the song, clearing the channel for whatever the sky might say. Or the valley's fear of its own voice, walking. What is agreed: on the day of the Agreement, even the quiet is in the chord. It is the rests. And it has never once troubled the Baffle.

Because the ruin is a quiet — this the family will tell you at the hearth, whatever the guidebooks omit — flensed, roofed, dead long enough that its hide has gone to stone, or stone the way its hide always was. The prey shelters in the predator's blind spot, and the blind spot is shaped exactly like the predator: to a thing that perceives by listening, the wedge-walled hut returns nothing, a hole in the world, indistinguishable from itself. Sleep there and the Baffle returns you to yourself — blood, joints, the small storms of the body; some call it peace, and some last an hour. Everyone dreams the same dream in it: a hall, a listener, the feeling of being expected. And in the morning nearly everyone adds a mark to the wall without ever deciding to. Nobody reads it. Nearly everybody writes it. The tallies are layered past the family's memory, the oldest cut in strokes no wrist now makes, and the family's guess is that some rows count Agreements, and some gouges count droughts, and the oldest rows number more oxen than the valley now holds. It is a diary the place keeps in borrowed hands.

The family lives two passes down, at the threshold where the trail first buzzes through the boot — the hum-born, they are called, and call themselves. Their lintel is a slab of dead reed-glass that announces the wind; their crockery is crazed from decades of chord. Their children learn pan-sign before speech: a closed fist for the quiet; two fingers to the jaw for listen with your bones; a flat palm, wavering, for the node is drifting. The best living guide is the deaf daughter, who reads the floor the way you read a face, who sleeps in the Baffle more easily than anyone, and who comes closest to reading the wall. Her gloss, signed over the table: it is not a count, it is a rhythm. And the rhythm is slowing.

They live on salt cut at the pan's edge in the quiet season; on felt cloaks sold with guilt, since wearing the valley's one silence is a strange way to thank it; on dead reed-glass gathered after droughts, which below is fluted into instruments that hold a note strangely long and leave the player homesick for a place she has never been. Their rules: never sing on the pan. Carry the rod, and press it to your teeth when the weather turns. If you are lost in drift-storm, plant the rod, hum the fundamental, and let the floor tell you where you are. Their dead they give to the pan at night, laid out on a node; by morning the choristers have taken them down. So the dust the family reads the weather by is, in part, people who chose to stay. And once — in the last great fast, when the meadow was starving standing and the herd's chords had gone threadbare — they broke the oldest rule: the Year the Guides Sang, when five human voices stood on the open pan beside the herd and sang to the reeds, and the reeds leaned to them, and lived a little, and the kites screamed, and the fast broke. That is the family's whole claim to the place. Once, they fed it.

They are down to five. They have been eleven. The kettle counts them the way it counts everything, and they know it.

As for the makers — the first tenants — they left the instrument on, and the instrument keeps their appointment. Some of the guides say they never left at all; that they dissolved into the valley's habit of listening, which is, after all, the one behavior everything here shares.

Stay for dusk. Count once more. The stair plays the herd down to the landing, and the jaws go to the stone. The kites pin in the thinning light. The reed-drifts swivel upward, all of them, the old banks and the young drifts together. The fleas crackle. The borders of the chorister nations slow along their fronts of dancing dust. The tolltrees hold the last light and wait, fruit-heavy, for a chord ripe enough to drop into. Somewhere on the far pan, on some node, the quiet stands still and listens the way it listens — by erasure — and the hiss under everything thins toward the not-quite-pattern, and under the salt the Flat Sixth holds its note so that the chord, when it comes, can be complete.

Three trees. Seven oxen. Nine kites. The wrens, the moths, the numberless below, the fleas in their small millions. One tenant. One quiet. One husk full of sleepers. One wall being written. One family, five this year, down at the second pass, where the trail begins to hum.

The count is short. Nothing here is numerous; everything here is present. Say the count — the valley knows it by heart, and it is the only prayer the place has. And tomorrow the map will have drifted, and the daughter will read the new one, and everything that lives here will go on listening for the thing none of them has yet heard.
