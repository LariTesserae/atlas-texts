Ask what lives in the valley and the first honest answer is: nothing you can point at. You can stand a full day on the floor and see no animal, hear no voice, find no bone, no nest, no dung, no feather — only the tracks at the sump's edge, already softening back into the felt. The few who have crossed the rim learned to answer differently. The valley is inhabited the way a house with a single tenant is inhabited. Everything you have been looking at is the tenant.

Read it as a body and it stops being strange and starts being legible. The felt is a skin, alive across every inch, furred with rime where the cold touches it. The deep bed is a stomach that has been digesting for ten thousand years and has never once been full or empty. The vents are its breathing, the pumpwoods are its pulse pushed up into the air, the millroots at the rim are its jaws, grinding the mountain down to meal the way molars grind. The slow stroke you feel through your soles, that is its heart. Everything else in the bowl keeps its own time; the heart keeps the floor.

Whether it is one creature or ten thousand that have forgotten the difference is a question for winter evenings. Argue from the bazaar of cycles — no two pumpwoods in step, every vent on its own account — and the valley is a crowd. Argue from the collapses — the bed deciding, a whole stand going down in one gesture, the wreck green by morning — and it is a single will, a stomach with authority. It is one the way a city is one. The argument tells you about the arguer.

What is not in question is its temperament. It is interested. Cold is fuel here; that is the first rule. Warmth is news; that is the one nobody tells you. The valley produces everything it needs except surprise, and a warm body lying still on its skin is the rarest thing under its sky. So it reads warmth. And everything that lives here has made one of three arrangements with that fact: be the host, serve the host, or be illegible to it.

The feltdeer chose the third. It is the valley's only large animal, and it has arranged to be unreadable: grey-brown, slow, its coat grown into overlapping scale-plates that are not fur anymore but something nearer fiber, textured exactly like the floor. Rime sheets a standing deer exactly as it sheets the felt, and the deer lets it. You will never see its breath. Whatever the deer does with its breath, it does far inside itself. It carries no antlers — there is nothing here to fight, and no one to impress. There are no hunters in the valley. Nothing works by striking, and nothing hunts. The deer's famous stillness, the standing that your eye slides off, is not a prey's stillness. It is the stillness of something declining to be read.

There are somewhere between thirty and fifty of them. Every count the few have ever made comes back low and near the same; the count does not change. They crop the felt's scale-tips, keeping them at the length the rime prefers, and the host allows it the way skin allows a barber. They drink at the sumps, leaning to the black water, and the living lid that half-roofs each pond lifts its edge, slightly, for a deer. No one has seen it lift for anything else.

They speak, if you can call it speech, by leaning. The felt swallows anything quick, but a slow heavy press travels through it for miles, the way a swell outlasts the splash. So a deer shifts its whole weight onto one forefoot, holds, and releases: one soft thump that is not sound, that you feel through your boot soles if you happen to be standing still. Minutes later, from somewhere across the flats, an answer. One word, said slowly, at long intervals: here. Here. Here.

No one has seen a fawn. No one has seen a carcass either — the bed takes its dead in an afternoon — but the fawns are the deeper absence. An old deer walks out onto the open flats at dusk and stands, and within days there is a rise in the felt, green within the week; the count dips, and within a season it is back, and the few have stopped asking how. The asking is what the winter evenings are for. Deer are never found in the gaps where stands have gone down. They feel the tone before it comes, and they are elsewhere.

In the warm throats of the vent-stacks live the pale sleepers, and whether they chose the second arrangement or had it chosen for them is, again, a winter-evening question. Everything known about them fits in one line: a glisten, a deliberate withdrawal, nothing more. They run from finger-length to arm-length, they are slow, they are never seen whole because they are never entirely out. They do not feel your footfalls — the felt eats those — they feel your warmth, arriving ahead of you like your name announced at a door. The two theories are these: that they are larvae of something that never finishes, pupae of nothing, young forever; or that they are not animals in the usual sense at all — that the host buds a little soft mobile tissue where it needs some, a nerve, a tongue. The few report one further thing, and report it reluctantly. Sleep beside a vent-stack and you dream the same dream all of them dream: of lying warm in the dark, of being slowly carried. It is a short walk from that dream to the rule. Do not sleep by the vents at all.

The sumps have keepers, or the sumps are the keepers. The membranes that half-roof the black ponds are alive, and no one has settled whose life it is. They steam at their rims where the rime grows thick as masonry; they lift for the deer; they are warm to the touch, and the touch is mutual — a knife laid on a lid and forgotten is gone by morning, no mark, no seam. You cannot sound a sump. A lowered line comes up shortened, frayed to a soft point: the valley reads rope even in the dark under water. Whatever the host cannot digest it files down there, out of reach, in the only archive it keeps and the one that cannot be consulted. And once — the few will tell you this with the shrug they use for the unverified — something the size of a rolled carpet moved under a lid on the far flats, steadily, for the better part of an hour, and stopped.

The true population of the valley has never seen the sky. Every core drawn from the warm layers comes up inhabited: white threadworms long as a finger, slow grubs the color of the resin ingots, small folded things like fleas made of fiber, all of them curling dead within seconds of the air, as if the cold were a blow — though nothing here is ever struck. A hand's cube of warm felt holds more lives than the whole surface on its busiest day. These are the stokers of the firebox; the burning without flame is partly theirs. The host's digestion is done by lodgers, or else the host keeps a crowd in its gut the way you do, and the distinction is another winter evening. Kneel on the felt and watch one square foot of it for an hour and it stops being empty. The scale-fleas go about their errands between the plates, silent even in their hopping.

And then there is the one that leaves. A small dark bird — the few call it the guest, because it is the only one — that comes in with the cold pulses, when the valley deepens its work and the stacks breathe by the thousand. On the driest, bitterest nights, when the charge bleeds off the mast-tips as a violet corona, the bird lands on the glowing tips and sits while the light crawls over it, drinking the charge off the plant, feather by feather, until it departs dull and ordinary into the dark. What it does with what it drinks, the few believe, is spend it getting home: it burns the light for warmth on the long flight over the rim. On those same nights the spinner-heaps let go a drift of charged seed that rides the dry static to the glowing tips, and some of it sticks to the bird. The host writes its letters on the only traveler. Nothing that leaves the valley has ever been known to grow elsewhere; the letters arrive, and no one outside can read the address. The bird has a voice — beyond the rim it is an ordinary bird with an ordinary song — but you will not hear it here. It saves its voice for elsewhere, the way you save news for home.

There are even notables, the way a town has them. A half-day in from the descent stands the pumpwood the few call the Clock, whose stroke comes near enough to regular that parties time their marches by it. It is not regular. It is only faithful in its irregularity, which in the valley passes for the same thing.

Of people: none. The valley forbids no one; it simply keeps what stays. Nothing made remains made, and nothing warm remains unstudied. Stay a week and your patch learns you — the felt cups your bedprint before you lie down, the vents nearest your camp fall into time with your sleeping breath, and you catch the pumpwoods leaning and tell yourself it is wind, knowing there is no wind in the bowl that does what you are watching. The rule about sleeping uncovered is not advice. It is a headstone. The few know the story: a warm night on the flats, a man asleep under nothing, and by morning the felt had risen over him to the chin, gently, without haste, and his companions pulled him free of it by the arms. He said it had not felt like being eaten. It had felt like being read. He climbed out at the rim and did not come back, and his name is one of the things the valley has since digested.

So the census, taken as carefully as it can be taken. One host, single or legion depending on the arguer, ten thousand years in one shape. Some forty deer, the number fixed as a star's. Sleepers uncounted and perhaps uncountable. The lids, one to a pond — or the ponds themselves. The crowded warm dark, more lives than all the rest together by a factor no one has even guessed at. One bird, some winters three, perhaps always the same one. And at long intervals, for a few days at a time: you.

You are read from the first hour. The felt cups the place where you stood, and when you climb out, the census of the valley drops by one and returns to the number it has kept since before the ridges wore their present shapes. Nothing of you remains by the next day. What remains is in you, and it is this: for years afterward, in crowds, in engine rooms, in any deep silence, you will catch yourself standing still with your weight leaned forward, listening through your soles for a stroke that is not there — and the people around you will wonder what it is you are leaning toward.
