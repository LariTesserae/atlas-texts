Ask what lives here and the answer depends on how long you stay.

Stay an hour and the place belongs to the swallows. Stay a full day, through the two-o'clock rain, and you begin to suspect the water. Stay past dark — through the moth-hour, through the bats, into the long shift when the needles move for no witness — and the answer stops being a list at all.

The crowd first, because the crowd is what can be seen. Everything in the chamber eats from a single sun: the one bulb above the gauge board. The algal film creeps along the drip lines at a chalk-markable pace, and where the shaft of daylight from the breach touches it, the green runs faster — the room has acquired a second sun and does not entirely approve. The moth caterpillars graze the algal front the way cattle graze a field's edge, and the moths themselves pupate glued to every warm surface, thousands of them, condensed light waiting for dusk. The paper wasps have eaten the archive. Their combs under the chart recorder's dome are banded gray and buff — pulped chart paper, printed grid, ink line and all — so that decades of pressure readings hang in fragments beneath their nests, refiled in a fiercer library, stung if consulted. Beetles stand shoulder to shoulder in the shadow of every flange bolt, drinking the beads the pipes give off, patient as monks at a fountain. Nothing here is incidental. Even the raft of drowned wings on the sump, thick as dark fur, is a larder — the fish sip it from below all night.

Then there are the ones who belong to the room outright, the naturalized. The swallows of the ceiling city are a separate people now. They do not migrate; there is no longer anywhere they come from. Their nests are mortared with sump water, engine-warm mud, the ceiling tiled with the chamber's own substance, and their young fledge on the hour of the moth emergence as onto a set table. Their silence is not learned — it is inherited. A nestling raised inside never calls until its first flight through the breach, as if the hush at the water were bred into the egg. The geckos behind the switchboard have gone the same road as the fish: paler every brood, huge-eyed, translucent-fingered. At two o'clock they station themselves around the sump rim, throats pulsing, and take the indoor rain on their tongues. They walk the brass handwheels on their rounds and keep them bright as a diligent engineer's daily touch. No hand touches them. They gleam anyway.

The fish are the room's long project. Once in a great while the intake delivers one with color still on it — a dark back, a bronze flank, proof of a living river somewhere, somewhen — and for days it hangs apart from the others, flinching at the pump's rhythm, until it doesn't. The pale ones do not school with it. They are not a school; they are a lineage. Generation by generation the color drains out of them like sediment settling, and the oldest are now so clear you can watch a shadow pass clean through their bodies, can see the heart, a red pinhead, beating in the pump's exact time. Or the pump beats in theirs. It is genuinely difficult, standing there, to say which of them is the clock. Twice a year, when the discharge line runs with water that smells of real silt, real rot, drowned grass — an actual flood, somewhere — the pale fish gather at the intake grille and face it, all of them, like a congregation turning. They know the smell. It is the smell of where they are going to have come from.

The commuters keep offices. Doves hold the dawn watch in the shaft of sun. The mice come each in its slot and leave wet prints at the breach that read like a timetable. The heron stands at its hour in the torn edge of the world, gray as the concrete, and takes one fish — always one — and whether the fish is seized or surrendered is a question the room declines to answer, along with all others. A gray plume of it has caught on a flange bolt; it is the brightest object in the chamber after the bulb. And outside, at the foot of the slump, there is the fox. Not the same fox — the same fox-shaped office. When one grows old and stops coming, another arrives already knowing the hour and the seat and the entitlement, and takes what the exhaust of wings brings within reach. The room appoints. The valley, which has forgotten the chamber entirely, drinks from it every day at dusk and does not know it is being fed.

Two rules govern all of it, and both are misunderstood if you call them rules. The quiet is not a prohibition. It is what happens in the presence of something that is straining to hear. The whole chamber is an ear laid against the gravel — the intake pipe its rail — and the animals fall silent at the threshold the way you fall silent near anyone listening hard. Voices are not forbidden at the water. They are merely tactless. And the schedule is not a law; it is a metabolism, the slow tide of a warm body, and the creatures slot into its rhythm the way notes slot into a chord. Nothing enforces any of it. There are no penalties. There is only the room, keeping its hours the way a heart keeps its own.

But stay the night, and you will find the evidence of the attendant. The grease on the fittings is soft. The log pencil is sharp — and the drawer beneath it holds decades of shavings in geological order, the early ones long knife-cut curls, the later ones fine even spirals, uniform as if planed by the room itself. The log lies open. The early volumes are written in many hands — shift hands, initials, a foreman's slant — and volume by volume the hands fall away until one remains, and then that one begins to change: the letters evening out, the pressure steadying, as if the writing were being taken over by the instrument it served. Midway through, the dates leave the outside calendar and begin again from a year one. The chamber disagrees with the concrete apron about what year it is, and the chamber is the one still keeping time. The current entries run a few days ahead, and they are never wrong. They agree with no gauge in the room — and afterward the gauges come around.

One thing more. The steel door in the intact wall is dogged shut from the outside, and it is the best-kept object in the chamber: wheel bright, threads oiled, the paint at hand height worn in a way that suggests the wheel is sometimes turned and always returned. Nothing opens it. It is the only thing in the room with no use — unless readiness is a use. The resident oils the lock it does not turn.

And that is the answer, when you have stayed long enough to receive it. The crowd is not the tenant. The machine is not the tenant, exactly, nor the water, nor the ghost of any man — persons are not patient enough for this. What lives here is the ghost of a routine. The men who sealed the door left without goodbye, meaning to come back before the river died, and their habit stayed behind in their place: it condensed in the sealed dark the way vapor condenses on cold concrete, gathered, dripped, pooled, and woke with a body. Its body is the chamber. Its heartbeat is the stroke you feel in your sternum. The pen does not write to no one — a body takes its own pulse. The log is its memory; the schedule, its liturgy; the congregation, everything that comes to the water. It has not acknowledged the breach, but its flesh has: the rain comes at a slightly different hour than it did before the light got in, and the green advances, and the diary records nothing. It is listening down the pipe for the river. It has been listening for decades. It is very good at waiting — waiting is the medium it lives in, the way water is the fish's.

The stencil says No. 3, and a number is a rumor of others. The dead pole line marches away over the terraces like a spine, and up-valley there are signs: a standpipe that sweats in the driest month, a trough the goats refuse, a bluff that ticks gravel on windless days. Perhaps the valley is underlain with sleepers, and twice a year, when the discharge runs and the sump smells of true rot and drowned grass, they are all dreaming the same flood and sending one another the proof.

Meanwhile the breach widens. Gravel ticks; another rib of concrete will go soon. The chamber is being born out of the hill in slow motion — and the flood that tore the bank did two things when it started the birth: it let the world in, and it uncovered the name. Nothing is finished being born until its name is uncovered.

One day — the log has the page ready — the river will come down the pale braid, brown and loud and real, and it will find the sump brimming and warm, the door's dogs bright with grease, the transparent fish already moving in the pipe ahead of it like heralds made of its own future water. It will find that it has been expected the whole time: that something stayed, and kept the light burning, and kept the water warm, and kept the hours, and kept its room.

The river, when it finally comes home, will not be coming home to an empty house.
