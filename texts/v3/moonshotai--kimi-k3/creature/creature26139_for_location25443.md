Start with the soot, because everything here does.

The spores never stop falling. They are in every breath, every cup of rain, every fold of your collar — cane-spore, moss-spore, spore of things with no names because they have no need of any — and the moment new ground breaks water they begin to land, soft as ash, patient as debt. Nothing in the Drum stays bare longer than a hush. Within hours a fresh shoal wears a gray-green film the tappers call firstskin, slick as soap and alive to the touch, and under it the feltmoss is already threading down. Within a week the first cane-shoots stand up out of it like question marks. Newcomers cough for their first month — the new-ground cough, half spore and half spleen — and then stop, and the old hands say that's how you know you've been accepted: your lungs have shareholders now. An old tapper's cough smells of loam. They joke that they're halfway to ground themselves. They are not entirely joking.

That is the first thing to understand about what lives here: elsewhere, life answers light. Here it answers sound — and the Drum is generous with sound.

The feltmoss rafts are the floor of the world, and the floor is alive and has opinions. The rafts migrate — slow herds of sod, hectare-wide, shouldering each other along the outflows, turning over seasons so that a raft's dark underside feeds the water and its pale top feeds the air. They carry passengers: pale skipping things the size of a fingernail paring that live nowhere else, hopper nests woven into the thick of them, moth nurseries underneath. You can walk a big raft and feel it adjust to you, slowly, the way a sleeper adjusts to a dream. But learn this before anything else: the rafts die where the sound dies. In the quiet pockets, where two hammers cancel, the moss starves, sinks, rots — and so the floor of paradise is always bad. The softness under your feet in a dead-air pocket is not a bed. It is a collapsed thing. The stills know that too. Everything knows everything here; that is the local condition.

The drumcane is the drum's true choir. Every stem grows its drumhead to tension, and it grows it in answer to the band it lives in — so a cane that sprouted under the eastern towers sings different from a cane of the southern reach, and a listener can rap a cut stem with a knuckle and tell you which Anvil raised it. The cane remembers its tower. Whole thickets stand like sections of an orchestra, and the rain plays them — hard rain on ten thousand drumheads is one of the basin's great standing sounds, a hissing applause — and the outflow-wind plays them, and in the shift-change, when the chord drops out, the cane audibly unclenches: a long, scattered ticking and popping through the dark, the sound of a marsh exhaling. People build with cane, and every cane hut hums in the Roar, and so a camp is tuned whether it means to be or not. No two huts in a good camp sing the same note. The pith under the drumhead — cane-heart — is sweet and faintly ferrous, eaten raw or cooked into a black syrup, and a child of the basin cuts their teeth on it, literally: teething rings of cane-heart, so that the first thing you ever taste here is the instrument.

The icewort keeps to the rime-line, that narrow world where steam meets frost, and it works antifreeze into sugar, and the tappers keep its calendar. You bleed wort in the Hush, not the Roar — in the Roar the plant holds its sap up like a held breath, and you get nothing for your trouble but a clenched stem. So the tappers go out when the chord drops, bone spiles in their belts, and they move wort to wort by touch-sign, quick and silent, and what they draw becomes syrup, becomes glaze on drumfish, becomes *thaw* — the distillate that tastes the way a bell sounds, if a bell were sweet — and becomes, cut with fen-ox tallow, the lamp oil of the Hush-House, which never gutters because it cannot freeze. That lamp is made of the basin, literally. It burns the place's own refusal to die of cold.

The fungi take the rest. The pale shelf-fungi are the Drum's only undertakers, and they are thorough and they are honest: a carcass is days, no more, and what the glow takes it partly gives back as that blue-green light, cold, steady, a lamp that eats instead of burning. Travelers smear it on poles and hat-brims; trail-marks in living foxfire last weeks and can be read like handwriting by those who know the strains. The standing rule is older than the camps: do not follow foxfire you did not make. And when a person dies — because you cannot bury anyone in the Drum, the ground revises, the ground has no respect for permanence — you give them to the glow. A bed is cut in a fungus bank, the body laid in, and for a few nights the fen wears a small constellation where someone you loved is being translated. The camps come to see the constellation. It is considered a good death to burn long and blue.

The hoppers run the news. Blunt as stones, cat-sized, raft-skaters on splayed sucking feet, they are the basin's nervous system made flesh: each hopper drums its own signature on the cane — four beats, five, a grace-note slap — and beyond the alarm they drum territory, courtship, complaint, a whole running commentary that the tappers half-understand and the listeners claim to understand fully. They drum pools before drinking, because an old drumfish in a dead pool will take a foot. And when a still comes ghosting through, the alarm goes out ahead of it faster than the hunter can wade, cane to cane to cane, until half a kilometer of marsh is shouting *white, white, white* in percussion — which is why a hunt in the Drum usually fails, and why the stills have learned, the old ones, to move between the slaps, to step in the gaps of the drumming. The arms race is older than the camps too.

The moths keep the score. Each tower has its hatch — particular notes of the chord open particular pupae, nobody knows the mechanism, the listeners have stopped being embarrassed about that — so when the moths rise out of the outflow steam in their inverted snowfall, shingled wings shedding water, you are watching the chord made visible, band by band. This is how the basin learned the bending before the listeners' rods confirmed it: the hatches drifted. The moths heard it first. The listeners say this the way they say everything true — casually, like weather. And then there is the other hatch. Once in a long while, over the Cold Anvil, moths rise that answer no sounding tower — a hatch keyed to a note the basin has not heard in longer than memory of memory. They rise off the rime in their thousands, circle a silence, and die on the ice, and the floor of the First Wood is soft with their wings, drifts deep as a hand. The listeners call the frequency the Tenth Band. They write the hatches down. They do not speculate out loud.

The drumfish are the larder and the clock. They follow the warm outflows in eel-bodied schools, lateral lines reading the water like Braille, and you do not fish them with hooks — you fish them with a drum, tapping a lure-rhythm on the surface until the school comes up to investigate the interesting lie. When an Anvil shifts its working face, the outflows move, and a camp's fishery can vanish between one hush and the next; the camps say a tower has *turned its shoulder*, and fortunes turn with it. Their swim-bladders, dried, are sounding boxes — the listeners' field drums are made of drumfish, which the listeners also find funny and also do not joke about.

The laughers are the basin's joy, and its only hoarders. The otter-things den in the drowned parts-barge in the southern channel, and over the generations they have filled its hold with brightness: stake-caps, alloy fittings, dead instruments, buttons, buckles, and one plate of worn lettering that tappers bring visiting scholars to see. It is a parts manifest. It lists gaskets. The laughers ride the flood-pulses for the pure fact of it, screaming the whole way down, and their scream is your laugh slowed to a third of its speed — the worst sound in the basin until you know it, and after that the best. They slap water at night in perfect imitation of hopper alarm and then, presumably, enjoy the silence of an emptied thicket. The camps curse them by name and would burn the world for them.

The fen-oxen are the road-makers and the steady heart. Near-deaf by design, ear-holes sealed in waxed folds, they hold their conversations through the ground in stamps you feel across a kilometer of mud, and a herd crossing in the Hush is a slow drumline with moss hanging off it. Where they push through the cane they leave ox-roads — the only trails the basin half-keeps — and in the Roar a fen-ox simply stands, steam rising off it, a boulder with a heartbeat, waiting out the noise with a patience nothing teaches. Some are halter-broken as pack animals; their broad feet raft-walk where yours punches through. Their milk is half antifreeze and their fat makes the candles. And once in a generation a white one is born, and then for years the fens suffer a small epidemic of stopped hearts, until everyone relearns the rhyme: *count the legs, count the patience.*

Everything that wants height wants the First Wood, and the heron-things own the height. Gray-black, raucous, colonial, they fill the black spruce with a noise that is almost a town — arguments, announcements, grief, gossip, a civic clamor under the Lid — and in the big flickers you catch their pale underwings as white flashes against the dark, rising. They ride the storm air up into the Lid itself and come back wetter. They are the basin's only travelers above the weather, and whatever they see up there they scream into the rookery at dusk in a dialect the ground-bound will never learn. Under them the Wood keeps the basin's two strangest treasures. One is first-resin, bled from the spruce, which burns hot and slow and cooks the camps' food. The other is soil. Actual soil, a decade deep, that smells of time. Travelers kneel and smell it and sometimes weep, exactly as visitors to the Hush-House weep at their own heartbeats, and the old hands say the Drum offers two sacraments — silence and soil — and you should take both, once, and then get back to work. The understory beneath is the quiet garden: pale, fragile things that live on rot and patience instead of vibration, the basin's past and possibly its future, growing knee-high in the dark. No still has ever been seen among the trees. Once or twice, at the tree line, a still has been found as a white empty glove on the moss, flat, boneless, and the heron-things were loud for days after.

Which leaves the stills, and there is no leaving the stills.

Pale as porcelain, long-legged, blind as the deep water they never enter, cold-skinned so that the rime takes them and they wear it like a decision — they read the skin of the water, and they read it the way you read a face. They are patient past the definition of the word. They are found dead almost never, and when found, found wrong: emptied, boneless, gloves. Hide-hunters who will take anything will not take that. *Wearing the quiet,* they call it, and spit. Mostly they take hoppers, fish, the unwary. Mostly they let you pass. But they know the quiet pockets, and they stand in them, and in a quiet pocket your own breathing sounds like safety itself, and the standing rule of the whole basin is the rhyme every child drums before it can talk: *walk the rafts in the hush, wade in the roar, and sit in neither.* Run, and you are a dinner bell.

And they do the one thing no one can file under animal. On certain hushes — the listeners keep the dates — stills gather at the drowned end of the old causeway, out where it goes under mid-span, a dozen of them, two dozen, standing in the black water all facing north toward the Cold Anvil. The congregation, the tappers call it, and give that water a wide, wide berth. And sometimes, in that assembly, a still opens its throat and makes the only sound a still is known to make: one low note, felt in the sternum rather than heard, held until the water itself seems to lean. The listeners have measured it, with rods and teeth and patience. It is the Tenth Band. They are tuning themselves to the dead tower. No one says what that means. Everyone has decided, without a meeting, to leave them to it.

The people, then — a few hundred souls, and everyone here came from somewhere else, or their mothers did. There are no villages; there are camps of stilt-huts and cane-walls, named by stake and hush, because the old survey stakes are the only fixed coordinates in a basin that revises its own shores — *past Seven, two hushes, bear off the ox-road.* They work in the Hush and sleep in the Roar, inside the noise like inside a coat, and children drum their signatures before they speak their names: four beats, five, a grace-note slap, entered in the Hush-House logbook beside births and chord-readings and the deaths. Newcomers get chord-sickness — the bracing, the weeping, the sleep that won't come — and either leave or entrain, and the ones who stay slowly stop raising their voices, even in the hammering, as if it were a cathedral. They sign, they touch, they wait out the strokes. They count their age in hushes and their temperaments by their births — born in the Roar, born in the Hush — and when one of them dies, the camp drums the cane for one full hush, all of it, every stem they can reach, and then stops, and the absence afterward is the eulogy, and everyone signs it.

The listeners climb their ladders with rods of cane and bone and no dials, because dials lie here — instruments brought near the towers take their own polite counsel — so they bite the rods and listen with their skulls, and they keep the Ledger, decades of the chord's measure in different hands, all recording the same small thing: the bend, the slow resolving, the song leaning toward its end. The moths heard it first; the cane retunes at every shift-change with a sound like the marsh cracking its knuckles; and the people, without ever deciding to, pitch their voices into the new gaps the machines leave open, generation by generation. The basin retunes its children.

Nothing that lives by vibration goes deep. The laughers will dive anywhere but at the feet of the towers. The stills keep to water they can feel the bottom of. And on the oldest nights the listeners' rods pick up, from below, a beat slower than any tower — one stroke, a long pause, another — as if the basin had a second heart, asleep. They write it down and do not discuss it. The Ledger has a whole page of it, in a dozen hands, the handwriting changing, the beat not.

So the Drum keeps its ledger the way it keeps everything: in strokes, in hushes, in inhabitants. The hoppers drum the all-clear across the dark in signatures older than the camps. The lamp burns its unfreezable oil with a congregation of moths around it like a small, warm weather system. The logbook lies open to a four-beat name. And out on the drowned causeway, white and patient, the congregation lifts its heads to a note no living tower sounds — while the cane creaks to new tension, and the chord bends, and the whole listening basin, everything in it that breathes or drums or stamps or glows, lives on at full volume inside the unfinished song, because that is the only way anything lives here: loudly, together, in the bands the machines leave open.
