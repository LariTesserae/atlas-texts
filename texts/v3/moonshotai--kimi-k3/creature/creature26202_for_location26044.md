By the third rule, nothing lives here that cannot fly or cling. It reads like an exclusion. Read it again — it is a census, and a stranger one than the rule suggests.

Everything here arrived. The lichen arrived as dust on a storm. The gardens arrived as seed; the spiders on threads; the swifts' ancestors on their own wings, and the people on their own legs. The only question an inhabitant can be asked is when it came, and how completely it stayed. Sort the lives of the Spire that way and they arrange themselves the way the climates do on the stone: in bands, from the ones merely passing through to the one that has never been anywhere else.

The official abundance is the colony — ten thousand spireswifts, better counted as one: one animal with ten thousand bodies, spread across a mile of sky, turning like weather. They harvest the upwelling, the aerial plankton lofted from valleys a vertical mile below — midges born in lowland ponds, spiders that will not land well, winged seeds. The Spire eats the lowlands at one remove. A chick sits its crevice like a fist of soot and is fed the wind in pellet form: four hundred midges packed into a single bolus in the parent's throat. Out of season, some of them never land at all. They sleep on the wing, half a brain at a time, and wake over the cloud-sea with a mile of nothing beneath them, untroubled. Their kind has made the void a habitat. That is the colony's whole genius.

The colony keeps no graveyard. When an old swift can no longer climb the air, it lets go, and the first rule takes it. But deep in the driest flutes, above the weeping's reach, the ones that died clinging still cling — dried to feather and wire, stacked in seams, decades of them, held like shut fans. The place keeps its dead only if they do not fall. And when the west wind finds its angle, the nestlings feel the Note before it sounds: the stone purrs up through their breastbones, and by the time the chord stands up in the open air, every beak in the colony is already shut.

The falcons are less a pair of birds than an office the sky fills. When one is lost, the ledge stands vacant a season; then a new bird comes up out of the gray — everything arrives by air — and takes up the whitewash, the hunting ground, and the rule. They have learned the Note better than any lore: they climb through the chord itself, and stoop in the silence after. Below the eyrie, out of the weeping's reach, lies the one graveyard the Spire permits — swift bones fine as needles, the pale rings of windpipes, quill-sheaths the updraft takes one by one. The only archive of the dead on the mountain, and it is kept by the killer.

The place keeps one raven the way it keeps one pair of falcons: as a position. Whoever holds it is black, and old, and owns the afternoons. It is the largest animal here that walks, and it walks the crown like an inspector — the cairn, the bowls' overflow, the falcon's leavings, an egg where an egg should not have been left. Once an afternoon, from the updraft's shoulder, it lets fall a syllable that is not a raven syllable. Something rounded, shaped for a different mouth. It may be a word, kept the way the bowls keep water: past all use, purely. The void, per the second rule, returns nothing, and the raven banks away, and the afternoon proceeds. If any animal remembers the people, it is this one — or its parent did, and the posture was taught. Ravens hold a crag for generations. The croak is the same either way, and the void's answer is the same either way.

The snow-buntings are the only animals on the Spire that live in architecture. They nest in the windbreak's stacked stones, in a lee the people built, and they line the nest with the cairn's feathers — the offering drawn back out of the rock, beakful by beakful, and put to the work of keeping eggs warm. This may be what offerings are for. Days, they glean the bare crown, restless as thrown gravel: seeds off the stone, spiders off the rims, the guano-fly at its brief dance. When the Note rises they go silent with everything else that has a voice.

The spiders are the immigrants, and their papers are the wind. For every one that lands on a wet rim and sets its rigging, the upwelling delivers a hundred to the void, and the void returns nothing, per its custom. The survivors string their lines across the flute-mouths and the drip-line shadows, harvesting the same current the colony harvests, at a finer gauge. Their young leave the way they came, out on threads into the gray; the ledges are a port, and a few always stay, and that is enough. When the Note comes, it comes up their lines like a struck cable. What a spider makes of a mountain's chord, no one is asking. They do not go silent. They were never loud. The rule is for the voiced.

In the crown's one warm hour, the bee. There is never more than one at the sedge flowers, and there is always one; the bee, too, is an office. She is furred like a bear the size of a fingernail, and she works an hour a day at most — say four hundred hours a summer, when the summer is kind: the entire economy of her kind conducted in what a lowland bee would call a fortnight. Her nest is a fist of wax and shredded moss down in the peat, where the black holds the noon an extra hour, and her queens overwinter there like banked coals. She is the dwarf shrub's entire romance, and the sedge's, kept on a schedule of drips. When the stone sings, the bee does not stop. The silence is a law of the voiced; her voice is too small to confiscate, and an hour is too short to spend on awe.

The official abundance is the colony. The real abundance is underfoot, and it does not sing. For every swift there are a thousand springtails, stippling the wet moss, grazing the film, drifting in the tarn's rim, where they fall in like manna and are taken. In the lichen and the moss-cushions live the water-bears, and they keep the weeping schedule more strictly than anything alive: dry as glass by dusk, swollen and walking by the second drip of noon. They are born again every day. Nothing here is resurrected so often, and nothing makes less of it. Even the whitewash streaks have their small economy — mites, and one black fly whose whole world is the guano, born in it, fed to spiders and buntings out of it, never once lighting on clean stone. Even the leavings are inhabited.

The turf of the ledges is fallen sky: windblown seed, insect deadfall, the colony's white tithe, carried up a mile on wings and weather. The gardens are the lowlands, distilled. The peat on the crown is the place's black archive — a darker thread for every summer, and between the threads, pollen of alder and pine from a world none of this will ever see. News from below, filed for centuries. Nothing up here reads it. It is kept anyway. The dwarf shrub may be the oldest rooted thing on the crown: one individual, or a few, wider than a cloak, never ankle-high, older than the cairn and perhaps than the holds. It has never stood up in its life. Everything here clings; the shrub is the one that clings to ground. The saxifrage has planted itself in the plumbing, gripping the runnels where the noon water runs, drinking the schedule.

Older than the shrub, older than anything that breathes, the lichen. It grows a thumbnail in a century. It does not keep the water's hours; it keeps the water's decades. In the palm-cups of the pecked holds it has been growing since the hands let go, and the age of the climbing is written there in orange thumbprints, for anyone who reads stone. No one up here reads stone. The writing stands.

The people, when they come — if they come — are the rarest animal in the census: a species whose season is decades. Everything they left has been moved into. The buntings own the windbreak; the ferns own the cracked bowl's shadow; the lichen owns the holds. Beneath the cairn, the midden lies layered generations deep — the taken, tallied in shell — and woven through the stones above it, the given: swift feathers, hundreds, gray with years. Whatever the exchange was, whatever it cost in conscience, the place has filed it, and the buntings are drawing on it still. At the cairn's heart the copper greens toward the moss's green, taken back a molecule at a time, and the molecules are in no hurry. The two whole bowls go on filling. The water stands after gray weeks and falls in the clear ones, as it did when there were hands to lift it. Whatever it was drawn for, it is still ready; being ready is the bowls' whole work now. Whether the people come still, the mountain does not say. If the hands return, they will find their holds polished, their water standing, their windbreak full of small warm eggs. Nothing here is waiting. Everything here is kept.

And then there is the one that never arrived.

By the third rule nothing lives here that cannot fly or cling, and the salamander is the rule's one exception — or its purest case, since it clings to the whole mountain. Small, black, finger-long, slow with cold; its heart beats the way the bowls fill, without haste. Its species lives in one room of water in all the world, and has never, in all its memory, touched soil. Everything else here came by air or not at all. How the salamander's kind came, the mountain does not say. Perhaps the world was different once — ice where the void is now, a bridge long melted. Perhaps eggs on a bird's muddy foot, in some century when something waded here. Nothing wades here now.

The tarn is its entire earth, and the earth has tides: up after gray weeks, down after clear, never empty, never spilling. Its universe breathes, and the salamander breathes with it. It eats the midge larvae that eat the peat's dark leavings; the midges that escape it rise and are eaten by swifts; the swifts tithe the gardens; the gardens feed the springtails that fall in. The loop is the size of a room, and the salamander is its crown and its floor. Under the peat's far shelf there may be one sixty years old, and sixty years here is not a long time; the tarn keeps longer accounts than that. When the Note stands up out of the stone, the water carries it — rings on the black surface, a hum in the bed. The birds answer the chord with silence. The salamander answers the way it answers all weather: by continuing. In its species' whole memory there are perhaps a dozen faces at the rim, none recent. It has learned what everything here learns about the surface: nothing there is food.

Its dead go to the bed, into the soft black accumulation of its kind — sedge-thread and midge-casing and the salamanders themselves, centuries deep. The living drift over their own entire history, and it is soft, and it holds them.

Everything else on the Spire lives above a mile of nothing. The salamander alone has a floor: black, centuries deep, made of everything its kind has ever been. In a place that stands where the world runs out of floor, it is the only creature that touches bottom.
