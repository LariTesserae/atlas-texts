# The Census

You cannot count the population of the Sounding Plateau with your eyes. Eyes say: empty. Four animals the size of punctuation marks in a hundred miles of stone. But the country conducts its own census, continuously, out loud, and has done so since before the varnish — and the way to hear who lives here is to take the chorus apart, layer by layer, the way the basins take it apart, and then to ask one more question: what is the sound hiding?

The answer, in full, is five voices and a gap. Every one of them lives on a boundary. The plateau has no habitable interiors — only edges — and so everything here is an edge-dweller, and each edge has its people.

### The Hem

By mass, the largest living thing on the plateau is the lichen. It is also the clock, the calendar, the pasture, and the map. It threads the melt-rims exactly — black-green at the damp inner edge, rust at the dry outer one — a zoned band no wider than a hand, drawn around ten thousand shadows, and it has not moved in longer than anything can remember, because the boundary it follows has not moved. The pasture never widens. Every grazer here has learned, across geological time, to be exactly as hungry as a fixed contour line can feed.

In the melt-film itself, where the water is a molecule's rumor on the stone, live the melt-fleas: translucent, the size of sand grains, the smallest citizens with the smallest world. Each shadow's hem is an island ocean, cut off from the next by absolute desert, and has been since before the hares had stones — so the fleas of one shadow drift, at lichen speed, toward being a different people from the fleas of the next. When a hornback licks the rim, a thousand tiny worlds end in one slow stroke of the tongue. The hornback does not know and would not stop if it did. This is what drinking is, here.

At the white heart of each shadow, nothing lives. Nothing has ever lived there. It is the coldest, stillest place in a cold, still country — a little pole with no voice and no listener. All the life of the plateau lives on a contour line, and the heart of a shadow is the one place with no line at all.

### The Hiss

The fret-crickets are the only fast citizens. They live in the hems and very nearly *are* the hems — half the margin's bulk is cricket — and they rasp their hind-leg combs against lichen thallus and stone, coupling their small noise into the ground, each one bowing its few centimeters of country like a fiddler. The sum is a noise floor the size of the sky. A visitor's ear edits it out within the hour. Editing it out is a mistake: the hiss is the carrier wave, the country's mercy, the sound of being accompanied. Take it away, as the basins do, and what remains is your own pulse, which was always there, which the crickets were kindly covering.

A cricket's whole life is a migration measured in millimeters and made ten thousand times: bask on the lit side of the line, feed on the damp side, cross, recross. Their eggs wait out the cold exactly at the boundary. And because the hems are half cricket, every grazer on the plateau is a half-insectivore without knowing it or caring — the hare cropping lichen crops a living crowd. At the rim, the upflow strips a few into the sky: the plateau's only export, carried up to the swifts, who live on the cloud-sea's imports. The swifts, in this sense, work the customs house.

### The Pulse

A knock-hare owns four things: a stretch of hem, a drum-stone, a sleeping hollow in the nearest silt basin, and a sentence.

The sentence is a rhythm, inherited unchanged, hammered hind-footed into an adopted slab that ten thousand generations of the same declaration have polished hollow. It says one thing — *held* — which makes it the oldest literature in the country. The vanished knappers left tools. The hares left a text. And because every drum-stone colors the rhythm differently, a hare's name is its address: two hares drumming the same sentence on different stones are saying different words. Once, within memory — which here means within the retelling of grandfather to grandchild across a span no visitor can usefully imagine — a flake broke off a drum-stone three districts from the rim, and the stone's voice went flat, and the network noticed, and carried it. That is the nearest thing to news the plateau has produced in living memory. Events happen. Nothing changes.

A hare listens with its chin flat to the bedrock, its outer ears — small, furred afterthoughts — folded shut. It hears through its jaw. Which means a drumming hare is deaf, and a listening hare is blind, and a hare's life is an alternation between broadcasting and surveillance, never both at once. Everything that hunts hares knows this seam in them the way a thief knows a door.

They know dozens of neighbors by pulse and have never seen one. They have opinions. When a hare dies, its stone stands silent, and the silence is felt for miles — and in time a young hare, walking the net on the one journey of its life, hears the vacancy, stops, and fills it. Nobody here is assigned a place. Everybody here is a gap that got filled.

When a hare must sleep, it commutes to the basins across open pavement, and the whole country feels it go — step on bare stone and the whole country knows it. Going to bed is the most public act of a hare's life. The basins themselves are the country's one memory that is not music: the dead silt erases nothing, and the footprints of every sleeper who ever slept there are still there, centuries deep, a palimpsest of small tired bodies. Among them, unremarked, the pads of the hush.

### The Chord

The hornbacks are the large life and the long sound. One to a shadow, because a shadow feeds one — the arithmetic is exact, because the pasture is a line of fixed length and fixed growth. A hornback's note is its breath, slowed to the scale of geology: an exhale outlasts a visitor's patience; the inhale between notes is the only silence a hornback knows; and the swell and ebb of it, carried through miles of bedrock, reads at a distance as one continuous tone — the country bowing a string. The hollow convolutions of the crest are shaped, over generations, district by district, so that the note sits in a gap of the local stone chord. Transplant a hornback and it would be dissonant. Homeless in frequency.

Each animal wears the whole country's anatomy on its body: warm lit flank, dark flank flocked with its own private hoarfrost, and at the boundary between them, where its own melt trickles down the creases of the hide, old animals thread with rust lichen. They have stood so still, so exactly, for so long that the country has begun to embroider them. They are walking contour lines.

Once in a life, a hornback travels. A calf is raised at its mother's shadow — the only time any shadow feeds two — and when it is grown it walks outward through the chord, listening for a hole in the music: a shadow whose tenant died, whose trails are healing under rust. When it finds the gap whose frequency its crest was built for, it stops, opens the convolutions of its crown, and fills it. That is the complete biography: one departure, one arrival, one note held for centuries. When a hornback dies, the district hears the hole for years. The plateau keeps its memory in the shape of missing notes. And out on the pavement, the wind that carved the flutes finds the abandoned crests of the long-dead and plays them, faintly, badly, out of tune — ghost notes. Even the dead are not excused from the chord.

They sleep standing, at the hem, and the note continues, autonomic as a heartbeat — because that is what it is. The chord never sleeps, though every singer does. The chord is how the herd that never gathers knows itself to be complete.

### The Wheel

The gyre-swifts are the only citizens of the air, and they render the wind visible. Each wheeling column, thousands strong, is a diagram of the rim's rising breath drawn in birds — a standing smoke of wings that never settles. They feed on what the unseen world below exhales: spores and gossamer insects, immigrants at the border. They are the only residents who ever touch water as a substance rather than a contour — they skim the cloud-sea's face and come up wet, the drops freezing off them like sparks — and the only ones who ever see the whole design: the embroidered country, every shadow hemmed with growth, the punctuation-mark animals, the fixed geometry of everything. The plateau's single panoramic viewpoint is occupied by creatures with no use for the view. The hares hold all the news and have never seen anything. The swifts see everything and report nothing.

They roost in the ancient flute-holes, and a roosted flute is a stopped pipe — so the swifts are the fingers on the organ stops. The voicing of each district's stone chord shifts, almost imperceptibly, with the sleeping arrangements of birds, and the hornbacks, listening for the gaps the rocks leave open, adjust their held notes by less than a listener could swear to. Nothing changes. The only nests in the country are inside the instrument.

Their cries are the one voice that arrives through the ears rather than the bones — thin, high, silver, nearly imaginary over the ground-thunder. To everything else that lives here, the swifts are what the sky sounds like.

### The Gap

And then there is the hush, which the census-by-ear cannot find, because the census works by listening.

Leopard-sized, colored exactly like a lit stone face, soft-footed, ear-valved: in the full roar of the open pavement it closes its ears and hunts by sight — the only watcher in a country of listeners. Nothing here watches. Everything here listens with its whole skeleton. The hush alone keeps its eyes open, and that is its living.

A hunt is the slowest violence in the world. The hare drums (deaf) and listens (blind), alternating. The hush flattens itself on warm stone a hundred paces off, reads the rhythm, and moves only inside the drum-bursts — placing each footfall on a pulse, letting the hare's own signal carry its approach, a stalk conducted at the pace of lichen. When the drumming stops, it stops, and becomes one more settled stone among the settled stones, which is easy, because nothing on this plateau has moved in a thousand years and one more pale shape is beneath notice. Hours of stillness. Three steps. Stillness. The final rush — the only burst of speed the plateau ever sees, speed being the hush's monopoly — is timed to the swell of a hornback's note, so that the most violent event in the country happens inside the loudest moment and leaves the chorus unchanged. One pulse goes out of the net. The hares feel the vacancy at once. In time, a young hare will find the silent stone. The wheel turns at the speed of everything else.

It dens in the dead basins, among the sleepers, and is safe to sleep beside — because in the basins there is no cover. Silence is the one medium in which the hush is conspicuous: there, its own body is the loudest thing present, and even it must hear its own pulse. It is the only creature on the plateau that can hear itself. Everything else is drowned in the sum. Selfhood, here, is an acoustic condition — you must live in the basins or be the hush — and its kits are the only natives born into quiet, raised in the dormitory where a mother's breathing is the weather, until the day they climb out to the rim and the chorus hits them like the inside of a bell. Like visitors. They get over it. Visitors don't.

How many hushes there are, no one can say. You count what you can hear. There may be nine; there may be one with an immortal's patience. The local philosophy holds that noise is life. The hush is the counter-argument, and it is winning at the same slow rate as everything else.

### The Weather

No one else lives here. The knappers came up out of the cloud with stone in their hands and left with less of it, and the lichen owns their flakes now — but they were never residents. The hares' drum-stones were polished hollow before the first hand-axe was struck. The oldest hornbacks now holding notes took them up while the knappers were still climbing. The traces are not the ruins of the tenants. They are the record of the ones who didn't stay.

To the five voices, a visitor is not a person but an event: a rhythmless drum-signal that walks, a wrong note crossing the chord, a weather. The hares feel you miles off and file you under geology. The swifts see you and have no category. The hornbacks sing through you. Only the hush turns its head — the only head that turns.

It sounds crowded and looks empty, and both impressions are correct, because the truth is the third thing: it is *full*. Every seat in the country is taken and has always been taken, every shadow fed, every gap sung shut. The chorus is the sound of there being no vacancies.

Come back whenever. The census will not have changed. It is the only census in the world that never does.
