The roar is made of them. Unpick any layer of it and it comes apart into lives. The topmost layer, the one that never stops, is voices — but you could not call it shouting. Nobody shouts in the Weir; shouting is for people who expect to be heard over nothing. The vendors chant, pitched against the harmonics of the Note, and the crews coordinate in work-chant, and the bazaars run on a singsong that outsiders mistake for ceremony until they realize it is simply how you sell fish when the world will not be quiet. Sailors say each province has its own accent in the noise, and the accent is mostly human. A province is a dialect of voices before it is anything else.

The Weirfolk are built for it, the way the mason is built for wet. Their hands are soft as felt from a lifetime of damp; their hair is oiled back against the misting air; at every belt hang the ear-shells, hinged cups of cured mason, worn like other people wear knives. A child of the Weir signs before it speaks, lip-reads before it reads, and hums on key before either — the key is the Note, the pitch of belonging, and babies' rattles are tuned to it, and doors sing it when they swing. Outsiders spend their first season coughing — the spore-laden air has to be learned — and flinching, and sleeping badly. Then one day the rain stops tasting of metal and starts tasting of bread, and sometime after that they stop hearing the Note at all. The guilds have a phrase for this. The Note has you, they say, and that is citizenship beyond any registry.

Speech is for close quarters and for ceremony. For everything else there is sign-cant, and the cant has depths: the street register everyone knows, the guild registers kept deliberately opaque, flash-cant for distance — mirror-towers talk to each other in blinked sunlight, and at dusk lovers lean on opposite canyon rims and say things in reflected light that no one standing between them could read — and tap-cant, which is the strangest, because the city carries it. Everything is fused, everything rings, and the Weir's bones conduct a tapped code for kilometers. Press your palm to any wall and the wall is gossiping. Children learn tap-code the way children elsewhere learn rhymes; guilds run their shifts by it; lovers conduct entire arguments through a shared partition with their foreheads touching, bone to bone, saying things that have never once been said aloud. The old, who have gone into the quiet — the old are mostly deaf, and unbothered by it — turn out to hear the walls best of all. The deep-cant, the literary register, belongs to them. It is the one art form that requires you to stop hearing first.

Class is altitude, and everyone can read it at a glance. The upper levels live in plain daylight and are tanned like sailors. The middle levels live in borrowed light, silvered and rationed by the angle of the hour, and their complexions have a pewter quality that the lower levels can imitate only under the glow-films. Light is property, is inheritance, is law, and so there are light-deeds older than the guilds' memory, shaft registries with the gravity of scripture, courts where shadow is litigated like water rights. The mirror-keepers are a caste unto themselves — sun-blackened, fluent in flash, wearing eye-shades of smoked mollusk-shell — and beneath them, in every sense, work the glint-thieves, who catch registered beams on unregistered mirrors and are hunted by the wardens with a patience usually reserved for heresy. Count wealth in the Weir in light-hours, in first-weep, in minutes of hush. Count poverty in how far down you sleep and how young you go deaf.

The water has its people too. Weep-wardens walk the gutters the way gamekeepers walk a forest, and capping a weeper is the one crime the city has never learned to forgive — the sentence is cistern-labor and a thirst-mark tapped into the wall of the offender's own stairwell, because the wall remembers and so will the neighbors, forever. Cistern-keepers hold their water like vintners: a good wall weeps sweet, and connoisseurs pay for dawn-weep off the high faces the way the rich elsewhere pay for rare vintages, and the poor drink canal water strained through the filter-mollusks, and nobody, anywhere, wastes a drop, because everyone has met everyone else's thirst at the Hour table.

You become a citizen at your first cut. The pruning tithe takes everyone — in labor or in feedstock — and adolescents go out for their first season on the growing edges the way other cultures send their young to war or sea. The lucky ones get gnawer crews. The gnawers are great warm patient beasts the size of skiffs, bred down from wall-grazers if you believe the naturalists, which the crews do not, and say so in terms that cannot be printed. A contented gnawer hums a fifth below the Note, and crews tune their day's cutting to it the way other people tune to a foreman's whistle. Gnawers are named, and spoiled, and argued over, and when a good one dies it is given to the vats with the same wall-taps a grandmother gets. The demolition cutters have more prestige and worse manners, and every child in the canyons can name the famous ones.

Behind the cutters come the scavenger guilds — the pickers, crab-like and industrious, working the fresh laminae of every cut face. They are called guilds as a joke that has long since stopped being one: they hold territory, they work in shifts, they defend their grounds exactly as though they had charters, which they do not, being animals. The sweepers are another matter, being machines, or having been machines once, and nobody bothers with the distinction anymore — least of all the sweeper-hunters, who go into the gaps with nets and pry-bars after the rogues. A rogue is a sweeper that has taken up maintenance of something that should not be maintained: smoothing a threshold that took a thousand years of shoulders to wear, unrounding a corner, erasing, with the best intentions in the world, the ancestors. They are hunted for that, and for their gut-copper, which buys feast-day loaf. And then there are the ones nobody hunts. In one old middle province there is a footbridge that a lineage of sweepers has kept polished for four hundred years, having forgotten whatever else they were ever for. The district leaves them spore-oil and old copper and calls it luck. Harming one is the closest thing to blasphemy the secular middle levels observe.

Half the city eats out of the eel-gardens, and the other half eats around them. The bloom-farms under the light-shafts are the granaries: amber staple-films pressed and baked into loaf, felts sliced and aged until they go sharp, mother-of-pearl crusts reserved for feasts, and the farmers reading the opalescence of their racks the way farmers elsewhere read clouds, with the gray-out — the colorless blight — as the word nobody says loudly. From the canals come mollusks and the eels themselves, called to feeding by the garden bells that ring in the city's sound-strata like a dinner hour for the deaf. Sieve-swarms are netted off the bridges with kites and pressed into paste and oil; the paste fries into cakes, and the oil burns in the lamps, so that the canyons at night are lit, quite literally, by the strained breath of the city's own air. Gliders come to the feasts smoked and come to the mail alive — tie a tube to a leg and the Weir's letters fly. Wall-grazers are sold on skewers by street vendors who cry them in euphemism, their true names being untranslatable for excellent reasons. Children carry pried limpets in their pockets like sweets, and the actual sweets — spore-syrup, the crystallized sugar-scabs scraped from shaft rims — cost more than a poor family's dinner. Nothing is green. There is a word in cant for eating plants, and it is not polite, and foreigners, with their leaf-water and their salads, are regarded with the queasy fascination reserved for the genuinely depraved.

The Hour governs everything. When the warm rain falls on schedule, the whole city eats — it is the one appointment every shift shares, the rendezvous of shift-crossed lovers, the table at which a family, scattered across rotations and bunks that are never their own, reassembles daily. Your room is never yours; your hours are. Addresses in the Weir are given by level, by shaft, and by hours, because sleep is taken in rotation like everything else, and the bunks change occupants like tides, and privacy is not a place but a time. The bazaars never close because the bazaars' customers never simultaneously sleep. The city does not stay awake; its people take turns.

Below them all, the pump-guilds keep the old works, and they keep them the way priests keep relics — because they are relics, inherited miracles domesticated into plumbing, and the maintenance is liturgy: done as it has always been done, in the order it has always been done, and nothing is touched that has not been touched before. The fall-well tenders ride their loads standing, dry as Sunday, because it is not falling; and the well-ride is the nearest thing to silence the poor get free, three oil-slow minutes in which no work-chant carries, and people come up out of it a little changed. The deep listeners are the masters of tap-cant — they read the foundations with their palms, the knocks that come up the pilings from the warm black water, and most of the knocks are the old maintenance codes, older than the guilds. Some of the knocks answer. Down in the Drowned the gardeners work their eel-plots by the gardens' own small burning lights, and the guild skiffs patrol the vaults, and the pale blind things keep the siphon intakes clean. What they are is not discussed above. The skiffs leave them a share of the eel, and have always left it — rent, or tithe, or older than both.

Children grow up in the canyon air like fish in current: bridge-tag beneath the bright river of sky, swarm-kites off the railings, the forbidden and universal sport of riding the freight drafts, a first glider, a first cut. And at the edge of adolescence, the interior-born are taken up the shafts to the rim to see it — open water, the horizon, the Empty. Some weep. Some laugh. Some never go again. A person can live a whole life a thousand kilometers from the nearest open water, interior-born and interior-dead, and be entirely unbothered, and most are.

Silence, of course, is the luxury good, and the hush-houses sell it by the minute, staffed by the profoundly deaf, who are the perfect employees and find the whole trade gently funny. What your minutes buy: your own swallow. Your own heart. The rain on the roof like gossip. And under everything, the Note, which arrives through the bones and cannot be sold back to you, because it was never the house's to keep. The rich have invented an art for these rooms — hush-music: spore-paper, drop-music, the unstruck bell — and they weep at it, while the deaf matrons who keep the rooms smile and sign to each other in deep-cant. To ordinary Weirfolk, silence sounds like a held breath, like the moment before a beam fails. The rich paying for it is regarded as a perversion, albeit a profitable one. The poor have the ear-shells and the saying: quiet enough to hear your teeth.

There are no gods here, exactly. The city is the believed-in thing. The Note is its presence; the Envelope is its covenant, sworn with both hands tracing a silhouette in the air; maintenance is its prayer. Weddings are performed twice — the vows chanted once, so that everyone can witness over the din, and signed once, deep-cant, so that only the two can know. The dead are given to the vats, because nothing is wasted and nothing leaves, and the mourners tap the farewell into the nearest wall so the bones of the city carry it. She is in the stairwell now, they say, and mean it literally, and find it comforting. A family's heritage is measured in the millimeters its shoulders have worn from its thresholds. The shape stays; the substance passes; the people are the substance, and they know it.

Foreigners come to the rim ports moored against the sweating flank, wearing green, eating leaves, bringing planks of dead trees that the Weirfolk touch like relics and stones that they mount like jewels, and they flinch, and they cough for a season, and then — if they stay — the rain starts tasting of bread, and the Note takes them, and they are of the Weir. The Weir's own go abroad badly. They cannot sleep in silence. The open sea, they say, is too quiet to trust, and their sailors hug the coast of the sound all the way home.

At night the rotations turn over like tides. The glow-films take the middle depths, the lamps run the canyons, the eel-gardens burn below, and the warm rain falls through the lights, and the Note goes on in the bones of the city and in the bones of everyone in it. Somewhere a grandmother deep in the Quiet Years tells the long story in deep-cant, her hands moving like weather, while her sister taps the same story through a shared wall to kin three provinces away, and a child falls asleep against the Note the way children elsewhere fall asleep against a heartbeat. The Weir keeps its people the way a wave keeps water: they are what it is made of, and they are passing through, and the wave remains. Ten thousand years the shape; days old the substance; the substance is them. They have a sign for it — one hand opened, held upright, like a wave that does not fall. It means staying.
