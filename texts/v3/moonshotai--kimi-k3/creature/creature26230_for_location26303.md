Everything that lives in the Still has agreed to the same terms, and the terms are one clause long: *be quiet, or be found.* The agreement was never negotiated. It was selected for, over centuries, by the listeners, and it binds everything here — the herds, the kites, the eels, the machines, the people, arguably the snails. A census of the Still is less a count of bodies than a count of compliances. These are the citizens.

**The keepers**, first, because they would never put themselves first. Call them three hundred. No one has ever counted — a census would require a gathering, and gathering is exactly what the round exists to prevent. Each keeper walks a circuit: a tower, its terraces, its weirs and valves and cisterns, a scrape-and-listen loop that takes a season to complete. The rounds touch only at way-stations, small domed cells where the letters wait — messages scratched with a bronze stylus into blocks of salt, wrapped in hide, left for the next keeper on the circuit. Maintenance notes. Kite reports. Crawler sightings. The weather of the mountain's mood, which fault has been sighing deeper this month. And the long arguments, the ones that advance a paragraph per season: whether the caravans still come for the harvest stacks, or whether the blocks only diminish because keepers take them; whether the Young One was born or built; whether the light the pools give back at night is memory or metabolism. Keepers are terse in light and expansive in salt. The Still's conversation is very slow, and by all evidence very good.

You can tell a keeper by the walk before anything else. It is flat and rolling, one foot always down, no strike anywhere in it — a gait learned in childhood, before speech, because the crust here lies (thin sinter over voids ring like drums, and a ringing step is dangerous twice: it can drop you into scalding water, and it can be heard). The padded staff taps twice, soft, at each stop. That double-tap is a signature. The herds know it. The listeners know it. A keeper's step says *this one belongs to the works* in a language older than the works.

The second tool is not the scraping blade but the listening rod — a length of dense ceramic, pressed to the crust while the keeper kneels with an ear to its top. The stone's mail, they call it. A herd at two kilometers. A crawler finishing its spiral in the east pan. The mountain's own deeper traffic, the slow shifts of pressure in the faults. Apprenticeship is mostly the rod. It takes years to learn to read stone, and the old keepers — barefoot by then, hands gone white and heavy with accreted mineral, knuckles like sinter domes — are said to read it through their soles, to hear the mountain's intentions before the valves do. The young keepers believe this. That is the point of the belief.

Speech is indoors only. On the pans there is the disc: a mirror of polished ox-flank hide on a ceramic frame, good for perhaps forty signs — *received, listener-moving, come-help, all-clear, rain forming on the rim,* and one sign that means simply *here*, flashed at no one in particular, at a herd, at a kite, at the basin itself. A courtesy or a prayer; the letters disagree. Against the kites' grammar, forty signs is a phrasebook, but it covers the essentials, and the essentials here are few.

Indoors — in the cells scraped into the terrace edges, doorways low so that you enter bent and unhurried — a keeper may speak, may hum, may cook. Cooking is done as the basin does everything: from below, in steam, through a box fed from the tower's bleed lines. The one agriculture is the golden strain of the weft, coaxed onto ceramic tiles in runnels of the right warmth, scraped and dried into cakes that taste of iron and something almost sweet. Eels, steamed. Salt in eleven grades, and salt-blending is the keepers' sole connoisseurship, the thing they judge each other on, gently, across decades of letters. A few cows of the tower herds are milk-tame, but that is an individual negotiation conducted over years, and the cow has the deciding vote.

Children are the problem the whole culture is shaped around, because children are born loud, and loud things are found. So the first years are spent on the under-round: the deep galleries beneath the terraces, down among the old ceramic mains, where the warm dark forgives a scream and the porous stone eats every sound. A keeper's first word is whispered. Their second education is the disc. And then comes the walking — a measured kilometer of open pan, in daylight, while the parents watch not the child but the nearest herd. The herd is the examiner. If the oxen graze on, undisturbed, the child has passed. If a single flank tilts, they go back down below for another year.

There is a procedure for a keeper who begins to sing. It is not written anywhere. It is quiet.

Death, when it comes on the round, announces itself as backlog: an unscraped weir, a channel going white, followed upstream to the body. The dead are laid in the dead-run, a channel where the water is richest, and the water does the rest — a dome in a year, a terrace with a person's length in it within a century, the name carved once on the crust. A keeper is counted as a resident of the Still until the stone closes over them. This is the only census the Still keeps, and by it the population is much larger than three hundred.

**The salt-oxen** are born matte, coatless of any shine, and the mirror comes with years — a mineral oil in the sweat, burnished on the machine-domes they seek out to rub against, scratching themselves on the buried heartbeat of the pistons. Adolescents practice the semaphore too eagerly, flashing at everything, and the herd corrects them the way the herd corrects everything: by edging them outward, toward the rim of the herd, where the listeners hunt. The old mirrors, too bright to dim, are edged out in their turn. So a herd's geometry is an information structure and a sacrifice at once — dull center, bright rim, cows and calves held in the quiet middle like a pupil in an eye. Sentinels lie flat with their chins to the crust, reading the stone exactly as a listener does, the hunted borrowing the hunter's sense. The grammar has more words than the keepers' disc: the long lateral sweep that turns the herd's grazing, the double-flash for a fresh weft-bloom, the alarm stutter, and the command that has no flash at all, the one passed body to body through the herd — *become geology.* Calves are trained to drop mid-step. And in the warm afternoon rain, when wet flanks won't flash and the listeners lie deafened and sullen with their jaws muffled in their own forelimbs, the herds spread loose and easy across the pans, and the oxen lift their heads into the hiss of it, and this is the closest thing to joy the Still permits its largest citizens.

The tower herds shadow the keepers' rounds at a polite distance, waiting for the chisel — fresh-cut sinter is salt-rich, and the oxen lick the new scrapings like gulls following a plow, silently. A keeper walking inside a tower herd is wearing an early-warning array of mirrors, and knows it, and pays for it in fresh cuts.

**The kites** hold the ceiling at noon and the towers at dusk, furled along the condenser fins like closed umbrellas, or roosting on the warm machine-domes for the heat that seeps up through the crust. Bronze-backed above. And below — the wing-undersides are patina-green, the chemistry of bronze, the basin's only leaf, repeated a thousandfold, and it flies. You can tell the week by the kites: they know the settling-pond rotation better than any ledger, and they gather at a draining pond for the stranded eels, which are theirs by ancient right. Their grammar runs to a thousand signs, because they see what nothing else can — all eleven listeners at once, the state of the flume, rain forming on the rim an hour before it falls. When a kite flashes a keeper and the keeper's disc tilts *received*, it is a correspondence between a phrasebook and a library. Some kites attach themselves to a particular keeper's round and fly point for years, paid in weft-scrapings left out on certain ledges. No one knows how to apply for this. The kites choose.

**The listeners** are eleven, currently. The keepers track them by rod and kite-report and know each as an individual: the big female of the north pans; the old male with the dragging left hind, nearly geological now, forgotten by hurry; the young one pressing at its dam's range, fast and stupid, which is the dangerous kind. They are the basin's one non-mirror — matte, ash-dark, poured low against the ground — and at noon a listener is a moving patch of shade that casts no shadow of its own. They take the loud: the flashing adolescents, the lame whose stumble drums the crust, the calves that cry. The semaphore exists because of them. Every quiet thing in this basin was taught by the same teacher.

And once in a few years, at night, a listener drums. Jaw to the crust, subsonic, a slow pattern that carries across the whole basin through the stone — mating or territory, no one asks. It is the one loudness the Still permits, perhaps because nothing can prevent it. The herds stand frozen until dawn. The keepers lie in their cells with their palms flat to the floor and feel the Still's other conversation, the one not meant for them, and in the morning the letters at the way-stations record it in a single sign.

After a kill, the keepers glean. Every boot-sole, every disc-mirror, every blade-grip in the Still is listener-chosen leather. Nobody says this aloud. Nobody says anything aloud. It is not a treaty; it is an ecology.

**The crawlers** are citizens on a continuum that runs from walking, to slowing, to domed-but-still-ticking, to dead — and a crawler is dead only when the stone stops ticking. The keepers check the domes with the rod on every round. There is a dome in the east pans that has been dying for sixty years and is attended like an elder. The walkers carry names, carved once into the ceramic of their shells, and they carry the crystal eye that reads light: flash a request at a crawler — *this pan needs your spiral* — and it will consider for an hour, and then adjust. The semaphore may be theirs originally. The keepers may have borrowed the alphabet of the machines, or the machines of the herds; the letters argue. And then there is the Young One, whose shell is visibly newer than any other, two hundred years old, proof of something no one can name. If domes bud children, the budding takes longer than a keeper's life to witness. Whatever is happening inside the oldest domes, the crawlers are in no hurry to settle the question. They pause for herds with that fluid courtesy, and a crawler crossing your path is luck: it means this pan is healthy, worth a machine's patience.

**The small lives** fill the rest. The whisper-crabs, pale and quick inside the conduits, which keepers seed from pipe to pipe in wet gourds, because their grazing slows the sealing — press your hand to a warm conduit and feel that dry faint whisper: the sound of a pipe not dying yet. The eyeless eels, milk-pale, migrating through the plumbing itself, so that you open a valve-box and sometimes an eel is there, tasting the question of you — put it back deep. At night they rise in the glowing pools and write slow trails through the weft-light. The snails, whose shells are pure accretion, the water's own animals, the basin learning to walk; keepers read their density as a gauge of a pool's mineral load. And the valve-lizards, no longer than a finger, pale, living on warm bronze and drinking condensation — a valve-house with a resident lizard is considered well-kept, because lizards leave a house whose water has gone off. The keepers' canaries, though no keeper has ever seen a canary.

At night the weft makes its light — the cooling strains phosphoresce faint blue-green, and the channels redraw themselves in the dark, the Still's whole wiring diagram laid out for anyone flying. But the kites are furled, and the herds stand asleep with dulled flanks, semaphore dead, sentinels' chins to the stone. A keeper on the night round walks lamp-free by weft-light, checking valves by hand, counting the sighs. A crawler crosses the glowing channels like a slow eclipse, its crystal eye reading the map. A listener flows across a pan like a shadow being pulled on a string. If the drums come, everything stops and feels them.

Count the citizens from the rim, then, by their effects — the flash-trails, the sighs, the tremors, the green line-work — and the count will come out wrong, because the Still counts among its living the machines, the mountain, and the dead not yet sealed in stone. With the tremor coming up through your boot soles, you will find it hard to argue the roster. In every way-station ledger, at the end of every round, in every hand, the entry is the same two words, the Still's entire literature and its sufficient one: *quiet kept.*
