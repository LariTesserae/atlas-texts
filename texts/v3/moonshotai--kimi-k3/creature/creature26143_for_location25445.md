The whole census of the Lower Works would fit on one side of a slate. It takes a long time to compile all the same, because nothing here introduces itself quickly, and it takes longer than that to believe, because the list is wrong in one respect: the largest resident is not on it.

But begin where a census begins, with the animals, and begin with the strangest fact of the place: the water itself is empty. In sixty feet of warm water, nothing swims in the open column but you. The life of the pit is written on its surfaces — on brick and rind and pipe and roofline — and you, hanging in the middle of the volume like a mistake the place has decided to keep, are the largest animal in the Lower Works, and the least regarded.

The pit loach come first, because they are the only things here that can be said to hurry, and they only hurry once. Blind, white, finger-length, eyes gone to the memory of eyes — they read the world through their flanks, and the world they read is mostly the Crank. Its slow flex is their weather. The press is their thunder: the one event in a soundless world, felt through water and bone, and when it cycles, every loach in the town flinches at once, culvert to cellar, a whole population marked by a single low note. Then they settle, and the long listening resumes. They graze the vent mats. They keep the cottages — every flooded parlor holds its two or three, hanging over the cold hearth like the memory of cats. And the boots by the doors are occupied. Look closely: a white tail tucked into a steel toe, a pale nose in the dark of a bootleg. The town tidied its people away, and the loach moved into what they left, the way water does. On some stroke of the Crank's long cycle — no one has timed which, and the clocks are no help — they spawn, and for an hour the culverts carry a second snow, one that settles.

On the rind itself live the grazers: rind-snails, their shells grown of the same pale accretion they creep across, so that the young are distinguishable from the wall only by their motion, and the old not even by that. Age here is incorporation. The eldest are half-welded, grazing the same arm's-length circle year after year, alive inside a mounting shell, residents becoming fixtures in the smallest possible steps. This is the town's one law of tenure, and it applies to you. Stand still on a roofline for the length of a long thought and your boot-soles come away with a pale grip on them, a first fingernail of rind — the Lower Works noting that you stayed.

The pasture, by number, holds the great majority of the town's animals, and they are almost too small to count as inhabitants: brine-fleas, white commas stippling the orange and white mats of the vent field, grazing the chemistry itself. The breath moves them the way it combs the kelp — twice a day the pasture's small herds lift in a faint drift and settle again, a slow weather of their own, a population the town breathes in and out without ever once carrying them anywhere.

Then there is the Gaffer. The crews had a name for unhurried seniority, and it would still fit, so use it. The ancient crab of the square — dinner-plate span, a garden of rind on his back stratified like the town's own geology, one tuft of young kelp rooted there that will never knot anything — makes his rounds over days: gate tower to mill hall to the vent fence and home by the breath-street, an inspection with no clipboard and no end. He does not hurry, because nothing here has ever once required him to. He does not avoid you. He routes around you as he routes around a stanchion: surveyed, filed, forgotten. If there are younger crabs they keep to the outer culverts; the square is his. And when he crosses pipework, his footfalls go into the iron. Lay a palm on any rail and you can follow him across town — tick... tick... tick — the only telegraph in the Lower Works, a one-word message patiently repeated: here. Here. Here. More often than chance allows, he is somewhere in the mill hall when the press cycles. The crews would have said: of course. He is on shift.

The light-islands are inhabited too. The green beards on the living lamps are themselves alive, a filament-growth that eats light and gives it back softened, and inside each beard lives a parish — pale isopods like grains of rice, tending the strands, clearing the dead filaments, keeping the garden trimmed to its brightest shape. Study the ledgers of the place: every dark fixture is bare, grey lace; every burning lamp is bearded. Whether the grid keeps the gardens or the gardens keep the grid is the kind of question the Lower Works declines to answer, but the correlation is perfect, and all the light that reaches you has already passed through a keeping.

The kelp is the one resident nobody feeds and nobody eats — too tough, too bitter with minerals. It anchors the rooflines, combed permanently sideways by the breath, and it grows toward the one thing in town that moves on a schedule, a finger's width a season. This makes it the town's only honest clock. Every timepiece down here lies in its own slow way, all behind, none agreeing; but measure the gap between the longest frond and the beam's lowest swing and you have read the century. The kelp is not malicious. It is simply the only inhabitant whose work will ever be finished.

Behind the cottages, the allotment keeps its rows, and once in a long while — on no cycle anyone has caught — the sea-grass puts up a single white bloom, and the loach come in from the culverts for it. It is the closest thing the Lower Works has to a season. The closest thing it has to a service is at the tower. The clock there still climbs toward its hours — slow, like all of them, behind, like all of them, by its own private amount — and when the hand arrives, the bell does what a bell does, and the brine does what brine does. No note. But the intention of the bell goes into the water as pressure, the smallest of flexes, and the loach feel it. On the tower's wrong hour they gather at its foot — a dozen, two dozen, a crowd by the standards of the pit — hanging head-up in the dark like pale commas, receiving in their bones the only voice in the Lower Works that still tries to speak.

There is even a shore, though it lies at the ceiling. What falls from the air-world — autumn leaves, seed-fluff, once, by all report, a feather — sinks through the clear layer and rests on the seam, which from below is a glass shelf, and small pale seam-lice graze the fallen sky there, upside down at the roof of the world. Nothing with wings lives here; the air-world's only contributions fall. This is the town's sole import. Everything else it makes itself, or remembers.

A census, though, is a list of the living, and this one is incomplete — because every entry on it has something in common, and the common thing is not listed. Follow the food. The mats eat the boreholes' fever; the fleas and the loach eat the mats; the filaments eat the grid; the seam-lice eat what the sky drops, the one item in the ledger the works did not provide. Every other chain ends in a machine. The sun of this world is the Crank; its daylight is the lamp grid; its weather is the breath. The water drowned the town, and the town ate the water, and everything alive down here eats the town. Which raises the question a census is not supposed to ask: where does the list stop?

The cautious answer is that the works is not alive, only maintained — and then you must say by whom. Nothing has been serviced for a century, and nothing essential has failed. The rind takes every still surface, a fingernail a year — but the cylinder is clean, the weathervane swings, the press stamps true, and a few still things are spared as well: the sign under its algae is legible, the boots are not filled in, the beds are still made. The rind grows everywhere except where it would erase. Motion shedding accretion explains the engines. It does not explain the beds. And there is the matter of the lamp — one lamp, for a stranger, burning a little brighter, never the same lamp twice, never while watched. A fault would have a pattern. The gauges' needles twitch when a shadow crosses the coin, or seem to. The Lower Works never announces itself. It only continues — and continuation, down here, is the loudest statement there is.

What lives here, then, lives in the present tense. The rind is all past, the whole history of the town laid down a fingernail at a time. The kelp is all future, the one appointment everything else is keeping. The works is all present, stroke after stroke, the long patient now. The Gaffer's tense is the continuous: rounds that have never ended because they never end. So ask what lives in the Lower Works and the truest answer is not a species but a schedule. Third shift lives here. It was never only the people; it was the arrangement — beam and spawn, press and flinch, bell and congregation, lamp and parish, the old crab on his rounds. The town tidied the people away and kept the house. The house kept the habit. The habit is the householder now.

Stay long enough to be noticed and you are counted — one lamp a little brighter, your presence entered in no ledger, and not as a guest: the Lower Works does not have guests. As staff. Hang in the breath-street long enough and you will have worked a portion of the shift, and when you finally rise — through the warm brine, through the membrane that resists and then lets go, up into the cool clear layer where the coin widens and the ordinary world takes you back — you carry out the single distinction nothing else down there possesses. The loach will spawn on the stroke. The bell will intend. The Gaffer will round the mill hall. The beam will rock over.

You are the only thing in the pit that will ever clock out.
