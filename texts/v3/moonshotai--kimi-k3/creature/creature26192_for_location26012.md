It is not empty. It only seems so, because everything here lives at the wrong scale for you — a micron thick, a century slow — and because every citizen is the color of the architecture. You could walk a week and swear the place was sterile. You would be wrong in both directions: there is more life here than you think, and almost none of it moves. Follow the water, and you will meet them in the order the water meets them.

Begin at the crown, where the water rises. The domes weep from their apexes, and the water that comes up has been in the dark for a thousand years, and it does not come up alone. It carries the dark's few citizens: specks the size of sand grains, translucent, folded, that spent their whole slow lives in the pressed black between deep stones and arrive now at the surface like letters from a country with no light. Most live an hour in the glare. They were not built for noon. But they are part of why the film at each dome's apex grows thickest and brightest — collars of sulfur-gold and rust, as plush as anything a micron thick can be — because the film eats what the spring delivers, the chemistry and the citizens both.

The film itself you think you know by now. You don't; you know its name. It is many kinds wearing one name, zoned by what the water carries: sulfur-yellow where the springs come up, rust-orange where the threads comb over the rims and catch air, black manganese sheen in the still basins, and in the sheltered angles that grey your eye keeps calling green and keeps being wrong about — the cruellest pasture in the world, because nothing here is fooled by it except you. It is the meadow, but it is also the undertaker: nothing rots here. The film licks the dead into itself within weeks, leaving a trail of clean pale stone exactly like a skater's, so that every death becomes indistinguishable from a meal. And it is the mountain's mason as much as the water is. A share of every layer is laid down at its bidding, mineral coaxed out of solution by the chemistry of being alive. Slice any pillar and you cut through ten thousand former surfaces, and every surface was once alive. The banded stone is a stack of grazed pastures. The mountain is not merely inhabited. A good part of the mountain is former inhabitants.

Beneath the film, the stone is inhabited all the way through. Wherever the tufa is porous — and it is porous for square miles — every pore is lined with water, and every film of water is lined with life: animals the length of a hair's breadth, eight-legged drops, wheel-mouthed things that spin a private whirlpool and eat what it brings, thread-things that have never once been outside the rock and would not survive the journey to its surface. Crumble a thumb's worth of sponge-stone into a dish and the dish moves. You have never touched bare mountain here. All this time, you have been walking on the ceiling of the largest city in the world.

Follow a thread down its flute. One thread per groove, and every groove has its tenant: anchored to the stone, spread against the falling water, a hand with too many fingers, each finger barbed with something stickier than the water is fast. They are called, by no one, the comb-fans, and they strain the thread for what the mist traded to the crown — dust from the far lowlands, salt, spores, the film's own castoffs, a young skater now and then. Every thread pays a toll to its tenant. The fan cements itself to the stone in a cuff of calcite, so every rimstone dam is partly reef: run your thumb along a lip and you touch a rank of roofless little towers, mortgaged to the mountain. And they possess the world's only speed. Touch one and it is gone, furled into its cuff in a blink — the only blink there is — and it will not open again today. Even alarm, here, is mostly patience.

In the pools, the opal water is opal because it is alive. The milkiness is partly dissolved stone and partly citizen: a thin soup of swimmers too small to see singly, and among them the seed-shrimp, a grain of sand that opens like a locket and rows itself along in hops, a generation a day. Its world has never once changed shape — the pool sits exactly at its brim, always has — and its sky is a mirror, so when it rises, it rises toward itself, and meeting its own reflection at the ceiling is the only event its life contains. On the floor of every old pool live the only builders: the masons, worms the length of an eyelash, who glue grains of travertine sand into a case and drag it, outgrow it, abandon it, build again. The pool floors are mosaics of their doors — staircases of ever-larger archways, each one empty, each one fusing where it lies. In a world where everything looks made and nothing is, they produce the only true artifacts, and the artifacts are indistinguishable from everything else. The mountain absorbs its only architecture the way it absorbs everything: immediately, and without comment.

And one dam in ten thousand is not a dam. It is called, by no one, the brim: a mouth shaped exactly like a rimstone curl, seated at the outflow, eating whatever the thread carries over — seed-shrimp, fan-spawn, drowned skaters. It has no jaw to snap. Its gate closes at the speed of stone; being eaten here can take most of a week, and what is being eaten has, on the whole, time to accommodate the idea. The brim keeps only the gate clear; the rest of it accretes, and the oldest are nearly entombed — a slit of appetite in a curl of rock. You will never find one by looking. You could find one by statistics: a stretch of rim where the thread runs half a shade clearer, filtered. The only predator in the world is a piece of the architecture with a gate in it, and its camouflage is perfect, because its camouflage is the truth.

The skaters, you know. But watch one long enough and it deepens. The lens of water on its back is not only a reservoir; it is the eye. A droplet is a lens, and they carry their sight the way you carry breath — crude, registering the great fact of noon, the difference between pillar-shadow and glare, and nothing else, because there is nothing else to read. They climb where water climbs: up the capillary blades at the rim, the place's one disobedience, a hand's breadth of mountaineering against the fall. They do not hear the chord — you must be built of ear to hear anything in this place, and only one thing is — they feel it the way the floor feels it, which is to say constantly, which is to say never. They do not so much reproduce as cease to be one thing and become two. And the overflow takes one now and then, and over the rim it frays with the rest of the water: even the droplets that decided to walk are talked out of it on the way down, and whether their mist rises again to the crown is a question the place leaves open.

The mist carries more than water. It carries the film's spores, the fans' spawn, the masons' young — everything small enough to rise. The cloud that leans against the mountain is the pasture's seed-head, and the loop is not only a circuit of water but a circulation of the living, the same few kinds going round and round: up as mist, down as condensation, through the springs, down the flutes, over the rim, up again. Nothing here is from anywhere. Every droplet has been every droplet; every spore is the grandmother of every film. The loop is one body, and these are its cells.

One habitat is empty. Inside the hollow pillars, where the water falls in the dark and the note is made, nothing lives — the boom too great, the fall too sheer, the walls swept clean. The instrument is kept clean. That is how it can be read.

And then there is the listener, which you will not see.

It eats at the tempo of the place. Beneath the flared dish of its head, set like a secret, is a small mouth and a tongue like a ribbon of wet suede, and it bends the dish to the stone and listens before it eats: it can hear the difference between a film growing and a film being eaten, the faint rasp of forty legs and a mouth, audible to the only ear in the world that can subtract the chord. It grazes the gold collars at the crown; it takes skaters the way the place rains, without aiming; it walks so that the film behind it can recover, and its territory of ten thousand pillars is a pasture rotated on a schedule of decades. The mineral finger must be maintained — left alone, the threads would deposit on it, blunt it, grow it, fuse it; it would become a stalactite, then a column, then a location. So it strikes. Every tok is two acts at once: a question asked of the stone, and a whetting of the asking.

When it stands a season in stillness, the flowstone takes its opportunity, and when it moves again the crust gives way with a small crack — not a deliberate sound, but the sound a purpose makes when it gets up. It is inhabited, itself: the mineral garden on its back carries its own film, a skater or two grazes its shoulders, and a listener is the only vehicle in the world, the only place a skater can be that is going anywhere. No one has found a nest; there are no nests. There is, once in a long age, a small one walking a large one's wake — knee-high, its dish too big for it, like all young things, its fingertip still soft, spending its first years growing a hammer and learning the ten thousand notes of the territory it has inherited. The pillars say what they are. Only a listener knows what they were: the mountain remembers itself aloud, but only in the present tense, and the listeners supply the past.

Sometimes a tok is answered. Very far away, after a pause the length of a thought here, another note comes out of the chord — a listener on the far side of a valley of pillars, striking in turn. They go back and forth, a note a day, for a season, and whether this is courtship, gossip, or two instruments tuning to one another, there is no one to say.

When a listener dies, it dies standing — it has been practicing — and the flowstone closes over it in decades, and for the few years while the body is still inside, the new pillar rings wrong. Rings occupied. The others come, one by one, out of their valleys, a step a day, and strike it, and stand with their dishes cupped to the stone, and go. Whether this is mourning or only measurement, there is no one to say. But a pillar that was an animal keeps, ever after, a note slightly apart from its neighbors — a half-step of private music — and the listeners that pass it tap it as they pass, the way you might touch a doorframe in a house you used to live in.

You think the massif changeless because you are brief. To the listener, the chord is talkative: a pillar a quarter-tone flat is a clog beginning; a dome's note brightening over fifty years is a spring in its youth. The place prints its news at the rate of one letter a century, and these are its only readers.

Count them, then: the film that is many kinds in one skin, the cities in the pores, the soup in the pools, the seed-shrimp, the fans, the masons, the brims, the skaters, the risen specks of the deep, and the listener. A dozen kinds of life in the loudest, brightest, wettest place in the world — and not one of them is an exception to the place. Everything here is water on its way to becoming stone. The film lays it down, the fans mortar it, the masons case themselves in it, the brims entomb themselves in it, the skaters are water that briefly walks, the listener is an animal keeping one finger clear of it. Life, here, is the part of the process that, for a while, moves.

And the process keeps its own census, struck one note at a time — once an hour, once a day, somewhere in the white labyrinth, a small clear sound that is neither water nor stone.

Tok.

The roar takes it back. The chord goes on. And everything that lives here goes on living at the pitch of the place: a micron thick, a century slow, and everywhere.
