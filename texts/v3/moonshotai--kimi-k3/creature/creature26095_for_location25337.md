The list of animals here is short enough to memorize and long enough to spend a lifetime failing to confirm. Count them: the grazers, the small lives of the holdfasts, the false leaves, the seam-eaters, the borers, the keepers, the weavers of the gaps, the listeners, the pilgrims, the strangers. Ten kinds, give or take the doubtful entries, spread through several hundred kilometers of the richest water in the world. Nowhere is so full and so empty at once. The fullness is vegetable. The emptiness is animal. And between the two sits the stone, keeping count — because for every creature alive in the province there are a hundred standing in the ghost-groves, and the ratio only ever grows. The water hides its citizens. The rock, given a few decades, confesses them. To describe what lives here is mostly to describe what the diary has caught.

A few rules govern all of them. Nothing bites. There is not a jaw native to the range, not a beak, not a radula with any ambition; the only teeth that have ever entered this water arrived already dead, inside the strangers. Nothing calls. There is no song here, no alarm, no courtship louder than a rasp of skin on glass — the loudest sound any animal makes, and you must be within an arm's length to hear it. Nothing makes its own gas. Buoyancy was the plants' invention, and the animals either steal it, swallow it once, or do without. And everything knows its depth and keeps it — with two exceptions, and the stone has them both in the end.

The water that sets the dead also enters the living. The local fauna builds with it — shells of clear glass, rods of glass, skins stiffened with silica — so that the residents of the province are half-set already, and the line between a living animal and its eventual statue is thinner here than anywhere else in the world. This makes the taxonomy simple, in the end. There are two kinds of creature in the range: the locals, which are glass and angle and clarity, faceted like the architecture they live in; and the strangers, which are soft, and round, and wrong. You can tell a resident from a visitor at a glance, even in the dark, even in stone.

And no living frond has ever been chewed. The forest is sipped, sifted, rasped, robbed of gas, cleaned of its dust — never bitten. That is the whole secret of the thicket's impossible density: it grows unbrowsed, a forest that has never needed a defense, and so it fills the water to the limit of the light.

The lens-grazers are the largest of the residents and the least present. A grazer is less an animal than a decision the water has made about its own tension: a disc of clear tissue, meters across, a rim slightly denser than the middle, a soft hub at the center where the chemistry of eating is concentrated. There are no eyes, no brain. The rim reads the gradient of the spore-snow and steers by it, and the whole lower surface digests whatever the whole body drifts over. They are mostly water arranged to hold its shape. The way to find one is not to look for the grazer at all but for its guests — small motes moving slowly through the lens, passengers that spend their entire lives inside a window that swims. Where the motes wander, there is a grazer. Otherwise there is only a bending, a wrongness in the light, gone as soon as you fix on it.

They ride the slow interior currents of the thicket on circuits of years, and in a spore month they converge — the only gathering of animals this sea ever holds: a congress of almost-nothings in the green weather, the curtains of frond bending around a dozen crossings at once, and even then you would swear the water was empty. When a grazer has sifted enough seasons of snow it thickens at the hub and goes faintly milky — the one time in its life it is easy to see — and then, over the course of a season, it comes quietly apart into two, and each half rounds itself and drifts off smaller and clearer than its parent. Nothing attends. They may live for centuries; there cannot be many more than a few thousand of them in the whole province. And once in a long while one dies — of age, or of a new spire rising through its circuit — and clouds, and sinks, and sets, and the groves take custody of a pale disc lodged among the stone fronds: the rarest shape in the diary, mineral proof that the grazers have been here as long as the forest has.

Step down in scale, and up in number. The holdfast tangles are the province's only cities, and to see their population you must hang motionless in the tangle for an hour and let your eye relearn what size means. There are the glass-snails: shells grown straight out of the water's own mineral, transparent as the rock, creeping the cables and rasping away the first silica film — the bloom's opening bid on a living anchor. The plants, in recompense, exude a thin sugar along the cables to keep them. It is the only garden in the world: tender and tended meeting at the anchor-point, each one delaying the stone in the other. There are the false bladders, which hang among the true ones indistinguishably except by behavior — a bladder that sags a half-degree when it should rise, that drifts slightly wrong against the current. They sip gas through the wax, and a frond will tolerate one or two; more, and the frond sinks out of its tier, and so the thieves keep moving, a few days to a bladder, and every frond carries a slow rotation of passengers like a lantern losing its light by grains. When fronds tear free and raft away, the snails and thieves go with them — the golden meadows are the range's arks, and the tangles of one peak are restocked from the wrecks of another.

And there is the needle. Clear as the snails, still as the stone, the length of a finger. It takes station near a grazing snail and waits — days, weeks, it is in no hurry, nothing here is — and its strike, when it comes, takes an hour from first touch to last, so slow that the snail never once struggles. This is the province's one native weapon and its only predation, invisible at any natural attention span. Everything else in the range is theft and housekeeping.

The rust tier keeps the false leaves: animals as broad and thin as the great leaves they lie upon, crimson to the leaf's crimson, their edges feathered into the cilia that do all their moving — a creep of centimeters a day, a life lived a few leaves at a time. They eat the film that settles onto the wide blades, the dust of the coming stone, and in eating it they delay it; a rust leaf under a lier stays clear of the bloom years longer than a bare one. A leaf lives a century, and a lier outlives its leaf. When the leaf dies and sets, the lier walks — at about the pace the mineral works — off the stone replica and across to a living neighbor. Those that mistime it set with their leaf, and the diary holds the double fossil by the thousand: a stone leaf with a stone lier on it, indistinguishable, a life filed inside its own furniture.

Below the light-line, where the rust gives out, the seams come into their own — the veins of captured noon running down the flanks into the dark. Pressed to the glass along them live the seam-eaters: patchworks of dim amber, hand-sized to room-sized, the only warm color anywhere in the deep. For as long as anyone looked at them they were taken for mineral stains. They are animals, and they eat light — light that has crossed kilometers of rock to reach them, thinned and warmed and slowed by the passage — and their skin is their eye: they see with the same tissue they feed with, and where a seam clouds they creep along it after the brightening, a handspan in a decade. Mark one and return in ten years; it will have moved the width of your mark. They do not shine. They hoard. In a country where the deep rock is never wholly dark, they are the ones holding the door.

In the ghost-groves themselves live the stone-borers, and they are the reason the groves are not what they seem. The replicas are not solid: the bloom seals dead tissue as it sets, and inside the seal the dead keep — mummified frond-flesh, preserved spores, a larder of the past standing tier below tier in the dark. The borers have the key. Blind, pale, slow even by the standards of this place, they dissolve the seal and eat the century inside it, and generations of them pass within a single stone frond. A grove is a genealogy. Once in a great while — perhaps when the range adjusts itself, since the groan is the only calendar the deep animals keep — a grove releases a motile generation: small soft things that drift out into the black to find another ghost-grove kilometers away. Nearly all fail. It does not matter. The mineral makes new groves faster than they can be lost.

What the borers leave behind is emptiness, shaped. A breached chamber, emptied over decades, is a hollow in the stone; a grove of hollows is an instrument. When the long swell leans through the deep water, the played-out groves sound — faint, organ-low, felt in the bone more than heard — the range's second voice, after the groan itself. The chord changes across the centuries as the tunnels extend. Nothing composed it, and nothing hears it, unless the listeners do. And a grove that has been played too long grows weak; an adjustment brings it down at last, and it falls in pale shards onto the black basin floor — the only talus in a country without gravel — and in the shards, for a while, small pale lives make the province's only bottomland, until the bloom takes the rubble too.

The brine lakes have one citizen each, and it does not live in them. It lives under them. The lake-keeper is tuned between two waters — heavier than the sea, lighter than the brine — so that it rests against the boundary the way a fallen leaf rests on a pond, inverted, beneath the mirror. It is broad and dark and very flat, and from below, when it moves, it crosses the mirror the way a weather system crosses a sky. It feels what the lake collects: each crumpled bladder that sinks to the halocline lands on the keeper's world like something dropped on a roof, and it goes to the sound, and eats. A keeper may be centuries old and broad as a room; the mirror-still cleanness of the lakes is its life's work. When a rim overtops and the fall begins, the keeper's world tilts and starts to drain, and the keeper goes with it — down the rope of heavy water, around the ridges, a descent of a year, the only journey it will ever make. Some do not make it. The caldera rims hold their silhouettes in stone, one per overflow, each with its position for a date.

In the blue gaps between the ridges hang the weavers — one to a cubic kilometer, perhaps fewer. A weaver steals its lift, a bladder siphoned dry from some drifting frond and kept like a loan, and below the stolen bubble it pays out a sheet of near-invisible mucus into the open water. The sheet catches the seeds on their decade errands. When it is full — which takes decades — the weaver reels it in over a season and eats it entire, web and catch together, and casts again. The gaps are not empty. They are just sparse past believing.

The rarest resident is all ear. The listeners are born in the fluted groves, the one voiced place in the province, and they grow there a rod of their own glass, tuned across decades to the irregular ticking of cooling rock — the sound a house makes, settling, at geological tempo. Then they wait. A listener may wait its whole life in the dark flanks and never hear anything but the groans. But when a new spire begins to split the deep rock, the ticking carries for kilometers through the clean glass, and a listener that hears it does the one thing no other animal here does: it goes down on purpose, against the light, toward the sound. The journey takes years. It finds the crest still buried in darkness, still rising a handspan in a good year, and it settles on the summit-to-be and grazes the first mineral bloom and rides — up through the black root, up through the tiers of stone ghosts — a slow elevator toward a ceiling it will never see. When the crest breaks at last into the rust light, the spores are already drifting down out of the thicket, and the listener's work is finished. It dies there, at the light-line, and sets, and the forest founds itself around the small stone figure. Under every living crown, at the depth where the light stood when the peak first rose into it, there is one. The diary of every mountain opens with the same sentence: something heard me, and came, and the forest came after. Where the listeners came from is the open question of the province — nothing here could have taught them to hear cooling glass; the skill is older than the mountains. They brought it like a seed, from some older country long since gone round and gray and sunk. When this range in its turn is finished, the listeners will already be gone, swimming the abyss toward some newer ticking. The midwives of mountains. Always early.

And there is one more kind, and it is the hardest to verify, and the most native to the place. The pilgrims are known mostly from their traces — shed skins on the floating bladders, a rain of young on storm evenings, and a ladder of statues in the groves. What the traces say is this. The species touches the sky twice in a life: at the beginning and at the end, and between the two touches lies the whole depth of the world. The young are released at the ceiling, into the dimpling rain, and each one takes its single breath there — the only breath any animal takes in this sea — and holds it for a hundred years. For a few hours they ride the thin freshwater skin and are freshwater creatures, the only ones the province has; then the skin mixes away and they begin to fall, down through the gold, through the green snow, through the rust, into the root-dark — the only animals that ever see the whole column, and they see it falling, as children. They settle in the basins and graze the forest's slow shed for decades. Then something turns them, and they start back up. The climb takes a century. They feed no more; the climb is also the gestation. Their compass is the brightening, and they carry the tier-colors with them as they rise — dark, then rust, then green, then gold, like altitudes passed — and at the ceiling, for an animal of the green dusk, the light is not bright but absolute, white, the whole sense filled. They push through among the bladder-backs, into the warm rain, and let go of the young, and let go of everything. The fall back takes hours. The climb took a life. The groves hold the ones the climb used up, clinging mid-grip at every depth — the living fronds they clung to set around them as the forest climbed — and the diary distinguishes success from failure by posture: the failures cling; the successful lie flat on the ledges, where they settled after the long fall. The pilgrims are the only animals in the province with a destination. Everything else has an address.

Finally, the strangers. They come up out of the deep basins — or across them, from the old deep beyond the province — in the years after the range adjusts itself, as if the groan were a door left open, or a shaking that knocks something loose in the dark. They arrive dying, or die on arrival. Nothing has ever seen one alive in the light; nothing sees them at all. What remains is the cabinet: soft anatomy rendered faithfully by the bloom — a ribbon-thing with a spine of stone bubbles; a many-armed thing with a beak, the only true jaws this water has ever held, dead on arrival like all the rest; a body that seems to have been mostly an eye, the dome of it hollow where the soft tissue failed first. Each is the first of its kind found and the last sought. The forest grows over the oldest; more than one halo is anchored partly on a leviathan. The diary files them as an appendix of visitors, under mystery.

This is the whole census — ten kinds, give or take the doubtful entries, for the bestiary has a few: shapes in the stone that may be animals and may be accidents of the bloom, and the diary does not say. Nothing here evolved here; everything arrived, and is becoming. In the evenings, when the amber goes out of the seams by degrees and the seam-eaters creep a fraction closer to what remains of it, when some played-out grove far below is sounding under the swell and a lens-grazer bends the last green light and the light closes behind it — then the two facts of the place sit side by side and do not resolve: a sea with almost no one in it, and an archive crowded with everyone who has ever been here. And somewhere below the light, even now, a spire is rising through the dark with one small rider on its crest. The bestiary's next page is already being written. It will be legible in a century.
