Nobody takes a census of the Anvil. You tune it.

You stand in the breath-fog or on the rim or in the lee of a valvewood and you listen until the chord separates into voices, and every voice is a heat contract being honored in real time. That is the population count. Nothing here is counted in bodies — bodies freeze, bodies are interchangeable, bodies are teeth on a gear — but contracts persist, and you can hear each one: the knock, the note, the hum, the tick, and underneath them all the small high descant that nobody believes in until they've spent a season in the fog. Every living thing on the plateau has chosen one of three strategies, and the whole bestiary is just the elaboration of the choice: walk, wait, or tend the warm dark.

## The Mill-Herds

The herd is not a species. It is a guild that has phase-locked itself across species, the way the valvewoods lock across a hillside, and for the same reason: a rhythm shared is a cost divided.

The broadbacks are the ones you will remember, because they are the ones the world rides. Slab-sided, six-legged or eight depending on the lineage, tall enough that their backs make their own weather. And here is the thing the rim can't show you: an old broadback bull carries a garden. The blackfelt grows on him, seeded by the gauze, watered by the melt-halo his own body maintains against the cold, kept warm from below by the rumen, and on a bull of sixty circuits the back-garden is a century deep and as tended as any orchard — because it is tended, and the tenders are not the bull.

The rumen is the other thing. The herds eat the standing black hay of the previous day, and the felt's banked, flameless burn does not stop in the stomach — it continues, stoked, a compost-furnace the animal carries in its belly. The herds do not digest so much as they stoke. That is why the breath-fog walks with them, why milk comes at blood heat, why the column is warm to lean against. The herds are the warm side of the world, and they move.

The bellheads carry the note. Their skulls are mostly resonating chamber, alloyed heavy from a lifetime of bloom-fruit, and the single sustained call that maps the world in echo comes off them the way sound comes off a bell — and when a bellhead falls, the note does not fall with her. Another skull takes up the pitch mid-breath, and in all the years anyone has kept count, the note has never once been dropped. The count is kept, as it happens, by the riders.

Calves are born walking. A calf has perhaps a heartbeat to find its legs, because the column does not pause — pausing is a thing the column has never done — and what fails to rise is gone behind the front within the hour, given to the gauze. The dung, by contrast, is wealth dropped at walking pace: warm, mineral, instantly colonized, a brief town of brightlings and felt-spore that freezes into a cobble and is finished, deferred, at next year's thaw. Nothing is wasted on the Anvil. Some things are postponed.

The herds pay their way in dung and hoof-work — their bloom-metal hooves fracture the young basalt exactly as the valvewood roots want it fractured — and the trees pay wages in fruit. The herds crack the low blooms in passing and ring the high ones with a horn, testing ripeness by pitch. Ripe metal sings true. The herds know the difference. So do the riders, who are listening for it.

## The Keeps

They are small, black, long-fingered, built like the answer to a cold that never relents, and they have lived their entire existence on the backs of the mill-herds since before the song they keep was finished being learned.

Call them keeps, because keeping is what they do. They keep the gardens on the bulls' backs, weeding the felt, sowing it, harvesting it against the lean stretches of the route. They keep the herds groomed — picking rime from lashes and hoof-clefts, combing out the static before a broadback overcharges and spooks, cracking the high blooms the herds can't reach and dropping half, always half, because that is the contract. They keep the count of the still-plumes on the dusk side, watching from their height the way sailors watch a coast. And they keep the Long Song, which is the map of the world written in echo, sung against the herd-note, corrected every circuit, forty thousand kilometers of terrain held in verses that no single keep knows all of. Each lineage keeps its own stretch. Where columns converge at the passes, the verses change hands and are harmonized, and the song settles the way the valvewoods settle — drift, syncopation, lock. It is the only parliament the Anvil has ever needed.

They are paid in warmth, in milk, in transport, and in the fog itself — because the breath-fog is the one soft-lit place on the plateau, the only room where the light does not draw blood, and the keeps have the only functional eyes you will find up here. They can see perhaps a body-length. It is enough to groom by, enough to mother by. For everything else they do what everything does: they listen.

A keep owns a skull or two, the song, the contract, and nothing else. The skulls are the dead. When a keep dies, the family changes pitch — a particular interval, reserved, never sung otherwise — and the stripping caste comes up out of the gauze like weather turning, and the funeral takes minutes, and the skull comes back clean and tuned, alloyed, resonant. It is struck once a year, on the date, and it answers, and the dead keep their verses that way. A carried skull is the closest thing to an artifact on the Anvil, and the keeps would be offended by the word, if they had it. Nothing is made. Some things are kept. The distinction is load-bearing.

They plant, too — valvewood seedlings, tucked into hoof-fractured basalt along the route a century ahead of the harvest. No keep who plants a tree will hear it knock. Their grandchildren's grandchildren will assay its fruit by ring. This is not altruism. It is accounting, done at the only scale the Anvil respects.

There is one sin in the fog, and it is falling. It is not punished. It is simply that the world continues without you at three kilometers an hour, and the noonthings are back there, and the stills are back there, and the note gets quieter, and that is all.

If you are asking whether the keeps are people, stand in the fog at a confluence when the verses change hands — ten thousand voices locking into a map of everything the world has done since last year — and ask again.

## The Answerer

There is exactly one thing the Long Song refuses to describe.

The keeps teach it to children as a rule, not a story: in the fog, count voices. Know who is near you. If the count comes out wrong — if something answers from the white in the voice of someone missing, on pitch, half a breath late — hold your verse and hold your breath and do not answer back.

It is never seen. Seeing is not how it works. The skull-records count the taken every circuit, and the number is small, and it is never zero. The song contains everything the world has done and one thing it does, and the difference between those two tenses is a silence shaped like a door.

## The Tenders

You already know the shape of them — the darting small warm things of the valve-ports — but you should know about the codes.

Every valvewood has a rhythm signature, inherited from the phase-lock of its hillside, as individual as a voice. The tender family that keeps a tree holds that rhythm, and the tree opens its intake port to the correct knock and to nothing else. Inside the port it is sap-warm, resin-sweet, and the work is precise: rime picked from the valve seals with grown-metal teeth, the ring of lignified tissue kept clean enough to close tight, because a seal that leaks is a chamber that bursts, and a burst chamber is a stage of the pump lost. The wage is sap-warmth and the resin oils they groom themselves slick with, and it is paid fairly, and the trees are exacting employers.

Codes are inherited. A tender line that loses its tree's rhythm — through bad luck, bad memory, a generation that listened poorly — is locked out of its own house, and the trees do not forgive, because the trees do not punish; they only keep the schedule. The locked-out line goes to the gleaners or dies in the bark, and the port it kept is found within a season by a younger family that has spent a generation listening through the wood, learning the rhythm note by note, waiting for the vacancy. Every port on the Anvil is kept by someone. There is no unemployment in the trees. There is only succession.

## The Gauze

The sky has castes.

The crown-drinkers hang in the steam-condensate at the valvewood crowns, sipping the trees' exhaled water, paying in pollen carried crown to crown on feet and wing-margins. The dusters work the blackfelt fields all through the long day, low and thick, turning the light grainy at ankle height. The strippers are the ones with manners: they take only what has stopped. Mostly. A calf that fails to rise, a noonthing spent at dusk, a keep sung up out of the fog — minutes, all of it, and the bones left polished. The keeps forgive them. Mostly.

The gauze has no citizens. It is weather with appetites, and it pays its way, and when dusk passes over a valley the gauze folds where it is, mid-wingbeat almost, and the ground behind the dusk is crusted a finger deep in frozen wings — a snowfall of them. At dawn the thaw-crackle of a trillion wings rejoining the air is part of the startup artillery, under the boom of the ice breaking in the chambers, and the hum that rises after is the bass note of the world returning.

## The Noonthings and the Brightlings

Everything else on the Anvil ambushes, waits, or keeps a schedule. The noonthings chase.

They are the pursuit predators of the Walking Morning — long-limbed, hot-blooded, spendthrift, the only open runners in a world of patience — and their whole life is one day. One day, here, is most of a year, so do not call it brief. They hatch from eggs laid at the dusk edge, eggs that froze solid and waited, and they hatch on time, because the morning is a schedule and the morning returns to the very spot where they were laid. They rise in the thaw line already inside the band, and they run it: hunting the herd's stragglers in flat chases across the ticking scree, growing, mating on the move, burning their whole inheritance at full flame. The spent drift back through the band as their pace fails, mate once more in the last of the warmth, lay in the lee of something dark, and the dark takes them mid-stride. Nothing in the band dies of age. It dies of pace.

Below them, in the hoofprint ponds and the melt-rings around every warm body, live the brightlings: generations between a footprint's filling and its refreeze, an antiquity measured in minutes. Most lives ever lived on the Anvil have been brightling lives, and most of those were lived in the footprint of something that never noticed. Do not tell them. Caring is slow, and they have no time for it.

## The Gleaners

At the dusk edge, in the failing weeks of the light, the gleaners work against the dark.

They take from the spent noonthings and the stripped bones and the last of the felt-hay, and they cache it — in crevices, in bone-drifts, in any pocket the coming night will freeze solid, because the night is a larder if you stock it first. Then a gleaner dens beside its hoard and lets the dark have it, and thaws next dawn next to a stocked freezer, and eats at dawn without working, which no other creature on the day-side of the world can claim.

Each gleaner keeps its cache-map the way the keeps keep the world: in song, thousands of sites in order, sung once a year, at dusk, alone, at speed, as the light fails — a solo performed against a deadline of ice. They share nothing. The herds invest in motion and the gleaners invest in time, and both beat the dark, but only one of them ever has to sprint.

## The Stills

You know what they do. What you should know is the shape of the life.

A still is born warm. The litters come in the herd season, and the pups run hot — the only time in its life a still is visible on warmth — and the whole of a still's long career is a cooling. Skill is thermal transparency. Age is approach. The eldest stills run within a degree of ambient, lie in the herd's path for weeks undetectable, and drink the heat out of whatever touches them — and they are dying of their own excellence, because a still that reaches perfect ambient has no difference left between itself and the night to leverage into waking. The coldest still is one meal from being a stone. The plume of steam it raises after feeding, dumping the stolen warmth back into the dusk, is therefore two things at once: the most feared sight on the plateau, and a confession that the animal is not finished yet. The keeps count plumes from the herd like sailors counting lighthouses, except that a plume says the opposite of safe.

And stills are silent — the only silence in a world built of sound. You do not hear a still. You hear where the world stops. The chord goes around them the way water goes around a reef, and experienced ears navigate by the gap.

They take the late, the old, the slow — the pace-failures, always. They are not cruel. They are the part of the law that walks.

## The Warm Dark

The rule says: walk with the morning, or not at all. The rule has a footnote, and the footnote is underneath.

Deep in the old felt-beds, where centuries of unrotted mat have compressed into coke, the banked smolder never stops — not for dusk, not for the months of night, not ever — and a whole economy runs on that gradient in the dark. Coke-worms graze the char. Ash-mites work the crumbs. The stoker-beasts, slow and broad and patient as the smolder itself, tend the draft-chimneys that keep the beds breathing, and they are paid in the only currency that matters down there: the wage is the workplace, heat itself, the one warm address on the night side of the world. Soot-swifts — a caste of the gauze that chose the dark generations ago and never looked back — nest in the chimney-throats where the sweet smoke threads rise, and on the frozen night half of the plateau those threads are the only motion, rising like the world's slow breathing.

The keeps' song mentions, once, a slower breathing under the basins that is not the smolder. The verse is very old. No lineage will claim it.

## The Sleepers

Remember, when you count the living, that the living are the minority party.

Most of the Anvil's citizens are, at any given moment, frozen solid in the inventory of the night side: eggs, spores, larvae, folded animals stacked in every crevice, waiting their turn at the thaw. Some wait one night. Some wait eleven dawns, waking once in eleven years into a world that has not changed, eating a season's worth in a day, and folding again. The moving band is a hundred kilometers wide and it is the only place where anything is awake on purpose, and it governs a continent of sleepers the way a spark governs a coal-bed — by passing over it, once a year, and waking what it touches.

## The Dead

Nothing rots, and bones are metal, and so the dead accumulate.

At the passes, at the dusk edges where the stills hunt, along the old routes, the bone-drifts rise: alloyed skulls and bell-chambers and leg-struts, sharp as the day they stopped, never rounded, never worn. The drifts are the only fences, the only shelter, the only architecture the plateau has — tender dens, gauze nurseries, gleaner vaults, all of it mortgaged from the dead. Audit a drift and you read the history of the world in alloy: which species ran which column, when the stills were many, when the noonthings were fast.

The keeps' skull-rows are the smallest drifts, and the only ones that are tended.

---

Stand on the rim now and take the census. Hoof-tick under gauze-hum under valve-knock under the one sustained note — and under the note, if you have learned anything, the small high descant of the keeps, counting the world. The plumes on the dusk side. The snowfall of wings. The smoke-threads rising off the night half like breathing. The chord is the census, and it is never finished, and it has never once been dropped.

Walk, wait, or tend the warm dark. The Anvil offers three ways to be alive, and every one of them is work — and the Morning comes around at three kilometers an hour, forever, to collect.
