Taking the census of the Kettle is slow work, because first you have to decide what *counts*. In most places you count what breathes. Here you count what keeps time. The tenders — when you can get one to speak, which means when you are both standing on the rim in the wind — use a simple test: strike it gently. If it answers, it may be alive. If it keeps the answer going, holds it, builds on it, it *is* alive. And if it refuses all answer, then it is either dead stone or the hush, and the whole practice of the place is learning to tell those two apart.

You think, at first, that the plants are the scenery and the animals the inhabitants. Stay longer.

Each organ-palm is less a tree than a building with tenants. Down in the fused pipes live the tuners — finger-long, soft, pale as the inside of a reed, and you will not see one unless a tender taps a pipe and holds the bore to your ear. They run the flues day and night, eating the settle that cakes the walls and the husks the beetles drop, and in cleaning the pipes they *voice* them. A palm with a full family holds its chord for years; a palm without goes flat, then ailing, its ember-pulses ragged. The tenders keep them the way beekeepers keep bees: by tenancy. A failing palm gets a stoppered gourd of tuners tapped in at the root, and a gourd of tuners, held to the ear, sounds like a far-off chord looking for its instrument. The palms pay their rent in the only currency they have: a palm leans, over seasons, toward its best tenants and its loudest beetles, angling leaves to catch more of the rim's song. The grove is loud because the grove is hungry, but it is *shaped* because it is a landlord.

The small loud, you already know by ear. What you cannot know until you have sat very still through many dim hours is how *governed* it all is. The drum-beetles' territories are not held by strength but by endurance: two beetles claiming one leaf-face will drum until one of them drops the beat. There are no wounds. There are only tempi. The loser walks — there is a landless class of beetles out on the bare ringing-stone, drumming poor stone, waiting for a leaf to fall vacant — and the winner inherits more than the leaf. Each palm species' seed-pods open to a particular rhythm, and that rhythm is its beetle's war-beat, slowed to a ceremonial pace. The battle-cadence and the birth-cadence are the same song at two speeds, and a palm will only open for a drummer who has won. Shake a pod in late season and the true ones rattle *in tempo* — the loose shot striking in phase, somehow, the whole gourd keeping its species' time — while an unripe pod, or one full of larvae learning time in the dark, stumbles. The tender's gourd-rattle is voiced to the true rattle. That is how the harvest is scheduled: you walk the grove shaking things, and you listen for the ones that answer correctly.

The rattle-moths navigate by chord. Each quarter of the bowl has its own slow harmony, the way valleys elsewhere have their own weather, and a moth reads its position off the blend the way you'd read a skyline. Caught out in a lull, a moth simply folds and waits, deaf as a pebble, until the chorus restarts and the world becomes legible again. And once in a great while a rising mass of them will clatter in just the wrong pattern and fire a pod by accident — a false harvest, seed spilled out of season. It is rare. The pods' tempi and the moths' clatter are staggered apart with what looks very much like intention, and the tenders say a staggered clock is a kind of mercy.

The fluters are the Kettle's vernacular. At dawn they go leaf to leaf licking off the settle, then down to the seed-bars for kernel-meat where the snails have been grinding, and all morning they practice. There are dialects: the east-bowl fluters ornament, the fluters near the center sing plain and slow because the standing waves there punish anything fancy. A young fluter learns its quarter's chord the way children elsewhere learn roads. And when a bell-ox tolls, every courting fluter in earshot stitches its highest, frailest thread into the decay of the note — the big sound carries the small one the way a dust-river carries a seed, and it is the only way anything that size gets heard across the bowl. After a lull-alarm, when the panic-drumming finally thins, the fluters do the thing that will stay with you longest: roll-call. A stitched circuit of small notes goes around the grove, each one answered, quarter by quarter. An unanswered name is how the fluters count their dead. Some of them come out of the alarm hoarse, voices frayed to a whisper, and they sing anyway for days — because the rule of the place is that a voice spent is still a voice, and the rule is to spend it.

Even the rivers are partly alive. The hiss has gravel in it, and the gravel clicks. Under the surface run the dust-skates, flat as fallen leaves, leather-sheened to match the channels, eating whatever the braids carry that is past its usefulness — seeds too old to sprout, beetle dead, moth husks. When a tremor jumps a river from its bed the skates strand, and a beached skate has to click itself back across dry stone to the water-that-isn't, working like a heart and sounding like a slow castanet. At the downstream bars the barsnails do the grinding, spiring shells of accreted dust, opening the seed-coats the tumbling has only cracked. A seed needs the river, then the snail, then the bar. Old tenders can hear which braid is running hungry — carrying few seeds — because a hungry braid hisses *high*. They read the irrigation the way farmers elsewhere read clouds.

Then there are the seven.

You will be told their names before you ever see one: the tenders name them by interval and will correct you, gently, if you misname one in the dark. The Root, whose toll you feel in your teeth. The Third. The Fifth, who answers storms first. The high young Seventh, whose note is still brightening year by year as its skull flares. They graze the bellows-shrubs and fallen drum-fronds, and you can hear an ox *eat* — the jaw-click like a knocker, and beneath it, when one is content, a gut-drone like a drum in another room. In the cold dim hours the fluters crowd into the flares of their skulls for the warmth, and when the ox tolls it launches them like shot from a pod; they come back scolding and settle again, and the next toll does it again, and nobody on either side seems to consider this a problem.

No one has ever seen a bell-ox born. The herd is simply seven, always seven — you will never count eight, and you will not count six for long. What you may see, if you stay long enough, is one dying, and it is unmistakable: the tolls space wider and wider across a season, the ox going down the scale of its own silence, until one dim morning it is standing in the lee of the great boulder and is done. The herd tolls all that day, out of sequence — the same fury as the lull-alarm, but slow, and no one who has heard both confuses them. The skull goes to the chapel at the center and sits there with its flare outward, and sitting in its field is the deepest quiet in the quietest room in the world. The tenders do not call it scripture, but they also do not call it anything else.

Because here is what the oxen are *for*, and grazing is only what they do between work. Their work is the fence.

If you are lucky, or not, you will be there for a walking-out. The hush comes over the rim in the still season like fog deciding to have a purpose, and the grove goes out leaf by leaf ahead of it — no panic, that is the terrible part; the lull-alarm is for the silence that *threatens*, but for the silence that *arrives* there is no alarm, only the small shutting-down, like lamps going out in a house that has decided to save oil. And then the oxen move. Unhurried. They space themselves around the dead patch and begin to toll *in relay* — each note landing exactly as the one before decays, so that there is never a gap, never a breath of unguarded edge — and the dead patch shrinks. It cannot cross the sustained pure tone, so it yields, and the oxen walk it across the bowl the way you'd walk a cow to a gate, toward the one place in the rim where no tuning-stone stands: the wide gap between the ninth stone and the first, where there is only a spoon-worn socket and the wind makes no note at all. The tenders call it the Rest. The drums come out of the hut then, every one of them, graduated sizes planted in the gap, and the tender plays a fence where the stones left none — with a door in the middle of it. The oxen walk the hush to the door. It goes through, out onto the Long Quiet where it belongs, and the door is drummed shut behind it. Seven bells, the tenders say, and the belfry makes eight. Everything in the Kettle is a fence, a crop, or the weather in the field. The oxen are the fence.

Below the loud nation lives the quiet one, and it is larger than you'd think. The copper lichen eats the settle where the settle lies longest — the sound-shadows, the lee of boulders, the wakes where the hush has passed — and in its felt live the Excused: hare-sized, felt-pelted, copper-dulled, and wholly, blessedly voiceless. No alarm, no courtship song, no thump. They find one another by touch and by scent and they are the only citizens of the Kettle exempt from the tax of voice — and therefore the only ones the hush has no hold on, because it drinks voices and they carry none. Nothing hunts them; everything here hunts by ear. They den at the chapel's fringe, and a tender resting their ringing ears in the managed silence will often have an Excused or two beside them, keeping the quiet the way two people keep a fire. The tenders do not pet them. You do not pet a neighbor.

And then there is the sexton. It follows the hush at a fixed distance — you can locate the hush, some seasons, by watching the sextons converge — and it takes apart whatever the silence has starved: the muted beetles, the fluters who will not sing again, the slack pale leaves. It makes exactly one sound in its life, a single small tick when it finds the dead, and hearing that tick from under a boulder means a death has been registered and the count of the chorus amended. Its burrows are lined with beetle-casings that click when the wind is right. An archive. Nothing in the Kettle is wasted; even the voices of the eaten go on the shelves.

Of the hush itself, the tenders have three theories and no fear. Some hold that it is the Long Quiet's own creature, sent in like a tax-collector to foreclose on the one loud acre in all that white immensity. Some hold it is the Kettle's own tiredness given legs, which is why it cannot be killed — only walked home. The oldest keepers called it the Debt: the first silence, the one that was here before the first note, still owed, and every song in the bowl borrowed against it. Inside it — and you may be inside its edge once, briefly — the last sound left is your own hearing straining, a high thin ring that is not in the world and therefore cannot be drunk. That, the tenders say, is the hush's lesson and its limit: it can take every voice but the listener's. It is why the old deaf keepers can work at its edge without panic. They carry their own noise with them, paid out year by year, and it cannot be foreclosed.

Which brings the census to the tenders themselves, the *whoever* of the place, and the strangest entry on the list. The Kettle is kept by one pair of hands at a time. Before the stills, the re-cutting of the stones; in season, the drumming of the pods, species by species, an orchard of beats held entirely in memory and taught hand over hand on the hut drums; after a walking-out, the planting of bar-sprouts into the hush's wake, and tuners re-housed to any palm the silence has flattened. The bright dust drawn out with the lodestone comb is the crop that is not sound — sorted by color into the stoppered pots — and it is how the grove pays for its keeper's water, since what the keeper needs cannot grow here and what the grove gives cannot be drunk. Sometimes the rattle vanishes from the hut and comes back weeks later with new shot in it, and no one will explain this. The tending is larger than the tender by exactly one rattle's worth, and the fellowship of the keepers is a thing you can only infer, like wind from bent grass.

They pay a price and they call it a wage. Ten years in the bowl and your ears ring the tonic day and night forever; the tenders call this *the stipend*, and in old age, when the world goes quiet on them entirely, the stipend is all they hear — paid in full, forever. Then they tend by bone: the figure you may see from the far rim, striking each tuning-stone once, is listening with its forehead and its palms, taking the fence's pulse through the skeleton. They do not speak down in the bowl — speech below is a theft from the chorus — and if you wander in off the Long Quiet they will find you before your second day, because the Kettle can kill a guest and not notice, and not letting it is part of the tending. You will be watered from a skin, fed something from very far away, and led up the spoon-dished steps, and your questions will be answered only on the rim, in the wind, where the stones sing. Some of them, at the end, walk back out into the world to be ordinary and half-deaf in it. Some lie down in the chapel, in the managed silence they spent a life keeping, and in a place without wet the dead keep, and the carpet grows over them politely, the way it grows over everything that holds still in the quiet. The living rest their ears there in the afternoon. The dead keep it always. The carpet is deep for a reason.

No one applies for the work. The Kettle applies to *you*. Every keeper is someone who left once — crossed the Long Quiet, went home, and found the hum installed in their bones, fading by grains and then not fading at all — and came back years later, arriving already in time, tapping their sternum, counting in sevens. The old one knows them on sight. A season of teaching, hand over hand on the drums; and then one figure walks out across the white and one stays, and the one who leaves goes the way a note leaves a bell — gradually, and then entirely.

And the census closes where it has to: on the bowl itself. Because a thing that holds its note for a thousand years is a thing with a body. The warmth that seeps up through the ringing-stone; the rivers that find their ancient beds again within the hour, as if remembered; the toll after the lightning, always the tonic, never drifting though every stone on the rim has drifted flat — the Kettle keeps its pitch the way you keep your name. The tenders are right about it, in the end: everything else — the palms and their tenants, the beetles and their wars, the seven bells, the quiet folk, even the Debt itself — is just what lives in its throat. The grove is not a garden in a crater. It is a choir, and the crater is the chorister, and the keeping of it is simply the long work of helping an enormous singer warm its voice.

There is one more entry, and it is yours. By the time you have walked three days out, you already know: the chord does not stop when you stop hearing it. Your bones keep the time now. Somewhere behind you the bowl pulses like an ember and drums like a heart, and you — tapping your sternum at odd moments, counting things in sevens — are the smallest thing that lives in the Kettle, and the only one that carries it, rattling in time, like a seed.
