There is usually a person in the Green Well.

Not always. The tally panels suggest there have been stretches — decades, once or twice — when the chamber held only its proper census and no one at all. The herders have two phrases for the two states of the world. They say the Well is *kept*, or they say the Well is *open*, the way they say a cistern is full or dry. A condition, not a person. And they say that when it has been open long enough, someone in the villages begins to dream of dripping water, and goes on dreaming it until they walk to the saddle.

The keepers are not an order. They are a habit the country has. Nobody is chosen, nobody is sent. One dry morning a person sets down what they were carrying and goes down the stair. Sometimes they come back up after a day, or a year, and nothing is said about that either. The Well keeps who it keeps.

The one who is down now came at nineteen, with her ears ringing from the world. Nothing chased her — that is important, because the herders' stories always want a chasing, and there was none. She visited once as a child, with the other children, and afterward the hills sounded wrong to her: too wide, too scraped. So one morning she walked to the saddle and did not come back up, and her mother, who had expected this the way one expects weather, told the flock-tenders *the Well is kept*, and no one mourned. You do not mourn weather.

That was a long time ago. She is no longer any age; the Well does not keep ages. She is pale the way the fish are pale — not white, exactly; unlit. Cloth does not last down here, nothing woven lasts, the petrified rope by the basin is the proof of fiber's fate, so she wears what the chamber offers, which is warmth, and goes barefoot on flowstone the temperature of her own blood. At blood-heat the skin's edge becomes information only; air and body are one warmth. She has lived for years at the precise temperature at which a person stops being separate from a room. She stopped asking what the place is around the time she stopped asking what she is. The two questions had the same temperature.

You will not see her when you come down. Your eyes go first to the oval of gold, then to the blanket-forest, then out into the dim, and the dim will seem empty. Sit. Wait fifty drips. Something in the moss-zone will move at the speed of stone, and it will be her, and the reason she moved is that the light moved. That is the only reason anything moves here.

She obeys the light like everything else. Her day is the oval's slow journey, and she sits at its edge, never in it — the light is spoken for; a hundred leaning stems have prior claim. She follows at its hem the way the frogs gather at the pool's edge: close enough to warm, never close enough to take. When the oval climbs the wall and dies, her day closes with it. The bats' leaving is her evening weather, the one moving air that ever touches her, and she turns her face up to it, hundreds of wings you hear as nothing, and her face is the country that wind happens over.

Her work is almost nothing, done perfectly. Once a year, on the day the light first strikes the black pool, she presses one ribbon of wet clay onto the basin's rim, fitting her fingers into the prints of the one before her, whose fingers had fitted the prints before that. The drip hardens it within the year. A handspan a lifetime. It is the only thing being built in the Green Well, and it will never be finished; that is its nature, and hers.

She takes only what falls. Figs, in the season the tree at the lip drops them inward — what lands on stone is hers; what lands on the blanket-forest belongs to the forest; what lands on the pool belongs to the fish, and she has watched a fig taken down by three pale mouths. Fronds the forest has already finished with. After a rain, mushrooms, brief as the frog-song. And water — always water, the one abundance, drunk from the basin in cupped hands, never from the pool, whose skin has not been broken in years and must not be. She has never once been thirsty. It may be the whole reason people stay.

Her tally is the low panel by the basin, the marks grouped in fours and ones like all the others. A mark for each crossing of the oval. A mark for each rain. And the fish count, which she keeps because someone must: there are three. There have been three for as far back as the marks run. It is the oldest continuous fact in the Green Well, and she is its only witness.

The residents have made their arrangements with her. The snake comes down the root-column at dusk hunting by heat, and it reads her as it passes — a large slow warmth that does not hunt — and crosses her feet the way a hand checks a familiar stone. The hunting spider keeps its own counsel in the root-curtain; she leaves the moth-wings where they fall, for the springtails. The owl's pellets drop through the oculus all night, and she leaves those too. Gratitude, down here, is just efficient dismantling, and she does not interrupt it. When a beetle blunders through the eye of the dome she watches the floor convene, and convene, and by morning there are only isopods, conferring, and she does not grieve. Grief is a loud thing. She filed it down years ago to something that fits the room.

Visitors descend rarely — a herder's curious boy, a surveyor, once in a century a priest — and she does not greet them and does not hide. She simply continues. And the visitor watches a person move at the speed of light on stone, drink from cupped hands, wait out a drip — and understands, without one word being spent, that speaking would be a kind of breaking. People whisper here without deciding to, and they think it is the place. It is. But the place learned it from keeping people, or the people from the place; after so long the teaching runs in both directions, like the ants on the fig-root.

When a keeper ends, she ends in the clay along the north wall, where the flowstone is young and patient. The one before her lay down there — a man, that time; the line is not a family, it is only a habit — and she lay near him through the last of it, and the floor began its long work before his warmth was gone, and he did not hurry it. Hurry is for the upper world. Now the folds along that wall are only folds, unless you have looked as long as she has, in which case some of them keep the shape of folded hands, of a bent knee, of the hollow a temple makes. The drip welds a grain a year. The Well forgets nothing and keeps no names.

The herders say the silence is made of keepers. They say a dome like this should ring, and this one doesn't, because it is lined, grain by grain, with the quietest people who ever lived, and sound dies against them the way heat dies against deep water. She does not claim this. She claims nothing. But she has noticed that the deepest quiet in the chamber is the spot where her predecessor lay, and when she is tired, she rests against it.

Then, rarely — rarely as a comet — it rains in the world above.

The oculus becomes a waterfall. The stopper is drawn from the hush, the dome's throat opens, the frogs find their voices, and the whole chamber fills with calling. And on that night, only that night, she finds hers. She stands where the falling water mists her, and she says the names she carries — her predecessor's, her predecessor's predecessor's, and her own, the whole liturgy of the place, three names and nothing else — into the one hour when sound can carry. The dome that swallows a shout at arm's length every other night of every year lets it rise: up the light-shaft, out through the fig, into the loud wet dark. Whether anyone above is listening — whether some herder wakes and tells his wife *the Well spoke* — none of that comes back down.

By morning the hush has resealed itself, as if nothing had been said.

Something had been said. That is allowed. That is the entire liturgy: three names, rarer than rain, spoken by a person the country above remembers only as a condition.

What falls, stays. She fell thirty years ago, on an ordinary dry morning, and stayed.

What stays is changed. She is changed past age, past voice, past the edge of her own skin.

The light decides. She moves when it moves.

The hush is kept.

Someone keeps it.
