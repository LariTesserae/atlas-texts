The population of Gallery Two is a constant, like the temperature, like the note. You could take a census — grazers, singers, flies, the tall carriers — and come back in fifty years and take it again, and it would not merely be the same number. It would be the same *list*. Nothing lives here the way things live elsewhere, briefly and replaceably. Things live here the way the mist-curtains hang: in fixed positions, maintained. A resident of Gallery Two is not an individual. It is a pattern the gallery keeps, the way salt keeps the shape of a wound.

Begin with the congregation, because the congregation is most of what moves. A grazer is born mid-step, almost. The block cracks along its molded seams and the thing inside unfolds, damp with oil, creased like new paper, and within the hour it has straightened its legs and begun the only walk of its life that is not counterclockwise — the short walk from the combs to the track. It does not search for its place. The procession has gaps in it, grazer-length, kept open, traveling around the nave with the rest: places being held, a parade with its absent marching in it. The new one reaches the track and is already in its gap; it has only to arrive, and the gap closes behind it like water. It is not a new one. The grinders keep each meal separate, one beast's dust never mixed with another's, and the press gives back the one the grinder took — creased, blank, and certain of its place. Identity here is not memory. It is shape, and shape is the one thing the gallery never loses.

A grazer's crust is its portrait, painted by its route. Since the route never varies, the portrait only grows more itself over the years — a dome of accreted habit, salt on salt, until the oldest ones are half-statue and move like weathered boulders that have somehow not stopped. The end of a grazer is not a death but a setting, like plaster. It slows. The crust locks its plates. And for some days before this — they always know the day — one of the carriers will be standing nearby, at a courteous distance, the way the lift waits.

There are nine carriers. You will not be told this; you will count them over hours, and then count them again to be sure, and the second count will be the first count. They are the tallest things in the gallery, small bodies carried high on stilt legs, and they are the only things that hurry — a quick, high-stepping, stitched gait, careful as rain, stepping between the domed backs of the procession without ever touching one. You will learn to tell them apart in the dim by their footfalls, each a different small note on the salt. Everything in Gallery Two that changes state does so in a carrier's arms. They carry the fresh blocks from the press to the warm cells of the combs. They carry the dying singers up to the chapels. And when a grazer's hour comes, the waiting carrier folds down — the legs going slack in sequence, the body lowering like a drawbridge — and it does not lift the grazer at first. It walks it to the grinder on the grazer's own last steps, a hand beneath its chin, gently. The last thing a grazer does is the only thing it ever did. It walks. Then the carrier takes what is left up in its arms, and feeds the mouth, and hurries back, because a death on the floor is the one untidiness the gallery permits and it is never permitted for long. Once — only once — a carrier will stop and regard *you*. It is checking whether you are a body. Finding you upright, warm, unscheduled, it files you among the things that move but do not matter: weather. It will never look at you again. You should be grateful. The other category is worse.

Above the pipes, the choir. A singer is an appetite with a comb for a body, and the comb is made of eaten sound — the young ones all knees and hunger, comb no bigger than a thumbnail, the old ones leaves of pale crystal with a creature still clinging underneath. Sound has a short life here. Your shout is drunk out of the air within arm's length; the reason you must shout is not the loudness but the thirst. Cup your hands around one — they allow it, they are beyond fear in the way of things that have never been harmed — and for three breaths the chord goes missing a tooth. Then the others give more, and the tooth regrows in sound, and the singer in the dark of your palms does not struggle. It leans. It has found your pulse, which by now beats in step with everything, and it drinks. When a singer's comb outgrows the creature — when it is more window than animal — it climbs, or is carried, to the chapels, and backs into a cell comb-first, and the salt fuses over it like water closing over a stone. The cell stays warm. It is still, faintly, stridulating in there; the entombed choir goes on eating sound in the dark, and their warmth is the warmth of the combs, and the warmth of the combs is what cures the pressed blocks set into the cells below. The beginning of every grazer is warmed by the end of a singer. The nursery and the ossuary are one room, because here they are one event. And one day — you will not be there; the gallery's calendar does not include you — a cell high in the stack will crack along a seam, and something small and many-jointed will climb out with a thumbnail comb and go up to the pipes and take its place in the chord. Not a new singer. Not a descendant. The same one. The crystal keeps the pattern the way salt keeps the shape of the wound.

In the side bays, the mouths. No one has seen a grinder arrive; perhaps the walls extrude them, perhaps they are the part of the wall that is permitted a mouth. They have two hungers — the salt-face they eat back to keep the wound open, and the dead — and to the gallery these are one hunger: raw material. Their grinding is the slowest note of all, under the rasp of a hundred tongues, under the drone, steady as surf in another room, and what they drool is the fine gray meal the press is hungry for.

The small folk are one creature in two phases. The whisker-turf is the plant phase, frost-grass on the old fittings, and where the grazers cannot reach — high on the porcelain, in the blue garden of the corona — the turf fruits, and the fruit is a spark-fly: a whisker grown past itself, curled, charged, lifted. The flies do not leave because the field will not let them; they are seeds orbiting their stalk. When one bridges a gap and pops, its metal falls back as vapor, plates the fittings, becomes turf, begins again. The flock is not a population. It is a standing wave of metal, the way the curtains are a standing wave of air and the chord is a standing wave of sound. Everything in Gallery Two is a standing wave of something, and a standing wave does not use itself up. The grazers that crop the turf come away with plated tongues — the sting leaves metal in the mouth, and the metal is the tooth. The pasture arms the mouths that crop it. Nothing here gives without also building the thing that takes.

And the wax-bloom, gray and patient, is the one true pasture — the only producer, fixing the Engine's oiled breath into food. It grows where the mist stands, and the mist stands where the choir holds the nodes of the chord, so the map of the pastures is the map of the song. The route never changes because the bloom never moves; the bloom never moves because the curtains never drift; the curtains never drift because the singers hold the wave; the singers hold the wave because the wave is their food. Pull any thread and you hold the whole knot. There is no loose end anywhere in the gallery. That is another way of saying the law.

If anything here is the tenant — the one true resident — it is the Engine. Everything else is replaced on schedule and is therefore furniture, however warm. The Engine is only *kept*. The shine on its cylinders, polished by the shoulders of the procession passing and passing, is the closest the congregation comes to worship: a brightness maintained by touch, forever. And it pays its way like a tenant. Power comes up the armored conduit from below, oil in a lagged line, warmth rising up the shaft like something's breath — and nothing material goes down. The lift descends empty. Only the note goes down, the drone and its stacked partials, conducted through the rock, held at a constant level forever. The gallery pays its rent in music. It has never once been late.

The plate says GALLERY 2, and a two implies a one, and from here the shaft goes only down. If the numbering begins at the bottom, then everything you have seen — the congregation, the choir, the press, the Engine itself — is the upstairs of something. Whatever lives in Gallery Two lives above a neighbor that has never once come up, and for whom, all the same, the lift keeps its courteous interval. Perhaps the schedule is commemoration. Perhaps it is preparation. The bell dings; the cage waits; the dark below answers with warmth, and power, and the courtesy of feeding a thing it never visits.

You are the only thing in the gallery that is going to end. The rime on your forearms is the first courtesy — the place beginning, politely, to keep you. If you stayed, the wall would take you halfway and hold you forever, a mark left for whoever comes next, half-legible as the plate. Or the carriers would attempt you, gently, and fail, because you would not grind into anyone, and the ledger has no column for a stranger. So climb, while you are still an event and not a fixture. Nothing below notices your going. You were never counted; there is no gap in the procession to close behind you; the census is the same census. A singer leans into the last of your footfalls and drinks it. The carriers wait beside the ones who are about to set. The chord holds, the droplet hangs, and at the bottom of everything the lift holds its door a courteous interval — for no one, or for the one who has not come up yet.
