Ask what lives here and the chord goes on holding its note. The question is yours to answer, and your first answers will all be wrong.

You will look first for the keeper. A place that runs must have someone running it — an organist for the organ note, a miller for the mill, someone whose hair stands up near the walls and who knows why. There is no one. Nothing was built, and nothing tends what runs; the keeping and the kept are the same thing here. So you will turn to the census, and the census will answer with its usual patience: one elk, one heron, dippers, salamanders, moths, and one fish. All of them are here the way the chord is here. Only one of them is here the way you mean.

Consider the elk. It breathes — you can watch its sides work — and its breath goes out into the mist and the mist gives it back, because the mist is the same mist and the breath is the same breath. Its heart beats at one rate. The fire on its antlers neither warms nor consumes, and by the time it rounds the far sedge everything else has also returned to where it was, as if the whole cirque were wound to its stride. It is a clock in the shape of an elk. Nothing has ever happened to it, and a life, you will remember, is a thing that happens. The heron is a held note in the shape of a heron. The dippers are a habit in the shape of birds — the walking kept after the reason for walking is gone, like a word whose meaning has been forgotten, except that nothing here was ever learned, so nothing can be forgotten. The salamanders lie in the warm sockets like appetite after the meal: warmth was all they ever wanted, and they have it forever, and they are what wanting looks like when it is permanently over. The moths sip the green light and the light is not less, because nothing here takes anything from anything, not even light, not even a sip. All of them persist. None of them is hungry, hunted, young, old, tired, or new. They are alive the way the rising braid is a river: every property of the thing except the essential one. They are kept. Kept is not the same as lived.

Perhaps, then, the place itself is what lives. It has a body, if you want to see one — the walls weep, the water commutes like blood, the ferns breathe with the hammer-beat, and deep in the young cliff the piston keeps a heartbeat. A body of stone and water, with the animals for its cells. But a body lives by spending: it eats, it burns, it heals, it ages. This body spends nothing, heals nothing — the wound is held open, not healing, not worsening — and ages not at all. It has the anatomy of a living thing and none of the economy. It is a diagram of a body, drawn once and held open at the page.

And yet there is one true entry in the census. You will look up early — everyone looks up eventually — and understand late, and everything else in the cirque will turn out to have been preparation for the understanding.

A fish lives here.

It is a plain fish, and that, in the end, is the strangest thing about it. Everything else in this place announces itself: the elk burns, the boulders spin, the water climbs. The fish is the length of your forearm, deep through the back, brown going olive going pale along the belly, spotted like a trout — it is very like a trout — and nothing about it sparks, nothing glows, nothing hums. You could see it in any stream and not look twice. It hangs head-down in the rising braid, high enough that you must tip your head back, low enough that you can watch the gills work. Its tail beats steadily, a small quick clock against the water-hammer's slow one, and its gill covers open and shut, open and shut, and when they open you see the red inside, the color of a coal under ash — the only warm color in the whole pewter room, the color of a thing spending itself in a place that spends nothing. It casts no shadow, as nothing does. The pools will not return its image, as they return nothing's. Everything else in the cirque watches you with that unbearable calm. The fish does not watch you. The fish is busy. It is the only busy creature in the room, and its business is endless, and that is the whole of its biography.

Watch long enough and the mechanics clarify. The braid rises at one rate, always, because the rate is a fixed quantity, like the lag, like the loudness. The fish swims downward at the same rate — not approximately the same, the same, the way two sides of an equation are the same number. One beat harder and it would come down out of the braid onto the moss. One beat softer and the climbing water would carry it up toward the rim, into the mist, out of the grammar of the place. It does neither. It has never done either. The exactness is not a skill it has. It is a fact it is.

If you have ever stood on a bridge over a stream, you have already seen this fish's whole life. Trout hang in a current, noses up into it, fins sculling, and let the river bring them what a river brings — nymphs, gnats, the news from upstream. It looks like stillness and is all effort, and the effort is for something: the current is a conveyor, and dinner is coming. Now take away the dinner. Take away the hunger, the otter, the winter, the spawning gravel, the dapple. Stand the stream on end and make it climb. What remains is this: the holding. Everything contingent about trout has been distilled away, and what is left is the verb itself, performed perfectly, alone, in midair, forever. Everything else in the cirque was relieved of effort. The fish was relieved of everything except effort.

Bring any frame you like; the fish completes it. If the cirque is a factory, it is a factory with one worker, on a shift that does not end. If it is a church — and the organ note and the votive moss and the weeping walls say it might be — then the fish is the one unceasing prayer, the only thing said here. If it is a body, then here at last is the heart you can see: the cliff keeps a heartbeat, the flywheels keep a hum, but only the fish has a heart, small and red and fed by blood — the only beat in the place that is paid for.

Do not call it patient. Patience is a way of waiting out time, and the fish is not waiting; there is nothing it expects. Do not call it tireless, though it never tires — tireless is praise, and implies that tiring was possible. Do not call it trapped. Trapped is wanting to be elsewhere, and elsewhere is not in the grammar. Our words were made for creatures in time, and they come off in your hand here. Three creatures in the census are one of a kind, and they are the place's three persons: the elk is its circumference, walking the edge true; the heron is its future, held forever at the hinge of about-to; and the fish is its present tense, the only tense the grammar has, the tense that goes on. The elk's circuit takes the time it takes. The fish's circuit is a single point, and it completes it with every beat of its tail.

Look into a pool, at the sane version of this room — sun, moving sky, a tarn, a falls that falls — and there must be fish in that water, an ordinary tarn keeps ordinary trout, and they must hang in the inflow current doing exactly this gesture, for reasons, briefly, until it ends: a heron's beak, a winter, a bed of gravel and then done. There, the gesture means something. Here it means nothing, and so it is finally, perfectly, only itself. The ordinary world cannot afford this fish; a creature that only swims, that eats nothing and fears nothing and is never finished, is possible nowhere else. It is endemic to the impossible. That is how you can tell the pools are only pictures: any version of this place that leaves out the fish has left out the one thing in it that lives. And the heron stands at its pool all day — all the one day — staring down into that other water in the attitude of a strike, staring through the one window onto a world where striking happens, and does not strike. Above the pools the fish hangs with a moving sky beneath it, looking down, if it looks, if looking is among the things it does, into the world of reasons — and goes on swimming.

You brought your leaving in with you, and when you use it, the fish is what you will carry out. Not the chord — the chord fades from memory the way loudness fades from notice. Not the sparks, not the pools. The fish. You will find it again afterward, everywhere: on bridges, in clear streams, in every trout hanging in every current, working at stillness for a season and a reason. The world, you will learn, is full of brief commemorations of the fish. Each one performs the gesture for a while, for food, and stops.

Only here does the gesture go on, emptied of everything but itself, exact, continuous, paid for in full — the braid climbing, the tail beating, the small red doors of the gills opening and shutting on the climbing water, the one thing in the cirque that can be said to live going on living in the one tense there is: downward against the rising, going nowhere, unhurried, unwavering, at home.
