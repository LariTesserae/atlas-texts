Everything, is the short answer. Everything that can fit itself into a tier, a seam, a shell, a breath — does. The long answer takes the climb, and it never really finishes; the country is large and its tenants have had longer than the causeway to specialize. But you can start the census at the bottom, after dark, when the Drown announces itself.

**The Drown, after dark.** The water is too warm to hold much air of its own, so the fish carry theirs. A thousand times a minute, all night, something rises to the black surface and takes a small deliberate sip — a dimple, a soft tick — until the whole marsh sounds like slow rain falling upward, and the egrets shift their weight on the last pier, and the pier transmits it through the stone to your feet: the Drown, sighing in its sleep. Stand there long enough and you will start to breathe in time.

The big shapes under the piers are silt-mouths: filter-fish the length of canoes, mouths held permanently open in an expression that reads as patience because that is exactly what it is. They strain the broth. They do not hurry. Sometimes one surfaces alongside the causeway and lies there with its back awash, and the egrets step aboard and ride it, and the fish does not object and the birds do not remark on it, and the arrangement may be older than the orchard terraces. Nothing here wastes a surface. The causeway has simply grown a kind that moves.

The drowned trees are islands, and every island is staffed. Crown-frogs pass their whole lives thirty meters above the marsh, breeding in the thimbles of rainwater cupped in the crowns; their tadpoles have never seen open water and would not survive it. A swimmer-snake commutes between crowns at dusk. Under the causeway's arches, in the only hard country the Drown offers, a swallow-analog plasters its mud gourds in ranks of thousands — for them, the road that goes nowhere is the center of the world. A road is still a road, if what you needed was a wall.

And below all of it, in the two tiers the Drown has taken, the old rooms are full of fish, and diving turtles go down into the flooded stairwells carrying their breath in silver beads along their shells, like lamplighters doing rounds. What else lives in the cellar, the turtles know and the silt-mouths know. No one else has checked the census in a very long time.

**The tended half.** Up the switchbacks, past the first honest evidence of hands, you will learn the country's open secret: the orchards remember being orchards because something does the remembering for them. The orchard-pigeon is a heavy, slate-blue bird the size of a goose, and it plants in rows. Watch one for an hour and the pattern stops looking like accident: it carries fruit to open ground, feeds, and kicks soil over the stone — one deliberate stroke — in line with the last. Nobody taught it. Somebody taught its ancestors' ancestors, and the lesson has outlived the teachers by millennia. The terraces fruit on their old schedule because the pigeons keep the old appointments. The gardens did not go feral. Only the management changed.

In the under-rain, the noon-frogs time their small concerts to the canopy's secondhand weather, beginning an hour before the drip peaks — they feel the leaves cool, the forest's intake of breath — and they are the first listeners you will meet, though not the famous ones. The canopy itself is tiered like the country that hides it: three strata, four, each with its own frogs, its own midges, its own green light, every species with its own floor and ceiling. The Stair is shaped like itself at every scale. Get used to that. You will be seeing it again.

**Provinces that walk.** The tower snails are not individuals so much as real estate. A big shell carries moss; the moss carries water; the water carries shell-frogs, a thumb-sized species that has never, in its kind's whole memory, touched the ground. A pink beetle no longer than your fingernail farms the algae in its own snail's silver trail, hurrying the length of the calligraphy and back, tending a crop on a moving field. The colony migrates up the up-well seams at the speed of growth, following the climbing water as it shifts across the decades — and most of its citizens have never seen the street.

**The seams.** Where the water climbs, everything gathers. The lush bands along the threads are grazed by seam-crabs and worked by snail-kites, and where the reduced weight softens the rock, the seam-swallows mine it — colonies of ten thousand tunneled into cliff that has forgotten how to be hard. At dusk they pour out in columns like smoke going home and join the cicada wave climbing the tiers, and for a minute the cliff has a second sound inside the first.

In the warm season the eels come up the threads, and the whole cliff turns out for it. Cliff-fowl mob the seams at dawn; the kites drop their usual concerns; the run feeds every ledge it passes. Months later the larvae come back down over the falls — the plunge basins seethe gently; the falling water is full of them, a downward weather — and far below, the silt-mouths rise to meet the rain of them, so the migration ends where it began. Hold one of the climbing adults — you can; they are unhurried — and it is warm as your own wrist. Nothing on the Stair has ever been cold. There is no cold here; there is only less hot. The fauna have built their entire philosophy on that fact, and the philosophy is: *why hurry.*

**The tenants of the breath.** The Works have tenants. Up in the intakes, in the years-long indraft, hang the vent-bats — a hundred thousand to a housing, riding the inhale the way other bats ride evening, sifting the same broth the machine sifts. Deeper in, where the condensation galleries run, live the ones that never leave: pale midges, blind crickets singing to the chord in its own key — and why not; it is the loudest thing in their world, and every singer wants an accompanist. Out on the open cliff a katydid does the same from the hollows where the hum runs strongest, using the Work the way a street singer uses a doorway.

The ceramic seams of the housings are full of waiting. The ten-year moths spend the whole interval there as pupae, tucked into the crazing of the gray-gold hull, doing nothing with a dedication that puts the snails to shame. They are the only pollinators the vent-flowers will accept — the strange geometries, the night blooms that track nothing, open for them and for no one else. Between breaths the moths are a rumor in the stone. In the exhale year they are a blizzard, and the vent-mouths become the richest ground in the country, and everything with a calendar comes.

Everything with a calendar knows in advance, because the chord bends. In the last year of an inhalation the hum shifts a fraction of a note — felt in the fillings before it is heard — and the whole Stair reads it at once: the frogs, the moths, the tortoises, the keepers. The country has one appointment, and everything alive keeps it. The rain-frogs are never wrong for the same reason. They are not predicting the storm; they are listening to the intakes answer the pressure — the country's intake of breath, an hour before the sky's. Prophecy, here, is just good equipment.

**The Roof's long memory.** The Roof belongs to the tortoises the way the Drown belongs to the silt-mouths. Bog-tortoises the size of low tables graze the orchid drifts and the kettle-hollows, and their shells are not shells so much as acreage: moss, ferns, an orchid or two. One famous individual carried a sapling tree-fern for eleven years — a walking garden with a canopy — until a deep wallow settled the question. They are the only animals whose clocks run at the country's speed. When the chord bends, they begin the long walk toward the vents, a pilgrimage of years that arrives exactly on time, because they have nowhere else to be and all the time there is to get there.

The kettles have their thief. The kettle-heron is a tall, gaunt bird that has learned what the plants know — that the country drops things — and stalks the hollows spearing drowned birds and foundering moths out of the pitchers with a surgeon's care, taking the catch and leaving the plant its broth. The plants, fertilized at the rim by the heron's leavings, come out ahead. Nothing here wastes a drowning.

And then there is the Back — the hull of the largest Work, a low continent in its own right — and the Back has endemics. Soil has made itself in the crazing; a stunted forest stands on it; and every tree leans. They lean toward the vent-valleys, the whole forest inclined like listeners, because the inhale has been drawing the fog inward for thirty years and the trees grew up inside the pull. Hull-finches — small, gray, engine-colored — occur there and nowhere else, and a vent-vane gecko times its nesting to the exhale's first season, fledging its young into the richest air in the world.

**The keepers.** Which leaves the people. Whether they are the builders' descendants or a later kindness of the country's, they are not saying, and the difference has had long enough to stop mattering. They are few — a keeper to a Rise, some years fewer — and they live dispersed, alone or in twos, each keeping a stretch of the Stair the way the pigeons keep a row: a switchback cleared of a fallen limb, a shrine floor swept, a lamp. The oil is pressed from orchard nuts. The cut fruit set out on the balustrade stones is for whoever is coming. Someone is always eventually coming; that is the whole of their hospitality and, as far as anyone has determined, the whole of their theology, and it serves well as both.

They reckon in breaths. A keeper's age is the number of exhalations she has sat; childhood ends at the first and you are old at five. Between appointments they follow the country's slow circuit — the fruiting, the eel-run, the drip — at walking pace, because everything they keep is kept at walking pace. And when the chord bends, they begin, like the tortoises, like the moths, the long drift vent-ward, converging from every tier across the final year, to sit in the warm engineered fog and breathe what the machine has spent a decade composing. Ask what the exhalation is *for* and you will get the country's only straight answer. One old keeper, asked, said *weather* — and meant: what is rain for?

The lamp in the Third Rise shrine is lit every dusk without exception, and it is not for the machines. The machines have never needed light. It is for the stair itself — for the chance of a climber caught out by evening, which happens perhaps once in a generation and is therefore, by the country's arithmetic, a certainty worth preparing for daily. You will not see the keeper. You will see the path her rounds have worn at walking pace, the way you see the tortoises' tracks: a habit, made visible by years.

**The largest resident.** All of this is prelude to the answer you will eventually give, standing on the Roof at dusk with the chord settling into its night register and the mist coming off the warm stone: the thing that lives here is the Stair.

The Works breathe; you have heard it. They feed, after their fashion — the inhalation is a tasting of the whole living broth of the air. They hold their own warmth and then, as an afterthought, hold the country's. They garden: the vent-forests persist at their doors like flowers at a gate, naturalized, half-wild, kept. They keep time, and everything else keeps time by them. Whether they were built or born, whether they are machines that became a country or a country that happens to be made of machines, is a question for a longer visit than yours. The naturalist's answer is shorter. If it breathes, feeds, regulates, gardens, and has held a climate across five vertical kilometers for longer than the orchards have had pigeons — then it lives here. It is, in fact, the only thing that lives here at the proper scale. Everything else — the sighing fish, the planting pigeons, the waiting moths, the walking tortoises, the keepers and their lamps — lives *in* it: in its warmed air, its fog-years, its chord, the way the shell-frog lives in the snail's garden. Completely. At walking pace. With no notion of elsewhere.

A snail carries a garden. A tortoise carries an orchard. A Work carries a country, and the country carries all of them up through the warm air, tier on tier, between breaths — full of waiting things, every one of them on time, every one of them home, every one of them, in the only sense that has ever mattered here, kept.

The lamp is lit. The snails are out. The Drown is sighing, the chord is settling, and the census goes on without you.
