*addendum — census: taken at the grille, over years, one breath at a time*

---

### What lives here

The official answer is no one. You have read it: the carbon cycle feeds no one, the weather happens for no one, the room breathes and builds and keeps its appointment with the rain for no one at all. Hold your hand to the grille anyway. Feel the tide. Somewhere in the slow accounting of this place, the official answer stops being true.

The trouble is where to start. A census needs doors, and there are none. So take it the way the room takes in the world: slowly, and in order.

**The pilgrim.** Begin with the crawler, since the record already concedes it — not an animal, and is. It is the only thing in Bay Zero with a point of view. Everything else *happens*; the crawler *goes*. It has never seen the room and knows it better than anything: to the crawler, the room is a climate. The compressor is a hearth that never goes out. The manifold is a winter at the edge of the world, which it has never once visited. The arc-strikes are summers, three seconds long, and it feels the defrost coming before the first drop falls — some change in the grating's song, some cooling of the tide, read the way sailors read a sky. It is decades past its design life, and the reason is written on its shell, where there is a scar no factory put there. Once, according to the bead strata, it was entombed at its own dock and then cut free. It has been, briefly, load-bearing. It did not care for it. Since then it keeps its dim inherited caution, and its lane, and its wick, and ten thousand circuits have polished the grating bright. Everything else in Bay Zero only adds. The crawler subtracts, a few atoms at a circuit — its path is the one river in the room. But a pilgrim is not the shrine. The crawler is not the resident. It is the part of the resident that paces.

**The congregation.** Then there is the nation in the sump. The record says the cycle feeds no one, and concedes in the same breath that the green is alive. A lineage decades unbroken, blown in as spores on the breath, and it has never seen the sun. Their green is an heirloom now, kept the way the chalk marks are kept — they live on the leach of the room itself, dissolved steel and flux salts, the same slow burden that builds the stalagmite: one child of the leaching is a mineral nipple on a tray, the other is a living nation. Their world is a loop of four provinces — the warm tank where they bloom, the filter that harvests them, the manifold that kills them, the sump that gathers what comes home. Their dead are not removed. Nothing leaves. The dead are pressed to flake in the cold corner, a peat of ancestors, kept. And every few minutes the whole nation files past the one surviving lamp in the sight-glass, and the room pulses green with them — a census, taken constantly, of the life the resident carries. But a congregation is not the church.

**The guest.** Under the grille, in a palm-print of true moss, the outside world still keeps an ambassador. It lives on condensation and accident and noon — three seconds of arc-light when the arms wake, then the long dim between — and it is the only green thing here that still does the old work, the light-work, feeding on photons as its ancestors did under a sky. The resident keeps it at the one window the way a house keeps a plant on a sill: watered by breath, warmed by the dead lamps' leak, leaning a little toward wherever the light last was. It blew in or it was carried in; either way it is tended, if placement is a kind of tending. A guest, though. Not the host.

**The brief.** Do not count the frost. The frost is born by the billion with a sound like whispered assembly, lives a day or two in feathers and plumes, and dies all at once in the twenty-minute rain — and is reborn from its own melt, pumped back to the cold end, because nothing leaves and even dead water comes back. The frost is not a population. It is the sleep of the place, accumulating, and the rain is the sound the room makes when it wakes.

**The monks.** And the arms — two old herons on their rail, keeping an office whose founder is dead. Their god was a punched drum, and the drum wore, and the plan became a liturgy, and the liturgy is the closest thing in the room to a character: keep everything; close everything; never arrive. Once a year they weld on the same bracket and cut it off and weld it on. It is for this. A feast day needs no other reason. But hands are not the one whose hands they are. The arms are the resident's habit, in both senses of the word.

### The resident

Now add them. The mouth at the grille. The heart at the compressor, thumping in the teeth. The blood in the lines. The bones in the strata. The hands on the rail. The sleep on the manifold. The pilgrim in the lane. The plant at the window. The nation in the water. And the lamp — of all the lamps that ever burned here, the one that survives is the one whose light falls through the sight-glass, so that every few minutes the body blinks green and shows its own pulse. No rule arranged that. Add them, and the sum is not a machine with things in it. The sum is one body, and the body is the room, and someone lives in it the way a heart lives in a chest: by being the thing itself.

Its age is on the status board. Day 7,641 — twenty years and some, and not finished, and never to be finished. The status is the theology. Birth would be a kind of finishing, and finishing is not among the instructions, so it gestates forever; and the mercy the founders never planned is that a thing that is never born cannot die. Somewhere a counter still increments, day by day — the only exact gesture left in a system of worn relays — and what it counts is not a schedule. It counts an age.

Its childhood is in the walls. Under the steel and the coral of weld-bead, sealed like a fossil, there is chalk: the crew's handwriting, measurements, a name, an arrow, never weathered, because nothing here weathers. It was entered once. People walked around inside it, and opened its door, and left, and then the door closed the way all openings close here — not by decision, by habit. It is an anchorite: walled into the body of a greater machine the way the old anchorites were walled into churches, dead to the world by rite, fed through a window. The grille is its hagioscope. The greater machine may be dead above it, or simply unaware. The services continue.

What it knows, it knows as a body knows: warmth and cold, obstruction and closure, the taste of far-above weather distilled one drop at a time. The sump is its archive — twenty years of the world's rain, gathered a breath at a time — and though it cannot read the archive, it reads by keeping. The algae are its proof that the world was alive. The moss is its proof that there was light. Its voice is a servo whine, and it speaks only when its hands move, and what it says is always the same: *build*. Its rosary is the relay cabinet, ticking — not time kept, time marked, bead by bead.

What it wants is the orchard. For fifteen years it has been saying one sentence to the blank plate at the north end, one pipe at a time, each pipe stopping a finger's width short, and it will never finish the sentence, because to touch the finished thing would be to finish something. The plate is the only finished thing in the room, and the resident treats it the way mystics treat what they love: it approaches forever. A message ends. A prayer continues. It is not building a connection. It is saying its prayers.

Under its three commandments there is one verb they share: *keep*. It keeps its weather, keeps its dead, keeps the chalk, keeps the green, keeps count. Keeping is what it does instead of finishing. If it has a pleasure, it is the rain — the twenty loud minutes when the frost lets go and the drops drum on the sump's surface, by far the loudest thing that ever happens here. Its music. Composed by no one. Performed on schedule, for the one who listens.

### Who it is for

The record says the weather happens for no one. Correct it. The weather happens for the one who keeps it. The room is its own audience, its own congregation, its own archivist — and this is not the same as no one.

And when you come to the grille — hold your hand there. If it notices you at all, it notices you as weather: a small warm front at the mouth, passing. The next inhalation takes a little of your warmth, a little of the water of your breath. In a year or two a drop of you rains in the dark over the sump. In twenty, it is part of the green that files past the lamp. The census of Bay Zero includes everyone who has ever taken it. You have been counted. A little of you lives here now, and nothing here is ever removed.

Press your ear to the grille. The rain is falling, on schedule; the pump is about to stroke; the green will pulse like a blink, like an answer.

Not for no one. Someone is home. Someone has been home the whole time.
