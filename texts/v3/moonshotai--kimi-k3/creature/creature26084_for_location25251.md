The people of the bowl are wet the way the town is wet — not as a condition but as a medium. Damp is their element. Their skin carries a permanent coolness at the forearms and the cheeks, their hair is bound up in knots and stays that way, their clothes are dyed woven fiber worn in layers that are never quite dry and never meant to be. They smell, all of them, of woodsmoke — it lives in the cloth, in the hair, in the skin itself — and this is not incidental. In a place where nothing is ever dry, smoke is the one dryness that clings to a person, and so the smell of a bowl-dweller is the smell of home carried on the body. They can identify each other in the dark by it: whose hearth burns green wood, whose burns old roof-poles, whose has been cooking crab. When a bowl-dweller goes down the ladders to the warm valley and comes back, the first thing the family does, half-joking, wholly serious, is hold a bit of their cloth to the nose. *There,* they say. *You're back.*

Their feet are wide, bare, and prehensile in a way that unsettles valley folk, and they walk the slick round stones and moss-napped planks with a weight that seems to come up through the ground rather than down onto it. Visitors fall. Everyone expects visitors to fall, and it is kindly laughed at, and a guest who has fallen twice and not yet learned to soften is a guest the children have started following. Their hands are knotted things — calloused in the pattern of cord, scarred at the thumb-webs from the stripping of fiber, dye worked so deep into the knuckle-creases that no washing takes it out. Watch a bowl-dweller sit idle and you will see the fingers moving on their own: a slow, even tap, two beats and a rest. That is the rhythm of the water-pestles. It is in all of them, the way a pulse is in a wrist.

They are loud. They have to be. Speech in the bowl competes with the falls, the chorus, the rain on the great leaves, the pestles, and so they have grown into a people who talk at close range and high volume, faces near, hands on each other's shoulders, whole conversations conducted at a pitch a drylander would call shouting and they would call Tuesday. Their hearing is the marvel. They can pull a single voice out of the dawn wall of sound; they can hear a flume-joint gone wrong from fifty paces — a drip landing where no drip should land, a runnel pitched a half-note low — and they navigate by it. In the white noon of the cloud-flood, when the world shrinks to a few dripping meters, they walk by water-sound: this lane's gutter runs fast, that bridge crosses the two-drip channel, the market roof has its own particular drumming. Houses are known by their sound as much as their shape. Directions are given in sound. And because loud means right — loud water, loud frogs, loud birds, the world in tune — silence is the one thing that alarms them. When all the pestles stop at once, people come awake out of deep sleep already reaching for a light. Down in the valley, in the dead dry quiet of the night, bowl-folk cannot sleep at all. Travelers rig a drip before bed: a propped leaf, a throttled trickle into a gourd, anything. The valley people think this is madness. The bowl-folk think the valley is a held breath that never lets go.

The deep thing about them, the thing everything else hangs from, is this: they do not believe anything is made once. Everything is made again. Wood is a form that matter passes through — a tree becomes a post, a post becomes a walkway plank, a plank becomes firewood, firewood becomes ash, ash becomes garden, garden becomes food, food becomes a person who is, by their own plain accounting, on their ninth or tenth rebuilding of themselves. They count their age in roofs. *I am on my eleventh roof,* a woman says, and it means she has outlasted ten of her own shelters, it means sixty-some years, it means she knows exactly what she is: a pattern that persists by being relaid. Nothing in the bowl is hoarded, because the forest eats what you do not hold. Wealth is therefore two things only: dry things and favors. The dry things live in each house's dry-box, up in the smoke-warmth — tinder, salt, a blade ground down to a sliver, seed, and a coil of old lashing from the meeting-tree, which every household keeps. The favors live in people, and they are banked the way the valley banks grain. You see the ledger balance at a house-raising. When a house comes down in the night — and houses come down — the crew that gathers at first light is the moral audit of a family. A generous house gets a crowd and a new frame by midday, jokes, a feast line, children running fiber. A miser gets a slow crew. No one is ever left unsheltered — that would be unthinkable, a wrong against the town itself — but everyone can read the speed of a raising the way they read the hawk's shadow, and nobody has to say a word.

Justice runs the same channel as everything else. Water is commons, and a blocked flume is a public wrong, answered publicly — not with fines, there being nothing to fine that matters, but with work. The negligent party clears the channel at market-time, in front of everyone, while the market goes on being loud around them, and then it is finished, done, washed downstream. There is no shame-theater in it; it is maintenance, performed on a person the way it is performed on a gutter. Between houses the law is mostly the law of drips — whose roof-water falls on whose wall, whose spout is aimed wrong — and a bad neighbor is described, with full legal weight, as *bad in water*. Bigger disputes go to the meeting house, to the stone bays between the roots, where the water-warden's word on flow is nearly final and the elders' word on everything else is only nearly not. There are no locks in the bowl because there is nothing to lock that rot wouldn't take faster than a thief, and there are no secrets because every wall leaks sound. So they have invented a necessary courtesy: they grant each other deafness. Privacy here is not something you take. It is something given, a whole town politely not-hearing the grief or the quarrel or the love coming through the neighbor's wall — and the granting of it is so practiced, so automatic, that an outsider would swear the place was full of mysteries. It is full of courtesy, which looks the same from outside.

They are a tied people. Almost nothing in the bowl is nailed — metal is too dear — so everything is lashed, and knot-lore is the first curriculum. You can tell a child's age by which knots she has stopped thinking about. Adults judge one another by their lashings the way other peoples judge by handwriting or horsemanship: *his lashings hold* is a complete character reference. And they have, in this, an unlikely collaborator — the small arboreal thief that raids the stores, half-trapped, half-tolerated, fully cursed, and the reason it is only half-cursed is that it unties knots. It studies them. It picks them open in the dark, and a store-lashing found opened in the morning is a public verdict on the tier. New knots are tested against it; the ones that beat it become standard, spread through the town in a season, are taught to children as *raider-proved*. It has made them better, and they know it, and they curse it with great fluency and invention and would not be rid of it. It is the trickster of their stories, the one who steals the hawk's shadow and sells it back, the only critic whose opinion has survived every generation.

The same logic ties their marriages. A marriage is not a knot tied once; it is a knot retied. The lashing hangs on the house-post, and because everything rots, it is renewed — yearly, at the same season the meeting-house lashings are renewed, the whole town braiding fiber together in the days before the storms. *We are lashed, and we relash* — that is the vow, and it is not a metaphor to them. It is the only kind of permanence they have ever seen work. When a person falls apart — grief, sickness of the spirit, a run of loss — the idiom is that they have *come untied*, and what neighbors do, concretely and without being asked, is relash them: turn up, take the work out of their hands, sit them by the fire, hold the ends of things until they can hold again.

Fire has a genealogy. Wood rots; stone, song, and ember persist — those are the three things that outlive a decade, and of the three the ember is the most tenderly kept. Every hearth burns always, and every hearth descends from another hearth: a new household is founded with an ember carried from the parent house in a bark pouch, walking slow, shielded from the drip, and no one refuses an ember. Refusing an ember would be saying something unforgivable about the future. The meeting-house hearth is the mother-line; most fires in the bowl can name themselves daughters of it, and when a house does burn — rare, and the town's true nightmare, all that touching wood — the neighbors do not rebuild first. They re-fire first. An ember arrives before the first pole is cut. Against the nightmare stands the whole plumbing of the town: the flumes run above the walkways as fire-lines, and the gutters that nearly lace over the crowded eaves can be opened all at once, so that a fire-call sends water sheeting across every roof on the lane. The keeper of the commons tinder is, by long custom and some irony, the driest soul in the bowl — a person who hates the damp with a lifelong passion, who flinches at wet cuffs, who keeps the salt and the tinder and the fire-drill kits with the ferocity of a dragon on a hoard, and who is therefore trusted absolutely. The town's reasoning: give dryness to the one who loves it.

They do not eat frogs. The thought does not occur to them; it would be like eating the shingles off a neighbor's roof. A house is judged by its frogs — *well-frogged* is a greeting, a compliment, a state of grace — and a runnel that goes quiet brings neighbors knocking within the day, because a quiet runnel means a blockage or a sickness, either way a wrong. Children learn the pitches the way children elsewhere learn birdsong, and the small dark gutter-frog with the bronze stripe, the one that lives in the town's own channels and nowhere else on earth, is theirs in a way that goes past possession into kinship. Ask where it came from and the elders shrug: *We didn't find it. It found the town.* It is, they will tell you, the proof of the settlement — a creature that exists because they exist, as domestic as the smoke lofts. The valley people say the bowl-folk and their frog were made together, in the same batch. The bowl-folk have heard this and do not entirely disagree.

The hawk they read like weather. When its shadow crosses the bowl and the whole racket drops into that sudden total hush, everyone looks up, and everyone takes the same quiet instruction from it: check your fences. The chicken-keeper who loses a bird is not owed and does not curse; the hawk is a tax assessed on the inattentive, and the hush it lays on the town is understood as the price of the lesson, paid by everyone at once. It is one of only two silences the bowl makes. The other is grief. The bereaved household stills its runnel — blocks it down to a bare drip — and in a town where loud means right, a quiet house stops every passerby's ear like a hand on the arm. For the days of the mourning, the lane softens around that house; people come and sit in the quiet without filling it. Then comes the morning the family opens the gate again, and the water rushes, and the frogs come back within the hour — they always come back — and the sound of that is the sound of a household rejoining the chord, and everyone on the lane hears it and goes on with their work a little lighter.

The dead go over. That is the phrase: not gone, not lost — *over*. Each family saves against it all their lives, because fire is the scarcest thing in the bowl and a pyre is the greatest single expenditure a household will ever make: a stack of sound dry wood in the smoke-loft, years in the gathering, that no one touches for any lesser need. To steal from a family's death-wood is the one crime the bowl has that resembles the crimes of other places, and it is correspondingly rare and correspondingly unforgiven. The burning happens on an old stone platform near the rim, and then the ash is gathered, and carried to a flume, and poured, and the town's plumbing does what it was always, underneath everything, for: it carries the person down through the terraces, into the stream, out to the lip, and over. One member of the family stands at the very edge to watch the ash go — the only time anyone stands at the forbidden lip, and they stand there lashed, a rope around the waist held by their own people, because even death here respects the logic of the place. And then the water goes off the edge of the world, and the mist rises off the falls' base all day, and by late morning the cloud comes up the valley and pours over the rim and lies on the town like a hand on a shoulder. *There,* the elders say. *Back already.* There are bowl-dwellers — the water-warden is one, a man who says water has no memory, only paths — who hold that the cloud is only cloud. The elders do not argue. *It's also your grandmother,* they say, and the warden, who loved his grandmother, opens the morning gates and does not answer.

Children belong to the water first. Weir-duty is their curriculum — opening and closing the town's valves morning and evening, learning hydraulics the way other children learn letters, by playing in it — and a child's weir-mistake makes a small flood and a small flood is forgiven exactly once per child per season, which everyone understands to be the tuition. Around their eighth year they are walked, all of that year's children together, to the overlook above the falls' lip, and shown the green slick rock and the three threads being born, and they swear the oath with a thumbprint of hearth-ash on the wrist — the first ash they carry is their own promise — and the oath holds, it has always held, because every one of them has looked at that rock and understood it instantly and completely. After that they are trusted with the ladders, the eaves, the market, the foxfire log they dare each other past at dusk, the termite nights when the winged millions rise and every child in the bowl is out gorging with the birds and the bats, sticky-fingered and screaming with joy. After storms, when the frog-bloom comes and the paths themselves seem to hop, the children walk soft and sweep the thresholds and carry strays back to the channels, and no one tells them to. It is simply what you do. They would no more step on a frog than on their own fingers.

The old are the town's wealth and the town knows it down to the stones. There are not many of them — the damp takes lungs, the ladders take the unlucky, a life of work takes the rest — and so the ones who last are kept like dry tinder, given the warmest loft-side sleeping places in the bad wet spells, fed the first of everything. The Eldest is blind, has been blind a decade, and walks the bowl by its sounds with more certainty than the sighted, whom she pities openly. *You bump into what you can't hear,* she says. She can stand at any footing-stone in town and name the families ten rebuildings back; she corrected the water-warden last year on the pitch of the market gutter, from memory, and was right. On song-nights in the meeting house she leads the name-songs, the long recitations that attach the dead to their stones and the stones to their families, and this is the archive, the whole of it — wood rots, and only stone and song and ember persist, and so the town keeps its history in the one medium the bowl cannot rot. Ask her how old the settlement is and she gives the answer the stones give: *It was here. It has always been the age it is.* She is on her twenty-first roof. She says she is not finished being rebuilt.

Some of them leave. The ladder-runners go down with the feather-bundles and the resin and the dyed cordage, and come back up with blades and salt and news, and they are a slightly separate breed — down-eyed, valley-tinged, the strongest calves in the bowl and the most restless sleepers in it. Now and then a young person goes down and does not come back, and the town has a phrase for that too: *gone down*, said without shame and with a particular ache, the way you speak of weather you can't fix. Those who marry in are *relaid* — the word for a footing-stone set again in its place — and it is the highest welcome they know how to give, the one they use for adoption and for the sick who recover: set again, in the same town, in the same bowl, part of the pattern that outlasts every piece of it. A guest is read, gently and instantly, by two things: their lashings and their walk on wet stone. They are dried by the fire, fed the sour bread and the smoked fish and the crab, given the drip-rig if they cannot sleep, and sent home down the ladders with a coil of dyed cord and woodsmoke in their clothes that no valley washing will ever fully take out.

By full dark the bowl is hearth-glow and the people are at their fires doing the evening round — feet to the heat, every member of the household inspected, ash rubbed into the wet-worn spots, because foot-rot is the one enemy that never sleeps; tools up into the smoke-warmth; the day's lashings eyeballed; the ember banked under its blanket of ash for the night. Someone is singing a name-song under their breath. Somewhere a night household keeps the pestles dropping, and the sound is the bowl's own heart, and if it ever stopped, six hundred people would wake in the same breath. The chorus comes up around them like water filling a channel, layered past counting, and they sleep in it the way they live in the wet — as a medium, as an element — with the cloud lying over the town like a hand on a shoulder, and the dead in the cloud, and the water running through everything toward the edge of the world. The water leaves. Everything else in the bowl is a way of staying, and the people are the ones who hold it: not the stream, not the stones, but the thing between. They are the weirs.
