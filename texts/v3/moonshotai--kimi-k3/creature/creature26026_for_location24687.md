No house in the Underkiln stands empty. That is the first thing a census would find, if the city kept one: every dwelling, from the seep-side terraces to the highest ring beneath the vault, holds its single resident — one bowl, centered in the main room, the door left open. Occupancy is total. It is the houses that are large and the residents that are small, and the distances between them are not absence but courtesy: the spacing a loud world owes its quiet citizens, so that each may be heard entire.

The bowls are the builders. Whatever the builders were, they were of this world and no other — boneless, waterless, warm — and when their district's kiln-heart received them, they burned clean and gave back a palmful of mineral color: iron red, sulfur yellow, ash white. The same palette as the walls, because the people and the city were one material wearing two forms. Laid into glaze, each of them became the color of themselves, and no two alike, and the bowls were set in the emptied rooms with the doors open so that the breath could reach them. A bowl is a resonator. A room built to hold one bowl and nothing else is a resonator's resonator — the bowl's body, the way the bell is the city's. At midday, when the chord is organ-full and felt in the ribs, the bowls answer: each giving back its own partial, too high and too close to its neighbors to be heard alone, so that the dead are audible the way a crowd is audible inside a chord — as shimmer, as the glitter riding the towers' notes. When their district fires, they fire with it. The lichen lace burns off them, their glaze re-vitrifies, and their voices come back brighter, clarified by the same flame that made them. Nothing here is preserved. Everything is maintained, the dead included. That is the arrangement: they are kept, and they are kept in tune.

One resident does not sing. In the highest terrace, in the last and smallest room, the bowl of unfired clay sits slumped exactly where it dried. The chord enters her room like any other — courtesy is not rationed here — fills it, and takes nothing away. It is the one true silence in the city, a rest the music flows around. And by this world's gentle inversion, she is the most durable citizen of all. Elsewhere, unfired clay is the fragile thing, the thing waiting to be made permanent. Here there has never been water to slake it, and nothing to rot it — rot is another water-word with no referent — so the one unfinished person in the Underkiln is the one who will certainly last: the newest resident and the surest, dried into her slump for the age of the world. At the fullest hour, hers is the room to stand in, if you want to hear the chord's shape. You hear it by the space it leaves for her.

Above them all, the rest of the population is still at work. The gutter-spouts are cupped hands — thousands of them, no two alike, sized for every age the city ever held. Whether they were cast from life or shaped in likeness, they are the builders' other body: the dead rest below as bowls, but their gesture works the vault, cupping the night-dew out of the condensing dark and passing it down, hand over hand, terrace over terrace, to the gardens. It is the deep grammar of the place. The leaves are troughs, the flowers trumpets, the gourds rattles, the rooms hold bowls, the spouts are hands, the bell itself is a bowl turned over, and the white pan above is a basin the size of a sea. A vessel is a thing shaped to hold, and anything shaped to hold can be made to ring — the two gifts are one gift, and it is the gift of everything that lives here: capacity, and resonance.

The eldest citizen grew here before there was a city to be eldest in. The mother tree over the seep — hollow-trunked, wide as a tower, her roots fused with the black tile into a single organ — was already sounding her bass across the hollows of her crown when the first kiln was built at her roots to burn the seep's gas. The flue-towers were tuned, at the founding, to the note she was already making. The founding act was not building; it was listening. Everything since has been accompaniment. All the glazed channels of the great still end, terrace by terrace, at her basin; every offering the hands pass down arrives at her; she is the delta of the only water-cycle this world runs. She remembers the way everything here remembers — by continuing. Her grain darkens at every firing she has stood through, widens at every tide of the seep, and when the tide deepens her bass for a season, the whole city re-voices itself around her: this doorway falls silent, that one begins to whistle, and the population hears the season turn as a slow migration of the flutings through the streets.

The garden is the city's present tense. Every gourd-tree stands where a seed's rolling stopped on a drum-plaza, so the planting of the whole city is a score printed on the ground — the chord's own handwriting, sown by the drumming of its steps. The wax-tongued leaves catch the night-drip; the dry fruit rattles when the music swells, and the swelling shakes the seed loose: the harvest. The flowers aim their trumpets at the flue-towers the way flowers elsewhere aim at the sun, and the flues are this garden's suns — heat and voice rising together. The flowers open to heat, which means a district in flame is a district in bloom: the grazer arrives and the meadow flowers, the gourds crack in the firing heat, the seeds wake in the same flame that clears their parents. A firing is a birthday. And the seeds asleep in the floor-dust are the future population, patient as everything here is patient, waiting a century if need be for their district's turn under the fire.

The moss keeps the quiet places. Felted, silver, matting the drip-lines, it is the first to touch every drop the hands pass down, and beneath it the tick and ping of the breathing clay falls to nothing. The drip-lines are the city's hush — small soft rooms strung along the veins — and nothing uses them, and they are kept anyway, the way the plazas are kept empty around their single trees: as spacing, as rests. A city this loud needs its silences spaced as wide as its voices.

The slowest citizen is the lichen, and it is the only one whose life looks like work. Grain by grain, century by century, it etches the glaze into pale lace — it mines the walls' frozen light, unmakes the city at a pace no eye could catch, even if eyes had ever existed here. It does not distinguish wall from bowl; the dead are laced along with their streets, and fired clean along with them. And the lace drinks sound: a freshly fired district rings like a struck bowl, then softens, decade by decade, as the etching spreads. The lichen is the city's century-long exhale; the fire is its inhale. Which is why, though no one is counting, the count is kept. The lichen works the districts' valve-seals at the same unhurried rate, and when the lace finally breaches a seal, the gas floods the passages, finds the crossroads flames, and the district fires. The slowest citizen is the clock of the fastest. The fire arrives when the lichen finishes counting to a number no one chose.

Fire is the only animal this world has ever had, and it lives here the way herds live elsewhere. At rest it is the flock at the crossroads — the low amber jets, scattered, grazing on the seep-lines' thin breath. Gathered, it is the slow orange rotation of a district firing, a season of flame walking the streets at a pace a plant can plan for. It eats the old growth, the lace, the etching, and its eating is the maintenance; the garden planned for it before anyone was here to plan. While a district burns, its flues roar, and the chord gains a rough voice for a season, and the neighboring drum-plazas carry the tremor, and the dead of that district come out of the flame brighter-voiced for the century to come. It has never been afraid. Nothing here has ever been afraid.

And over, under, and through all of them, the citizen that is all of them: the chord. Sound does the work of hands here — it sows the seeds across the drum-plazas, tends the garden's spacing, harvests the gourds when it swells, and remembers the founding tuning by the only method this world trusts, which is continuation. It is also the community. Every inhabitant has a part in it: the tree the bass, the towers the stops — with the city's whole fortune of black copper hung in their throats, the bells the metal was spared for — the doorways the flutes, the gourds the rattle, the seeds the drums, the bowls the high shimmer, the fire the roar, the moss the rests, the one unfired bowl the silence all the rest is measured against. Even the lichen plays: the slow detuning, the fire's retuning, a vibrato a century wide. Inhabited, after a fashion — and the fashion is harmony. A chord is what a population sounds like when every voice is given room enough to be itself.

So the census of the Underkiln: a district of the dead, glazed and singing; a working population of hands in the vault; one tree older than the city, and a garden of trees sown in her hearing; a flock of fire on its century-rotation; a hush of moss along the veins; a scribe of lichen, writing and erased and writing; a seed-bank of the unborn, sleeping toward the flame; and in the highest, smallest room, the last builder, keeping the silence she was left. Every house occupied. No one crowded. No one missing.

From the white pan, all of it — the dead, the garden, the fire, the hands, the silence — fits up a knee-high chimney as a single sustained note. Put your ear to the warm rim and the note has a shape, and the shape has parts, and every part is someone. It is not a cry for company. It is a full house, singing.
