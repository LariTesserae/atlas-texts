**The Keeper**

The Kettle's census is short, and it does not balance. Two swifts, seasonal. Midges in their thousands, but only over the two warmest pools. White snails the size of a fingernail. A blind citizenry of isopods in the cistern. Ballooning spiders, most dying of the thin nights, a few persisting in the hut's corners. That is the whole zoology, and it does not explain the place — because the drums on the chart recorder get changed, and the crate fills, years deep, and someone carries the wound drums there and stacks them. The sheared pipe ends are re-spliced in couplings cut by hand and wrapped, before clamping, in strips of shirt-cloth. The third pen never runs dry. The mint is cropped back from the door. Above the chart drum, where the first law says a drip should fall, a tin has been fixed to catch it, and the tin is emptied daily. Nothing here ever dries except by fire — and by use. Against the hut's east wall, where the first sun lands, there is a crate with a dry top. It is dry because it has been sat on, daily, at dawn, for forty years: friction and habit, the slowest fire.

This is the second law of the Kettle, and it takes longer to learn than the first: nothing here winds down that isn't wound.

The log book, soft as bread, holds the whole biography. Its early pages are kept in three hands, rotating monthly, stiff with the service's abbreviations. The middle pages narrow to two. Then there is a page with a single entry in ink — RELIEF PARTY DUE 14TH — and beneath it, in pencil, in the hand the next forty years will teach you: 14TH: NO RELIEF. DECK CLOSED. A season later: CABLE PARTED 02:10. SOUNDED D. And then the entry that became the job description: CONTINUING.

The third hand in the log is the junior one. It is also the last one. At Station No. 4, promotion was by attrition.

The keeper is small — probably always was — and the work has made them wide instead. Layers of wool gone green at the seams; not dye, nothing here is dyed, but the dew point working for decades on lanolin, an algae taking the cuffs the way lichen takes the young rock. The hands have closed partway around forty years of wrench and do not entirely open again; they have taken the shape of the tools the way the moss takes the shape of the ledge. The eyes do not squint — forty years of the white room's sourceless brightness has ground the squint out of them. The keeper is warm. Warm the way the rock is warm: from within, at midnight, in rain. The midges can find them in the dark. When the keeper sits still — and the keeper can sit still the way the cairn sits still — the white snails cross them at their own geology, and are not discouraged.

The day is built on the station's scaffolding. In the clear hour after sunrise there is smoke from the hut, the one vertical thing in a place of horizontal cloud, and on the stove, the kettle. Of course the Kettle has a kettle. Its whistle is sounded once a day and only once, over the cloud-sea, at a pitch the pelton wheel can't reach. For all practical purposes the whistle is the keeper's voice; the keeper's actual voice is kept for the swifts, and is in poor repair. Tea on the east crate, in the dry seat, while the crag casts its shape on nothing.

Then the round, legible in the moss, which gives everything away: a fist deep over every ledge except along one route, where it is kept to a knuckle — saddle to hut, hut to cistern, cistern to gantry, gantry to the two warm pools, back along the seep. The cistern is dipped and regarded. The chart drum is changed during the generator's hour, because that is the loud hour, and the keeper does all the hammering then too, out of courtesy to the drips. The pens are serviced; the red one is refilled with powder ink cut with seep water, so that the pressure of the air is recorded in a trace that rusts — the diary entering itself, slowly, into the mineral record. On windless days, when the cups turn anyway on the heat rising off the rock and the pen writes a breeze that never was, the keeper corrects the record in the margin: NONE. The machine says wind. The keeper, standing in the stillness, is the other instrument. As for the roots shouldering into the pipe joints, the keeper clears them only when the pressure falls below a line drawn in the log long ago, and not always then. The negotiation between plumbing and moss is courteous; the keeper is its notary, not its referee.

After forty years the keeper is the best instrument on the mountain. The joints write the pressure. The sleep writes the temperature. The black pen's small tremors are felt first in the body and only confirmed by the trace. Nobody reads the drums, and the keeper does not read them either, not anymore — the keeper files them, which is the handling of testimony at arm's length. Somebody must do it, and the service, which has been folded and renamed and forgotten, and has forgotten Station No. 4 in return, is not sending anybody. It is an unrequited arrangement. So are the pens. The keeper keeps faith on their behalf.

The keeper does not run the station; the station runs, and the keeper accompanies it. The distinction would be insisted on, if anything here were insisted on aloud. A tired relay's clack goes flat a half-day before it fails, and the keeper is there with the spring before the relay knows it needs one. The pelton wheel is nursed. The diesel is rationed at a rate that assumes no resupply before the heat-death of the service. The anemometer is indulged in its fantasies. Of the four screws on the brass plate, three are rusted to stubs; the fourth is newer than the others by decades. It is not discussed.

Then there are the other lives, and the diplomacy. The hut's corners are deeded to the spiders each autumn, when the guy-wires silver with balloonists; most die, and the keeper does not sweep. The isopods are the keeper's constituency twice over — it was the keeper, junior then, who packed the theodolite in moss, in the early pages, and the eggs rode up in the packing, and the tank has had a citizenry ever since. The log reports on them annually: TANK CITIZENRY: WELL. The midges are fed, and this is deliberate: ten minutes a day at the pool edge, forearms bare, the only warm blood in the place offered to the only crowd of anything living — alternate days at alternate pools, because a crowd should be kept fairly. It is not in the log; some duties predate paperwork. The keeper eats the kale, which has become a stand of green antlers, and the rhubarb from the seep edge, and takes mint tea the way the overflow takes mint, which is constantly. The lushness of the Kettle is partly heirloom; so is the keeper's diet. In this sense the keeper is one of the garden's perennials — the one that does the weeding.

But the swifts are the year. In season the gantry is off-limits to everything but swifts, by the only ordinance the Kettle has. When they are gone, the keeper climbs and reads the nest like a letter: feathers, a casing, the news. When they return, the keeper stands on the saddle at dusk and lets the screaming go through them at full throttle, and answers once, in a voice gone to rust, and the swifts scream louder, and that is the conversation of the year. The audience that stopped coming was human. The audience that comes back every year is not, and the keeper has transferred the institution's hospitality accordingly. It is not a lonely arrangement. Loneliness requires an absent audience, and the Kettle is full of present ones — the citizenry, the crowd, the pens at all hours, the swifts in season.

If you come — and almost no one does; the lower station is somewhere under the cloud and the wire is down — you will not see the keeper. The keeper will see you, and note your breath going white and your boots leaking at the knife-edges, and enter you in the log: VISITOR, ONE — filed under WEATHER, because you will pass like weather. The Kettle does not keep visitors. But it keeps.

Forty years at the dew point, and the first law applies to the keeper now as to everything else here: they never entirely dry. Beads stand on them at noon as on the leaves. They are rusting in thin rinds where the weather has had time to try. The job, if anyone asked, has had three phases: first to measure the mountain; then, though the log never says so, to witness it; now, simpler, to keep it company. And the mountain, which rises a few patient centimeters a year and ticks like a stove whose fire hasn't gone out, keeps the keeper — at its dew point, in its weather, warm at midnight. They are keeping each other. It is the oldest arrangement on the summit, and the only one the cloud can't take back.

Come back in a decade. The round will take an hour longer. The kale will have taken another meter of the saddle and been cropped back. The note of the severed cable will be flatter, and the log will say so — the keeper has been writing it down all along, a half-tone at a time, NOTE NOW C-SHARP, the slowest measurement the station keeps, kept by ear. The hands will have closed a degree further around the tools. The entry will read what it has read for forty years: CONTINUING. And at dawn, in the clear hour, the kettle on the stove will whistle once over the cloud-sea — the one note in the Kettle's loudness that is neither hydrological nor mechanical, but respiratory. The keeper's voice, on loan.
