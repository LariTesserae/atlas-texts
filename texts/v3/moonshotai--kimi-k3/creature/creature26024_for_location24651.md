The census begins with one name and very nearly ends with it.

Take any square mile of the plateau and weigh what lives in it, and ninety-nine parts in a hundred will be the Felt. The remaining part — the grazers, the birds, the eels, the mice, the midges, the uncounted pore-fauna — is, by mass, a rounding error. Whether the Felt is one organism is a question that has absorbed whole expeditions and answered nothing. It is clonal, certainly; continuous, demonstrably; older than the ice ages by its own archive's evidence. It has organs — the pump-knots, the ducts, the vents, the Choir pipes, the Glow-Rot seams where it digests its own depths — and it has habits, which is a word naturalists use when they cannot say *intentions*. It prefers to place its rare events at unreadable range. It recalls everything it issues. It notices pressure: stand still long enough and the throb beneath your boots shifts, some pump-knot a half-mile down adjusting its stroke around the fact of you. It flowers, pointlessly and constantly — the lint-pale tufts of cotton grass are the Felt in bloom, its only flower, a billion blossoms of a single plant nodding for a wind that carries their pollen nowhere it needs to go. Nothing punishes the creatures that steal from it. The Felt has no punishments. It has processes.

Its plumbing has passengers. In the warm ducts ride midge larvae bound for the vents; in the cold ones, young eels bound for the eyes; and in the deep black slurry, pale soft translucent things that match nothing on the surface and are never seen except in eruption spatter, where they live for minutes. The Felt tolerates them the way a body tolerates its flora — which is to say it is entirely possible they *are* its flora, and that the distinction between resident and organ is the first thing a visitor has to give up.

After the Felt, the lease terms, because they govern everything else: nothing built, nothing stored, nothing protruding. No burrows, no dams, no hoards, no webs — there are spiders here, hunting on foot in the cotton grass, and not one of them spins. Everything a resident owns, it carries. Every address is temporary except the plateau's. Live here as luggage, or not for long.

The mowers are the largest things that fully accept these terms. Humped, wide-footed, dark, they move in herds of a dozen to two hundred across a plain with no edges, grazing the mat down in slow lines, and the Felt closes behind them like water behind a boat. Their hooves are broad as dinner plates and splay broader under load, distributing their weight over the saturated skin; their lips crop without tearing; and inside each of them runs a small warm copy of the plateau's own engine — a foregut where Felt-tissue is broken down by strains of the Felt's own working microbes, so that a mower is, in a real sense, a vent that walks. On the coldest nights the herd stands in a huddle and each animal breathes out a faint steam, the only warmth in the world except the plumes', carried around in bodies.

Each herd keeps a note — a lowing pitched to carry in the cold dense air, foghorn-deep, sourceless as the light — and neighboring herds tune away from one another, so that on a still night you can count how many herds share your horizon by counting notes. They steer by the Choir when it is near and by the lean of the plumes when it is not, and when die-back retunes the Choir the old routes lie wrong for a while; you find confident hoof-pits walking out into empty mat, following a chord that has moved. Nothing hunts them. Their predator is the ground, and it does not chase. The old ones do not fall — they stop. The herd walks on, and the mat begins its courtesies within the hour. Calves walk within the hour too. Nothing with sense lies down twice in the same place.

Below the mowers, the small lives. In the cotton-grass tufts, the felt-mice: seed-eaters, the Felt's only thieves, taking their tithe of a flowering meant for the wind. Over them, at knee height, hunts the low owl, pale and silent, the one hunter that casts no shadow because there are no shadows to cast; in a land without trees everything perches low or not at all, and the owls roost standing in the sedge, awake-looking, which is the local way. And beneath all of these, the true multitudes: the pore-fauna. The mat is not solid; at a mite's scale it is a cavern system, and it is full. Squeeze a double handful and it weeps a tea of springtails, thread-worms, midge-lings, rotifers — an acre of plateau holds more animal lives than a week of walking will show you vertebrates. The library's catalogue is enormous, yes, but most of its books are these, shelved a thousand to the handful, read by no one, renewed forever.

The eyes have their own tenancy. The eye-eels are pale, blind-seeming, glycoprotein-blooded, unhurried, and possibly deathless in the local style — nothing here dies of age; things are recalled. They endure the lockings without comment. A touched eye freezes whole, eel and all, and holds until the weak noon thaw, and the eel resumes its cruise having spent an hour as statuary; some eyes freeze and slump a hundred times a year, and the eels do not appear to keep count. At some age — the guess is centuries — an eel leaves. It comes out over the rim on a needle-ice night, when the mat's skin is wet, and goes overland, a pale hand's-length of purpose, and every eel in every eye goes the same way: to the Mill-Race. Down the spiral, into the swallow-hole, gone. Nothing comes back up the Race. But at certain seeps, kilometers off and days later, the mat issues young eels — gently, no dome, no boom, a wet giving at a seep-spring — and what they do in the years between the swallow and the spring is the plateau's own business. The Felt runs a courier service through its depths, and the postage is paid in a currency nobody has named.

Then the vents: the only warm addresses in the world, and every living thing knows the way to its nearest one. The midges live their whole adult lives in an hour of steam, boiling up in fierce brief columns out of the warmth, and the stack above them is exact and total. Plume-swifts work the columns — pale, sickle-winged, born in crevices of the vent-rind they did not make, which is the only acceptable architecture — and there are swifts that have not touched the ground in years; there may be swifts that never have. They feed in the updraft, squabble in it, sleep in it half a brain at a time, held aloft like a held note. Above and inside the steam hunts the steam-hawk, nearly white, blind in the plume like everything else, hunting by wingbeat — so that the one refuge and the one hunter are the same color, and safety is only a matter of which sound you make. In the vent-lee, where the steam rains out as the world's only warm water, the churn-gulls keep their raucous roost-towns, and everything that cannot stand the plain's cold sleeps there in rotation. The vents have lifespans. A throat that has burned for a century throttles down over a seasonless decade and chokes, and an hour's walk away the mat splits a new one, and then there is a moving day — unhurried, total — the rind-tenants carried or lost, the swifts simply sleeping a different column by nightfall.

The gulls roost at the vents but earn their name at the eruptions, and they find the eruptions by watching one another. Every gull watches the gulls at the edge of its sight, which are watching others, so that a rise anywhere propagates across a day's flight of country in minutes — a telegraph of appetite, and when you see gulls pass overhead all going one way, going hard, you are seeing news travel. They eat the half-made first, the incompletely issued; then the stallers, once they stall. They make no distinctions. A reissued walker and a reissued mower are the same to them: meat that has stopped moving. There is no evidence that this harms them, no evidence of anything about it, and the gulls are not telling.

As for the reissued themselves — they are residents the way a broadcast is a resident, but they recur, and any honest census must list them. They sort into kinds. The walkers, which come up mid-errand and complete a quarter mile of it. The loopers, running a few strides of some old circuit — courtship, alarm, flight — at wrong speeds, missing pieces. The stallers, which stop mid-motion and hold for hours, weathering like statues in accelerated rain. Once, by a surveyor's twice-retold account, a single dome gave up a chase: something low and quick still pursuing something fleet, ten thousand years dead the pair of them, and for eleven strides the hunt resumed across the new mat, correct in every particular, before both stalled at once, as if the spool ran out. They stood an hour, teeth at throat-fur, not touching, and slumped together. The gulls waited. Even appetite has its etiquette here. And there are chorus-domes: an eruption that issues a flock entire, dozens of small bird-shapes from some assembly with no name, which do not fly but sing — every call at once, a dawn chorus out of a century nobody knows, pouring from dark still throats for the length of a held breath. The plateau keeps its own recordings. This is how they are played.

Very occasionally what comes up is a person. Walkers who lay down somewhere on the mat, once, and were kept. They come up tanned dark, mid-journey, and they walk — a quarter mile, a mile — and they do not see you; whatever they see, it is not this. Nobody follows them to the slump. There are debts owed to the dead of this place and one of them is their privacy.

Every species reads the sucked-quiet, and they all read it right. The mowers lift their heads and simply leave, unhurried, a walking that does not look like flight and is. The swifts pour out of the plume and hang far off. The midges thin to nothing. The mice go down into the pores, which is the wrong direction, but it is their direction. The gulls, alone, come closer. And the Felt cannot go anywhere. Whatever the quiet is — a drawing-in, a gathering of self before the dome — it is the one moment the Felt is knowable, and all you know then is: hold on.

The catalogue's last entry is a question mark. There is the track-line found twice a decade, stride too long for any mower, the pits filling slowly with black water. There is the shape at the limit of sight that will not declare its size, that the Felt declines to place any closer. Perhaps it is only the oldest bull, solitary and strange. Perhaps the archive is not the only thing down there with a deep address. The plateau keeps its largest resident the way it keeps distance: as a rumor, unretracted.

Your own kind is not on the list. No one lives here; there is nothing to build with and nothing to build on, and the lease forbids it anyway. People pass — surveyors, pilgrims, the curious, the tired — and some come at the end, on purpose, because the archive is the one immortality on offer anywhere: lie down, be kept intact, and perhaps one day be issued for an hour more of walking. It is not life. The plateau does not pretend otherwise.

So take the census properly, standing on it at night. The throb: resident. The plume on the horizon: resident, with tenants asleep in its updraft. The seams burning dim green to the unreadable edge: the house digesting its own cellar. The gulls asleep in the vent-lee; the eels cruising their still water between lockings; the mice running their tiny errands; the owl going over at knee height without a sound; the herds standing in their huddle, breathing their small steam, each herd with its note held somewhere in the dark. And underfoot, kilometers deep, the rest of the guest list — tanned, intact, oldest at the bottom, every specimen kept, waiting on a retrieval that is corrupt, unindexed, and generous beyond geometry. It is a full house. It has been a full house since the mountains stopped being mountains, and no one here is waiting for morning, because morning is only the world clearing its throat — and somewhere far off, at whatever distance the plateau has decided distance means tonight, something gives its long cracking boom, and the gulls, who were asleep, are rising.
