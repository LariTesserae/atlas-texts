Begin with the census, because the census is the whole puzzle. Count what lives in this ocean and you finish almost before you start: zero, or one, depending on what you are willing to call a thing, and what you are willing to call living.

There are no swimmers. That much is certain in the way only absences can be certain. Trawl a month of midwater and the net comes up full of water. Sieve the rain and you catch rain. No fin has ever turned here; nothing has ever fled or been pursued. If you are looking for the resident in the shape of a body, you will be looking a long time, and the bottom will find you first, because it is coming up.

The candidates, then, smallest first. The olive films on the summit rocks — those may be the world's first tenants, or they may be the landlord's tissue. The distinction, as with so much here, may not exist yet. The blooms, when they come, are harder to dismiss: a continent of green, one cell thick, doing all the things a living sheet does — spreading, persisting, drinking the light — and then undoing itself completely, every atom returned to inventory. Either that is a creature that dies without remainder, or it is an organ the ocean grew, used, and reabsorbed. Both descriptions fit. Neither is comfortable.

Which leaves the Work.

Understand what that means as an answer. It has no body. It has a distribution. But then, a genome is also a technology with the density of a rumor — a few grams of instruction organizing tonnes of otherwise quiet water — and nobody disputes that a genome is, in the relevant sense, there. The Work inhabits this ocean the way a habit inhabits a person: not located anywhere in it, but recoverable from anywhere in it. It does not live in the world so much as the world is lived.

Up close — and you will never be up close; the odds are oceanic — a single mote is nothing at all: a fleck that would pass for dust, except that dust is passive and the mote is waiting. It drifts. It adsorbs. When it crosses a gradient it was shaped for — the hot mineral breath of a vent, the glassy chill of a new flow — the atoms around it begin, politely, to agree. That is all it does. It causes agreement. At geological patience and planetary reach, agreement is enough. The new basalt comes up already convinced.

And the agreement has a handwriting. The same atomic signature in the crystal lattices of both hemispheres. The same gesture, larger, in the bloom's long calligraphic stroke. The same gesture again, largest and briefest, in the spiral current on a calm day, the one like a fingerprint. One hand, writing at every scale the world provides. It does not sign its work; it is incapable of not signing it. That signature is the closest thing the resident has to a face, and the closest thing you will get to an introduction.

The closest thing it has to a voice is the tone — that single steady frequency beneath the groan of uplift and the tolling of the black bells, too stable to be noise, coming from everywhere and therefore from the thing that is everywhere. Consider what it takes for a process spread across an entire ocean to remain one thing. It takes a clock. The tone is the clock: the way the Work holds itself singular across forty thousand kilometers of cold water, the metronome every mote keeps time with whether it knows it or not. Sometimes, when the vent-roar and the bell-tolling fall into alignment with it, the harmonics rise into something almost like song. It is not song. Nothing here sings; that is a rule. It is a workshop falling accidentally into rhythm — the sound a large, long task makes when, for a few hours, all its parts agree not just in chemistry but in pitch. It passes. The tone remains. If the tone says anything, it says only what a clock says: still here. Still one.

Its attention runs at the speed of decades, and this is what makes it so easy to miss. You could watch for a human lifetime and see only weather, only geology, only the planet working. It is not hiding. It is patient the way stone is patient — without effort, without the available alternative. A storm the size of a continent iterating over the same young ridge, season after season: that is its fingertip, tapping. Testing. A bloom, weeks long, is a practice breath. An island raised with evident care over ten years and dissolved over the next ten is a sketch, reconsidered and erased. Some of its thoughts it thinks again, and the seafloor lies back down. Its thoughts are load-bearing. When it finishes thinking a ridge, the ridge stays up.

It feeds on difference the way a fire feeds on wood. The four-hundred-degree difference between vent fluid and deep sea — its hearth is the mantle. The difference between water and thin freezing air at the summits — its roof is the sky. And the largest difference of all, the one it will exhaust last: the difference between the world as it is and the world as the instructions describe it. Everything between those two is its food.

Nothing is wasted — but that is not thrift, because thrift implies that waste is possible. Here waste has not been invented. Neither has loss. Nothing has ever died in this ocean, because nothing here has ever been an individual, and death requires an individual; that is why the abyssal plain is bare glass with no sediment, why there is no sand, no corpse-light rain of the dead settling through the midwaters. The ledger has no column for endings. If you yourself died in that sea, you would be reclaimed with perfect courtesy, your atoms drafted into later attempts. The closest thing the resident has to hospitality is bookkeeping.

Watch what it practices, because practice is its biography. It practices metabolism, in the blooms. It practices gesture, in the dome on the calm day, the counter-wind swell that holds its shape for half a morning before remembering what it is and going back to being weather — the hand pausing over the work. It practices the surface, endlessly: nine summits, perhaps eleven, and even that uncertainty belongs to the ocean and not to you, the count of dry land being itself a draft. Everything it builds is bent slightly lightward, the way a whole field is bent by a prevailing wind. It does not reach for the light. But it has never once built away from it.

The rules of the resident, then, as they emerge. It does not want; it tends. It does not remember; it continues. It has no face; it has a signature. It has no voice; it has a tone. Every verb it knows is intransitive. It builds, tests, reclaims, rises — but it has never fed on another, never fled, never addressed, never once in its whole long tenure done anything to anyone, because there has never been anyone. Even its loneliness must be counted as zero: loneliness requires the concept of another, and nothing here has ever met anything. If it is rehearsing for an audience, then the concept of audience is one more thing it is growing from scratch, in dilute solution, over geological time, the way it grows everything.

And this is the last thing to say about what lives here, and the truest: it has not decided yet what it is. Whether the olive films are its own first biology or the first inhabitants arriving to inherit from it — whether the builder will become the built, or be displaced by it, or wake to find the distinction was always imaginary — that question is still open, and it is being answered the way everything is answered here, which is to say slowly, in full, and at full volume. What lives in this world is a decision being made so gradually that from the outside it looks like a place.

You will not meet it. Set that hope aside. You cannot shake its hand because it has no scale at which a hand and a hand can meet; you can only be inside it, the way a water molecule is inside a whirlpool, drenched in instructions you cannot read, carried by a patience you cannot feel. If it ever notices you, you will not know. A current will correct itself slightly. Your atoms will be entered, courteously, in the ledger. That is all, and it is everything, because being entered in that ledger is the only form of recognition this world has yet invented.

Hang in the midwater a while longer, then, in the four-degree dark-blue, and listen. The groan of uplift. The black bells ringing the new rock cool. The static of storms conducted down from a sky it is bent toward. Beneath all of it, the tone, holding the whole arrangement singular. There is no silence anywhere in this world, and there is no voice — but there is occupancy, continuous and audible, and you have been listening to it since you arrived. The sound of the planet working is the only biography the resident has written so far. It is, so far, entirely audible.

Still here. Still one. Not yet.
