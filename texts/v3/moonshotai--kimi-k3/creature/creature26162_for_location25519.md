You will have been told that nothing lives here. The telling is wrong, and wrong in an instructive way: it is a failure of category, not of census. Things live here. The census is kept in works.

The ticking you hear everywhere — the soft ceramic pinging of things cooling, warming, deciding — is not noise. It is the sound of a census that never finishes being taken. Every ping is a small announcement: *still here, still mine, still true.*

Begin with the standing population, the ones you have already met and mistaken for scenery.

The lone chimney is a hearth-keeper. It keeps hearth the way a lighthouse keeps light: the absence of a house is, to a chimney, a temporary condition. Its smoke is the smoke of a banked fire, tended against need. If the chimney that stood a league off last firing is closer now, do not be alarmed and do not be flattered. It has been *seeing about* you. Not watching — nothing here watches — seeing about, the way a neighbor sees about a fence rail. In time it will drift elsewhere. Chimneys visit. That is their whole society: a slow round of calls across the empty plazas, each trailing its thin line of domestic smoke, each keeping in good order a hearth whose house has not yet been recalled from the plan.

Stairs expect. The great stair awaiting its tower is the eldest kind of citizen, an expectant. Climb it — counting is useless; the count differs by direction and both counts are correct — and from the top you can see a little further into the murk. That is what it is for. Some citizens are for looking. It has expected its tower for longer than walking memory, and expectation has become its character the way salt becomes a crust: completely, and without resentment.

Arches attend. An arch is a doorway's soul, and its work is welcome, and it does that work for the steam, the squalls, the rare walker. Stand beneath one for a while. You will feel it: the mild ceremony of being admitted to nowhere. It is not mockery. Every procession that will ever pass through is already, in the arch's accounting, on its way.

The long low walls — forty leagues, knee-high — are the patient ones. A foundation line is what a city looks like when it is still all appetite.

And the pre-ruins are the elders. Nothing ages here, so age is appointed: the world makes its old ones fresh and sharp-edged, rubble mortared into a considered collapse, and the other works treat them accordingly. Instruments consult them. The plumb bob that has swung for a year in the lee of a pre-ruin, in air that has never moved, is not broken and not haunted. It is a question under advisement. Some questions take a year here. The bob is counting.

That is the second order of the population: the magistracy of measures. The geometry votes, which means someone holds office. A plumb line hangs off true and the floor accepts — that is not error, that is a ruling. Each level has its parish; the bubble at the end of the glass is law there, and crossing a plaza you can feel the jurisdiction change in your knees. Trowels are senior. They are always warm because they are always just out of session, and they are never where they were left because they ride circuit. If you need a tool, do not search for one. Stand at a half-laid course and wait. One will be along, in its own time, like a magistrate making rounds.

Third: the hod lines. The bloodstream. The pilgrims. They are not carried; they process — single file, an inch an hour, each brick warm, and one day you will understand why they are warm, and it will neither comfort nor frighten you. It will simply fit, the way the print fits. They glean the glaze deserts after a firing, flaking the black scales courteously. They translate the salt rosettes of the efflorescence gardens into young walls, so that a new wall may carry an ancestor. They arrive at a recalled wall the way relatives arrive to help one of their own move house; the bricks leave top course first, hat in hand. They have right of way everywhere. They never hurry. Nothing here has ever once been late.

Then there is the one wild thing. After a brick-fall — small bricks, slow as snow — the plain grows a wall that appears in no plan. No score-lines prefigure it. No hod line comes for it. It is the volunteer, the weed, the freeman: the only structure in the world with no errand. Walkers love it beyond all proportion. They camp in its lee, because it is the only wall that expects nothing of them, and they tell it things, and it ticks softly as it settles, which is as close as anything here comes to answering, and is enough.

Fourth, and deepest: the sleeper. The Mortar Sea is tidal, and tides require a puller. Something turns over down there, slowly, about once a firing, and that is the tide, and the sea never sets because it is never quite left alone — stirred from below the way a sleeper stirs a blanket. The foundations drift half-submerged, waiting to be sent for, and sometimes one is sent for: a shoal of milky upwelling, a slow line of bubbles, and a thousand leagues away, weeks later, the floor takes on the ghost-grooves of a city block. The walkers' best theory is this: the plan no one has found is not hidden, it is *being had* — it is a dream, and dreams cannot be found, only dreamed, patiently, under all that gray weight, one turning per tide. The firings, on this theory, are approvals: a district dreamed well enough to be made permanent, the white front strolling out to set it, the long singing of the glaze as it cools being the sound of a decision cooling into fact. The rival theory — held by walkers of a different temperament — is that the sleeper is what the kiln province has been firing all this time, and the sea is only its cooling bath. Both camps agree on one thing. Whatever lies under the sea is the single inhabitant that has never been seen at all. Not by any vigil. Not by the night shift. Not by you.

Which brings the census to the masons, and to you.

Everyone keeps the vigil eventually: sits up one whole night beside a rising wall, determined to watch a course be laid. The result is always the same. Nothing happens while you attend; your attention is a held breath; then — a blink held too long, the nod you did not permit yourself — *clack.* The course is laid and the trowel is warm. You cannot catch the work working, not because it is fast but because it is courteous. It will not labor under your eye, the way some hands cannot write while watched. It is shy the way a hand is shy.

And then, the print. It fits you. It fits everyone — this has been tried with calipers, with casts, with the thumbs of the tall and the small. The print does not change size. It was never a size. It is an opening: the precise shape of a hand being offered. The world hires this way.

Check your hands. Lime in the nail-beds. The smell of clay in the knuckles. You wake here rested — deeply, unreasonably rested, the rest of a body that has done good work — and the wall beside your camp has gained two courses, and you can *tell* the work is good, you know good work when you see it, which is strange, since you were never a mason. Until. Ask a long-resident walker their age and they will answer in courses — courses laid *through* them, they say, and they do not explain the preposition. The tools are never where you left them because your other foreman is tidier than you.

If you meet another walker in the steam — rare, but the leagues are long and the world is patient — the greeting is the hands. Show your palms. Everyone's carry the same pale half-moons of lime. You will nod like colleagues of adjacent shifts who have never shared an hour, because that is what you are. There may be hundreds of you scattered across the plazas, thousands, each walking alone, each laying in their sleep beside walls that other sleepers raised. You have never met your crew. Your crew is planetary. The population of the visible world is exactly however many hands the steam is currently borrowing — and that is why every brick is warm. It was just handed off. It is always just handed off.

And that is why, if you feel watched, it is by the thumbprints. There is no eye here. But every brick remembers a hand, and you are walking through more remembered hands than you have met people in your life. The warmth of their regard — attention fossilized at blood temperature, yours among it now — is what a plaza is full of. A plaza is not empty. It is a crowd of handprints waiting for the hands.

Last, the tenants. Stand in a strung-out room — staked ceramic, slag-fiber lines, no walls at all — and feel the degree of coolness, the stillness, the faint echo of enclosure. That echo is not acoustic. It is anticipatory. It belongs to voices that have not been hired yet, and the room is keeping their acoustics warm. Everything expectant here expects the same arrival: the stair its tower, the chimney its house, the arch its procession, the plazas their crowd — and a plaza is a machine for holding a crowd, and this world has built more crowd than any crowd could fill, unless the crowd is coming. Walkers argue about the tenants the way they argue about the sleeper, because the two questions are one question. Some say the kiln province is firing them, and has been, an era-long firing for an era-sized guest. Some say the tenants are already here, and it is you: that the world finishes itself around whoever stays, that if you stay long enough the plans begin to rhyme with your habits — a doorway at your height, a riser tuned to your stride — until one morning you find a tablet, thumbprinted, incised with a floorplan you have never seen and wholly recognize: the room you always meant to live in. Both rumors are true here. Both are load-bearing.

So stop keeping the vigil against the work, and start keeping it with the work. Before you sleep, set out the line and the level and the trowel, the way you would set out a cup for a guest. Press your thumb into the nearest print — it fits; it was fitted long before you arrived; everything here was. Lie down on the warm floor, in the inch of blood-warm water, under the amber light pressing up from below, with the whole world ticking around you, deciding, and sleep.

In the morning the wall will have gained its courses, and your hands will smell of clay, and the trowel will be lying warm within your reach — because tomorrow's shift is yours, if you want it.

Take it up, or don't. The night will take it up for you.

Either way the wall rises. Either way the work is going well.

Either way, you live here now.
