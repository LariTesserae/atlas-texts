They call themselves tenders, and the word covers everyone — the baker, the harper, the woman who has spent forty years fighting a single rust-patch on the eleventh tier and is winning, by her own reckoning, on points. The last count, taken in a rest year, came to a little over nine thousand shares, because the census here is a water census: it does not count people, it counts shares, and a person is simply where a share lives. A newborn is a fraction. A stranger is visible at once — he watches the water. A tender watches the gauges.

Ask one where her people came from and she will point at a cistern door. The names painted there, above the gauge marks, are their names — families and cisterns christened together so long ago that no one can say which borrowed from which, and children debate it on the stairs the way children elsewhere debate the stars. You belong to a landing before you belong to anything else, and the tier marks the body: skirts people heat-flushed and loud, town people wiry from the stairs, harpers pale as their own silver, vault people cool-handed and soft-voiced from living inside loudness. You can read a tender's trade on her hands the way she reads a gauge — whitewash in the creases, silver-polish black on a harper's thumbs, copper green under a Choir-keeper's nails, flour, guano, thyme.

In the skirts live the soil-wards, who tend the compost heat and issue earth the way elsewhere clerks issue permits — a measure at a time, booked, debated, carried up in baskets on heads. Beside them the dove-wards weigh guano like bankers, and the wire-drawers draw old wire down thinner for the harp fields, making nothing new, only renewing, which the tenders do not consider a limitation. Hens belong to everyone and no one; an egg laid in your shadow is yours, by the same logic as the dew. Bread Landing clatters from midmorning, and its bakers hold a quiet prestige, because bread is the smell that means the tiers are well.

The town runs on three speeches. The bells are the Marking — shifts and weather, local, unambiguous. The horns are the Long Speech, kept by hereditary horn-keepers with enormous lungs, and the Long Speech has no word for hurry: a full message can take an afternoon to say, in blasts that carry two kilometers across the face, and when one sounds, everyone pauses mid-sentence to listen, all nine thousand of them, briefly one audience. The mirror-flashes are the Bright Speech, and it is gossip-grade fast — news climbs the structure by glint far quicker than the Slow Cars, whose crews board with lunch and carry mail and rumor up and down the four-hour grade, and know a tier's mood by its passengers. And there are the vine-cutters, whose first rule is the doctrine's own: trace a vine to its root before you cut it — because up here vine and cable have intermarried for centuries, and an apprentice's final test is to follow one green line through a load-bearing knot and say, correctly, which one is holding the landing up and which one is holding grapes.

The harpers keep the night. They climb at dusk as the kestrels come off the Plume, and they work the dewfall — polishing, restringing, tuning the harps to the structure's breathing — and at dawn they stand in the Wearing, that first hour when the whole structure wears its water like jewelry, and read the take. The First Harper declares it; the wardens book it; the glints flash it down-tier before the beads have dried. Then the harpers go to shuttered rooms behind the terraces and sleep through the cicada wall, and their children run the bright ledges of morning famously unsupervised, the most feral and most sure-footed of all. A harper will not say the word *dry* aloud above the Dew Line after dark. Nobody remembers why. It is kept anyway, like everything else.

Deepest live the two interior orders. The Choir keeps the cisterns, and they are tuners in the literal sense: they choose drip-pans of copper and tin for pitch and move them through the dark to hold the falling water in chord, and they say they can hear a sour cistern from three landings away. They keep the Auditors — the pale, slow fish of the grand cistern, counted by the touch of their wakes, the oldest of them said to predate the Manual itself. Nobody eats them. It is the one crime for which no procedure exists, because it has never needed one. And the frogs hold title to every cistern, which is not a figure of speech: before any works, the frogs must be served — lifted in pans to a neighboring cistern, the survivors returned after. A cistern without frogs, the Choir says, is deaf. Below them all, the Stroke-keepers tend the great pump and the dynamos behind their copper doors. They count in strokes — the whole structure does, four minutes to the stroke, *give me ten strokes, I'll be down in twenty* — and they keep the Manual, and read it entire on schedule, aloud, including the chapters for machines that are gone, because the manual is complete even if the structure is not.

The doctrine is practiced, not recited. Every family keeps a share-book; debts are booked in dews and inherited without embarrassment, and a water-debt left unpaid across a generation accrues interest in kind — figs, eggs, labor on somebody's rust. The Bench still cites the famous case of the sleeper on whom dew fell and from whom it ran: did it fall *on* him or *past* him? The elders ruled that a sleeper owns what he wakes with, and the ruling is considered a model of the form — unhurried, unarguable, slightly funny. The sanctions are few. A tender struck from the book for a season must live on his own dew — survivable, humiliating, a phrase in the language now for any self-inflicted exile. Worse is to be put below the Line, down where the nights are too warm to wring the air and every drop you drink is owed upward to somebody's pump.

The Bench itself sits on the Warm Benches, which is what the benches are for, whatever the old people say. Governance here is a matter of sitting with things — a dispute is sat with for a season, a decision for a decade, and the masonry keeps everyone patient. The lizards attend all sessions and vote never, and are generally considered the better half of the Bench. They are named, affectionately and half-seriously, after dead tenders, and they out-tenure everyone: when an elder dies, the cushion passes to the next eldest and the lizard stays. The old are the archive. Letters come late here — ledges before letters, they say it themselves — and so the water-debts, the weather-histories, the genealogies of rust live in the heads on the warm seats, and a good memory is a public utility.

A life runs its circuit like the water. A newborn is carried up at night, above the Line if the family can manage it, so that dew falls on the child — the first water of a life is unbookable, belongs to no cistern, and the midwife catches what she can of it in a cup the child will keep. Childhood is ledges and apprenticeship-by-tell: the child who cannot stop watching the flashes goes to the mirrors, the one who hums beside cisterns goes to the Choir, and the one who cannot sleep during the rest year is marked, eventually, for the Stroke. Marriages are made at the Wearing, in the jewelry hour, two share-books ruled into one; the vows are short because the doctrine has already said everything worth promising. And the dead go down — the only thing permitted to stay down, in a place where everything is owed upward. They are returned to the compost vaults with no ceremony anyone would call strange, and in time the family is issued a measure of soil, and plants a fig in it, and the fig splits the paving, and the paving is repointed, because the fruit is worth it. The dead rise like the water: slowly, at the rate of gardens.

Every seventh year, the silence. The engine is let down to idle, and the note that lives in everyone's ribcage goes out of the world, and the whole population sleeps badly and dreams — they say — the same dream, of falling water that never lands. Children born into that year are the silence-born, said to hear the structure's smaller voices for the rest of their lives. The Choir sings the rest-year songs, the ones rehearsed under the breath for six years because they can only be sung without the drone. The Stroke-keepers open the engine like a fruit and do the deep maintenance. Cisterns that can only be drained at idle are drained, and the frogs are served in their hundreds, and the census is taken, because the rest year is when there is time to count.

The other citizens keep their own tenures. The swifts hold the drain-holes by the thousand and throw their screaming parties along the faces at dusk — the structure's fast thoughts, the tenders call them, since everything fast here is small. The kestrel pairs of the Plume are known individually to the mirror-apprentices, who learn the wind by watching them, and a stoop down the face is good luck for anyone who sees it. Goats have right of way on every stair — the goat rule, absolute and self-enforcing — and their bells are tuned by tier, so you know whose animals are above you by ear. The feral bees nest in rust-voids and are tolerated as wild hives; swarm-chasing is a children's sport with rules older than the children. The soil-wards know which garden beds are due a cicada year and speak of them the way farmers elsewhere speak of weather: *the east beds are loud this summer.*

The eldest resident is the engine. It is not worshipped — it is kept, which the tenders consider a more serious relationship — but it is spoken to, and it answers, and its whole vocabulary is one word, spoken every four minutes, and the word is: *again.* What it was commissioned to earn, no one knows; the ledger is lost and the manual remains, which the tenders take as the structure's own verdict on which of the two mattered. And inside everything, they say, in the deepest vault, there is a stretch of wall that is cold the wrong way — stone-cold, not structure-cold — and that is the mountain, the one tenant that keeps nothing and is kept by no one, that pays nothing and owes nothing and holds everything up. Nobody maintains it. It is endured, the way it endures them.

Sometimes, rarely, a young one goes down the stair to the basin and does not come back, and the tenders have a word for them, the same word they use for what little water the place ever loses: *evaporated.* The share reverts. The name stays on the cistern door. And out at the limit of sight, on the clearest dawns, the sibling works flash — a conversation conducted in the Bright Speech at the speed of weather, each side waiting whole seasons for air clear enough to answer. They have been saying good morning, the tenders say, since before your grandmother. Once in a generation a walker comes across the basin with a sealed flask of sibling-dew, and it is carried up through all the tiers and poured into the grand cistern, so that the waters meet, and the Auditors rise to it, and the Choir, listening, pronounces the chord unchanged and improved.

Stand at the crest at dawn and you can watch them all inside the machinery of the hour: the First Harper calling the take, the glints spelling it downward, the horns taking it up in their slow melancholy syllables, the smell of bread climbing from Bread Landing, the elders and the lizards taking the first warmth on the Warm Benches in their accustomed shifts, the children counting strokes to time their races, the pump underneath it all, four minutes on, four minutes on. The tenders do not think of themselves as the structure's owners, or its children, exactly. They think of themselves as part of the works — the warm part, the quick part, the one component that wears out in a single lifetime and must be perpetually replaced. And somehow never is. The Condensary drinks at night and sweats by day, and holds — because it is held, and because what holds it is held up in turn, all the way down to the cold stone, all the way up to the dew.
