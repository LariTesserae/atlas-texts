Everything that lives here is descended from a crossing. That is the first thing to understand about the census: the room is an island, the fill is its sea, and every living line in it came over the water once — on the last free air down the stair, in the fold of a workman's cuff, through the interstices of the rubble before the newer brick set hard, or up out of the deep ground with the seep itself. The mold spores rode the draughts when there were still draughts. The moths' foremothers flew down when the passage was open, and the brick went up behind them, and the sky was cancelled. The spider's foremother arrived as a spiderling small enough to come through anything. The bacteria in the rusticles may not have crossed at all — they may be the fill's own citizens, older than the room, merely extended into it the way the sea extends into a tide pool. Everything else is an immigrant's descendant. And everything, across these however-many centuries, has been becoming room-shaped: pigment surrendered because pigment is expensive and useless, eyes surrendered or retuned, tempo slowed to the room's own. Haste is a luxury. The room taxes luxuries and pays out in time.

The flora you already know by its hanging names — the gray beard on the cold runs, the sulfur-yellow mats, the snottites — but know also its economy. The beard is grazed; you can read the grazing in it, pale crescents where the moth larvae have passed, always healing, never stripped, because the grazers starve before the meadow fails. The snottites are the room's only trees, and their twice-daily bead is a fruit that falls into the sump — a tithe to the deep, acid feeding the black eye's own dark chemistry. Rarely, once in a decade, once in a generation of spiders, a bubble climbs the outtake from below and the black eye blinks, and for a week afterward the sulfur mats burn brighter: the fill sends up a subsidy, and the gardens spend it. And the rusticle colonies are the engine's undertakers, patient as scripture, giving the machine a cave's funeral at a hair a year — the beam is not vanishing, it is relocating, translating itself downward into icicle, iron becoming its own grave goods. Nothing grazes the painted strata. The red-lead undercoats are a poison desert, and the knowledge of that desert was bought with larvae long ago and is bred in now: keep to the sweat, the rust, the lime, the film.

The springtails are the crowd — the plankton, the public, the stipple that walks. They work the puddle margins and the biofilm, and they are the first to feel the stroke and the ones who feel it most: when the beam goes over, the concussion flings them off their footing by the thousand, and for a moment it snows, outward and down, a snow that lands and walks away. The stroke is their thunder and also their harvest, because the grease cups screw themselves down a fraction and offer up a bead of fresh grease, and for a day or two afterward there is plenty, and the boom is written in the film. Their whole history is pulsed by it. They keep the nine-day week as faithfully as the machine does, and for the same reason: it is the only calendar there is.

The moths are the theologians. Their world is vertical and their astronomy is infrared: their star is low and near and fixed, burning at the engine's base, and they steer by it the way their foremothers steered by the moon, except that the sense they steer with is not sight but warmth. The meadows of their caterpillarhood are wherever the cold lines run; the high dry dark near the ceiling is where the pupae hang, safe from the wet; the middle air is where the adults live out a span measured in strokes — a long life is four, an elder is one who has known five thunders. Their ears were built across a million years for the cries of bats, and there has been no bat here in two centuries; the hearing has drifted down into the room's register, into the flame's hiss and the small grammar of the drips — or so you must believe, having watched them turn their heads in the dark toward a sound you can barely find yourself. When the flame gutters, they come in from the whole room, and some orbit at the exact distance where warmth ends and burning begins, and some misjudge. The singed are not failures; they are pilgrims who touched the star, and you will know them ever after by the curled margins of their wings. When they die, they do not leave. Nothing leaves. They fall into puddles that will never overflow, that hold their shapes like treaties, and the still water keeps them — a wing here, a whole small body there, years apart, undecayed. The puddles are the room's reliquaries.

And then there is the spider, who deserves a paragraph the way the engine deserves a room. Understand first that the web is the real resident and the spider is only its current hands. The web keeps the valve wheel that turns at the stroke, and it has been rebuilt around that interruption for longer than spiders should be able to manage — because it is not any one spider's work. At the hub, silk lies on silk on silk, generations of anchor lines accreted into a gray archaeology, and the wheel's turning grooms it, tears the sacrificial sector, and the spider eats the torn silk — protein is the room's rarest currency and she is its most careful banker — and reweaves. You could core that hub like a tree and read the whole tenancy in it, including this: the oldest anchor points stand wider apart than the new ones. The spiders have been getting smaller, as island things do, as starved things do. She fasts on a scale her ancestors would not survive; a moth every few weeks is abundance; she has slowed herself to the room's tempo, and slowness, here, is what longevity is made of. She knows the stroke is coming before it comes — the tumbler's trip has a fore-creak, a small intake of breath in the linkage — and she steps off her wheel onto the fixed pipe and waits out the forty seconds with her feet spread wide, and then goes back and reads the damage with her toes and begins again. She has done this, or her mother has, or her mother's mother's mother, until the doing is far older than any doer. She is blind, and has never seen the flame, and knows it only as a warm horizon at the edge of her world. And here is a thing you can verify: every drip in this room builds its monument — the boot-high cone, the stained bosses, the aprons of lime — except one. Beneath one drip, off in the dark past the second catwalk, the floor is bare, because the water is drunk before it can land. Its period will not hold still for you — forty-one seconds, then never again — because the interval is not the water's. It is hers. You will find her well the way you find anything's well: by the absence of what water builds everywhere else.

The census has one more line, and it will not take a name. A scratch, a shift — about once an hour, on average, low, toward the corner the lights refuse, behind the pipe bundle. You will attribute it to the fill settling, except that it is too regular and too near; to the machine, except that the machine's only event is the stroke and this is not the stroke, nor the ticks, nor the pen's dry devotion; to your own ear, except that it has a location, or the feeling of one. If it is alive, it is the most active animal in the room and the only one that has never once been seen — it has never touched the web, never gone to the flame, never left a shell or a wing or a track in the grease-felt. Perhaps it is the fill's own creature, come up the seep line the way the water comes, the buried body's emissary to its servant room. Perhaps it is heard — you will think this and never prove it — a little more often in the hours after a stroke, as though whatever makes it believes in the machine the way the moths believe in the flame. You will not set a trap. A trap balances nothing; it only makes the room's one mystery into meat. The ledger is allowed an unsigned line.

Count the processes, too, because they out-qualify half the fauna on any technicality you like. The flame eats, breathes, hisses at the edge of hearing, and has been awake — the only resident that has been awake — every second of the sealed era. Everything else here has died and been replaced a hundred times over; the fire is one fire, continuous the way a river is continuous. The ember-orange bulb is its dull moon, too cold to worship. The engine is the sleeper whose heartbeat is its own maintenance, and its forty seconds are the one experience every living thing in the room shares — the bacteria in their icicles, the springtails in their snow, the spider on her fixed pipe, the moths in their orbits, all of them inside the same thunder at the same moment: the room's communion, its one universal sacrament, nine days apart. The inward needles tremble on through all of it. The room is taking its own pulse. Call that, if you like, the closest thing here to a self.

If you ask who lives here — meaning a person — the answer is no one, and has been no one since the day the new brick went up and the last tender climbed the forty steps. There are guests, now and again. You will be one. You will be the largest animal in the room and the only one with an elsewhere, and to the room you will be weather: a warm front that stands for an hour and passes, and nothing here will file you under fauna, and when you go the dark will close over the shape of you the way the sump closes over a bubble.

So the census stands: one fire, one iron sleeper, one spider who is also a hundred generations, a small congregation of moths, the walking stipple, the hanging gardens, the pale chemistry, and one unsigned line. It is always the same census. It is never the same individuals. Everything here persists the way the pilot flame persists — not by lasting but by continuing. Up in the dark the tank is filling, bead by bead, and when it goes over, the thunder will stand the moths' hairs on end, and the concussion will gutter the star and the star will hold — it has never failed to hold — and the congregation will come in from the whole room to confirm it, and a few will singe, and the springtails will snow and land and walk away, and the spider will ride it out on the fixed pipe and then go back and begin the rebuild, and the snottite beads will keep falling twice a day into the black eye, and the eye will not blink, and the week will begin again: nine days long, and sufficient.

And the census will still be true.
