Nothing about the plateau prepares you for how inhabited it is. From the rim it reads as emptiness — a green line, a moving cloud-shadow, one column on the horizon — and this is a failure of the eye's equipment, not of the world's. The plain is crowded past counting. It is only that the crowd is small, or hidden, or disguised as weather, or slow enough to be mistaken for geography. Everything here lives at one of four addresses: the floor, the noise, the islands, or the edge. And everything, sooner or later, turns out to be somebody else's address too.

Take them in order.

The floor is the felt, and the felt is the largest citizen, and possibly the oldest. Whether it is one plant or many is an open question and may be a meaningless one: the mat does not respect the boundaries between bodies the way animals do. The silver-blue skin — the wax that combs the fog — might be a single organism kilometers wide, grown outward from one seed before anyone's grandmother's grandmother; or it might be a parliament of look-alikes so intergrown that the question dissolves. Dig anywhere and the roots are one tangle. The people, who are good at categories and suspicious of them, say the felt has one body and many minds.

Within the mat there are citizens and citizens. The scar-green — the deeper, softer species that colonizes the rings where giants once stood — is the felt's tender member, the only leaf a person can eat raw and the only fiber that makes rope worth trusting over the rim. In the drip-shadow of a standing giant, where the trunk's combed fog falls all day, grows a dark thirsty strain that dies anywhere else, so that every tree stands in a faint green halo of its own making: the giant's shadow, written in another species. And the felt blooms — rarely, modestly, small pale bells close to the surface — but it blooms in the wakes of fronts, a day behind the wave of sound, triggered by the same warmth that conducts the choir. From a tor you can watch it: sound passes, and then silver passes, as if the noise were watering the flowers. The people say the felt blooms for the departing singers at an emergence. Whether the singers return the favor by pollinating the world on their way out of it is one of the plateau's open questions, and considered a good use of an evening.

Below the living skin, all the decay in the world gets done in a band you could cover with your hand. Pale worms, glass mites, springtails — a thin, frantic economy of crumbs — and beneath them, nothing, because nothing rots, because nothing can get air. So the commonest population on the plateau is the past: the darkening pages, the black peat, the preserved trunks standing close as teeth. Most of what is here died here and is still here. The plateau keeps.

The second address is the noise, and the noise has tenants.

The singers come in species, and the species come in hours. The first hour of the day belongs to a small green thing with a note like a struck wire, tentative, solo. Midmorning belongs to the great synchronists, the front-builders. High noon belongs to a species tuned, as if maliciously, to the precise frequency where the human ear is tenderest — that is the one the wax plugs are for, the reason the old are deaf. Dusk breaks into scattered soloists, and deep night belongs to the bass choir, a heavier, wingless species living lower in the mat, singing through the ground, so that the night chorus is heard as much in the bones of the feet as in the ears. The people do not have hours, exactly. They have singers. Their word for late morning is the name of an insect.

A front, when you understand it, is a wave of agreement. No insect travels. The warmth travels, and each singer ahead of the line joins for its hour and falls silent behind it, so that the roaring body crossing the plain is made of new creatures every minute, the way a wave is made of new water. It is less a migration than a rumor, spreading at the speed of the morning. Which makes it, incidentally, a compass as well as a clock: a front is born in the east at the sun's pace and walks west all day, and a traveler caught in open country in bad light can take a bearing off the noise, if he can stand to.

The fronts have their remoras. The runners are pale, quick, long-legged things that live at the roaring edge and feed on the newly singing. All day they travel west with the front; at dusk the front collapses under them into soloists and dies, and the runners spend the night walking east in the dark to meet the next one. A runner's life is a few days long and is spent entirely on that commute. No runner has ever been still in daylight. No runner has ever heard the plain go quiet at noon. To a runner, the world is a loud line moving west, with silence behind it and silence ahead, and food exactly on the line.

The spiders of the felt abandoned the web long ago — there is nothing to hang one from — and became listeners instead, each crouched in a silk sleeve with eight feet spread on the surface, reading the mat like an instrument. A stridulator's first note is often its last. But a front is to a spider what night is to a hawk: the world goes blind. When the noise comes they go deep and wait it out, sulking, the people say, in the dark below the pages.

The third address is the islands. There are three kinds — the giants, the wanderers, and the people — three kinds of country with the green sea between them.

A giant is half leaf: the gray-green of the trunk is a working skin, drinking light in the fog-hours, and the fluted organ-pipe body is less a trunk than a root system turned inside out, drinking the air. How old a giant is, nobody knows. What is known is that a giant outlives its name. The flutes deepen as the tree grows, and the note deepens with them, so that over centuries a tree slides down the scale like a slowing bell. The old carvings on the tors record names — pitches — that no living ear has ever heard from those trees. Stand beside a carved tor on a windy day and compare the carved note to the note on the wind, and you can hear the age of the carving. The maps don't just age. They *go flat*.

Every crown holds its lake, and every lake holds citizens found in no other. These are the plateau's oldest families. Their ancestors swam in the rivers of the mountain range that stood here before the world was flattened — the tanks are the last refugees of the last running water, and each tree has been a separate country since the streams died into the mat. How anything crossed between trees in all that time, no one knows: eggs carried on a wanderer's hide, a climbing crab's once-in-a-century dash across wet felt on a cloud-spill day, a freak gust at an emergence. The people hold three theories and suspect all three are true. When a wanderer rubs its slow bulk against a trunk — they do, sometimes — the people say it is delivering mail.

The tree keeps its tenants as a stomach keeps a meal. The crown draws down the tank's leavings — the droppings, the molted shells, the small dead — and a giant's tenants feed it as surely as its roots do. The water itself is dark and sweet with tannins, and the people climb certain trees, by certain rules, to drink it. Every tank has a flavor and a reputation. There are trees whose water is famous, and a traveler will go a day out of his way to drink from a good one, and mention it afterward, the way other people mention wine. One tree holds a crab that has left the water entirely and hunts among the crown-leaves. One holds a frog no bigger than a fingernail paring that sings only when the wind makes its tree drone — a duet with its own house. One holds a race of shrimp transparent as the water they swim in, found on that tree, and no other tree, and nowhere in the world below.

A giant's childhood is a catastrophe's aftermath. The seed arrives in a wanderer's gut and waits in the felt for the one thing that never comes twice in the same place: a fall, and its wound of light. Then hundreds of saplings rise together in the brief marsh, and for a few decades the wound is a thicket — the only thicket the plateau grows. Then the poison begins. The saplings turn their chemistries on one another, and the thicket thins over a century until one tree stands alone in its parish and the scar-green closes over the bones of its siblings. And when the old giant fell to make that light, its tank spilled, and the tank's people were thrown into the marsh to die by inches as the felt closed. This is the occasion of the oldest argument the people have. The water-carriers say: bring jars, climb, carry the doomed water to a neighbor tree, and let the experiment continue in a new glass. The others say a tree's people belong to the tree and go with it; that to mix two tanks undoes a separation older than the rivers; that mercy here is only a slower kind of ending. Both sides have very old songs.

The book of the peat says the giants were once a forest — the buried trunks stand close together. The living trees stand days apart. The people, who think in centuries because the place gives them no choice, say the giants are leaving one another, that the world is becoming all sea and no islands. Whether the wanderers plant faster than the giants fall is an argument that will be settled, if it is settled, long after everyone now arguing is dead.

The wanderers are the second kind of island — moving ones — and the only citizens whose names are itineraries. There are fewer of them than giants, and the people know every one: not usually by sight, but by signature — the width of the trail, the depth of the crop, the tempo of the loop. A band on the move reads a crossed trail the way a scholar reads a letter. Who. Which way. How long ago. In what health.

The felt grows on them as it grows on everything. An old wanderer carries a back of silver-blue, and bedded down in open country is indistinguishable from a rise in the ground; more than one traveler has walked around a hill and had it stand up behind him. Each animal carries its own company as well: a race of small riders that lives on the hide and nowhere else, as isolated as any tank, so that a wanderer is an archipelago as well as an island, and a meeting of two wanderers is a contact between worlds. To drink, a wanderer kneels, and leans. The felt gives water only to being squeezed, and a wanderer's chest is the biggest press on the plain. Where one has knelt, the mat stays dark and soft for days, and the small things of the surface gather there, and whoever follows the trail knows the animal is well, and drinking, and was here within the week.

Two wanderers meeting is an event on the scale of weather. People walk for days to witness it. The animals stand flank to flank and do, to the eye, nothing — for a day, two days — while below hearing, everything is presumably being said. Then they turn and go, and their loops shift, subtly, and over the following decades the giants' new seedlings appear in slightly different places, and so the map of the next century is redrawn by a conversation no one heard. When a wanderer dies — rarer than a giant's fall — the felt takes it the way it takes everything, from below, over years, until the body is a long soft hill with a cap of deeper green. The hills are on the maps. They are called *the sleepers*, and they are the only features that are both grave and geography, and nobody camps on them, and nobody could say exactly when that started. As for hunting them: it happened once, in a hungry year, in a story. The band that did it is remembered, and not fondly. A wanderer, the people say, is not meat. A wanderer is a piece of geography that has not finished happening.

The fourth address is the edge, and its citizens are the rim-hunters — the great gliders that live on the wall of rising air the way the singers live in the felt: totally, and invisibly to anyone not looking.

A glider may not touch ground for years at a stretch. They drink the seep-falls on the wing, skimming the white threads in the hundred meters before the updraft tears them to mist; they bathe in the rising edge of the cloud collar; they sleep, the people say, in pieces, a hundred heartbeats at a time, one wing dropped into a half-arc of sky, and if that is wrong, no one has yet corrected it. Their nests are in the flutes of the upper wall, inside the collar, where the seep-falls begin, and their young fledge by falling — which, for them, is the same thing as flying. From their height the fronts are visible: the felt dimples under the weight of the sound, and a glider reads the plain the way a sailor reads water, seeing weather where a walker only hears it. At an emergence they come from all along the wall, more of them than anyone knew existed, stacking the updraft in wheeling floors, eating without landing for as long as the swarm pours off the rim — and the people below look up at a sky full of wings and are reminded that the plateau's emptiness is a matter of scale and hiding, nothing more.

The gliders are also the takers. The people give them their dead. A body carried to the lip at dawn is gone by dusk, and the people call it *going over*, and say it without flinching, because everything the plateau makes goes over the edge eventually — the mist, the seeds, the singers, the sound — and the people are the plateau's, and so are their dead.

The edge is the only door. Everything new that has ever come here has come over the rim on the wind: spores, seeds, spiderlings ballooning up out of the hot green country on threads of silk. Almost all of them die. The felt is a hard country to immigrate to, and it has been deciding about some arrivals for centuries. The people's word for visitor and their word for immigrant is one word, and it means, roughly: *those the felt is still deciding about*.

And then there are the people, who live at all four addresses — the only citizens who commute.

Everything permanent of theirs is on the tors, because the tors are the only permanence available. In the gray bone-stone of certain tors run thin threads of native copper, and this is all the metal the world provides. A copper blade is an inheritance in the strict sense: handed down with its genealogy, never lost, and when it finally wears to a sliver it is hammered into something smaller — an awl, a needle — and handed down again. The people regard the idea of throwing away copper the way other peoples regard mistreating the dead. Dig deep enough anywhere, though, and the book of the peat turns up its one black page: a charcoal seam, at the same depth wherever anyone has dug, the mark of a burning that once ran the width of the world. The diggers show that page to children, and that is the entire fire law, and it is enough. Hearths are stone. Coals travel in clay boxes like hearts. The people are not afraid of much, but they are afraid of fire the way sailors are afraid of the sea — lovingly, and every day.

To drink is to work. The felt gives water only to being squeezed, so the people carry presses — two boards and a twist of rope — and drink by the armful of leaves, water that tastes of wax and silver. At the tors, the dew-ponds are tended like fires, older than the families that tend them. The scar-green is the year's salad; the tank-crabs are taken rarely, by etiquette, from the climbable trees; and twice a year, at the emergences, the world feeds them straight out of the air. They go before dawn to the swarm's edge with long nets, and the singers are sweetest, everyone agrees, in the first hour, before they gain height. Dried and ground in the tors' hollows, the meal keeps a year, and tastes, the people say, like the sound the singers made. Which is a joke. Probably.

The plateau taxes hearing, and everyone pays. The old are mostly deaf — and here deafness is not a diminishment but a promotion. The deaf read the ground with their feet — a front's approach as a dimpling in the felt, a wanderer's far voice as a pressure in the chest — better than the hearing read the air. Children sign before they speak. Conversations do not stop when a front arrives; they change bodies. There are things that can only be said in sign, in the roaring noon, and the people say the noon hours have their own literature because of it. Their music is built for the deaf they expect to become: hide drums stretched over stone basins, long cords strung between cairns and bowed with fiber, songs pitched to be heard with the sternum. On windy nights they play in the gaps between the giants' notes, and the elders feel it through the soles of their feet, and it is considered ordinary, and it is one of the strangest things humans do anywhere.

They navigate by the chord, and take bearings off the fronts, and still days unnerve them — no wind, no chord, the world gone nameless. They date their history by falls, because a fall is the one event everyone alive remembers: *two falls back*, they say, *the winter after the East-Dome fall*. When a giant goes down, they come from all over that quarter of the plain, drawn by the sound. It is the only fresh timber the world makes — bowls, ladder cleats, spade-handles for a generation — and the brief marsh is a riot of frogs and dragonflies and general plenty, and the whole gathering has the character of a funeral that is also a harvest festival, the old argument about the tank-water carried on through it, politely, as always. The ladder-families keep the ways down the rim, two days and a ledge-night, and the less said of that the better. What comes up: salt, and news, and once in a long while a person. What goes down: fossil wood, and songs, and the names of the giants, which the lowlanders do not especially want and receive anyway. The lowlands might as well be another country. It is, in fact, the country of the plateau's own stories, and the people prefer it at that distance.

They are the newest thing here, and they know it. The felt, the giants, the wanderers, even the gliders were old before the first person stood on the first tor. They do not keep animals; they keep track of them. They do not make maps so much as borrow them. Their sign-name for themselves is a small quick gesture, like something scurrying, late to arrive.

At some point, though, the census stops being a census. When every creature is another creature's food, weather, geography, and grave — when the tree is a lake's only shore, and the wanderer is a forest's only planter, and the front is a runner's whole world, and the people are a glider's autumn — the word *community* starts to sound thin, and the lowlanders' word starts to sound exact. They say the mountain sings. Consider what a body is: a thing that keeps its own water, remembers its own dead, speaks to itself in frequencies its parts cannot hear, and pours its surplus endlessly outward. The plateau does all of these.

At night the bass choir comes on, and the giants hum, and the felt goes on dripping its under-rain, and somewhere a wanderer says something below hearing to another wanderer a third of a world away, and the gliders sleep on the wing inside the collar — and all of it leaks off the edge together and falls through a kilometer of dark into the heat, thinned to a faint high ringing, sourceless, weatherless. The lowlanders turn over and say the mountain is singing, and that rain is coming.

They are right both times, and for the same reason. The song and the rain are the same breath, leaving.
