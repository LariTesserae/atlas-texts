The census of the Kettle fits on one slate, but no one has ever chalked it to the bottom. It starts easily enough — twelve souls, sometimes fourteen — and then the trouble begins. The moths will not hold still to be counted. The isopods are past arithmetic. The garden declines to say whether it is one citizen or a thousand, and the cistern-vine that bridges the main is both resident and utility, which complicates the definition of either. So the slate keeps to what chalk can tally: souls, sightings, broods. Everything else gets in anyway, the way everything down there gets into everything.

The true majority is the garden, and it is mostly immigrants. Seed in packing straw, spores in the creosote of crate timber — and the fern forgotten on a landing, which is still there, or its daughter is, or its daughter's daughter; after so many divisions nobody pretends to know where the original plant ends. The tenders call all of it the Landing Fern and speak of it the way towns speak of a founder. Cuttings from it began half the rail-gardens in the vault. Whatever names these plants carried in the seed catalogues stayed above. Down here they answer to kettle-room names — beam-moss, lamp-crop, prior's-beard — and they are becoming something the catalogues would not recognize. The moss that packs the piston glands is not quite the moss of any surface ditch. Give the place a few more centuries and it will have species of its own. The tenders suspect it already does, and consider this the garden's business, not theirs.

One light in the Kettle is alive. The green lichen keeps the frontier: the fresh faces at the cutting end, the warm rock ahead of the spoil line. Nobody has seen it arrive. A face is cut — bare, glittering — and within the season it is lit, faint as breath on glass, and what the lichen finds to eat in new stone is its own affair. The tenders use it as a calendar and are careful with its obituaries. When the glow leaves a face, the slate says *glow out*, and the tenders say *settled*, and nobody says *died* — though no one has ever caught the lichen leaving, either, which suggests it does not die so much as finish. A face it has abandoned is done deciding what shape it means to be. The lichen has read the new stone all the way through.

Everything below answers the ration of light one of two ways. The plants answer by hoarding: every leaf near-black, turned up to the lamps like a cup. The animals answer by renunciation. They gave up color as an expense the light would not cover.

Hence the isopods, pale as old tallow, grazing the moss like slow sheep that no one shepherds. They are the Kettle's cleanup crew and its clockwork of least resistance; the pruning line doubles as their pasture fence, and every basket of greens carried to the kettle room brings a few of them home by mistake. These are picked off the chopping board and tipped into the deep sump, which is how the garden pays its tithe. A Kettle child is set, once, to watch an isopod cross a walkway and report when it arrives. The lesson takes the better part of an afternoon. It is never assigned twice.

Hence the moths, white as wet paper, the ceiling's only congregation. They walk the upside-down stone and take the beads at the point of a tongue kept rolled like a watch-spring, and at noon, when the shaft drops its single coin of daylight on the spoil floor, they gather to it — wings fanned at its rim, tilting like small sails. The tenders step around the coin out of an old unreasoned courtesy. Between the two courtesies, it is the one piece of floor in the vault that nothing walks on. The lamp-round keeps a tally of their broods; the slate says *moth brood, small* and means the world is holding. At weather turn they fly drunk in the indoor rain, and the round afterward gathers the fallen. The cistern-vine flowers for them — pale bells — and the moths work the bells, and the vine sets, and the vine carries the town's water. The dust-town above drinks by courtesy of moths and has never been told.

Beyond these, absences. No fish in the sumps. No spiders in the corners, nothing that stings, no song but water. Cats come down unwise and leave early: the moths fly like slow paper and a cat wants a quarrel, not a weather report; the isopods are beneath notice; there is no dry ledge worth the name. The record for staying is one season. The slate chalked that departure the way it chalks broods.

In the deepest sump, between the engine beds, lives the eldest citizen. One newt, as far as anyone knows — and the knowing is thin: black water, months between sightings, a ledger line in a hand that goes careful for the occasion. The paper ledgers that came before the slate mention it in a margin, in an ink nobody living has met. Newts above ground live a dozen years if the ditches are kind. Either the Kettle is kinder, or the office has been held in succession, and the sumps keep that secret the way they keep everything: blackly, and without ripples.

It is a lung animal, like the tenders, like the works. When it surfaces, it surfaces at the breath — the long intake sigh comes down the stair, the lamp flames lean, the gallery doors lean, and the skin of the deep sump dimples and breaks: a black-eyed head, taking the air the engines have just drawn. The tenders, who keep time by the breath, find this fitting and do not say so. Feeding is at weather turn: a teacup of the palest isopods tipped in at the sump's edge during the soft hours, while the rain falls indoors. By morning the tribute is gone, which is usually all the proof of life the ledger gets for months at a stretch. In the wet weeks of the burst main, years back, when water stood everywhere and the boundaries came down, sightings doubled — the Prior ranged its brief parish from sump to walkway and was twice met on the stairs by lantern-light, unhurried, in no one's way. When the repairs were done it returned to the deep water. Or a newt did.

Its sump may not be drained for any reason whatsoever. This is the only law the Kettle has, and the only one that has never needed writing twice. Children are brought to the sump's edge early, told the law, and told to watch. Most watch for years and see nothing. To see the Prior is luck; nobody agrees what kind.

Then there are the tenders themselves, twelve souls, sometimes fourteen, who keep the boundary and are kept by it. Their trades are the vault's moods: the line-walk with hook and basket; the lamp-round, which is wicks and glass and the brood tally; the furnace watch; the slate, which goes to the best hand in the hamlet. They hear in the vault's scale. A tender can tell from her bunk which drip has moved. A Kettle child finds middle C before she can write her name — the letters come late down here and the intervals early, and nobody's education has suffered from the ordering that anyone can prove. Above, their lips split within the day, the weathercocks hang still, and the town seems to shout; they come down early, and sleep. Twice an hour the works sigh, and the hamlet sleeps to it the way coast towns sleep to surf. In the town above, where nothing breathes, the stillness keeps them awake. Every few years someone rides down with the lamp-oil and stays; every few years a tender goes up and stays gone. The slate keeps both in the same small chalk, and the count holds. Only the dead go up for good. The vault has room for every kind of life and none for that; the town buries its own, and remarks at gravesides that they smell of the garden.

Last on the slate, and first by size, is the population nobody chalks. The word the place gives its people is the word for what they do to everything in it: they tend the garden and the lamps and the sick and the engines, and the grammar is the creed. Anything that dies when the tending stops is, by the Kettle's working definition, alive — and by that definition the works are the largest animal in the vault: fed, breathed, groomed of their fringes, packed with living moss, mourned in advance. The calcite alone tends nothing and is tended by no one; it glazes the oldest pipes with the patience of an heir, and may yet inherit the place entire, engine and garden sealed mid-breath in mineral. Or the garden wins. Or the two have already come to terms — in which case the whole vault is one household, and the census has exactly one entry, which is why no one has ever finished chalking it.

So the slate reads what it reads and no more. Twelve souls, sometimes fourteen. One newt. Moths uncounted, isopods past numbering, one garden or a thousand, one light that is alive, and the works, pending. Underneath the chalking the drip keeps its four seconds, and the breath comes round twice an hour, unhurried, like someone reading the list back and finding it correct.
