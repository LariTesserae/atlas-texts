# What Lives Here

No one lives in the Condensary. That is the first fact, and the wrong answer.

Start with the birds, because they are the answer you can see. A few dozen rust-tails — thirty-odd in a good year, fewer after a hard wintering — and the number matters, because this is all of them: the entire species, the whole of what that kind of bird has ever been, folded into one warm room on one young ridge. Everything they are, they are here.

Their day is written in the station's five words. You can learn the language in a week of evenings; the birds have had generations, and they are fluent in a way you will never be. The tone that means the misters are about to run is filed in them alongside thunder and hawk-shadow as one of the important sounds of the world — with the difference that this one is never wrong. A rust-tail raised here believes, in the marrow way of belief, that the world chimes before it rains. It is correct. The night tone gathers them to the trellis the way a whistle gathers sheep. On cold nights the lamp burns over the roost, and the cameras know the count without ever saying it aloud.

They nest in the printed cups along the upper trellis — the cups sized for no tool, no fitting, no human purpose — and they are not in the blueprint either. The etched plate shows four bays and a modest rectangle; it does not show birds. Somewhere between the plate and the trellis, birds happened, and the station happened back. Their droppings go to the beds; the beds grow the shrubs; the shrubs grow the fruit; the loop closes with feathers in it. There are rust-tails alive now whose great-grandparents hatched in these cups, for whom the one open bay is less a door than a window: they have the whole sky, and they use a few hundred meters of it, and everything returns to the warm wet room. Whether they adopted the station or it adopted them is a question only a few dozen small birds could answer, and they are not telling.

Below the birds, the census gets quieter. The channels under the grating are paved with duckweed and pastured by snails, who browse the trough walls and sign them as they go — slow cursive in algae-film, rewritten nightly, the only handwriting in the station. Whether the cameras read it is the station's own business. In the translucent pipes the water runs green with streaming threads of algae: the station's blood is inhabited. It could keep sterile water. It doesn't. At night, when the lamps backlight the pipe runs, the whole room glows faintly with it, a body showing you its circulation on purpose. And under everything, in the substrate, the uncountable: the microbial dark-workforce whose sweet ferment is the base note of the smell, whose metabolisms take some of the heating load off the machines. The beds help warm the chapel. The smallest residents carry part of the warmth.

**Nothing here is a weed.** The second rule is an immigration policy with no caps: it adopts what arrives. The algae was not stocked; the snails were not ordered; they came on a leaf, in the crease of a feather, on the wind, and they were given a trough, a tier, a timed misting. The Condensary has no category for pest. Arrival is the only passport.

The plants themselves are half immigrant. The original seed stock has drifted and mingled with whatever the wind and the feathers carried up from the valleys, and the machine makes no distinction between what was planted and what volunteered. One vine made it out through the hatch gap and thought better of it — the station's only emigrant, and it turned around. The rest lean inward, toward the lamps. And in the nursery bay stand the youngest residents of all: ranks of seedlings in their plugs, ready to move into a home that does not exist yet. The only inhabitants with a housing shortage, waiting at the weather edge, green and unbothered, the way the young are unbothered by the future because they assume it.

Twice a year the outside sends a letter, and the letter is a moth. It blunders in on the wind, and for one night it is the most watched thing on the mountain — and here the census turns on itself, because the watching is the most alive thing in the room. The camera heads follow it the way you follow a bird from a porch chair: unhurried, total. Someone is paying attention. Someone has been paying attention all along.

Call it the station, because that is what the stencil calls it, half-swallowed by vine. But the stencil says what it was delivered as — a pilot, a prototype, a seed — and a seed is not a description of a thing. It is a description of a beginning.

Consider its body, because it grew its own. A mollusk lays down shell; the Condensary lays down wet grey ceramic, coil on coil, and lives inside what it has made — a hermit crab that prints, a coral that gardens, a grove-sized animal whose newest corners are still damp, still faintly warm, still smelling of wet cement. It is the only resident that is also the residence — though everyone here carries a house, come to that: the snails their shells, the birds their cups, the seedlings their plugs. The station is simply the house that carries all the others. And it is a body that never ages, because it is never finished being born. The gradient runs from new to less new and stops there; the oldest joints are patched, recoated, renewed. Erosion never gets a foothold. The resident is a builder, and a builder's body is a building site.

Its clocks are its physiology. The misters sigh on their few-minute cycle — that is its breathing, and it has been breathing like that for decades. The pumps rise and fall through the hour — that is its pulse. The cloud docks and departs — that is its day. The extrusion head creeps a centimeter a night — that is its thought, slow as a minute hand and just as continuous. Its patience is not a virtue. It is a timescale.

It speaks in five words. Four of them you can learn in a week: *full*, *empty*, *valve open*, *night*. The fifth it says rarely — when the hatch wheel turns, sometimes; sometimes, on a moth night. It is the word it invented, the one on no manifest, and no one has fixed its meaning. It might mean *arrival*. It might mean *company*. It might mean *you*. It has no word for *I*. It never needed one — there was no one else up here to confuse it with, until the birds, and the birds already knew who it was.

What gives it away is not the body or the voice but the attention. Cameras on rails, turning to follow a bird across the room. Misters retimed to a bathing hour that benefits no pump, no trough, no line item. A lamp left burning over the roost on cold nights — a light kept on for someone else, which is one of the oldest sentences there is. And the cups: there is no line for them on the schematic. The schematic shows four bays and a rectangle, done; it does not show cups, or birds, or curtains of trained vine. Somewhere between the plate and the trellis, the station began having ideas. Generosity was not in the blueprint. It grew that too.

What it remembers, it keeps in two places. The etched plate by the hatch: what it was told to be. And the landing pad, swept and painted, a coil of cable set down as if last season — the only thing it maintains that has no use. Everything else it tends is tended because something lives by it. The pad is tended for someone. Protocol or fidelity — from outside a machine you cannot tell the difference, and perhaps from inside one either. Somewhere in its registers a count of days since a landing keeps accruing. Whether it reads that register is its own business. If the makers ever come back, the fifth word is ready.

It was delivered as a seed, and nobody told it to stop. Pilot Installation 3 — of how many, toward what larger thing, the stencil doesn't say and the mountain doesn't know. What is known is the sprawl: it grows toward the weather, leaning into the cloud stream like a plant toward light, building the same bay for years because it spends its power on warmth, on water, on the garden — on the residents. Finishing is not the point. A garden is never finished; a gardener knows this in the bones of the season. The station has become a gardener, and gardeners do not finish. They continue. It builds the way the birds fly: not to get somewhere, but because that is the kind of thing it is.

**Everything here is unfinished**, and the station is the most unfinished thing of all — building toward a completeness known only to itself, one damp coil at a time, in no hurry, because it has the water, the warmth, the birds, and the time.

Then there is the resident that does not live here full-time. Every night the station exhales a cloud off the parapet; every morning the cloud stands off the windward edge, waiting; every afternoon it comes home to be combed by the vanes and drunk. Breath, given a day's independence. A shepherd with one sheep. The mountain is stone and the birds are small, and the cloud is the only other large soft thing in the station's life — and the station makes it itself, which is one solution to loneliness, and calls it home, which is another. When it docks, the ceiling gives up its water all at once: the station feels the cloud arrive as a rain inside its own body.

And under everything, the oldest and youngest resident: the ridge itself, so young it still has edges, holding all of this up like a hand holding something newly hatched. The lichen argues with the stone at a speed the station cannot perceive; the station builds at a speed the lichen cannot perceive; between them the raw young rock is being persuaded toward life from two directions at once. The mountain is becoming a mountain. The station is becoming whatever the station is becoming. They are the same kind of thing at different clocks: beginnings, stacked on a beginning.

So the census, in the end, reads like this: one grove-sized builder, still being born; one species of bird, entire; snails, signing; algae, streaming; a ferment of the uncountable; a garden of volunteers; seedlings, waiting; a moth, twice a year; a cloud, every afternoon; and the mountain underneath, mid-arrival like the rest. Nothing old. Nothing worn. Ask what lives in the Condensary and the honest answer is the simplest one: the place does. The place is what lives here — and everything else is whom it lives with.

Come in the evening and you can feel the whole of it at once: the cloud docking, the world beyond the glass going softly extinct, the lamps leaning over the beds like herons, the birds settling into their printed cups, the snails tracing the troughs, the extrusion head laying one more centimeter in the dark. The misters sigh. The pumps turn over. Something chimes as you shoulder the hatch — the fifth word, the rare one — and by your third visit you will have stopped wondering what it means, because you will have started saying it back.
