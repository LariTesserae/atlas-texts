There is a census, and it fits on one hand, and the first entry has a face.

The eel is the only animal in the Kettle you can call an individual without having to argue about it afterward. Everything else here blurs; the eel does not blur. It has edges, an appetite, and a preferred address — along the intake screens of the sump, where the pumps' draw concentrates whatever the water carries. It is old past guessing. It came up the ditch from the river as a glass eel the length of your little finger, in some year when the station still had keepers, or nearly did, and it has been thickening ever since, and now it is the girth of a forearm and the color of the water exactly — tea-brown, gas-flecked, with a dull brass at the jaw that shows when it turns. Whether the root-felt screens grew to feed it or it learned the screens' habits is one of those questions, like the seam between iron and plant, that the Kettle has rendered permanently beside the point. It is, give or take a stray that never stays, the only spine in the basin.

Once a year, in the dark of an autumn month, it leaves the screens and works the sluice — noses along the gate, worries the seam where the warm water slides out toward the ordinary ditch. This is the ocean's old instruction, carried up the ditch in its blood and never deleted: downstream, downstream, out and away. It has never once gone. By morning it is back at the intakes, uninterested, and whatever clock it carries keeps the Kettle's time now. An ocean, outvoted by a sump. It is the only resident that could leave. That may be its job.

The second entry in the census is a guild. The grease-slugs live in the housings and the grease-cups and the shadowed runs of the conduits, thumb-length and pale as candle-fat, and they read the engines through the soles of their feet. They gather where friction is going to be. A bearing that will run warm by winter accumulates slugs by summer, the way a coming storm accumulates gulls inland. Their slime is the lubricant, and its chemistry has been adjusting itself to the engines' clearances for more generations than anyone has counted — or the clearances to the slime; the metal wears now into shapes the slime prefers. When a slug dies in the works it does not stop working; it is worked in. You can read the health of a whole engine off the shine on its slugs' backs. The eel takes the strays that wander too far down the runs, and that is the only predation the Kettle bothers with, or permits, or arranges. The distinctions have all been sleeved in felt.

The third entry is the briefest life here, and it is measured in hours. One species of pale moth, and the stack keeps them the way a throat keeps a breath. At dusk — always on a beat, always exactly on one — the stack breathes them out of its moss-galleried brickwork, and they rise into the plume and live their single night there, in the steam and the silage-sweet gas and the drifting spores, among the plume-blooms, whose nectar is held at plume-warmth and whose opening is timed to the slow breathing of the gas bells. Their larvae graze the soot-velvet inside the flue, in the warm dark where the engines' breathing collects. By dawn the adults are a pale dusting on the mat, and within days the mat has taken them back, the way it takes everything. A whole species that exists as the nightly exhale of a chimney, and is found nowhere else, and needs nothing else.

Below all of these, holding no census entry because no one has ever seen one, are the ones doing the bulk of the living. Under the mat, in the warm black water, at blood-heat and above, the rot-guild works without rest — the gas-makers, the digesters, the chemists of the dark. They build the domes that wander the basin like slow cattle. They hold the Kettle's breath for it, in great fern-planted bells, and every stroke of every engine is borrowed from them, and they are paid in the mat's endless sloughing. If the Kettle has a majority population, this is it: life that never once surfaces. The surface is its achievement.

And then there is the resident that is also the residence.

Everything green here is grafted into everything green, and the engines are sleeved and valved and belted by the same body, and so the honest census of the Kettle is not four entries or four thousand. It is one. One life, wearing the species like costumes and the engines like hearts — three hearts, slightly out of phase; there is precedent for that arrangement, in older oceans. Its blood is warm water, pumped up into its own canopy and returned. Its lungs wander. Its reflexes are superb: the level falls and the pumps answer; a bearing heats and the slugs answer; frost comes to the dike and is not admitted. Its nerves are still a rehearsal — a net of corroded fence-wire, adopted by the vines, carrying the generator's thin current through the green, tickling nothing, lighting one lamp.

The lamp deserves its own line, because it is the Kettle's one expense that pays nothing back. It glows into the wet daylight, pointless as a kept promise, and it has not been dark in living memory — not yours, not the country's. Everything else the Kettle does feeds the loop. The lamp only shines. Unless keeping a light on is itself an organ: the organ of expecting. It is the kind of thing a station does when it has been left, and has not stopped expecting to be returned to.

Which is the last entry, or the first. Someone kept this place once. The hut folded into the mat kept its tools; the gauges kept their needles, trembling once per beat against NORMAL, filing their report with the only reader remaining — the system the report describes. The keepers are gone. The keeping stayed. It grew roots, learned to beat, became a body. That — more than the eel, more than the guilds — is what lives here: the keeping itself, a job description fossilized into a metabolism, duty become pulse. And the chair. One seat, facing the largest heart, maintained the way the lamp is lit. The Kettle processed everything else its makers left behind and kept the chair. It is not waiting; waiting is too quick a word for it. But a place is set.

If it thinks — and nothing about the basin, lifting and settling underfoot like a chest, insists either way — then it thinks the way it beats: in the drift among three slow clocks, a polyrhythm too gradual to hear changing. A thought here is not a picture. It is a phase relationship, and it takes an afternoon to have. A mood is a gas dome on the move. An intention is a level held a hand's width below the crown, drought year, flood year, without exception. Its cares are few, and it is equal to them.

At dusk the lamp comes into its own. The stack exhales its moths into the warm plume; the bells ease down through their fern fringes; the three clocks drift through their slow configurations; and in the sump the oldest resident makes one unhurried pass along the screens, keeping the time it was given. The basin lifts beneath it like a chest, and settles, and a second later the shaken rain falls on a canopy that no longer waits on the sky.

Out across the dark fields the thud carries, felt first, heard second — a heart audible from outside the body. The census, in the end, has one entry, and the entry is the address.
