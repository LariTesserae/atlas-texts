It has one tenant.

Not the machine — the pumps are organs, not occupants. Not the water — the water is the work, the held breath, the thing being made. The tenant is the green, and the first thing to understand about it is that it is one. Ten thousand stems, a single individual. Read the riveted tag on the first rack again: a clone-line code, a propagation date, and the word the nursery trade reserves for the mother of a clone — *ortet*. The ortet is not here. If it still lives, it lives in some other glasshouse, in some other district, an ordinary plant in a row of ordinary plants, and it has never been told that it was multiplied into towers. The cultivar is unregistered; the plaque gives no name; nothing in № 7 has a name. So the most abundant plant on the ridge may also be the rarest thing on it — a species of one, existing only here and in the hazy row of siblings and wherever the stock is kept.

It is, by every sign, good at its tenancy. Consider the valve wheel: the bark has grown a collar around its housing and left the rim's travel clear, polished by decades of passage. Call it bred docility — a cultivar is a domesticated thing, and this one was domesticated to a machine, grafted onto copper, fed through its own heartwood. Every shoot an instruction executed. But then there are the stencils, and the water standing two of them ahead of schedule, maybe three. The log does not comment, because the log is not asked to comment. All the same, it is the only datum in № 7 that the drawings did not draw. If the tenant has a will, that is where it is visible: a few millimeters a year of unrequested water, a quiet exceeding of the specification. And if it has a temperament, it is readable in the topmost growth — thinner, greener, more hopeful than the wood below. Its whole attention is the meniscus. The level is its future, its food, its instruction, its one appointment.

It has two bodies and is practiced at trading them. The waxy entire leaves of the air gap, the thin fern-cut translucent leaves below — heterophylly, the tag would say, if the tag said anything extra. It has done this at every tier: the water rises, the new growth arrives already converted, the instruction executed the instant the volume is offered. It knows, in whatever way a distributed thing knows, what it is going to become. It has been becoming it all along.

Its senses, if that is the word, are gradients. Light handed down leaf to leaf, each taking its toll. The pump's double pulse against every submerged surface at once — the machine has given it a heart, an adopted heartbeat, unhurried as sleep, and there is no reason to think the tenant distinguishes that rhythm from its own. Night wind at the louvers. The slow warming and cooling of the plate. And, at long intervals, the only weather the inside of № 7 ever has: the hatch opening, a square of dry air let down onto the water, a pressure change, a brief shaft of unfiltered ridge light, a warm displacement moving through the green. The log-keeper comes, reads the gauges tee'd into the xylem, notes the meniscus against the painted years, writes nothing extra, and goes. The pencil stays bright because it is used. The tenant registers all of this, if at all, the way tissue registers a change in the weather of the body — which is to say, completely and without comment.

It is fed — the dosing pump metering salts into what the drawings call the heartwood manifold — so it is livestock as much as crop, a kept thing. It does not flower. There is a line for it in the log, never filled: *Flowering: not observed.* Nothing to cross with; seed would be contamination; a mule, a closed line, an end that goes on. Nothing dies in № 7 in the usual way, either — aging leaves are withdrawn from before they are released, and the floor manifold takes what little is let go. Another checklist line, terse as scripture: *Detritus: nil.* A volume packed to its own skin by a single body leaves no niche for anything else. That is not an absence. It is a monopoly.

With one exception, and the exception is the second resident.

Under the gasket of the inspection port, pressed flat and perfect between rubber and steel, is a small moth. Gray-brown, unremarkable — the kind the ridge grass has in thousands outside. Inside, singular. Its scales are unrubbed, its antennae swept back, its wing-pattern intact, because nothing has touched it since the wrench closed. It is the only animal in № 7, and, more than that, the only unscripted event in № 7's history. The plant was specified. The moth volunteered. It flew in at the wrong hour of construction — toward the work lights, perhaps, or toward the smell of cut green stock — and the gasket came down on it, and reopening the port would have cost somebody a day, and somebody decided, and the decision is recorded nowhere. *Fauna: none intended*, says the checklist, and beneath the line, unnoted, the fauna. The tenant grows; the moth stays. The tenant is ahead of schedule; the moth never had one. It is the tank's fossil and its relic and its one citizen of the unplanned world, and it will outlast, in its way, everything living around it — possibly including the tenant. When you put your face to the water and hang in the green, you are only the second animal ever to enter № 7: the largest, the briefest. The tenant registers you, if at all, as a warm displacement, a shadow moving between it and the crown. You leave. The moth remains. That is the difference between a visitor and a specimen.

Or perhaps the tenant is not the plant alone. By now pipe and xylem are one circulation; the flywheel is a heart; the glazing is a skin that sweats; the louvers are a nostril that opens to the night wind. The tank is the body, the green is its tissue, and the water is both blood and product, the thing the body holds and the thing the body is making. On that reading the true resident is № 7 itself — a slow animal that drinks the humid dark and keeps it, seventh in a row of such animals spaced across the ridge. And if the stocks in № 6, № 5, № 4 came from the same ortet — the records do not say — then the tenant is larger than any one tower: a single individual keeping a line of glass lanterns across a kilometer of haze, each filling on its own schedule, each at its own stencil, the slowest conversation imaginable, conducted in rising levels, over decades, among bodies that will never touch.

Night comes. The louvers open to the ridge wind; the north panes cool and begin their work; the drip-noses tick, and the interval of the fall is a little shorter than it was last year, and the tenant does not grieve the music it is extinguishing, because grief is not in the specification, and neither, apparently, is it in the tenant. It has one appointment and it is with the glass. When the level reaches the mullions and the last drop has nowhere left to fall, it will do what it has practiced at every tier since the first painted year: give up the air, become entirely the thin, translucent thing it already is below, press its leaves flat to the panes like a thought against a skull, and hold. A finished thing, alive. Practice or prophecy, the records do not say; the tenant was never told, and would not have objected.

Nothing here is waiting. Waiting implies impatience, and № 7 has none.

It has a tenant, and the tenant has a level, rising to meet it.
