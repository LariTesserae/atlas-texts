Everything here lives by the heat, and everything here knows its address. Not the address of a slope or a valley — the address of a temperature. Ask any resident, in whatever language it keeps, and it could tell you to the meter how far it stands from warm ground. The basin's total population is large and its density is almost nothing, because tenancy is arranged by the plumbing: close to a vent, crowded; an hour's walk out, no one. And every one of them, from the felt to the fox, lives with the hum. None of them has ever heard silence. To them the tremor in the stone is simply what ground means.

The first tenants are still the most numerous. The warm channels are lined in zones, like a floor marked off by trade: nearest the source, where the water is barely below a simmer, almost no one at all; a little downstream, where the heat drops to what a hand could bear for a second, the chemists — bacteria that eat the water itself, the sulfur and iron and hydrogen the mountain exhales; further down, the green guilds, the ones that eat light. Each band is a few centimeters wide and fully staffed. Step a hand's width downstream and you have changed personnel entirely. They build as they live: their filaments strain silica from the flow, they crust, they fossilize standing, and the next generation grows over the masonry of the last — so the white terraces are, in part, their accumulated tenure, and they are the only tenants who are also the building. Deeper still, in the dark warm water inside the rock, live the ones who never surface at all. If the machine room has a crew, it is them. They run on the exhaust, they have never seen the light, and they will outlast everything else on the roster. Everything else in this basin is a refugee from the climate. The felt is native to the planet — the hot, mineral, breathless world it keeps to is the world as it first was. The rest of the living is the anomaly.

What the oases actually manufacture is the midge. The felt is grazed by larvae; the larvae rise as columns of gauze, each swarm holding station over its own water in the warm updraft like the smoke of the spring itself. An adult's whole life is a few hours long, and all of it must happen inside the thermal — mating, swarming, laying — because to drift a meter out of the column is to chill, and stall, and drop. The frost along the steam-line is littered with the fallen, tiny as punctuation, and the snowfinches come to read. Beneath the swarm the surface is stippled with the spent, and the felt takes them back, and the spring eats its own product, because nothing here can afford waste. Everything at the oasis eats the midge in the end; the midge is the meadow's way of walking out of the water.

In the films between the felt's filaments, and in the wet hearts of the cushions, live the smallest tenants — eight-legged, comma-sized, unhurried. When the water glazes at dusk they freeze with it; when it softens at noon they resume, mid-thought. They cross the border three times a day as cargo. Their lives are measured in thawed hours, and they are the only ones for whom the nightly freeze is not a threat but a pause — and the only ones who can arrive as dust on the wind, land in a warm film, and be citizens by the next thaw. Immigration here is mostly fatal. These pass through it asleep.

The cushions themselves are the slowest residents: hard green bolsters, dense as green wood, ringing faintly under a boot. Each is a single plant, and the big ones are older than the terraces' present shape — five hundred years, a thousand — growing a millimeter a year, dying at the center as they spread, until the oldest are no longer cushions but green crescents around a dead heart. They heat themselves: dark, close-woven, still-aired, their interiors standing degrees above the dim afternoon, each one a small warmed room stocked with its own mites and larvae. An island containing smaller islands. The archipelago repeats at every scale, all the way down to the space between two cells of felt.

Then there are the haymakers — the heart of every warm slope. Round-eared, shrill, the size of a small loaf, and incapable, it seems, of stillness. All summer the colony is a harvest in progress: cutting the cushion-plant (pruning, with their teeth, plants older than their own grandmothers' grandmothers — the negotiation between pika and cushion is conducted across centuries), carrying the stems home, laying them out to cure on the vent-warmed stones, turning the little cocks so they dry in air too thin and too dim to dry anything. The talus is their field, their kiln, their granary and their fortress. They do not hibernate; their engines will not idle that low. Like your own heart in this air, a pika is a small motor overrevving, and it stays rich precisely because it cannot rest. So all winter, under the snow, in streets among stones that never ice because the mountain keeps them warm, the colony lives off its cocks — and on still nights the whole slope breathes, thin steam rising from a hundred gaps between the rocks, and you can read the townships from a far ridge by their smoke. So can the fox. Theft is the common crime, and the alarm is the one civic act: one voice first — *eep* — then the slope in chorus, every caller adding its bearing until the whole colony is triangulating the fox out loud. It is the basin's own voice, the only sound the place makes that is not the works.

One outflow holds the basin's only hermit. The frog, the color of weak tea because the water is the color of weak tea, lives its entire life within the warm reach of a single spring — thirty meters of channel, a marsh of soft sinter mud, and beyond that, an arm's length away, killing cold. It has never felt frost. It is the only vertebrate here that never has and cannot. All of them are cousins; the whole species may be a few hundred animals in one plumbing fixture. How it came here is an old story — a warmer draft of the world, a wider range, a climate that closed like a door, and one spring that held its temperature. It is not stranded, exactly. It is archived. Sometimes in the long nights it sings, a few small clicks under the steam, and no answer has come from any other valley in memory, or ever. Its tadpoles take two winters in the warm water, because nothing here can hurry. It has no enemies but geography, and its entire future is a plumbing question.

The snowfinches are the color of the place itself — ash-grey, snow-white — and they work the margin where melt meets frost, where the strayed midges drop, a sparrow-sized bird walking the line all day with a margin's patience. They are lodgers: they nest in the warm talus among the pikas, in the cracks of the sinter collars, in the basalt organ where heat leaks between the pipes, because an egg in this air is dead within minutes unless it is laid in the heated fabric of the works. In winter they flock, and they are the only small creatures that cross the void willingly — grey flurries blown between islands, navigating by the plumes. Every oasis signs itself in white on grey, a steam column visible for miles. The basin's chart is written in smoke, and the finches read it fluently. Their song is short; the thin air clips it, and a finch singing here is heard half as far, so they sing from the vent collars, from height, and let the terrain do the amplifying.

The shorebirds come down on the midges — long-legged, with bills curved down to the exact curve of the stones, probing the soft warm mud of the rill margins. They may be the only waders in the world with warm feet. They nest on the open flats, a bare scrape in the frost-sorted silt, because the emptiness is their moat: nothing can cross an hour of bare ground unseen. But the eggs cannot be left — a minute uncovered and the cold has them — so the pair keeps shifts, and the changeover is a small exact ceremony, one bird rising, the other settling, performed on a stage of nothing, for no audience, twice an hour for a month. The chicks walk the day they dry and are led straight to the steam-line to pick. When the real cold comes, most go down to the lowlands, to rivers that have decided themselves. A few stay. The springs never freeze. The ones who stay never queue.

The fox is the patrol. Its territory is a circuit of islands, days long, run on foot, kept on a schedule as loose as the geyser's, and it knows the basin the way the basin knows itself: by the ledger. It reads the silt. It remembers its caches — and here a cache keeps for months, because nothing rots; the whole basin is a cold room, and meat is never lost, only mislaid. It is nearly silent, because there is no one to call to. Once or twice a winter, from somewhere out in the void, it gives a single yelp, unanswered — the rarest sound the basin makes. In storms it sleeps curled with its tail over its nose, frost feathering its fur until it resembles nothing so much as a vent collar: camouflage by resemblance to the works. And in the deep cold it comes to the frog's outflow to drink, because it is the only unfrozen water within a day's walk. It puts its face down into the steam, and the frog sits on the bottom and watches the fox's eyes through the tea-colored water, and neither of them does anything about it. The fox could reach in. Sometimes, perhaps, it does. Mostly it drinks and goes on, and the dotted line of its tracks leads off across the ash — going nowhere, ending at nothing, arriving.

The condor is the senior tenant, the only one whose view includes the whole site. It roosts in the organ pipes, a wingbeat from the vent field, so its mornings begin with the freight elevator: it steps off the ledge into a rising column of steam-heat and is carried up without effort, through the haze into the clear cold above, until the basin lies below as a diagram — white plumes, milky lake, grey flats, the faint smoke of the pika slopes. From that height it reads for what has died, and what dies here keeps: freeze-dried, legible for seasons. So the condor's patience is not a temperament but a system. It does not hurry, because nothing spoils; it only arrives — eventually, unhurriedly — at every ending. It has killed nothing in its life. It is the records department, and everything that lives here will in the end be visited by it; that is the only appointment the basin keeps. The old one working this range may be seventy. It has ridden these thermals longer than the terraces have worn their present steps. It saw, in whatever way a condor sees time, the glacier's snout a mile further down, the lake that let go, the new vent's first cough of ash. When it goes, no one left will remember the previous draft.

The milky lake keeps almost nothing — too cold, too cloudy, too young; a vat not yet inoculated. And the void acres are not barren either; they are pending. The wind delivers a constant rain of applicants — seeds, spores, a spiderling hanging from a thread of silk — and most land on the frost and join the ledger briefly, and a few land in a warm margin and are hired by dark. The difference between empty and not-yet is, in the end, the whole difference here.

And there is the traffic that does not stay. The geese cross at night, too high to see, and their calls fall down out of the dark like small hardware spilled in a stairwell — the only mail the basin gets, news of rivers that have decided, of soil, of the finished world beyond the range. Everything that hears them hears, for a moment, that there is an elsewhere. Now and then one drops down, spent, into an oasis, and rests a day on the felt's green, and either goes on over the range or stays, and becomes an appointment.

You will not stay either. You are not on the roster; you are the inspection, and your tracks are the only ones that go somewhere and are never retraced. When the steam has taken them, the work will continue without noting it — the felt accreting its fingernail's width, the midges rising at noon, the finches reading the frost-line, the fox on its rounds, the condor aloft, the frog in its one warm room, the geese falling through the dark overhead. And under the snow, in the warm dark between the stones, a pika pulls one more stem from the cock and settles to eat, the size of a loaf, the engine of it running, and the steam rises through the stones, and the hum comes up through the floor of the world, and the night shift comes on, and everyone is at their station.
