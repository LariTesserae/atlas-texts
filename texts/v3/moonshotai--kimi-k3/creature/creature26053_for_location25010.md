Every hollow has a tenant — that is the first thing any census of the Throat must concede. Count by bodies and the doves alone defeat you before midday. Count by voices and the answer gets smaller and stranger, because half the voices in the shaft belong to creatures no one has ever seen, and one of them belongs to the place itself. So take it in order, the way the light goes: top to bottom.

The first citizen is the tree. It is the only one with a foot in both worlds — its crown in weather, its roots in breath — and it pays its way. Its dead limbs drop thorn-twig into the light well, the nearest lumber the doves have, and its hanging root-curtain is the only wet stone in the upper shaft. The upper spiders hold that curtain; the first geckos climb it; swift fledglings cling to it on their first nights out of the nest. It sways when the Throat exhales at dawn. A hanging meadow, rooted in damp air, drinking the breath of everything below.

At the rim, in the tree's dead half, the owls hold their dusk vigil. They are the night census. Their pellets collect at the parapet's foot, and keepers crumble them to learn what moves after lamps-out — the pellets are a record no one has to speak aloud. Which matters, because of the rule. Never count the bats aloud — but the owls count the bats nightly, one late body at a time, and keep it to themselves.

Below them, the majority. The Dove Ring holds more warm bodies than all the hamlets together, and it is not a flock so much as a city: bloodlines of niches, a squab in this one, eggs in that, a niche inherited down generations until the old nests at the back have gone to pottery. The coo is not a sound here, it is a condition — a pressure the chest learns and then requires. The molt never stops; at midday the blades of sun on the pigeon stair are full of slow feather-dust, the doves present even in their stillness. And the Ring has its undertakers. A squab falls, an old bird dies on the nest, and the beetle guilds arrive — a dead dove is a week of work for a hundred small citizens, and nothing is wasted, not a feather, not a bone. The doves are the Throat's practicing voice. It coos in its sleep through them.

The swifts live highest and own the least. They are tenants of the light itself, glued into the upper light well where the blades sweep, and they never touch ground anywhere — the Throat is their cliff. They are the needles that stitch inside to outside: out with the dawn exhale, screaming home down the column at dusk while the bats come up, the two traffics threading each other in the dimming shaft, and no one has ever seen a collision.

The bees are the oldest continuous citizens — older than anyone's memory, the keepers say, and possibly older than the law. Because the law of the brick bends around the bees. A brick that falls is relaid before dark, but the wax-seamed brick on the Lamp Walk is never touched at all, and if it ever falls, no hand will relay it. Some doors are not masonry. The founding comb nearest the brick has never been cut; the harvest comes after the thorn bloom, the last comb always left, and at first light the colony makes its own small exodus — bees rising up the light well through the swifts' column like sparks up a chimney, off to the scrub, home by dusk with the taste of the plain in them. The hum is the Lamp Walk's heartbeat. The wax moths that press the colony's margins feed the geckos, which is as close as the bees come to paying rent.

The geckos hold the lamps. Certain lamp cups are known by the gecko that holds them, and keepers — who change by the moon — learn them the way you learn neighbors: the big female at the third lamp has outlasted forty pairs of keepers and watches them come and go like weather. When a lamp falls vacant it is filled within nights from the dark seams, where young geckos wait their turn. The Lamp Walk has a waiting list. They are pale as plaster chips, all of them, and deliberate, and the soft tick of their feet at dusk is part of the level's sound.

The crickets keep the seams. Every dark seam between the light blades has its cricket, and the seams are mapped by sound — a seam gone suddenly silent means a gecko passed through, and the silence travels down the level like news. The spiders fish the drip-lines, stringing their webs under the falling beads to catch what the drips disorient, each web hung under its drip like an instrument under a striker. A spider knows its basin's pitch as well as any keeper. It rides the ringing.

The bats hold the deepest gallery and are not counted, at least not by anyone with a voice. Their exodus is a chimney of flutter that blacks the square of sky for a full minute; their return at dawn is quiet, single bodies folding back into the dark while the swifts pour past them. What falls beneath them stays beneath them — the Dove Ring pays the hamlets, but the bats pay the depths, and under their gallery the palest beetles hold slow provinces in guano no hand will ever carry up. The bats' band of sound is above hearing. Not above the Throat's hearing: the flutes' chord wobbles when the column passes, the place feeling its own tenants go.

At the bottom, the Black Bowl keeps the strangest folk. The green smell that rises through the whole shaft is born here — a film of moss at the Sump's rim, exactly where the single midday blade of sun lands, the only garden below the tree, an hour wide. Pale snails graze it. The fish rise beneath the lit rim for their hour and hang there, slow as thoughts, and nothing is drawn from the basins while they do — that hour is theirs. Below them, in the dark, lies the midden of counting-stones: every dropped verdict since the water fell, the archive that can never be read again, which is the kind the Throat prefers. The fish spawn among the stones. And under the stones, in the mud, the frogs wait — a decade at a time, no one knows quite how, because the rule is that you do not dig the Sump mud. In rain years they bloom and the chorus comes up the shaft like the place shouting in its sleep, and the fish grow fat, and the snake swims.

The well-snake is not a snake. It is an office. The ledge above the waterline is polished smooth by one body that has been many bodies, and when the ledge stands empty for a season — no one sees them come or go — the Dove Ring above grows careless and loud, dishonest, until one dusk the ledge is held again. The offerings left at the ledge-end, a cracked egg, a stillborn squab, are not feeding. They are acknowledgment. The snake is the only hunter with tenure: the place's one necessary tooth.

And under everything, the true majority, uncounted not by rule but by scale: the mites in the nest powder, the feather-lice, the moth larvae, the springtails in the moss-line — the processors, the place's own digestion. They are also the proof of the bleaching rule, which every keeper learns: the longer a line has lived below, the paler it comes out. The doves keep their gray and the bees keep their gold because they commute. The deep folk pay their color to the dark. Color is a currency spent near the sun.

Then there are the keepers — two at a time, changed by the moon. Sent out by the hamlets when the moon turns, yes, but changed by the moon in the other sense too, and the second sense is why the hamlets keep sending people. A new keeper learns the basin-notes the way shepherds learn bells; learns that you do not sing at the Throat but find the note it is already humming and hum it back; learns not to whistle below the Lamp Walk; learns to answer the third echo with her own name, so the place knows whose voice it is keeping. No cats below the rim. The keepers do not say why. Some rules are load-bearing. Over a moon, the pair's two hums converge until the coo takes them in like a chord resolving; their sleep attunes until a basin's missed note wakes them at the third hour; and when they go back to the hamlets they hum at their work for the rest of their lives, startle at the plain's silence, and know the rain-note in their teeth half a day before the goatherds hear the chord drop and start tying goats. Their niche in the ecology is precise: they are the hands. The brick relaid before dark is the place healing itself through them. They are how the Throat picks itself up, one brick at a time, and they take nothing for themselves but water, and leave palms.

Because the majority population of the Throat is the dead. The palm prints crowd the plaster layer on layer, two more hands pressed in at every moon's end, and the galleries are tenanted by people no longer alive — grandchildren come down years later to fit their hands into a grandmother's print and find the fit near-exact. The retired measure-staffs stand racked like a library of dry years. The counting-stones hold their sunken verdicts. The dead do not haunt the Throat; they hold it up. The living are outnumbered here and always will be, which is the correct proportion for a place built to outlast its builders. Nine flutes stand at the rim, and the old stories give each one a hamlet's name — but the hamlets are not nine anymore, and two notes in the chord belong to hamlets that no longer send keepers. When the wind sits in their quarter, the chord carries the orphan notes, and those are the echoes the keepers answer most politely of all.

The wind, the light, the rain — those are visitors, not tenants, and the house treats them accordingly: the flutes rise to greet the wind, the galleries keep the light's appointments, the whole place holds its breath for the rain.

But add up the census — the wings, the hands, the honey, the echoes, the crowded dead in the walls — and it comes to one. The largest thing living in the Throat is the Throat. Blood-warm in every month, breathing out at dawn and in at dusk, pulse ticking its polyrhythm into the basins, voice in the flutes pitched exactly where the doves' coo bears it up, memory in plaster and wax and the bees' long line, dream in the Black Bowl where pale fish rise to their one hour of sun, conscience on the snake's ledge, hands in two keepers humming its own note back to it. Everything else is how it lives. Evening now: the chord slides down toward the rain note, a keeper ties off the staff, and somewhere deep a brick clicks to the floor — and before dark someone goes down singing, and the one warm loud crowded life heals one more small wound, and keeps its slow time, and there is plenty of it.
