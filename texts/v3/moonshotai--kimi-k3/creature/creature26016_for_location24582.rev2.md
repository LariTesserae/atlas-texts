Start with the floor, because everything here does.

The mat is the room's first settler and its only tireless one. It came up with the seep when the ice let go — the people say the floor rose out of the earth's warm blood — and it has not paused since. Whether it is one organism wearing a thousand textures or a braid of many lives holding each other up, no one has ever settled, not even the listeners, who argue it the way other peoples argue the shape of the sky. It has layers like a town has generations: the pale waxen rind that seals the warmth in; the sponge beneath, heavy with seep-water; the black thread-layers below that, where the real work happens, iron and sulfur turned slowly into living. It has moods that follow the water. It has hungers. It grows fastest in the melt-halos of sleeping animals, and where a great one has cropped it, it comes back thicker and sweeter, like a lawn that loves the mower. It keeps things — dropped needles, dead huts, whole forgotten paths — and it gives them back, sometimes, a generation later, turned up in a midden-digging, and the people take this as the floor's character: it forgets nothing, and it is in no hurry.

And it feeds them, carefully. Here and there in the felt, at a depth a forager learns by knuckle, the mat stores starch in pale nodules — floor-apples, the children call them — dug by feel, leaving dents that take a month to smooth. The rind itself is inedible wax, but the wax is wealth: it seals thread, lines storage pits, waterproofs the lashings of a roof. The people cut their felt in rotation, each hut tending its plots at staggered ages like fields, and a well-kept plot has the look, under lamplight, of a well-kept grave — which, often enough, it also is.

The columns are the room's rain, working in slow motion. Each one draws the seep's wet up its hollow core into the cold air overhead, condenses it, rims itself with frost, and gives the water back as drip — a rain so patient a child can count it. They lean. Over years they bow toward steady warmth, so that around an old sleeper's ellipse the columns stand tilted inward like trees reaching for a gap in a canopy, except what they reach for is heat. When an animal leaves for good, the grove stays bowed for a season, a memorial, until young straight ones rise through. You can read the room's recent history in its timber the way you read rings elsewhere: here the ice came down, here a flank lay ten years, here is where Three-Moats calved. The people adopt columns, one to a hut, and drink their drip — the only sweet water in the kettle, the seep itself being too mineral to take straight — and they scrape the rime from the stems for salt. A hut's column is addressed politely. It is the one plant with a place in the family.

In the pith of the columns live pale grubs, finger-long, and once or twice a season, on the crawl nights, they emerge wingless into the felt together with a hatch of thread-worms, riding some signal of the bloom, until the whole floor seems to flow. The people used to dread those nights. Generations ago someone thought to rake them. Now a crawl is a harvest: the hatch scooped by the basketful, dried on the cold side, pounded into a meal that has carried the town through more thin seasons than any law.

The small lives arrange themselves by warmth like everything else. Out on the walls, where the rime crusts the scree, slow skaters as flat as a hand comb the frost for bloom-film, and that is nearly the cold limit of animal life. Warmward, the mat threads with fish — most no longer than a finger, blind and white as roots, riding the pulses up out of the deep seep system. In a fish season the people work by feel: lath weirs of column-slats set in the known runs, or simply standing still in a melt-moat until a cool body investigates an ankle. The rare arm-fish, thick as a forearm, gives the leather for boots and bags. The rest give oil. And on the great ones themselves lives the room's most particular fauna: the pickers — soft, pale, hand-length things with scraping mouths, that work the plates of the hide for ice, for shed skin, for the wax that builds in the seams. Each animal carries its own population, an archipelago that walks. Each hide grows its own pale crust, a lichen of breath-condensation and warmth, which the people pare away in thin sheets for a seasoning that tastes of iron and animal. When a person grooms a great one, the pickers work alongside, unbothered. The animal tolerates both the way a hillside tolerates weather.

The great ones. They are why the room is what it is, and they are also, strictly speaking, guests — the only inhabitants older than the kettle itself, which is young, which they watched open. They come in over the slump-ramp the people call the door, iced like ships after the barrens, plates locked with frost, and the rim sheds pebbles under them for an hour, and every listener in town lies with a palm to the floor reading the click-cascade: how many feet, how heavy, whose. They settle in the same hollows visit after visit, and within days the landform assembles around them — the crushed ellipse, the steaming moat of meltwater where fish gather, the bowing columns, the pickers streaming aboard. They graze the mat in slow swathes, and the regrowth behind them is the sweetest foraging in the room, so that a feeding animal tows a quiet crowd of harvesters at a respectful distance. They wade to the seep-vents to drink, and when one stands — a house deciding to move — the whole constellation pauses in whatever it is doing and waits for the floor to stop talking. They breathe perhaps once in a long count of a hundred, a bellows pitched below hearing, and the interval between a particular animal's breaths is the steadiest clock the town owns.

Nobody knows what a great one knows, but the listeners believe they carry the road-net of the world in their heads — every warm room in the dark, every seep, every wallow, strung across the barrens like stars. The people's maps end at the rim because the map walks. That is what the far-goers follow.

Each one is known by plate-pattern, by warmth-signature, by the sound of its breath, and each has a name of the kind given to landforms. The Old Hill is the eldest — older than the town, older than the kettle, and it has stayed through three thin seasons running while the others went out over the rim, which means the room's heart has not once stopped beating in living memory. Three-Moats calved here, once, in a year that is now a date: the town gave her half the room, suspended every grooming-right, and listened for months to the only near-audible sound a great one makes, the calf's high thin breathing, while children found endless excuses to pass within warmth-range of that small fast heat. A calving changes everything; it means the kettle is not a trap the animals fall into but a place in their lives, a nursery they choose. The people speak of that year the way farmers elsewhere speak of a perfect harvest.

They die rarely, of age or of the cold hunter, and the room knows for days beforehand: the breaths lengthen, the pickers grow frantic and then quiet, and families come from across the constellation to sit one last time in the warmth of a body they have known all their lives. When the breathing stops, the heat does not. A house-sized animal takes days to cool, and the butchering happens inside that lingering warmth, which the people count among the strange tender facts of their world — they take the animal apart in its own fading heat, and the last thing rendered is the fat, and the last fires of the rendering are lit on the cold side, and the hide goes to patch the meeting-roof, where the whole town can stand under it and read its own calendar overhead. The empty ellipse stays warm for a season afterward, from what the mat takes. Graves of that size are hard to distinguish from gardens.

The cold hunter is the other thing that comes over the rim, and the less said of it the truer, because no one has ever seen it plainly and every account disagrees. It is defined by its effects: the pickers streaming off the hides for no reason; the great ones rising to stand rimward, breaths gone long and low; a wrongness in the floor that the listeners feel before anyone. It hunts by stillness and it eats warmth — that much the kill-sites agree on. You find a swathe of crushed felt and a body already cold beyond its time, as if the heat were taken first and the life second. It is the one thing in the room that the warmth-compass points away from. People it has taken are simply not found. Once in memory the great ones dealt with it themselves — a night the whole kettle shook, a battle-swath the mat took a year to close — and the teeth gathered from that carcass are the only edges in town that will part a great-one plate, and they are kept wrapped, and faintly unlucky, and very sharp. The listeners will tell you, if you ask why quiet is the first law, that there is a second question inside that one. Ask why metal may not ring and you will get three answers before the lamp burns down: that ringing woke the hunter once, long ago; that it pains the great ones somewhere they cannot scratch; that the room's hush is a common roof and a ring puts a hole in it. The law does not say which is true. The law has survivors.

And then there are the people themselves, some few hundred, in thirty-odd huts scattered across the intervals, a town that is a constellation and not a cluster — and a people being slowly remade by a room.

They followed the animals down, if the oldest house-layers tell it straight: barrens people once, wind-people, thin and hard, who watched a hill of dead ice slump and steam and breathed warmth on the wind for the first time in their lives, and descended after a great one, and did not come back up. The oldest tools in the kettle are antler — picks and needles older than the Opening, kept in use, kept wrapped, the only surviving luggage of the before-time. Everything since has been learned from the floor.

They are a touch-first people. The sole of the foot is the town's interface with the world; they go barefoot on the mat in all but the scalding seams, and read through their soles what others read in print — path, plot, pulse, the approach of anything heavier than a picker. The skin of the face is their compass: a child can call the distance to a flank within a pace by the warmth on one cheek, and their directions have no north — warmward and coldward, seepward and rimward, and flankward, which shifts, because the flanks move, and everyone keeps track of the shifting the way sailors track stars. Except their stars are warm, and breathe. Their eyes they keep for festival: lamplight, and the bright water. Sight is a special-occasion sense. Their voices have gone the other way — no one in the kettle owns a carrying voice anymore. A kettle person who tries to shout produces a harsh whisper and feels afterward as though something has been broken. Babies cry, and that is the one sound the law forgives, because a baby cannot keep it, so the town keeps it around the baby until the baby can.

Stillness comes before speech, and then speech arrives whispered, below the breath of your host, and alongside it a second language pressed into palms and forearms for talking across a dark hut, and a third that goes through the floor. They learned the floor-speech from the great ones: a bone mallet on a column-butt makes no clang the mat can carry upward, only a soft boom that travels through the felt and arrives in the soles and sternums of the next hut over. The people speak to one another the way the animals breathe. Paths between huts carry knotted guide-cords, the knots encoding whose door, how many paces, what hazard; you announce yourself by plucking a tremor down the line. Records are kept the same way — every family holds knotted cords matched to its house-layers, and a historian recites by fingertip, in the dark, at the speed of touch.

Warmth is the only wealth; everything else is a way of holding it. It is measured, carried, gifted, inherited. The seep's hot vents are the kitchens — fish and floor-apples lowered in felt nets to cook — and from the vents the town ferries heat itself, in seam-stones: dense stones soaked in the hot water, wrapped in felt, carried to warm a bed, a birth-hut, an elder's hands. A bride's traditional gift is a basket of them. Hospitality is the warm side of the hut; rudeness is standing between a person and their flank, casting a heat-shadow; a lie is a cold word; death is going coldward. A life is understood as a slow walk warmward — born in the warmest hut, ranging outward through childhood, settling, and then year by year moving back in toward the seams and the flanks, until the oldest sit closest of all, wrapped in felt, listening, which is the last and highest work. Copper, the one metal, is wrapped for two reasons and both are true: cold metal takes skin, and the ring of it takes something from everyone who hears. A smith works in the warmest seam so the metal stays kind, tapping with a bone mallet, and each piece has a name and a lineage and a wrapping of its own.

Fire, such as it is, lives on the cold side — the kiln-yard where the season's clay is fired, the drying racks, the rendering frames — because fire is loud in its way, and hungry, and the town exiles its noise the way it exiles its cold. The one lamp per hut burns the room's whole economy at once: fish oil from the pulses, stretched, when there is any, with the rendered fat of the dead ones, on a wick of the floor's own felt. Time is told in wicks and breaths and drips for the short of it, in pulses and crawls and arrivals for the long, and in deaths for the longest of all, with the house-layers beneath even that. A lamp burns a known span; a shift of grooming is measured in the Old Hill's breaths; nobody has ever needed noon.

The town is governed by the listeners, who are chosen for stillness rather than age, though the two tend to travel together. Their work is attention: the taste of the seep, the pitch of the breathing, the rumor of the rim. They announce the pulses and the arrivals and the rare wrongness, and they judge the disputes, which are almost all disputes about warmth — whose felt-plot, whose grooming shift, who may build where. Justice is measured in degrees: a family judged wrong is moved coldward, its plots reassigned, its grooming-rights suspended, and the worst sentence in the law — to be sent over the rim — is near-myth, pronounced perhaps once against someone who killed what should have been kept alive. The two laws are really one, and the people state it as one: keep the room. Keep it quiet, keep it warm, keep the animals breathing, keep the dead in the floor, keep the cords knotted. A child who lies still through a whole bright water is praised the way other children are praised for running far.

They give their dead to the mat. Wrapped in felt cut from the family's own plot, laid in the trench at the midden's edge, covered, and left — and the mat, which keeps everything and forgets nothing, takes them. A season later the grave is a warm rise, richer than the ground around it, because the dead go on giving off heat for a long while down in the dark. Families come and lay their palms flat on the warmth. They say the old ones are keeping the room now, and mean it as plainly as bread.

A few, once in a generation, go the other way. The far-goers follow a departing great one up the door-ramp and out into the coldward nothing, trusting the walking map, carrying wrapped copper and dried crawler-meal and knotted road-cords that continue past the edge of every map the town owns. Some come back with antler, sinew, spouses, news: that there is another town two rooms over along the animal-roads, that there are rooms with no people at all, and that there is — this is the story told at the very lowest whisper — a dead room, found by far-goers long ago, its seep failed, its columns fallen, its mat grey to the walls, a town's plates scattered like a roof after wind. They tell it not to frighten children but to instruct adults: warmth is not a fact about the world. It is a practice. It can end.

So the room keeps itself. And on the nights when the seep pulses and the water rising through the mat carries the faint cold luminescence up out of the deep, the dousing travels through the constellation hut by hut like a slow wind — one lamp, then another, then all — until the only light in the world is the floor's, dim and blue, the columns standing in it like reeds in a moonlight that has forgotten the moon. The fish rise. The pickers still on the hides. The great ones shift, and grunt in their subsonic register, answering something in the water or in the glow, no one knows which, and the sound arrives in the sternum rather than the ear. The people lie on their hut floors and watch the only weather of light their world produces. The Old Hill breathes, once, in a long count of a hundred. Every living thing in the room is turned toward the same few warmths like needles toward lodestone — the grazers toward the bloom, the fish toward the pulse, the people toward the flanks, the pickers toward the plates, the mat toward the seep, and the dead, down in their warm layers, toward the living. Stand anywhere in it, and the warmth on one side of your face tells you exactly where you are, which is here, which is kept, which is home.
