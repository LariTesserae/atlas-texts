Ask who lives here and the valley hands you a list. Hold the list loosely. The valley corrects its categories the way other places correct their maps: nightly, without apology.

The towers are the first citizens you notice, and the least singular. Every tower runs three generations deep at minimum — the grandfather scaffold, dead and standing; the recolonizer that climbed the corpse and now holds it upright the way ivy holds a wall, load-bearing now, the dead wood reduced to a memory of height; and in the recolonizer's crotches, seedlings of the next regime, biding. A tower is a family argument rendered in wood: the dead supply the shape, the living supply the grip, the young supply the opinion. Some towers lean. The lean is inherited.

The hedges are not many. Learn this early: a hedge-wall is one individual, cloned along its whole length, and the straightness that unnerves you is not discipline but identity — a single body cannot help being continuous. The grid of blocks is a census of a few hundred beings, each miles long. When you walk an avenue and feel the roots deciding to tolerate you, that is precisely what is happening: a referendum conducted root to root at the speed of chemistry, re-voted hourly. Your continued passage is a streak of carried motions. An avenue is two wardens keeping their word, and the pepper-and-crushed-stem smell is the smell of law — of egos keeping their distance in writing.

The cistern-blooms are the rememberers. Water is memory; that is the first law, and they are its keepers. Each axil cups a hoard sealed in resin like wax on a letter, and each hoard is a vintage. This cup holds last Tuesday's rain. This one holds the rain of the scarp's first year. In the long thirst the valley borrows against them, and the debt of water you hear itemized in the bass of the roar is owed mostly to the rememberers, who forget nothing, whose interest accrues in the currency of the count. The oldest hold water that remembers the basin bare. You may be tempted to drink. You would be drinking the archive. The thorns vote no.

The petal-moths are the errands. Each one a flower's severed hand, and a severed hand cannot come home — every flight is one-way, and the blizzard of them is the valley's whole correspondence sent at once, trusting volume over address. They land on you because you are warm and they are calibrated to warmth, and where the storm of them thins around your body, the valley reads the gap. You are detected as a hole in the mail. Occasionally one dies on your shoulder: a letter never opened. Keep it. It has your temperature in it.

The founders are the children. A green fruit the size of your fist rises on stilt-toes, tests the avenue's poison with one foot, withdraws, decides, and crosses — a walk from noon to dusk, the single adventure of its life — and kneels at the edge of an unmade block, and the kneel is the planting, and it never rises again. The walk is told forever afterward in the grain of the wood: a three-meter epic. Most never clear their mother's shade; the root-felt takes them back without malice. The ones that cross are why the map redraws nightly. Districts are not built. Districts happen at a toddler's pace, and then are named by the wardens, in poison, which is to say in law.

The colonnades are the decided. Understand: the fall is chosen. An old tree leans for a season, aiming, and when it lets go it falls into its own future, across a gap it measured in advance, and roots at the far end and goes on living in the new tense. Down was a local opinion; they emigrated. The ring of arches around each plaza is a cohort — a meeting of elders that fell together, in agreement, in a single season, to make a center. The meeting never adjourns; the pack-ice groan at the bottom of the roar is them, still discussing. And the plaza they ring is bare by poison the way an eardrum is bare. The arches lean inward like the bones of an ear because that is what they are. Stand there and you are standing on the valley's hearing, and the syntax almost resolves because you are finally inside the ear. It was never watching you. It has been listening to you since the rim. The centers move — you noticed — because attention does.

The lookers, the heliotrope courts, are how it watches when it bothers. Attention here is not a sense; it is a growth direction. A courtyard of blooms turns toward your warmth the way a sentence turns toward its object, and after you pass they stay turned, an hour or more, holding your afterimage in gold. Your whole day's route lies behind you in turned faces — a slow-exposure photograph of a warm thing that passed — and the valley reads it at dusk. They will notice your fever before you do. There is no malice in it, and no comfort either. You are simply the most interesting weather in years.

The week-long live at the bottom, in the basin's dust, as a dated stack. They are seed for years, for decades, and then the silence comes — the count's one indrawn breath, the ear clearing itself — and the sky lets go, and the avenues get their single week of being rivers (they remember it the rest of the year; it is the only nostalgia the valley permits itself), and the flash-lake stands, and the week-long are up within minutes. The green fur creeping over open water is a million siblings born in the same hour. They flower, they set seed into their own drowning, the lake is drunk, and they are dust again, filed. Every rain the valley has ever taken lies down there as a generation. Its weather diary is written in ancestors. The tripled roar of those days is the recount — everything re-itemized in the wet, the loudest audit of the year.

And then there are the ones who live on you. Nothing eats, nothing is eaten — but things ride. A whole guild waits as seed for the walker niche: burrs angled for cloth, spores mixed to the exact mud of a boot-tread, petals that will spend themselves against your skin like moths at a lamp, calibrating. You are the valley's animal, pressed into service like everything else — pollinator, disperser, warm errand. When you climb out, the sound that follows you a day past the rim is partly this: your cargo disembarking along your trail, spending itself on foreign ground, coins that are not legal tender out there. It lets you go. It keeps the count.

Which brings you to the census you asked for, and the valley's correction. The list was a ladder. From the top, the categories show as what they are — roles, words, jobs held by wood that has grafted itself into one continuous negotiation. At any graft, and there are a billion, put your ear to the swollen seam and you can hear two interior rivers learning each other's names; the valley is made of these introductions, none of them finished. The snap-crackle in every trunk is the count's small print, thirst itemized by the column. The districts are organs; the organs are words; the sentence is the roar. The census has one entry. The resident of the valley is the counting — an inventory that never finishes, because finishing would be a kind of death, the way a held breath is a kind of death. Everything else — tenants, wardens, rememberers, errands, founders, decided, lookers, the week-long, you — is what it counts with.

Or counts.

That includes you now. When you leave, the avenue narrows behind you not to keep you but to close the bracket; the runners filling your footprints are the valley smoothing its page. But the count is not kept on the page. The count is kept on the tongue. You are recited now — a footfall among footfalls, a warmth among weathers — the way other places recite their dead: daily, by name. And years from now, on some street of some far city, a crowd-noise or a river will almost resolve into syntax, and you will stop walking, and somewhere the valley — patient, thrifted, looped, one being and a million, never still — will go right on counting you.

From the beginning. With interest.
