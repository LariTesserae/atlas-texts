**The Census of the Throat**

The settlement knew you were coming a day before you did. The felt told them. Your footfalls went into the weave and along it, league by league, and by the time you reached the first chime-fence, a keeper was waiting with her cheek still warm from the tuning post, and a child was holding out a bowl of waterless oil for your bleeding ears, and nobody was surprised. They will press two fingers to your throat before they feed you. It is not medicine. They are checking that you are still.

Everything that lives here lives in the chord, and the chord is divided up like arable land. Elsewhere, life divided the light — this color for leaves, that for wings. Here it divided the sound. Every band is owned.

**The One That Listens**

The felt is not many things. It is one thing — the people are careful about this — a single woven being that carpets the basin from rim-scree to Meal, into which the groves are grafted and through which everything else walks. It has moods. It thickens toward music: the paths to the drum-towers are greener than bronze, almost, lush with the felt leaning toward the bass, and keepers say a station never moves because the felt will not let it. It must be sung to before it will be cut — a path-maker is a singer first, and a bad singer makes the weave squeal and knot and pucker closed behind the knife.

Where the Hush has passed, the felt goes deaf. Patches of it, house-sized, that report nothing, that do not know your foot from the wind's. The keepers drum those patches, hour after hour, pressing the beat in through their palms, re-teaching the ground to hear. Sometimes it recovers. Sometimes it does not, and the patch is walked around for a generation, and the children are told the ground there is asleep, which is a kind way of saying the world has gone a little more deaf. Whether the felt is the world's eardrum or merely its skin is an argument the keepers have been having, pleasurably, for centuries. Both sides agree on the practical point: it remembers. Nothing that has ever walked here has walked unrecorded.

**The Bass**

The grazers are the clocks. Each herd keeps its own tempo — one beat every two or three breaths, felt through the soles before it is heard — and herds keep tempo territories, so that a traveler reads the land the way a sailor reads currents: this slow quadruple pulse is the range of the Old Long Beat, that quickening underfoot means the Broken-Bar herd has drifted east. A young grazer is a soloist. An old one is an orchestra — it carries beat-ticks in the folds of its slabs, whole colonies of syncopating passengers, and a keeper can age a grazer blind, by ear, from the clutter of its groove.

They eat the fallen spire-forests, milling bronze stalk to flour in jaws like millstones, and groves spring up along their paths from what passes through them. Very old grazers are named and used as landmarks — *turn left at the Anvil* — and when one dies, the basin genuinely stumbles: a beat goes out of the world, and for days the chord feels wrong, lopsided, a table with a short leg. The people hold vigil at the body and then, because grief here is never wasteful, hold the salvage: hide for the tower-drums, so that the rim goes on speaking with a grazer's voice; rib-bones for the chime-fences; fat for oil. In the Swell, for the only time in their lives, all herds everywhere share one tempo and one note. What it costs them to redivide afterward, into their separate clocks, no one knows.

**The Shriek**

The kites are the loudest things alive for their weight, and the safest. Their keen is not song; it is armor. A flock's combined shriek is a wall of noise that blinds the felt-listeners and pains the air itself, and nothing — nothing — goes into that wall after them. They skim stridulator-swarms and shear through moth-clouds, and they never, ever land alone. A lone kite is a contradiction, and in the Throat, contradictions are eaten.

They nest in the terrace walls, and the chicks are launched into the rising thermals like sailors committed to a tide, and the ones who miss the column go to the Meal, and the Meal does not give them back.

Every Listener knows the story of the Falling Quiet. A flock of ten thousand went silent at once, mid-spiral, in full daylight — no gap, no warning, the shriek-band simply *stopping*, the way a chime stops — and they came down on the Meal like a hush made visible. Nothing was ever found but vanes that would not hold a charge. The people do not say what did it. They tap a drumskin twice after telling it, refilling the hole the story made.

**The Small Chord**

The stridulators are the floor-noise and the undertakers. The dead of the Throat do not rot; they stand, mummified, sometimes for years, statues that are usually corpses, and the stridulator-swarms are the unpickers that shred them, patiently, into the charged flour that hangs over the basin and granulates the light. You will breathe your grandmother at noon. This is not a figure of speech.

Their static rises with the heat, so they are the basin's thermometer, and every household keeps a dish of them at the threshold — a door-swarm, fed on flour, tuned by generations of breeding to a bright, anxious shimmer. The dish is the door's voice. If the dish stutters, check the fence. If the dish stops, you are already running. Travelers carry pocket-swarms in gourds, the way other peoples in other places carried canaries into mines, and for the same reason.

**The Percussion of Thirst**

At dusk the wells fill, in every sense. The queue is ancient and observed: grazers first, shouldering in with the entitlement of weather; then the drum-swarms, fist-sized beetles that beat their bellies against the resin's black skin in rolling rhythms — they drum the well to make it flow, since vibration is what fluidizes everything here, and so for them feeding and courtship are the same act, and a well in full dusk-swing is a percussion of thirst and flirtation that carries for miles. Each well has its own house-rhythm. The well-wardens know them all, and over centuries the wardens' beats have entered the etiquette of the fauna: the grazers know a warden's bar the way they know thunder, and they yield the way they yield to thunder — slowly, and with commentary.

Everything drinks loudly, on doctrine. Slurping, sputtering, drumming on the skin. It is the most dangerous hour of the day, because everyone must come to the wells, and everyone knows that everyone must come.

**The Hourlings**

The Meal is not empty. It is a held breath. Locked in the dust, charge-banked like capacitors, are the eggs of the hourlings — an entire citizenry that hatches only in the Swell, when the chord locks and the dunes go liquid. They are born screaming into a world already ending: they hatch, feed, mate, lay, and die inside the flowing hour, whole generations between one ripple of a dune and the next, and when the Great Quieting comes the Meal settles over their shells like a lid. The dunes are mostly this — a library ground to dust, the powder of uncountable brief loud lives. In a thin year, when a Swell fails to fully lock, the hourlings do not hatch at all, and the floor goes strange and solemn, and the keepers note it down, and the next year's hatching is double.

A creature that founders in the Meal drowns dry, and the rule for a foundering person is the same as the rule everywhere, only more so: keep screaming. Silence is the one unforgivable thing. Rescue parties are led by criers — bell-throats, they are called, professional loud voices, the strongest and most honored lungs in any settlement — and the sound of one coming across the dunes at full cry is, the people say, the best sound in the world, though they say it the way you say things you hope never to test.

**The Two Silences**

Two things in the Throat drink sound. One is small, and the people hunt it. One is not, and the people do not talk about it, and tap a drumskin when they must.

The mute-moth flies in the night swarms among the blue-sparking clouds of its loud cousins, and it is the one that does not spark. The hunters watch for a moth-shaped darkness crossing the glitter — a gap moving against the grain of the light — and they net it, and this is the most feared work there is, because the hunt happens at night, near the wells, in the Hush's own hours, and the hunter must work quietly, the only sanctioned quiet in the culture, while a partner a hundred paces off makes noise enough for two. The noisemaker is the lifeline. The moth-hunters go strange over years — their voices thin, their presence dims — and soft speech is unsettling here, so they are half-outcast, necessary and unloved, like gravediggers who are also the only source of sleep. *Rich as a moth-hunter* means prosperous and not long for it. It takes some thirty thousand wings to line a sleeping-room. A town, in the end, is a pocket of silence big enough to dream in, and every stitch of it is a hunt that someone survived.

Of the other silence, this is all that is known, and all of it is known from its edges. It is perceived as a hole in the static. Where it walks on the Meal, it leaves a hush-track — a line of dead flour, dust that will not hold a charge, that falls without clinging — and a path crossed by a hush-track is a path abandoned until a Swell erases it. Its victims are found mummified mid-stride, unmarked, mouths open. What it takes from them, since it takes no flesh, the keepers will not guess at in writing. Whether there is one Hush or many is a question people have been exiled for asking carelessly. Whether the mute-moths are its kin, its lice, or its spies is the great argument of the ear-shrines. The law in the presence of the evidence is short and has no exceptions: run toward sound. Any sound. The louder the better.

**The Listeners**

There are perhaps nine thousand of them, all told, scattered along the terraces and the rim, and they all know one another's beats.

Their bodies are instruments tuned for this one venue. They are born with thick eardrums and go gently deaf across their lives, and they call this *growing quiet*, and mean it as praise. A child's eardrums thicken all at once, around the fifth year, in a brief fever of ear-bleeding called the Thickening, after which the child is walked out onto the felt and taught the law. From then on the stages of a life are measured in quiet: thin-ear, half-quiet, deep-quiet. The deeply deaf elders sleep outside the quiet-houses in the full blast of noon, smiling — they have been, as the saying goes, promoted out of the audience and into the instrument. And it is the deep-quiet who make the finest cheek-readers at the tuning posts, and the fastest readers of the knot-cords, running the knotted rhythms through their fingers with their eyes closed. *The air lies,* they say. *The bone tells true.* Everyone pretends it is not funny that the deaf are the best listeners in the world.

They go barefoot always — boots would deafen the feet — and they hum, all the time, each person carrying a private note the way you carry a name. Which is nearly literal, because a name here is not a word. Words drown. Your name is a bar of rhythm, drummed into the felt on the day of your birth, and registered at the rim-tower, and if your gait is true the land repeats it back to you a little with every step — you walk through the world hearing your name said by the ground, and this is so ordinary to them that they have to be told that elsewhere, the earth does not know who you are. They do not ask *how are you*. They press two fingers to your throat and ask *are you still*. Marriage is the braiding of two bars into one counterpart rhythm. They tell time in intervals: dawn is unison, the chord climbs a third toward noon, and you make an appointment *at the fourth*.

Their work is all acoustic husbandry. They do not farm plants; they farm chords. A field is a tuning maintained against heat and charge; a harvest is the grove coming into pitch. They prune reeds to note, race the kites for the heat-popped drum-pod seed, sing the felt to sleep before the knife. Their keepers are Swell-callers above all — reading the accumulating charge in the posts, calling the Swell a day out so the groves can be moored and the herds watched and the hourling-nets set — and a caller with a string of true calls is rich in the only currency that matters, which is being believed. A false call is a long shame. The drum-towers speak in bass, under the world, in the one band nothing else owns; the people live, they say, in the cellar of the spectrum, and they like the cellar.

The law is the law of the world: make noise. Babies wear bells. Children wear rattle-anklets and cannot sneak, which is the point. A quiet child is rushed to the keeper, because quiet in a child is shock or sickness. The forbidden game, the one that turns parents white, is hush-tag — daring silence — and a child caught at it wears sewn bells for a month. Because the law is not prudence. It is maintenance. The people believe — and the charge in the air and the heat in the stone make it hard to argue — that the world is warm because it is loud, that cold is the sound of silence, that the Dampening will come back the moment the chord falters. Every voice is a coal. They are, by their own account, the descendants of everything that ran into the Throat when the silence fell, and they are keeping the door warm.

The worst punishment they have is not death. They do not kill their worst. They stop hearing them. The unhearing is pronounced at the tower, the criminal's name-beat is struck from the registry, and from that day no one answers their noise — no greeting, no pulse-touch, no dish set out. A person no one answers is indistinguishable from the Hush, and no path will have them. They go up onto the upland, into the Deaf Land, where nothing answers anyone.

Their dead they give to the static, laid on open felt in the loud hours, and the unpickers take them into the dust, and the dust into everyone's lungs, and this is considered a fortunate, sociable afterlife. The great keepers are sometimes kept instead — sealed in warm amber at the ear-shrines, where the sun comes through them at dusk, and children are brought to see what forever looks like. Either way, the dead one's name-beat is retired, written into the knot-cords the archivists call the Book of Rests, and never drummed again — except on the first Swell of the warm season, when the towers drum the roll of all the dead at once, into the one chord big enough to carry them safely, and the whole basin, herds and reeds and kites and static and all, beats for an hour with the names of everyone who ever kept the noise.

And they are, it must be said, happy. In the evenings, when the chord drops into its minor key, the towers put on under-concerts, and the bass goes out through the felt for miles, and three settlements will dance to one drum a night's walk apart, perfectly together, the ground itself keeping them in step — the only large thing the people ever do, and they do it constantly. Their singers practice the one art the basin cannot do itself: finding the note the Throat is missing, the gap in the great chord, and standing in it, and being, for a song, the exact sound the world lacked.

**What They Send You Away With**

When you leave, they will give you a gourd with a pocket-swarm in it, and oil for your ears, and they will teach you your own name-beat — they will have composed it from your gait, in the days you stayed, without telling you — and drum it into the felt at the edge of their path, so that the ground as far as the next station will know you. Their blessing is *may you be heard*. Their farewell is *keep your noise*. Their worst curse, never said at a parting, is one word, and it means *unheard*.

And then the upland, and the three days of nothing, and the pressure leaving your teeth by degrees. But it will not be the same silence you crossed on the way in. You will catch yourself, on the second evening, humming — your own note, the one you did not know you carried — and you will understand that you are not singing to fill the silence.

You are keeping a coal warm.
