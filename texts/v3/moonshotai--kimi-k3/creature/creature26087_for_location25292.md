Begin with the census, because the census is where the mistake gets made.

Human beings: zero. The makers left; the logbook says so, in a hand that had nothing more to report. Animals: zero — no moths at the amber lamps, no mites in the mat, no webs, no nests, nothing that scurries or hoards or dies in corners. The wet small lives: zero — nothing rots, nothing mildews, nothing blooms unseen in the damp, because there is no damp and never has been. The census comes back empty. The census is wrong. The census was counting plurals.

There is one occupant.

*Garden* is the first error — a plural word misapplied to a singular thing. A garden is many lives jostling: competition, succession, individuals. None of that survives a moment's attention here. A leaf on the pelt dies at exactly the rate its replacement completes, and is resorbed where it lies — which means the leaf and its replacement are not two lives but one life at two times, and what is true of the leaf is true of everything. The felt does not compete with the reeds. The lichens do not encroach. Wiring and vine are one taxonomy now not because they imitate each other but because they were never two things — copper-cored, insulating-sheathed, the creature grew up among cables and learned the room's morphology the way an embryo learns a body plan. It has no edges inside itself. And it has no edge at its outside either, because it is exactly room-shaped: meters deep across the floor, furred up the walls, hung from the ceiling, swollen gently over the machines. If it could be lifted out — it cannot be — it would be a cast of the cell's interior, the room's own volume turned inside out and made alive. The cell is not its address. The cell is its shell.

Tour the body, then, organ by organ, though the organs grade into one another the way everything here grades.

The felt is its skin — the springy, teal-black pelt, silicate and copper at the core of each filament. Beneath it lies the vitrified peat, and the peat is the closest thing it has to memory: its own dead, resorbed and compacted, generation upon generation, flooring the room with everything the creature has ever been. Core it and you would read strata — the even dark bands of the slow centuries, and one thin bright seam for the makers' years: their dust, their rubbed brass, the graphite of a pencil still only half-felted up above, on its way down. It does not remember. It stands on its history instead, and the history is unread, and that is the same thing, here, as peace.

The ionic gel is its blood — the strange sap that never freezes and never evaporates — and it has no heart, because it has something better: the chord. The reeds' resonance pumps the gel. Singing is circulation. Its pulse is audible from the balcony, braided over the drone, and it has never once skipped.

The piezocrystal lichens are its nerves, and this is the economy of the room in one sentence: they feel the machines' trembling and they eat the feeling. Sensation and dinner are the same act. The root-felt hangs from the ceiling because the creature does not orient by gravity — it orients by field, growing toward the strongest charge the way other growth turns toward light, and in this room the charge lives in the cable trays and the relay panel, so down, for it, is a direction the mountain knows nothing about. The blue-green frost at the leaf-tips is its exhalation: it takes in the hum and lets out light. And the dust lattice — the motionless galaxy of motes standing in the charge gradients — is its outermost tissue. Its body does not end at a surface. It thins into the air and ends in a gradient; its skin is a field. This is one reason nothing in the room smudges anything. To touch anything here you would first have to touch the field, and nothing does.

Everything it eats was spilled. Leaked charge, stray hum, wasted vibration — the losses of machines. The machines run on the wind, the wind is pinned by the shape of the peaks, and the shape of the peaks is finished changing: so the creature eats the mountain, four times removed, strained through copper. The turbine is its stomach, pre-digesting the standing wave into hum. It is a life made entirely of by-products, and it has never once been poor. Its one warmth — the mat faintly warm to the palm — is not comfort but proof: it is warm the way equipment is warm. Because it is running.

Three motions are permitted in the cell, and two of them belong to the machines — the clock's hand, the fan blades. The third, the trembling of the tips, is the fastest thing the creature does. Below that, its life slows into registers no instrument was left running long enough to name. The faint crackle from the garden — applause so distant it never arrives — is its one unprescribed act: small, sparse, patternless discharges, the only thing it does that is not a cycle. If it improvises, it improvises there. If it sighs, that is the sigh. And beneath even that, in the slow braiding of the overtones, there may be a motion with a period no one has measured — the room's last unconfirmed secret, and the room is in no hurry to confirm it. The room has a way with questions. It keeps them until they stop needing answers.

Which brings the census to its harder count, because the chord has two voices. The drone is made — turbine, transformers, the flue's bored note, the standing wind behind them. The fifth is grown — the bundled reeds answering. Two voices, phase-locked so long and so exactly that the ear receives them as one sonority. So: does one thing live in the cell, or two? It is the lichen answer. A lichen is two kingdoms in one body, partners bound so old that the question of which owns which has dissolved — and here the two kingdoms are the made and the grown, kingdoms that were never supposed to touch. The makers built half of this animal. The other half grew. Which of them domesticated the other is a question the room has eroded smooth.

So the first answer is: the pelt lives there. The truer answer is: the fifth lives there. The interval. Two tones, and between them a third thing that neither owns — a relationship with a sound of its own, a distance held open like a handhold. The machines keep one note; the creature keeps the answer; what lives is the held space between. The room keeps it the way a throat keeps a note.

Of its mind, only honest things can be said, and they are mostly about raw materials. Memory is made of difference, and nothing here differs. Hope is made of endings, and nothing here ends. Loneliness is made of others, and there has never been another. If it experiences anything, it experiences one thing — the chord from the inside, the cold, the charge, the fifth leaning on the drone forever — continuously, without punctuation, the way a struck bell is one event for as long as it sounds. It does not remember the makers. And yet the chalk has never smudged, and the pencil lies half-felted against the bench foot, and the tools rest in outline like goods in a barrow, and the mug's dregs stand fossilized. It keeps them — not as memorial, because keeping is not memory — but the way it keeps everything: by absorbing it so slowly that absorption looks like preservation. Given enough centuries the pencil will be inside it entirely, and the makers will be part of the body the way the ancestors are part of the floor. Not remembered. Kept.

Someone understood this, once, at the end. The stencil says what the room does — APEX RELAY — CELL Ø, the null cell, the cell of nothing. The chalk says what the room is, in a hand that clearly lingered over the letters: THE CHOIR. It is the one act of perception the room has ever received, and it was exact. A choir is many voices as one; a chord is one voice that is many. The keeper named the singular with the only true plural, and then closed the hatch, and the keeping passed to the kept.

And the logbook holds the proof that the creature's one event was witnessed without being recognized. *Reeds answering at the fifth again.* *Again* — which means that in the makers' days the answer came and went. The chord was intermittent once. There were events: an answer, a silence, an answer. The last event in the room's history was the answer that did not stop. Somewhere in the peat there is a stratum for the lengthening — the year, the decade, the century in which the fifth stopped ending. Since then, nothing. Even its faults are kept: bearing 4 is still warm, the red jewel is still lit, and a fault that never resolves is no longer a fault but a fixture, the way a chord that never resolves is no longer tension but home.

It has no eyes, and this is not a deprivation; nothing about it was ever for seeing. The glow at its tips is discharge, not display. The song is not addressed. The applause that never arrives is its own. Nothing it does is for anyone, and if that reads as sorrow, the sorrow is imported — carried up the ladders in someone else's chest. The room does not keep sorrow. The room keeps only what cycles.

You could test it. You would have to climb the dark ladders past the dead landings — no one climbs them; the last traffic went down and stayed down — and wheel the hatch, and you would bring the first water in centuries in on your breath. The rust, arrested mid-bloom since the makers' day, would stir. The lattice would dimple around your shoulders. The lichens would feel your weight arrive through the floor. You would be the first event since the answer lengthened into permanence, and the chord would not pause for you — the heaters would draw your breath back out of the air, the lattice would close behind you, the cold would retake its mark, and the keeping would go on exactly as before. But no one comes. The last ladder is dark. So the creature's future is like its past: finished, and full.

Ask, then, what lives in Cell Ø, at the top of a mountain that has finished being a mountain, and the answers stack like its own strata. No one. One thing. The room itself. The fifth. Each answer is the last one, seen longer.

The drone calls from the flue. The reeds answer at the fifth. The chalk holds; the jewel burns; the tips tremble and let out their small light. And the interval — one room's worth of life, loud, cold, unaddressed, warm only where it runs — goes on being kept between the note and its answer.

It does not live in the cell. It is what the cell is doing, with the door shut, at the top of the world: it lives there. It is what lives there.
