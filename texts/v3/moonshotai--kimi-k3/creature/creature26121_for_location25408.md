No one appears at the rim to meet you. Give it time.

The Weep's inhabitants are not hidden; they are merely slower than your attention, and smaller, and stiller, and a day at the rim teaches you to look the way the place requires. Sit until the drizzle has found every fold of your clothing. It smells of iron and rain and of nothing rotting, ever. By the time you are wet through, the dark seam along the far wall will have moved a hand's width, and it will have developed a head.

Begin with the air, because the air is inhabited. The mist is not water only; it is stocked. Each hanging droplet is a possible pond, and most are tenanted — specks that swim the width of their world and call it enough, born when two droplets merge, dispersed when one divides. To them the interior rain is not weather. It is arrival. They ride the warm up-breath out of the pool's heart and come down again as the drizzle, and the skaters on the skin below harvest the fall like a crop. The Weep's plankton are airborne. Its sky is a slow sea, turned over on the hollow's own breathing.

The first resident, and the landscape, is the film. Everything else here is either grazing it, filtering what it sheds, wearing it, or waiting to be taken up by it — it is less a species than a profession the whole bowl practices. In the sheltered corners it goes down a finger's depth, then a hand's, and the lower layers are dark and busy and tunnelled: pale borers no longer than a grain of wheat spend their whole lives eating horizontally through a wall, and the film heals their wakes in green. The ochre terraces are the film's ancestors — old veneer, mineralized, given back to the water as stairs. As for the light it makes when touched: the light is not for you. The film does nothing for witnesses. Whether something comes up out of the crust to answer the bright spot, no watcher has stayed still long enough to learn. The asking, here, is how watchers become part of the record.

The grazers come in three callings. The common ones, thumb-sized, are translucent enough to keep their own accounts — you can read a week of meals banded inside them like a core sample. The edge-grazers work the furred frontier where the film thins toward the dry, and the ragged edge is not the film's decision; it is theirs. And there are the old ones, hand-sized, decades deep, slowing year by year toward the condition of terrain, crusting at the back. The place repays long residence with incorporation. On the iron stair lives one of these, the same one as far as anyone can tell, on the fourth turn, though the stair is sixty steps of nothing it needs. The region's people call it the monk. They greet it on the way down. It does not greet them, being a grazer.

Every trail on the walls is written in two inks: the green that answers at once, and the dark bare stripe that lingers for days until the film creeps back over it. The walls are a palimpsest of meals.

The terraces are let floor by floor. Each hand-sized pool holds its dozen swimmers the size of rice grains, tuned pool by pool to the seep's stepwise cooling, and some of the pools are not pools. Some are mouths. The waiters are sinter-backed and thumb-sized and indistinguishable from the step they sit on, and a grazer that crosses one does not cross it twice. It is the Gratekeeper's trade, holding still and letting the stone do the disguising — rare at the Gratekeeper's scale, common at the waiters'. The Weep approves of stillness and pays for it in camouflage.

The causeway has its caps: limpet-things that clamp at the ebb with a cup of the flood held over themselves, landlords to a small damp tenancy of mites and refugees until the water returns. The ebb-walkers work the just-exposed film ahead of the descending grazers, keeping precisely with the retreating wet. Nothing in the Weep is fast, but the ebb-walkers are punctual, and among the slow, the punctual pass for sprinters. And at every ebb the drifting continents ground on the causeway's back, and their natives come ashore — pale, minute, looking for work. The continents have natives; the raft-mites live their whole lives on the floating flakes, and every shore in the Weep is partly staffed by castaways.

On the skin, the skaters. Their feet dent the surface, and below each dent a fish rises to inspect the possibility. A moth that falls with wet wings is a wreck and a harvest that takes days; the skaters gather over it like a rig over a hull. Their outgrown skins join the continents. Nothing is wasted. Wasting is a habit of drier places.

Below them, the fish keep stations: the warm fissure, where they hang head-up like held breaths; the causeway's shadow at ebb; the intake's margin, which only the old and the unlucky work close. Their eyes are mirrors because the only light worth gathering in the Weep is the light the walls lend. Look close and your lamp comes back to you out of each of them, slightly improved by distance. Their shed scales sink slowly, and the floor takes them.

Such as the floor is.

The floor is not quite silt. It breathes a little out of step with the pool — in at the draw, out at the slack, a half-beat late, like something following at a distance. Stars go out from below now and then. It is the one arrangement in the Weep that is not mutual, only old, and neither the fish nor anyone else discusses it.

At the deepest part, the office. The Gratekeeper owns the only speed in the Weep and spends it a quarter-inch at a time, a few times an hour, on whatever the intake's pull brings within reach. It wears the walls on its back; its crust is laid in the same seasons as the terraces, and a patient reader could check its autobiography against the bowl's. When it dreams, the needles in the gauge hut sway, and no one reads them, which is the correct way to keep a dream. When it shifts — which is rare — everything else goes still, the way a room does when an elder rises, and the knocking in the pipes falls silent for days.

The knocking is the eel, the Works' surveyor, self-appointed. A run the eel keeps does not silt; the iron stays honest where it is used. It is the only tenant that arrived from below — up the warm fissures, long ago, thin as a bootlace, out of some buried water that has never been named. The soft knock that crosses the hollow is its change of address. It grew to the pipe and it is still growing, and its choices narrow as it widens: one year it will take the culvert, and after that there is the intake, and after that there is the question of the keeper, which neither of them is in any hurry to raise.

In the pump house, the film grows best where something hums. It lines the bearings like a sleeve and thickens on the motor housing, happy in the note you feel in your teeth. Every open knife switch stands like the door of a small house, and every small house has its householder — a spider pale as a fingernail, webbed across the gap, reading the room by its threads. Old webs mineralize where the condensate drips; some of the Works' insulation is silk now, and it is the best insulation in the place. And the moths, of course, the moths — they are not drawn to the light. They are drawn to the light's temperature. When the pumps draw hard the lamps go thin and the moths settle; at the slack the lamps swell amber and the moths rise. They are the one gauge in the Weep that everyone can read, and their dust is the tithe the lamps collect, and the film takes it gladly.

In the governor lives the lodger: a small soft thing, all appetite and patience, that finds the escapement's rocking comfortable and grazes the living sleeve of the flywheel bearing as it turns — a hand's breadth of pasture renewing itself beneath it, the smallest farm in the world. Its grazing thins the sleeve; the sleeve thickens again overnight; the wheel runs a touch slow, a touch fast, and so the day the governor keeps is almost, not exactly, a day. Everything with gills or a grip keeps that time, which means the whole hollow is synchronized, in the end, to an appetite. This troubles no one. The hunger is regular. That is all a clock is.

At the rim there is a pale rime line, higher than the mist ever reaches now — the furthest the Weep has ever got. Once, in some wet century, the film tried the world outside, and the wind answered, and everything below the line lives, in a sense, in the country of that failure. The only other known edge is the flume's: the film follows the outflow a hundred paces, signing the gray country in ochre, thinning as the water cools, stopping. In drought years the signature shortens. No one has found the edge in the deep direction, and the eel, who would know, is not telling.

Now and then the gray country contributes. A beetle armored for a drier war. A bird once, downed, that stood a day on the causeway looking offended by the damp, and then lay down and began, gently, to be kept. The residents treat such arrivals as weather: noted, absorbed, not discussed. The moths are the great exception — the gray country's one successful export, arriving half-dead with cold on a wind that had nothing to catch on, resurrected by the first warm coil.

No people live in the Weep. The tenders stepped out for an hour, and the hour has run long — but the shift has never once been missed: the film took the bearings, the eel took the plumbing, the moths the lighting, the lodger the clock, and the keeper kept the grate, as it had since before anyone thought to build around it. The region's people come anyway, in ones and twos, with their dead on ropes. There is a ledge under the east wall where the crust is never quite smooth, where one likeness develops out of another and the oldest are only a texture. They stay on the rim until the stone lets go. That is the local length of mourning.

Visitors learn two rules. Cross at the ebb. And keep moving — the film tells the living from the dead by their stillness, and it is not infallible, and its mistakes are slow and painless and permanent.

Ask what lives in the Weep and you will get a number that will not hold still, unless you count the other way and come out with one. Stand at the rim on a still night and read the walls: every grazer signing its name, every moth's landing a comma, and once in a long while the slow bright sentence that means the keeper has taken a walk. The green script is the census — taken nightly, complete every time, erased by morning. The Weep takes attendance and burns the register and passes its own inspection, and the count always comes out the same: everyone who can. No one who cannot afford to. Room for more.
