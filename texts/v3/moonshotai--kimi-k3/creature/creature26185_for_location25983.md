The surveyors' reports give a number — some thirty thousand, more or less, the census being as perishable as the maps — but a number is the least interesting answer to who lives in Hush. Better to stand in a lane at first light and wait. An outsider's first hour in the Soft is spent believing the city empty. It is not empty. It is full to the rooflines and operating at the volume of a held breath, and the residents reveal themselves only by motion: doors easing open, flags lifting over the press-works, a market crossing a courtyard at a murmur, six hundred people going about their morning with the collective footprint of a cat. The Soft-born are ordinary people shaped by an extraordinary habit, and the habit is physical. They walk the way sailors walk — weight low, feet flat, testing — and they keep the walk all their lives, on any ground, in any country. A Soft-born hand catches a door an inch before the jamb without its owner noticing. They palm a doorframe before leaning on it. They reach for the probe-rod at the hip the way other people pat a pocket for keys, and abroad, standing at some stone curb in a finished city, they reach for it, find nothing, and feel briefly unarmed.

Every child grows up bilingual — one tongue for the air, one for the hands. The air-tongue has worn soft at the edges like everything else here: plosives rounded to murmurs, sentences ending in breath. Precision lives in the hand-tongue, and it has registers. Street-sign is broad and efficient, legible across a crowded market. Hearth-sign is small and familial, full of shorthand no outsider could parse. Lap-sign — signed below table level — is how children pass notes in lessons, how merchants' families comment on the merchandise, how lovers conduct entire arguments in public without a flicker. At the Drift Bazaar a fortune can be offered, refused, and re-offered in the space of a handshake, by hands, inside the handshake.

Between the second year and the fourth comes what parents call the louding, and the city, which has planned for everything, has planned for this: felt-walled crèches, and the deep nurseries down in the Underfloors, where silt drinks the screaming and the lamplight is kind. The Soft-born are raised for a few loud years in the gentle dark and brought up into the light when they can be trusted with it — a phrase parents deliver with a straight face. Heave-drill replaces the war games of other childhoods; the yellow lamp is the most exciting thing a child can see, meaning *everyone, now, quietly, fast*. At nine you are given your first true probe-rod and the two rules of the basin: test first, and the ground is not angry — it is busy.

A people that lives this quietly hears everything. The Soft-born hear the accumulator hum the way you hear your own heart — mostly not at all, until it stops. Whole districts sleep badly on maintenance nights. Abroad, they are famous among innkeepers for two requests: a room away from the street, and could someone please hum outside the door. In the ports of louder nations there are people who make a living at it.

You are named twice in Hush: a hearth-name for the air, short and sibilant, and a hand-name that accretes over a lifetime, picking up history the way the basin picks up silt, until the hand-names of the old are long stories their friends tell in two fingers. And because sign requires eyes, attention is the city's true currency. To face someone is to acknowledge they exist; to turn away is the heaviest sentence Hush passes. Magistrates punish with turnings — a day, three days, a week in which no one in your district will meet your eyes or read your hands. Thieves have begged for fines instead. Forgiveness is formal: the wronged party stands in the lane and faces you, and the whole street exhales.

You can tell the trades by their stains and their stance. Turf-wardens go green to the elbow and walk with their heads slightly tilted, reading. Their examination is a wall: an apprentice is walked down a street and asked to say what the moss says — the public script every citizen knows (this line stable, this corner watching) and beneath it the denser warden-script, which records what a building has survived and what the warden expects of it. Wardens condemn by subtraction; they forbid planting, and a bare wall with a warden's seal is the basin's way of saying bad news without making a sound. But a script that is living can be written, and it is: lovers ask permission to plant a stretch of a family's wall, and a proposal may be made in moss, in warden-script, weeks before it grows legible — a promise that has to be grown into, which the Soft-born consider the only honest kind.

The Listeners are chosen out of the schools, by ear: they are the children who wake at the groan. In their kiosk-towers they keep the Heave Ledger, the oldest continuous document in the basin and the only history the city wholly trusts. On the record, the Ledger is mechanics — pressure, thixotropy, swell, settle. The margins are another matter. The margins keep a vocabulary the reports refuse: a swell can be restless, sullen, pleased, and the word the margins use for the groan is a word that means both *turning over in sleep* and *not yet*. It is strictly illegal to knock back. People do it anyway — wrapped mallets, deep hours, an apologetic three taps. The Listeners who catch them write the citation in the proper hand and then, in the margin hand, ask what answer came.

The only loud people in the Soft are the kiln-keepers, and they are hired for it: chosen young for big chests and bigger voices, licensed to spend all the noise the basin saves. At funerals a keener cries into the fire-mouth so the family's grief goes up the flue and not into the ground, and a good keener can carry forty people's sorrow at once. Off shift they are the softest-spoken citizens in Hush, resting the instrument. Everyone else owes heave-duty. When the yellow lamp goes up in your district, you report, whatever your trade, and take your place in the unbolting line. The choreography that so impresses visitors is not talent; it is rehearsal, a citizenship of drill, practiced monthly, so that when a street rises a hand's width, ten thousand strangers can move a market in an afternoon at a murmur and think nothing of it.

Inside, the houses are built around departure: runners under the furniture, bolts at every joint, chests two people can carry at a fast walk, nothing precious below the second floor. On the mantel, the shelf — every house has one — of Returned things. Dinner is smoked eel out of the last burst pond, coldwort from a rib-garden, bread from hill grain, milk-water that tastes faintly of the stone it was squeezed from. Districts are people, not geography: a street that heaves keeps its name and takes it along, so addresses in Hush are relative, given from landmarks that also move, and the city's true map is its gossip. *Two over from where the bazaar was* is a precise location, and everyone can find it.

Who lives deep? More than you'd think, and not the poorest — the opposite. Depth is stability; the buried streets have finished moving. The down-dwellers are paler, slower, lamplight-eyed, and their children grow up in the museum of their own family: great-grandmother's shopfront is the corridor outside the bedroom, and the chalk marks at the foot of the stair were made by a cousin who is now sixty. The blind beetles are kept like house spirits, a dish of seep-water on the sill, and a household the beetles leave is a household packing within the week. The deep has a second use, known to everyone and discussed above a murmur by no one: the loud-rooms. Thirty feet down, with silt for soundproofing, the young go to shout, stomp, drum, sing at full chest — the only full voices in the basin — and come up hoarse and gentle, like pilgrims home from a country of noise. Above ground, the music is otherwise: hum-choirs that tune to the accumulators and braid a line a hair above silence, teeth-harps, felted zithers, instruments you feel more than hear. There is one note nobody plays. It belongs to the ground.

The dead go to the kilns, and the reason is not hygiene — the cold would keep a body forever. That is precisely the problem. Everyone has seen what the Soft does with what it keeps: the grain raised on the wood, the doll's face faintly recast, as if the ground had been thinking about it. No one is willing to learn what the ground thinks of a face that was loved. So the kilns, the only fires in the basin allowed to speak, and the ash folded into the turf-rolls, and the funeral ending on its knees, planting. You may request your street. There are curves of road out on the old ribs where three generations hold the bend together, and their families greet them in passing, palm flat to the wall, the way you'd squeeze a shoulder in a crowd. The Soft-born do not say their dead are gone. They say they are load-bearing.

What the Soft returns has its own law. A Returned thing belongs to whoever it returns to: the finder displays it on the lintel-shelf above the door for a full turning of the seasons, word goes out, and eventually someone comes with wet eyes or a helpless laugh, and there is tea, and a story. And because the ground can be written to as well as read, it is. On a birth, the family buries a box — letters, a good tool, a toy, something with a face on it for the ground to think about — and the odds-makers of the bazaar will quote you, in fingers, when and where it surfaces. Nothing edible is ever buried, and nothing that ever breathed. Everything else is correspondence, and the Soft is the slowest, strangest post in the world, and everyone uses it.

The rest of the census is small, quick, and damp, and most of it has civil status. The heave-gulls are fed by ordinance — a loud, grey, feathered alarm system nobody begrudges. Burrow-owls are counted as tenants: a warden's survey notes their occupancy of a new arch alongside the moss report, and an arch with owls is trusted sooner than one without. The draft oxen have hand-names and retire to good turf; fine teamsters are said to be half ox themselves — broad, patient, unnervingly quiet. Moss-mice in the roof are endured the way weather is endured. And below, the pale things go about their lamplight business with the confidence of the original population, which, the down-dwellers point out, they are.

On rest days the city goes out to watch the world be made — the audience on the wicker platforms, flasks going around, eels arriving from nowhere, the moss rolling in like a slow verdict. Couples time the question to a rising arch, and a proposal accepted under new geology is considered the luckiest kind. Up on the rim, the hostel post rotates; nobody holds it long — the record is eleven months — and the city regards whoever is up there the way you regard a cousin who has joined a monastery: proud, worried, in no hurry to visit. Foreigners are felt-fitted at the basin's edge — boots soled, wheels wrapped, fifty signs of courtesy — and the surveyors among them go home and write their reports, and the reports say *anomalous accretion*, and the surveyors, for years afterward, sleep badly in the silence of finished countries and never put in the reports why.

So count the residents of Hush and you count three populations. The quick — soft-footed, fluent in hands, carrying their rods and their shelves of edited things. The kept — the dead in the roads, holding the curves. And the vast — the ground under all of it, which the Listeners will not call alive on the record and will not call dead off it, which has lately been extruding doorways with plumb lines, arches with lintels, stairs that climb, each season, a little further before they stop. Ask a child who lives in Hush and she will name her mother, her team, the beetle under the kitchen sill, the grandmother in the road out front — and then, after a glance to see who is listening, she will point down. Whether she means the Underfloors or the other thing is not a question the basin asks out loud. Then she will shrug the particular shrug of the Soft-born, which means: *so far*.
