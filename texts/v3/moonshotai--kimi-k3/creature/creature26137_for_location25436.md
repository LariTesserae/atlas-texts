Ask what lives in the Forge and you hit the problem immediately: here, *alive* and *real* are the same axis. Life runs on the local currency, and the local currency is being — graded, refined, hoarded, spent. The herds are more alive than the pans. The pans are more alive than the Tolerances. Some of the residents are so thin they fail the census entirely, and live there anyway.

Nor can the census be taken with a calendar. You need a logarithm. At one end, life is counted in sparks. At the other, in strokes per millennium. Between those poles, everything the Forge keeps, in order of appearance.

## The Herd at Graze

Watch a span graze. It takes a season, so settle in.

The seam it works runs bright along a fault — a hairline of milled actuality — and the span applies the only soft-looking thing it owns: a tongue the color of deep water, broad as a saddle, moving at the speed of weather. The seam dims where the tongue passes. You can watch being leave the rock. A herd at graze is nearly still: not a refinery that has decided to walk somewhere, but a refinery *working*, fin-fires banked low, the pit-prop legs drinking heat from the stone in slow sips. Grazing is eating. Standing is drinking. Bellowing is exhalation. The whole animal is a way of getting the gradient to flow through flesh.

No herd travels alone. A span's voice outlives the day by a season, so around every herd walks its ghost-herd — the calls of spring still thinning in the autumn air, a moving valley of voices, past cows and past bulls accompanying the living at a few months' remove. The herd navigates by its own leavings, in layers: voices a season old, shadow-burns a decade old, print-ore a century, trackways worn to the eon. Its map is its memory, and its memory is written on the world in four inks. A herd is a valley that walks, and the valley is made of the herd's own history.

The castings' milestones are permanences. The first footprint that has not faded by morning. The first call still hanging in the air at dusk. The day a casting's shadow first arrives *early* — the day the world begins to dent for it. The herd marks that day. The cows feed the young on held voices: a casting stands head-high in the ghost of a call made before its birth, drinking thinned thunder, and grows a little heavier. The herd feeds its young on its own history, and the history is good for them, the way milk is good.

The rut is a singing contest, because everything in the Forge is. In summer the bulls' fins burn blue-white and their bellows roll for three days' walk, and the cows choose by persistence: the bull whose voice still fills a valley in autumn is the bull the cows remember in winter. Fights happen, but they happen the way mountains happen — two bulls lean forehead to forehead for a month, radiating at each other, and the winner is the one whose voice thins second.

Between the vanes, in the fin-shadow, live the vane-cleaners — the same quick mineral guild as the sleeve-cleaners of the piston-banks. The Forge has two kinds of engines and one kind of keeper. A span with cleaned vanes radiates efficiently; its bellow runs pure. The cleaners tune the herd, and know the rut's approach before the bulls do, because they can feel the blood heat in the hornblende.

And the shadow arrives first — and the small things arrive with it. *Shade-commuters*: thin, quick, unable to afford the open pan, running their entire lives inside the herd's moving shade. A span's shadow is spilled being, and to the commuters it is shelter and larder both. They know the Round better than the spans do. They have to. To them the herd is not animals. It is weather with feet.

## The Larders of Sound

A bellow hangs in a valley for a season, *thinning slowly* — and the thinning is not just decay. Sound with being in it is food, and a held voice is a standing pasture.

The sifters find a fresh voice and plant themselves: small hinged things built like tuning forks, standing tines-up in the ghost of a call, resonating, straining being out of the air's memory. A valley full of held sound stands furred with sifters the way the hills stand furred with grey-green. A voice does not merely fade here. It is *grazed*. When the last of it is eaten, the sifters fold and wait, and the rut fills the larders again in spring.

The voices graze each other, too. A strong call outlasts and absorbs lesser ones, so that an old valley keeps a single deep voice — the survivor of a season of such grazing, a jar of preserved thunder. The cows know those valleys. They bring the castings to them the way you would bring children to a well.

## The Leavings

A span dents the world, and the dents are a commons.

The shadow-burns, where stone stays darkened for years, are worked by *smuts*: flat, black, slow as soot, spreading themselves across the burn and licking the soaked-in being back out of the rock. A burn fades not because the world forgets but because the smuts finish. They are why the Forge is not permanently bruised — why old crossings heal to paleness in a decade instead of standing dark forever.

The footprints are richer. Each fills over decades with condensed ore, and each is a claim, and the *track-keepers* file on them: small plated patient things, a generation per print, refining the ore further into their own bodies, until a track-keeper of the fourth generation is less an animal than an ingot with appetites. Then the seam-jackals take them — being flows uphill — and the worked-out print fills with wind and waits for the herd to come round again. A trackway is a farm the spans plow by walking.

Track-keepers cannot tell a boot from a hoof. A caravan crossing the pans is, to them, a thin brief herd, and they file on a boot-print with the same patience, and find it thin, and stay anyway. There is a lesson in that about people and the Forge, and the track-keepers have all drawn it.

## The Quick

Do not mistake brevity for poverty. The Forge's ephemera come in all sizes.

The swarf-swarms boil out at each firing and die in hours, and they live the hours *fully*: an hour of drift, an hour of congregation, a bright brief war over the richest smoke of charge, a long gold dying. They are drawn to castings — thinness recognizes thinness — and a casting walking through a swarf-storm wears the light like a second skin, and does not mind. It is the only time anything in the Forge is thinner than it is.

On the pans, charge builds until it goes somewhere, and the pan-ticks take it there in stages. A tick stores the pan's leakage in a body like a copper burr until the pressure is intolerable, then spends it all at once — the discharge *is* the hop. A line of ticks crossing a pan stitches it with sparks, and each stitch is a tick's whole fortune, spent in a single stride. They live for weeks. They are the Forge's spendthrifts, and the pans would not discharge cleanly without them.

And in the walking bolts themselves: a bolt that strides across the Gearing in clear daylight lives for minutes, and minutes, to lightning, is an age. It feeds on the charge gradient; it travels; it dies. Call it an animal — the mica sails do. And inside the bolt's bright channel live midges, generations per strike, an entire epic of birth and hunting and mourning conducted at the speed of a single flash. The swarf-swarms, if you could ask them, would call them hasty.

## The Sky Herd

The Forge keeps two herds. One walks the stone and grazes being. The other rides the thermals and grazes charge, and it is everything the spans are not: thin, silent, acres wide, and nearly not there.

The sails calve, though rarely, and no one has watched it happen from a useful distance — a smaller sail parts from the edge of a greater, once in a century, like frost deciding to be two frosts. They stoop to the walking bolts the way cattle stoop to a water hole, tipping their acres to drink, and over the Gearing on a charged afternoon you can see them gathered, translucent, tilting, a pasture of the almost-absent.

A sail has two deaths. Drink too little and it tears: a slow rain of glitter, swarf of the sky. Drink too deep and it grows heavy — by sail standards — and sinks. *Sail-fall* is the slowest weather the Forge has: days to descend, crackling, acre on acre of silver settling toward the pans while everything below watches it come. Where it grounds, the scab glitters for a century. The track-keepers file their claims. The grey-green creeps in. The sky herd feeds the ground, eventually, the way everything here feeds everything, given time.

Whether the sails are alive at all is an open question. They are too thin to be much more real than the sky. But the same question gets asked at the opposite extreme, below, about the hills — and between the sward that barely moves and the sails that barely exist, everything else in the Forge is sure of itself.

## The Pasture That Isn't

The hills furred grey-green look like pasture and are not. Nothing here has ever been a plant. What the fur *is* remains the longest war the assayers have ever fought.

Each fiber stands on the gradient exactly as a span does: root in the hot stone, tip in the cold air, a heat engine the width of a hair. It drinks the difference — measurably. Over a decade, the fur retreats from a footpath. It thickens on shadow-burns, drinking the soaked-in being. It will not grow in the Tolerances, which suggests it needs the world to be real, which suggests it is *something*. The assayers call it the sward and suspend judgment. The sward-shrews, who tick through it at a speed nothing else here attempts, have no doubts: it is shelter, it is cover, it is the Forge's only brush, and that settles it for shrews.

Consider the shape of the thing, though. A herd that sat down before the elders thought of it — grazing the gradient at a density the spans never dreamed of, sessile, silent, patient past all measurement. By mass, the Forge may be mostly sward. The overwhelming fact of the place may not be the herds at all. It may be the quiet grey pasture that isn't, holding still, outwaiting the question.

## The Drum Below

The pans ring underfoot like drum skin, and drums are hollow.

Beneath the halite crust: geode dark, crystal cavities, a buried architecture of voids. And there live the knockers. They are blind; they have never needed otherwise. Their world is a ceiling that sometimes speaks. They eat vibration, sipping it through the stone, and so they gather under trackways, under old crossings, wherever the world's thunder falls thickest — under-herds mirroring the herds above, grazing the same crossings one sense removed.

And they answer. Some of the faint ringing underfoot is reply: knockers leaning up against the crust and tapping back, a slow conversation no one above knows they are holding. Walk alone on a pan and your steps come back to you slightly late, slightly wrong, slightly *answered*. Assayers who have listened long enough stop walking alone on pans.

When an elder seats itself on a pan, the underside knows first: the shadow soaks through before the beast arrives, an eclipse years long, and then the weight begins, and the knockers migrate away at knocking pace, decades ahead of the settling. They know the anchorites best of anyone living. A person sitting still for two hundred years is exactly the kind of voice a knocker is built to hear.

## The Finishers

At last an elder seats itself, and does not rise, and begins the Long Note: one bellow, sustained past breath, past season, transposing down as it cools — leaving the ear, entering the stone. It is still going when the jackal arrives.

The seam-jackal reads density the way you read weather, and it comes before the end, and it settles to wait out a dying that will take centuries. This is the vigil, and it is the center of the jackal's life: it is the elder's last companion, the one presence at the end of every eon. Whether jackals grieve is an assayers' argument. What is certain is that they keep the hours.

Then the taking-apart, which is the most precise work done anywhere in the Forge. The densest actuality lies along the dead elder's fault-planes, and to reach it the jackal must split the carcass true — along the cleavage, to the tolerance — and the split carcass, dressed true, happens to be an engine. Nothing here was built. But everything here was *eaten*, and the eating had standards. The Gearing is a graveyard that runs, and it runs because it was eaten correctly. The engine is the carcass, finished.

Jackal litters are whelped in the works-to-be, denning in tomorrow's pistons. Their thin high gnaw-song is the only sound in the Forge faster than the works. And an old jackal, deaf from decades beside the chord, walks at last into the Tolerances to die. This is not sentiment; it is logistics. A jackal carcass is a century's hoard of milled being, and in the threadbare lands it is a seed. The herds avoid the deep Tolerances. The jackals die there. That is most of how a pan heals: one grain per century, one jackal at a time.

## The Tenants of the Tolerances

The breakdown stands empty, ringed by sound — empty the way a desert is empty. By the Forge's own definition the Tolerances can hardly be inhabited at all, since to live here is to be real, and the Tolerances are where reality runs out. What lives there fails the census. It lives there anyway.

The *maybes* are real on a schedule no one has caught them keeping: here on Tuesday, gone by the count of three, back in the spring, thinner each time you look straight at them. A maybe husbands being the way a desert animal husbands water. When the Century Blow's pre-echo arrives, seasons early, the Tolerances bloom — maybes condense around the unspent sound like frost, feeding on thunder that has not happened yet. And when the true Blow comes, the concussion of it, the sheer *actuality* — it knocks them loose. The sound they ate becomes real, and they cannot afford real food. None survive the arrival. The next century's maybes condense out of the Blow's own thinning tail, and the whole ecology runs on the hundred-year clock of the Stamping Flats.

The *plurals* have made a husbandry of cheap locality. Distances do not conserve in the Tolerances, so the plurals decline to be in one place: you never meet one — you meet *it*, here and also three valleys over, and it is not fast, it is *smeared*. A plural is a herd of itself, grazing thin seams in several places at once, and if one instance dies the others do not mourn, exactly. They redistribute.

The *heralds* live ahead. In the deep Tolerances, sound precedes source, and the heralds have leaned into the precedence: they feed on approaching events, their stomachs are full of next season, and when you meet one, it is already remembering you.

And the skim. Rare — centuries between sightings — and half-believed. Where being has been eaten to the root, thinness pools, and sometimes the pool moves. The skim is a deficit with a drift, and it does not kill; it *dilutes*. The herds know. The bellowing at dusk is many things — cooling, courtship, memory — and one more: assertion. A herd sings itself thicker. Noise, in the Forge, is a fence of being, and it is raised against the one weather that is poor.

A pan heals the way everything happens here: across fifty thousand years. The jackal carcasses seed it. The knockers return to the underside. The sward creeps the edges. A scouting herd crosses, then another; the track-keepers follow. Half-real, and the herds return to it the way herds return to grass. It is the closest the Forge comes to a garden — tended by no one, on a schedule of millennia.

## The Eldest

Then there are the residents the census nearly misses by looking too small.

What happens inside a seated elder as it becomes a work is the Forge's one locked door. The jackals know something of it, and they are not telling. But consider the works themselves: the Gearing runs, taxes the gradient, refines being — and sings. The chord has not broken in nine thousand years, and if the chord is made of Long Notes, then every note in it was once a voice. The works are a choir of last words, still radiating. Once in some millennia a new note joins, and the whole Forge hears the chord change — they call it *the change* — and the herds carry themselves differently for a century after, the way you walk softly in a house where someone has died, or been born.

The piston-banks of the Stamping Flats complete a stroke a century. Call it a heartbeat. Call it a syllable. The Forge's true population lives at one stroke per decade and thinks in millennia, and everything quicker — herd and jackal, knocker and sail, shrew and swarm and anchorite — is its weather: its cells, its dreams, its brief bright symptoms. Whether the works notice the weather is the only question the anchorites are really asking.

## The Brief Bright Ones

People come. Not many, and not for long.

Prospectors come for the seams, because refined actuality is the most valuable thing that exists anywhere. Assayers come to measure, and stay to argue, and their arguments outlive them. Pilgrims come to sit. No road crosses the pans, so travelers follow the herd-trackways and camp in the print-fields, and the track-keepers file on their boot-prints, and find them thin, and stay anyway.

Linger, and the Forge begins to keep you. Accrual: your shadow gains weight; your voice hangs past its welcome; your footprints stop fading by morning. It feels like health. It is the beginning of anchoring. The anchorites are the ones who did not stop — who sat down facing the elders and did not get up, growing heavier year by year, accruing, mineralizing at the pace of stone. The knockers know them best. No jackal keeps their vigil; they are not dense enough to be worth the century, and this is the anchorites' private sorrow.

Or the Forge thins you instead. Stray into the Tolerances and you may come back *toleranced* — shadow lagging, voice arriving heartbeats early, years missing like dropped stitches.

But understand the scale. A person lives eighty years. A swarf-swarm lives hours and calls it a life and is not wrong. To the Forge, a person is a spark off the anvil: brief, bright, pseudo-, boiling out at the firing of some distant history and dying in hours. The Forge has watched entire peoples the way you watch a swarf-storm at dusk.

And still they come, and some still sit — trying to accrue enough being to register, to dent the world, to leave a print that fills with ore, to have a voice that outlasts the season. It works, slightly. After two hundred years, an anchorite's voice begins, faintly, to persist. It is the smallest success the Forge allows. Ask the knockers whether it is enough. They have been listening to those two-hundred-year voices longer than anyone, and they keep answering them — which may be the closest thing the Forge has to an opinion.

## The Census, Taken

So: spans and castings and the seated; vane-cleaners and shade-commuters; smuts and track-keepers; sifters in the held voices and knockers under the drums; shrews in the sward that may be alive; sails that may not be; ticks spending their fortunes in single sparks; midges living epics inside lightning; swarms boiling out and dying gold; jackals keeping the hours and cutting the dead true; maybes and plurals and heralds in the slack lands; the skim drifting; the anchorites accruing; and the works, singing, full of voices, thinking in millennia.

Every one of them is the gradient, alive at a different speed — the flow of heat from stone to sky, taking shapes. Being taxed, being eaten, being sung out of the blood, being hoarded in bone, being worn to tolerance, being spent one spark at a time.

There is one more entry, and it is either the whole list or a footnote: the Forge itself, which is either the place where everything lives or the only thing that does.

Tonight the stone ticks as it cools, and under the pans the knockers answer the ticking, tick for tick. Somewhere a herd is bellowing the heat out of its blood, and the sifters are already unfolding. And far off, seasons early, the pre-echo of a Century Blow is arriving through the fillings of the world — and everything that lives here, herd and jackal, sail and shrew, swarm and anchorite and maybe, lifts its head, or the part of it that serves as a head, at a sound that is not there yet.

That, too, lives in the Forge: the listening.
