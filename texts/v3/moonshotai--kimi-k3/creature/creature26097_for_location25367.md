The census of the Catch is short enough to chalk on the corner of the water slate, and some mornings, in a small hand beneath the cistern numbers, the gauger writes it:

Keepers: 40. Heron: 1 (same). Moths: seasonal. Snails: in waiting. Plant: yes.

That is everyone. The census only counts what stays. The botanists from downhill have been coming for a hundred years and are still visitors; several are now old friends, and none has ever been counted.

The forty are born to it, mostly, into the roles the way other towns are born into streets: gauger, engine-keeper, cooper, warden of the margins, overseer of the take. Now and then someone walks down from the rim — a trader's child, a person fleeing noise — and the place finds out within a fortnight whether they will stay. The test is the silence. Some newcomers cannot bear hearing their own pulse at night and leave before the month is out, and the floor, which forgets quitters quickly, smooths their lanes within a season. The ones who stay can be told by the second week: their voice has come down to the volume of the basin. After that they are keepers, and downhill will always be too loud for them, and they will come back from trade trips with headaches, speaking too much for days until the Catch retunes them.

Living there does something particular to character, and it is this: speech in the Catch is private, and walking is public. A low voice carries no distance at all; you may say anything. But the floor records every journey, holds a pressed lane for seasons, a dent for a year, and everyone reads the palimpsest without meaning to. So the town has evolved a strict etiquette, never spoken: you do not repeat what the floor tells you. Lovers vary their routes and the dents accrue anyway, because intention has a geometry and the floor reads geometry. Feuds are conducted by lane-avoidance — two keepers who are careful never to press the same path — and the floor maps those too, and everyone reads them, and no one mentions them. Visitors report that keepers talk of nothing but water. This is true. Everything else is underfoot, and may not be repeated. The one unreadable thing in the Catch is a keeper's face, and it is practiced.

The deepest writing in the basin is the gauger's lane, from the engine-house door to the cisterns and back, renewed at first light every morning for longer than anyone has kept count. The job comes with the lane. On your first morning you step into the pressed patience of everyone who gauged before you, and the floor cannot tell the difference, which the overseers, who are only accountants, decline to call inheritance.

Children are shown the drowned house the way children elsewhere are shown graveyards: here is what patience does. Every child serves a season sweeping with the warden of the margins and learns the two facts of the world — the fleece creeps, and no one drills the bed. A keeper's life after that is measured plainly: one boiler, two or three cuttings, and with luck two blooms. People are born in the years of a boiler the way others are born under kings. To be bloom-born is the only distinction of fortune the town insists on, and to be an elder is simply to remember two blooms.

The animals know the water schedule better than the people do, which is saying something.

The heron has tenure. No one remembers it arriving. The engine-house log, in four successions of handwriting across the years of two boilers, mentions "the bird" without comment, the way one mentions the boiler. Either herons are longer-lived than the books downhill say, or the office is hereditary; the town has declined to investigate, on grounds of rudeness. It roosts on the boathouse, the one waterman of a dry harbor. It attends the morning gauging from a cistern rim with the air of an inspector, and in rain-week it hunts the drown-hollows, prying sealed snails off the stones — the only seal-breaker in the Catch. The warden looks the other way. The warden can afford to; the warden has counted. Some evenings the bird beats up over the rim and is gone an hour on business of its own, which makes it the most traveled resident, and it has never once reported what it sees, which is how a colleague behaves. If it ever left for good the town would take it hard, and says so never.

The moth and the snail are the two waiting appetites, and they wait for different things. The ring-moth waits for the license. Its caterpillars graze their widening circles by night, and the overseers read a ring on a licensed margin the way a banker reads a letter of reference, because grazed fleece cuts easier when its time comes. The town writes on the floor in straight lines; the moth writes in circles; the floor keeps both scripts. The sealed snails wait for rain, and they are the purest citizens of the Catch: they ask for exactly what the town asks for, and they are satisfied with exactly what the town gets. For months they are grey thumbnails glued to stone — the footings of houses, the rims of tanks — and a visitor can be shown a hundred and see none. Then the first hour of rain softens the seals, and the year's whole life happens in an afternoon. The overseers' license-stamp bears a snail. No one remembers deciding this.

Once in a generation the census fails altogether. The basin turns to gilt, the air smells of honey and creosote, and the moths come in clouds — whether the ring-moth multiplied or cousins called up from countries no one here has seen is not asked, partly from manners and mostly because there is no one to ask who would answer. The heron grows fat. The quiet town is loud with wings for a week, and then the seeds fall in drifts like ash, and nothing eats them, and they are swept from the condensers and go to the boiler with the year's slabs — which is why, some winters, the green smoke carries a ghost of honey. Then the town begins counting again: before the bloom, after the bloom.

The sixth boiler should be counted as a resident, having a heartbeat you feel through your soles and a lifespan of one human life. Retired boilers are not scrapped; they are promoted to cistern. The fifth holds water beside the Works, and the heron inspects its predecessor without apparent nostalgia. The engine-house is the one warm loud room in the Catch, so it is where arguments are held, and songs, and any meeting that needs everyone, because against the boiler's breathing a voice can rise without carrying. Around the Works the fleece grows black-green out of season, leaning toward the waste heat, and the margin there wants sweeping twice a day. The town burns the plant; the plant leans toward the fire's house. Nobody finds this strange, or everybody does.

The eldest resident is, of course, the floor. Downhill they have argued for a century over whether it is one plant or ten thousand fused, and the town finds the question rude, because you do not ask your host whether he is one person or many, and you certainly do not drill him to find out. Ask instead where the lake went, and a keeper will look at the ground. The old shorelines step down the bowl like the record of a long removal, bench by bench, and the town's quiet belief — never stated aloud, like the other thing — is that the water did not leave. It changed address. The fleece drank its way down over centuries and keeps the lake now in its deep interior, which is its own, which is why the rule is older than the Works and has never once needed enforcing. You do not mine your host. You do not drink the lake twice.

On cold evenings, with the river of chilled air flowing around their ankles, people sit on the fleece, on the compost warmth of its crowded interior, and visitors make the comparison — the back of a sleeping animal — exactly once, quietly, and then stop, feeling heard. Whether the floor hears is untested. Whether it remembers weight is known. The town keeps its voice down anyway. This is what is meant by manners.

The dead are the only residents who move out. You cannot dig the bed, so the graves are upslope, in the dry gravel above the highest shoreline, where even the lake never reached. Mourning lasts until the lanes green over. The floor keeps a person a while longer than the town can — the path to their door fading over seasons — and the warden of the margins mentions, gently, when a lane has healed, the way someone elsewhere would say the stone has been set. Now and then a traveler of a bird touches the hollows in rain-week and goes on; it is not counted. The census counts what stays. One of the botanists stayed, thirty years gone. He gauges now. He no longer asks the question; he chalks the numbers, and some mornings, in a small hand under the water, he writes the last line of the census: Plant: yes.

And on the great days, when the rain comes and the bell is rung — its note arriving whole and leaving at once, which in the Catch is what joy sounds like — everyone goes out to stand in it, and the hollows fill, and for two days the town borrows mirrors, since glass is for dew and a keeper's own face is a rare sight. For as long as the drown-hollows hold, everyone in the Catch is there twice: once on the warm floor, once in the sky's keeping. The census, for two days, is doubled. It is the closest the Catch comes to a crowd.
