Count what returns, and you have counted what lives here.

The drip returns in seconds, each one a note with a long silver tail. The breath returns at dusk and dawn. The bats return before first light, belly-full of sky. The frogs return with the rain, and again when the otter leaves. The light returns to the last dry step on certain dates. The Bell returns when the wind sits in the wet quarter. The keeper returns at first light, rarely. Nothing here stays but the lime. Everything else that lives in the Jar is a way of coming back, and every interval has its animal.

The bats first, because the colony is the chamber's largest citizen and its weather. In the last dark under the corbels they hang in their hundreds, and the seethe up there, the sound like a slow fire, is the sound of small corrections — wings folded and refolded, a whole population shifting in its sleep and never waking all the way. They are older than the rule of the lamp. Their leavings lie on the ledges in strata like the plain in section; dig them and you would find older colony, then older, then the first bats of all, long since taken by the lime. No one digs. At dusk the Jar exhales and the colony goes up on the breath, the rope uncoiling through the mouth, each bat threading the owl's gauntlet into the grey. Out over the Flats they divide the rooms among them. A bat works its pans the way the keeper works the rope: by rounds, by habit, by return, harvesting the haze that rises off the standing water. They come home down the shaft before first light — wings in a chimney, thunder folded small — and the sky comes in with them, and falls: on the ledges, on the water, in pale mats. This is the import the whole dark web lives on. The night feeds the water, and the bats are the night.

Below the mats, the grazers. Amphipods work the pale stuff with their comma-bodies; larvae take what the amphipods miss; and the films themselves — the pale, the microscopic, the uncounted — are the real meadows here, where everything small enough is cattle. The whirligigs write and erase their circles; the backswimmers hang beneath like punctuation, waiting for the sentence to come apart. Where a drip strikes, the whirligigs ring it. After rain, when the tempo climbs toward clatter, the whole surface is corrections.

There are no fish. The weep-water has come a long way through the made ground, strained by a day's walk of brick in every direction, and it gives up the clearest water on the plain; what it still carries is lime, and the lime is spoken for. Whatever falls through that water to the paved floor stays visible for years — a drowned swift, one of the colony, a beetle — pale first, then mineral, then part of the floor's slow archive. Nothing down there eats what falls. The clear water keeps it, and the lime finishes the work.

Above all this, the sun's last garden: moss to the fifth step, one fern at the lip, a thin green band that follows the light and holds its contour like a held note. Below the moss line nothing is green, because below it nothing eats light. The band's edge is the light's own tally, and it has kept the same count as long as anyone has carried a lamp past it.

The frogs are three nations. The small wall-frogs keep the pockets in the plaster, and each pocket is a sounding-box the builders left or the lime grew — a tick thrown from a pocket carries farther than a tick from open wall, and the frogs know this the way they know anything: by living where it is true. The big water frogs hold the margin, and boom. And out on the Flats the rain-frogs sleep in the brick-mud and come up when the rooms fill — the city's standby population, a chorus that can be stored dry. When the otter comes, the big frogs cut first, mid-boom, pinched like wicks; then the wall-frogs; then nothing but the drips. The restart is the reverse of an ending: a few ticks testing the air, then the margin taking courage, then the whole chamber loud again, section by section, like a crowd remembering the words. And when the Bell sounds, both sayings are true, in order. First they listen — the whole chorus dropping away under the note — and then they join, and the stagger goes out of the calling, and the pans across the Flats slide toward the tone and lock. It holds as long as the wind holds. Then the plain unravels back into neighborhoods, room by room, each frog keeping the key a little while after the note has gone, the way struck clay keeps its ring. The people within earshot keep both sayings — they fall quiet, they join — and pass them down the way the swifts keep the holes: by living where they are true. The sayings are returns too.

The island keeps a snake the way the curb keeps a keeper: by being returned to, for a long time. Thick as a wrist, patterned like wet rope. It hunts the margin by night, black water over black stone, and by day it keeps the coin — where the light slides across the water and touches the island, the snake is already there, taking its one hour of weather. The island's back is worn smooth. The rope flutes the curb; the snake flutes the island; the same record, written in different stone, by return. When the Jar speaks, the snake is the only creature that takes the note through its whole length, belly down on a block welded to the floor of a sounding vessel. It is not speaking to the snake. The snake does not ask it to. Its outgrown skins are found on the water now and then, a ghost of the snake floating beside the snake, until they sink, and are kept. And there is always one snake. Whether it is the same snake across a human lifetime or a succession no one has witnessed, no one has marked it. The interval between snakes is never long. It is as if the island, standing empty, sends for one — or as if the Flats, full of frogs and rooms, can spare exactly one rope-pattern at a time.

The gauntlet keeps shifts. By day the swifts hold the mouth. Their holes step up the walls where the scaffold poles sat, and the nests are built on nests, glue on dust on glue — the holes are the plain in little, strata of occupancy — and the lowest occupied hole marks the depth the swifts will go, and no deeper: their own moss line. Swifts live on the wing; the hole is the only ground they keep, and they are faithful to it from the far side of the year. Their screaming in the shaft at first light is the chamber's morning bell, and the keeper works to it. The swifts take the crumbs. Swifts elsewhere do not come down for bread; these do, and their daughters do, and the habit is as old in them now as the hole-faith. No one asks what the bread is for. Effect is enough. The keeping has entered the birds.

By night, the owl. It roosts the daylight out in the willow — the only tree within a day's walk is also the only roost — and at dusk takes its post at the mouth and cuts the stragglers off the rope's edge. Its toll is small and regular and endless; the colony pays it the way the weep-points pay the water, without being diminished by it that anyone can see. What the owl drops, drops at the willow's root: small grey purses of fur and bone. The one tree on the plain is fed by the one colony. Nothing here is wasted. Everything is routed.

The willow is the plain's one vertical thing, and everything that needs height comes to it: the owl by day, the swifts' last circuits at dusk, the keeper at first light, standing between the tree and the mouth in the path of the drawn breath. It drinks the pan that never shrinks, and the pan never shrinks because the willow found the Jar long ago — root-hairs at the weep-points, and deeper, in the dark, one root that the lime has taken and goes on taking, a slow argument at its growing tip between accretion and arrival. The tree drinks from the chamber without entering it. At dusk the exhale lifts its leaves all at once: the only green for a day's walk, breathing with the bell.

The widest resident leaves the smallest sign. The drain no living person has seen is the otter's door, and beyond the door its roads run through the drowned city — the standing water in the buried cellars, the lanes full of dark, the plans of rooms no lamp has reached since the razing. The otter is the under-city's only traffic. It comes on certain nights, and the frogs know before the water moves; and it takes what it takes; and at the overflow, where the drain daylights into the kingfisher's pan, it leaves its sign on the stones at the sedge's edge, which is how the place says the otter was here. The drain has two doors, and they are kept by opposites: the otter comes up out of the dark end; the kingfisher keeps the bright one — a struck spark, the one bright color in a landscape of grey and brick, working the pan where the sedge stands and the small fish never see the Jar. A mile off, the heron stands in someone's former kitchen, patient as the plain, and the dragonflies work the lanes, still obeying the streets. The Flats keep their own candles. But all of it is one water, and the otter swims everything of it that is dark. To the otter, the Jar is not a place. It is a door in the otter's house.

Now the plain's arithmetic, which no one states and everything obeys: the small lives are multitudes — the colony, the chorus, the swifts, the grazers — and everything larger is singular. One tree. One snake. One owl. One otter. One spark at the overflow. One Bell. And one keeper.

The slowest return is the keeper. Everything in the Jar is known by sign, and the keeper is no exception: rope-flutes that stay bright though a thousand years cut them; a wick trimmed neat in the niche; soot over the third step, old under new; a crust of bread, always a crust, always on the curb; footprints in the lime-bloom, heel to the willow, toe to the mouth, the bucket-rope's wet line between. Three buckets, always three, and the frogs know the count: one splash and the chorus cuts; two, and it holds its breath; three, and it comes back louder, as if answered. The keeper comes at the intake hour, when the Jar draws its breath in, and works to the swifts' screaming.

The keeper keeps two rules. One is understood: light the lamp at the third step and carry it down, and if it gutters low on the stair, go back up — the chamber makes its own weather, and some of it is unbreathable, and the lamp knows before the lungs do. When the lamp goes down, the chamber's few moths come out of the plaster dark to meet it, generations deep like the swifts; where they feed, no one has followed them to see — perhaps the films, perhaps the willow's catkins in their season, in the hours no one is watching. Some returns are unobserved. The other rule is kept the way the swifts keep their holes: three buckets, the bread, and on the certain dates a tally cut fresh in the old place beside the niche, in a hand that learned the habit from the wall. The count continues without its reason. That too is a kind of breathing: in, out, no longer remembering why. On the dates when the coin stands at noon on the last dry step, someone has been down before it — the tally says so. Whether they stay for the light or leave the step to it, no one has seen. Whether it is one person or a line of people, no one has asked. The keeper and the otter have never met, and between them they keep both doors of the drain — one at first light, one on certain nights — and each reads the other's sign without deciding to: the trimmed wick, the brightening flutes; the spraint, the margin's missing voice. In a place where sound comes back changed, sign is the native language. Everything here is fluent.

Of all that lives in the Jar, only the lime never arrives. It was not carried; it does not return; it grows out of the water itself, a fingernail a decade, healing the weeps, keeping what falls, raising its straws a grandmother at a time. The colony is the weather, the chorus the voice, the snake the tenure, the owl the toll, the otter the rumor, the keeper the appointment — but the skin is the resident. The Jar has finished becoming, and what persists in it persists by return: drip, breath, bat, frog, coin, note, keeper, each on its own interval, all of them held the way the vault holds a chord.

Come once at first light, the keeper's hour. By full sun the coin has left the water and begun its climb of the far wall; the colony seethes softly in the last dark; the snake lies where the warmth will be; the crumbs are gone and the swifts are far out over the Flats; the flutes are drying; the frogs are loud from being answered. There is no one here at all. Everything is home.
