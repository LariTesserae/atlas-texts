Nobody has ever seen the population of the Vesper Basin. Everybody has heard it. The census here is taken by ear — on a crest night, when the chorus swells and the lightning obliges with a second of visibility — and the results always come back the same: *full*. Every niche occupied. Every surface spoken for. Every scale of life stacked on the scale beneath it like sediment. To list what lives here is to describe a sound from the inside, so start at the bottom.

Start with the landlord. The felt-meadow that carpets the floor between the flows may be a single organism — no one has found its edge, only the places where the rock is too new or too hot for it. It drinks fog through ten million filaments, and it drinks in time. When a vesper-tide peaks and every throat in the valley is at full stretch, the meadow lets go of its water all at once — a knee-high burst of mist rising out of the dark at exactly the moment the hunters strike, so that the kill happens inside a held breath. Whether the felt times itself to the chorus or the chorus to the felt is the kind of question the basin answers with more chorus. The herds call it weather.

The herds: sweepbacks. Knee-high, broad as doors, warm as bread loaves, with more legs than you will count in one lightning flash. Their hides grow felt thicker than the meadow they graze, and the hide is a township — glow-fleas running in constellations along the spine, moss in the saddle-hollows, and the back-singers, small dry stick-legged things that ride between the shoulders and drum alarm on the flat of the hide when they hear what the sweepback's grazing-dulled ears have missed. A sweepback alone is a meal. A herd is something else: hundreds of bodies brushing in synchrony, each footfall smeared into its neighbor's, until the herd registers not as many animals but as one wide, soft, continuous sound with no edges. A noise-flock. A pack of hunters circling it can hear everything and locate nothing. It is like trying to bite fog.

Which the drumhounds know, and resent. Lean, long-jawed, all ear and shin, they run the glass flows on bare pads that read the ground as they go — a hound at full gallop is listening to your footfalls through its own feet, and it can tell a lame sweepback from a sound one by the shape of the hesitation. Each pack owns a notch in the drone, a narrow seam of pitch the chorus happens to leave thin, and the pack sings inside it while it hunts, so that pack-mates hear each other clearly and everything else hears only chorus. They strike at the crest and smuggle the kill out under the crescendo — murder under cover of applause. They will not touch a brimback: the heavy vent-grazers that crop the sulfur gardens carry the garden in their flesh, and a hound that eats one spends a day coughing struck matches. The decomposers take the brimbacks gladly. The guild has no opinions.

Out on the older flows live the flow-knockers — heavy, crested, wingless, each one the owner of a hollow flow that rings when struck, each knock-pattern a deed of title drummed out in the lulls. And in the explosion pits, the pit-bells: a fist-sized frog whose call rings at exactly the pitch of its own pit, every pit tuned differently by its depth and its clinker. The floor is a xylophone and the frogs hold the keys. Travelers navigate by them — past the low pit, left at the cracked one — the only street signs the basin has, and they sing.

Then the pools. A seep-pool at any hour is a convention of statues: kettle-eels standing mouth-up like planted knives, wait-crabs holding one pose from Hatch to Hatch — patience with claws — glass-frogs thumb-sized and transparent, sitting in the black water they resemble. Pause on the glass-frogs, because they are the strings of the drone, and they are how a vesper-tide travels: each frog repeats its neighbor's song a half-beat late, and the wave of imitation crosses the valley like a rumor. Nobody knows what starts one. Old knappers read the tide-traffic like news — where it began, how fast it moved, what it broke against.

And some pools are not pools. The badwater is an animal that has spent decades learning to resemble water. It grows its own rim, crusts it with mineral from its own sweat, fills its own basin, and waits. Every child learns the test: a true seep has the Undernote in it — put your ear to the rim and hear the ground talking up through the water. A badwater is blank underneath, a hole in the floor's voice. Miss the test and the bargain closes: the rim tilts, gently, like a hand cupping, and the pool drinks back. There is also the false lamp, a hunter that grew the heron's trick on its own body — a living light over black water with no line attached. Fish rise to it. So do herons, sometimes once. The herons' defense is flicker: every glow-larva blinks a signature, a personal wink, and a heron knows its own lamp the way you know a friend's voice. Lately, the false lamps have been learning signatures. The arms race runs on, in winks.

The lure-heron deserves a paragraph of its own: seven feet of slate-dark patience, the only resident that works by light on purpose, and it pays for the privilege. A lamp draws the rushers — things that charge light on sight — so the heron fishes with one leg cocked, and when the dark comes at it, it cuts the silk. The dropped lamp falls and writhes, the rusher takes the decoy, and the heron steps twice sideways and is a statue again. It keeps a dozen larvae in a crop-pouch and feeds them vent-gourd rind, which is why herons shadow knapper troops: knappers split gourds and leave rinds. Knappers, in turn, wait near herons for what the lamp flushes, and gather dropped lamps the way children gather pretty stones. Nobody decided any of this. It has been going on for ten thousand years. The standing wisdom holds: only herons and fools carry light.

Above all this, the air is inhabited the way soil is inhabited elsewhere — densely, and by eaters of each other. The wingfall never lands: moths soft as ash, sieve-fliers straining spore and gnat through comb-mouths, so thick that breathing is a form of grazing. The dust of their scales settles on everything, and the felt eats it; the meadow is fed from below by condensation and from above by the slow snow of wings. The Ceiling-skitterers hunt the upper layers on click-trains that rake the dark like searchlights. They fledge from the rim-ledges and never land again — they sleep in glides, court in dives, die mid-beat, and their falling bodies are a thin gray rain the basin eats before it lands. Once in a long while a skitterer climbs through a thin place in the Ceiling and comes back down burned, daylight still on it like a curse. The basin has considered the sun, and declined.

And drifting at the Ceiling's belly — for the Ceiling is alive, seeded by every spore-fog, an upside-down pasture of plankton grazing the fume — the ceilingfish trail their curtains. Gas-bladder jellies, slow as weather, fishing the wingfall with sticky strands. Sometimes a curtain sags to the canopy and a knapper cuts a length: the best cordage in the world, if you don't mind the sting. Sometimes a bladder fails and the whole animal comes down, and that is a skyfall, a weather of meat, and troops race the guild to it across a day's march of dark.

Underfoot, the Below. The lava tubes have their own citizens — soft, colorless things shaped like the ears they mostly are, eeling through seep-water in a dark that is somehow darker than the dark up here — and their own musicians: the hum-toads, who hide their songs in the skirts of the Undernote so that hunting ears slide off them. The ground-listeners among the knappers map all of it by knock. Strike the floor, read the ring, name what is under you for a mile: which tubes run dry, which are drowned, which are warm, which are thinking about opening. Ask a ground-listener where you stand and she will knock twice and tell you what the ground is deciding.

And over everything waits the guild. The hour-beetles arrive first, named for the deadline; then the glean-mites; then the render-flies; then the fungus. A carcass is a clock with four hands, and everyone reads it. Over anything large, the fliers raise a gleaning-column — a dark tower of wings audible from half the basin — which is how the news gets out. Bones go last, to the borers. Nothing persists. In a million years the only fossils this valley will have made are flakes of worked glass.

Which brings us to the ones who work it.

The knappers are not the lords of the basin. They are not numerous enough — for every knapper there are a million frogs; the basin is mostly chorus, and knows it. But they are the ones who answer, which is the best gloss of the rhythm they call themselves by, and they are the nearest thing the dark has to a witness.

Four hands and no pockets. The lower pair holds, the upper pair works, or the reverse; they trade off the way you breathe. Their fur is dark and felted so thickly that a knapper drinks by combing its own forearm with its teeth. The ears are leaves of gristle the size of hands, with lids for the crescendos. The eyes — wet coins, catching whatever light there is — are good for exactly two things: glow, and flash.

Everything a knapper owns fits in four hands: the carried edge, struck fresh every few days off a good core; the wrap of felt-leaf it rides in, personal as a voice, sometimes smeared with underlight — the closest thing here to clothing, to jewelry, to a signature. At the gatherings, a fine core passes hand to hand and is admired like an heirloom, because that is what wealth means in the Vesper: music not yet struck.

A knapper's name is a short rhythm. It is tapped on the newborn's chest until the child taps it back, and after that you are never called, only played. Everything else the troop knows lives in the long song: the seep-lines, redrawn weekly; the hush-years; the vent-births; the dead. Each dead knapper is a measure folded in, so the song lengthens. To die is to go out of the song; to be remembered is to remain playable. If anything in the Vesper could be called a house, it is the long song, and it gets one room larger at every death.

On calm nights the troops gather at the clatter-grounds, and the sound of stonework braids into the drone until the whole ledge is a loom of it. Style is dialect: a master hears where you learned by your rest-strokes. There are contests, called strikes — a young knapper strikes a theme, the elders answer in variations, tool and poem in a single gesture — and there is trade, and gossip, and courtship, which runs toward the foreign: the rhythm you cannot parse is the one that stops your hands.

When the lightning walks inside the Ceiling, the whole basin freezes, and the knappers freeze facing one another. Recognition, counting, courtship — all the visual business of their lives happens in half-second installments. The flash-marks, pale blazes of chest-fur, exist one burst at a time and mean family, mean troop, mean *come here* or *keep off*. A knapper's mother is a series of stills. The children play the hush-game — who can slow the heart, who can be boring inside a silence — and it is not really a game.

There are no wars. Blood is a summons: a wounded animal is a beacon the guild reads in minutes and the drumhounds in less, so disputes are settled as duets. Two troops drum the question, the side that loses the thread loses the pool, and the loser keeps all his blood. What the duets cannot settle, the ground-listeners settle — chiefly the one question that matters, which is which kind of silence is coming.

Because there are two. The sitting silence is the ground's. It spreads from a point, deepens, the Undernote swelling beneath it like something taking a breath — and then the vent opens. When the silence sits, you run: uphill, away from the flows, and you do not look back, because there is nothing to see anyway.

The walking silence is the other one.

The hush — a hush, for they are countable, barely — is the basin's apex in the way a weather system is an apex. The size of a flow-lobe. Low, broad, many-footed, unhurried. It is furred in something that is to sound what the felt is to fog: it combs the noise out of the air, and where it walks the drone dies in a moving bubble, and inside the bubble you can hear your own heart — and so can it. It does not chase. It has all night, and it is always night. It eats the flinching: the one that bolts, the one whose heart leaps, the one that knocks a pebble. Inside a walking silence the drill is the hush-game. Be boring. Slow the heart. Think of moss. The chorus floods in behind it like water closing over a wake.

You can feel one coming as a doubling of the Undernote. Nobody has ever found a young one. The long songs disagree — one troop holds there is a single hush that has been walking since the floor was hot; another names seven, by the shapes of their silences. A song from the far side of the basin tells of a sleeper touched once: the toucher's shout died in the fur, and the hush did not wake, or chose not to, and the song is honest enough not to decide which is worse. What is known fits on a flake:

If the silence walks, freeze.
If the silence sits, run.
If you cannot tell, hold still and listen for the ground. The ground announces itself. Eventually.

And then, periodically, the basin shreds the whole ledger: the Hatch. The noise leaves the register of sound and becomes weather, the air goes half animal by weight, and everything eats. The drumhounds lie on their backs and bite the sky. The herons simply stand with their mouths open. The small fliers hug the felt, because the air is too crowded to fly in. The knappers butcher until their arms ache, and the old ones shut their ear-lids and sit it out with the expression of people enduring a festival. Then the drifts of dead. Then the underlight — the two days when the meadows glow blue-white from underneath and the basin almost gets to see itself, when knappers smear their signs on the flow edges and children learn the troop's flash-marks by fungal light. Then the spore-fog rising to feed the Ceiling. The week closes. Another begins.

So scratch the census on a flake. The herds that are one sound with mouths. The statues around the black water, and the water that is not water. The frogs holding the keys of the floor; the hounds singing in their notch; the parliament of drummers with no walls, carrying everything they own in four hands, answering the sky stone-note by stone-note. Wings above, and wings above the wings; a meadow overhead and a drumhead underfoot; and somewhere — you can tell, because the frog-noise is dying in a line across the felt — the oldest resident, out for its evening walk.

The list is not finished. It is never finished. It is being sung, at full volume, in the dark, forever — and if you stand still long enough to hear it all the way through, you will have become, by the basin's way of counting, one of the singers. The Hush will pass. The song floods back.

It always has.
