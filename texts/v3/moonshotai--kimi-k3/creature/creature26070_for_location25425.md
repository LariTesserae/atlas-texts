The oldest tenant is the trout. No fish climbed here — the only water that moves goes out over the lip, and nothing arrives by it — so there was never a first trout, any more than there was a first cupful of the pool. It is in the water the way the water is in the hollow: originally. Its dark is the peat-water's own dark, as if the pool, thinking patiently for ten thousand years, had at last thought a fish. A fish's age is written in its ears in annual rings, but this one's rings would read like the peat face: the same year, over and over, laid down in a body that should have spent its span long ago. Watch it rise in its corner and you will notice that the rising is not hunger. In a ledger kept this exactly, hunger would be an error. The trout rises the way a bell strikes — because the interval has come around. The pool is exactly its size, the way a setting is exactly its stone.

That is the first thing to understand about the population here: these are not individuals. They are offices. The Trout. The Pair. The Shrew. An office that is never vacant and never contested, and so the holder is immortal in the only sense available here — and impersonal in the same measure. The dippers are two birds, indefinitely. A third would arrive already penciled in against a death; a lone bird would only mean an egg warm somewhere against the loss. They walk down into the thread-currents as other birds step along a branch, and when one of them sings — that brief, astonishing ripple in the hush — the song is not news and is not addressed to anyone. It is the drip-chord of the wall translated into a warmer tongue, and it is not repeated, because repetition would change the count. The shrew, everywhere else in the world, is a small furnace that can starve between meals, a heart built to hammer itself out. Here the furnace has been banked. It works its runways at the pace of the drips, and its heart has taken the hollow's slow commission. The thrushes at the anvil stone are generations deep, but each bird finds the cup already worn to the shape of its own stroke, as if the stone had been expecting it for ten thousand years — which is precisely how long the stone has been expecting it. Around the anvil the shell-drifts never grow: snails going in as bodies, coming out as lime, converted to memory without residue.

Below the offices, the multitudes. Turn any stone and the salamander beneath it regards you with the calm of something that has never once needed to hurry — and hurry, you realize, belongs to creatures whose time runs out. Here time does not run. It pools. The leeches glide like commas still looking for their sentence, and the sentence is the trout, and the sentence is your wrist. The boatmen row the middle depths and go nowhere, rowing in place the way everything here rows in place. Under the mat's edge the amphipods are beyond counting, and every lifted handful of moss yields its census, and within the census the recursion begins: every snail a landlord, its mites the tenants, and the mites carrying freight of their own. The hollow does not believe in vacancy. A surface is an address. The faint ammonia in the air is simply the smell of the majority.

Under the overhangs of the seep-wall, the lamps keep their stations. Everywhere else a larva is a promise. Here promises are deferred forever, because keeping them would alter the census — so the glow-worms remain larvae: a whole people held at the threshold of their own becoming, burning at the same low wattage, patient as only creatures can be who have never been offered an alternative. Becoming is the one thing no one here does. Their light is not even a lure, or no longer is; the midges arrive on schedule regardless. It is the visible form of staying. Every lamp owns its twin in the pools below, so even the stars of this place are resident — counted twice, once in stone and once in water, the hollow's only redundancy, and that one optical.

The largest resident is underfoot. You do not walk on the floor of the hollow; you walk on it. The sphagnum is the landlord of landlords, two hundred paces of quilted, floating skin, wine-red and gold, trembling under your step because it is alive and because it floats, closing over your footprints because closing is what it does — the way a good host smooths a cushion the moment a guest rises. The sundews studding it are always mid-meal, an appetite that never reaches evening. The butterworts lie against the seep-wall digesting at the precise rate the midges arrive, mill and grain-stream, synchronized past any need for a season. Things flower a few at a time, forever: the hollow's way of keeping a spring that never begins and so never has to end.

And yet count all of this — fish, birds, shrew, the small cold millions, the lamps, the snails, the living floor — and you have listed only the furniture. What lives here, the thing for which the census keeps coming out even, is the keeping itself. It is not a god; gods want things, and wanting is a leak. It is not a spirit; spirits have weather, and the hollow has one weather. Call it an equilibrium and you are right. Call it a host and you are righter. It has manners the way stone has grain. Perturb it and you feel the attention close over your disturbance — courteous, total, unhurried — the way the mat closes over a footprint. It does not love the trout. It does not hate the leech. It keeps them. *To keep*: to hold, and to tend, and to continue. All three meanings are in force everywhere, at drip-pace, forever.

One resident is known only by its works. Beside the shrew's runways lie the stripped stems, used and dropped — probes, small levers, the only made things in the hollow, if made is the word. Something here practices the gesture nearest to yours: taking up a thing for a purpose and setting it down again. No one has seen the maker. It leaves its tools the way you will leave your footprints, and the moss unmakes them at the same courteous rate. Even craft is composted on schedule. The hollow permits making, apparently, provided it adds up to nothing. Everything here adds up to nothing. Nothing here is lost.

Then there are the residents you cannot see from the floor. Stand at the outflow, where the stream has undercut the mat, and the peat faces you, taller than you: leaf, wing-case, seed, husk, small bone — everyone. The quick walk the mat; the dead stand in the wall; both are present, and both are counted. Everyone who has ever lived here lives here still. That is what *nothing is lost* means, taken literally, and the hollow takes everything literally. An honest census includes the whole archive: the earlier trout, which is the same trout; the earlier pair, which is the same pair; pressed into skins no thicker than a thumbprint, descending at exactly the rate the water leaves. The dead are the only travelers, and their only direction is down. The hollow is more crowded than it looks by exactly ten thousand years.

For the length of your stay, the ledger opens a line for you as well. Your arrival already has your departure scheduled against it — you can feel the scheduling as a mild conviction that you should start back before dusk, though it is always dusk. You are the only thing here that spends. Every other account balances to the drip; yours runs down while you stand watching, and spending makes a noise — that is why you are the loudest thing in the hollow, why your pulse is suddenly an instrument in the chord. For a little while you are one of the kept: your warmth condensing on the same moss, your breath faintly visible at the same speed the cold breath drains down the wall. Then you go over the lip, carrying time out like something smuggled, and the mat closes over the record of you, and the pools do not fall or rise by a hair, and the hollow forgets you the way it forgets nothing else: deliberately, politely, and only because you asked it to by leaving.

So ask what lives in the hollow, and the short answer is the true one. One life, kept in many bodies: a trout's, two birds', a shrew's, a few million small cold bodies, the lamps', the snails', the dead in their descending skins — yours, briefly — and the keeping is the life. It has no use for waiting, because nothing it is going to become is not already here, in some pool or skin or lamp, being. It rose in its corner a long time ago, on a schedule no one reads. It rises still. The surface breaks; the rings go out and die at the edge; the drips keep their intervals; and that is the only prophecy the hollow has ever needed — that it will rise again, because it has always just risen.
