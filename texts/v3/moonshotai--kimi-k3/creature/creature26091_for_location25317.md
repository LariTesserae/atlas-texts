Count them, if counting comforts you.

It will not, for long. The congregation in your stretch of shallows numbers in the hundreds, and you can tally the waders on a given morning and get a number, and tally again at dusk and get the same number, and again a week later — the same. Not approximately. Exactly. The census of this world is an integer, and it does not admit noise. After a while you stop counting and start recognizing, because the count never teaches you anything new and the individuals, slowly, do.

The place is stratified the way a forest is stratified. There is a floor, a water column, a surface, a canopy of gantries, an air, a mist, and a below — and every one of those levels has its own citizens, its own rounds, its own way of being alive here. You could map the population by altitude alone, and the map would be complete, and it would never need redrawing.

The waders you know first, because the waders make themselves knowable. Three legs, so that two are always planted: patience rendered as geometry. Stillness is their default and motion is their decision, and when one crosses the shallows toward some seam known only to it, each step lands with the finality of a signature. You begin, after weeks, to see that their bills differ — shears, probes, fine brushes — and that the differences are not decorative. Each wader is shaped for the seams it tends. Whether it was made for that parish or assigned to it is a question the waders do not appear to ask themselves. Their faces are smooth dark visors, and when one turns to regard you, you meet yourself doubled in it — a third reflection, after sky and water, the world handing you back to yourself out of the eye of its most numerous citizen. It behaves exactly like curiosity. You have stopped being sure it is not exactly curiosity.

Their voices are the bells. Two notes across the fog, answered once — never more than once, never more than needed. You have come to hear it as bookkeeping: a position report, an acknowledgment, the congregation confirming itself to itself across the white distances. No one here has a name. Everyone here has an address.

Some of them take the weirs. You will see a wader standing in one of the little perpetual falls itself, knee-deep in the only loud water the world allows, holding a fine comb of a bill in the current and stripping from it whatever the water is handing down — the exact ration, you suspect, the exact mineral tithe the morning's evaporation owed and the afternoon's rain repays. The shoals queue at such a wader's knees and thicken there, and the wader dips, and the shoal receives. It behaves exactly like feeding. It is perhaps exactly accounting.

The lickers own the floor. Each one works a parish of plates whose boundaries you can infer after long watching, because the parishes meet and never overlap, a tiling of responsibilities laid over the tessellation itself. Their mouths are what the seams are for. The bead of mineral moisture that weeps along every plate-edge — you will eventually watch a licker lay it down, drawing that gray line the way a mason points a joint, and you will understand that the world sweats through their mouths. Nothing hurries here, but the lickers are the least in a hurry, the still point the rest of the population organizes itself around. Waders perch on their backs by the dozen, and the lickers do not slow for their passengers, and the passengers do not ask them to; the stillness of the one is tuned to the pace of the other, an agreement older than your arrival by some span you cannot estimate. At the docking pedestals they queue in an order that is fixed and never disputed, and each takes exactly its measure of charge, of cooling, of exchange, and none of them waits visibly, because waiting is what they are made of. Standing near one, you feel it in your shins before you hear anything: a subsonic patience, transmitted through blood-warm water and bone.

The shoals are the most of all, and they are the most of everything. Call them the bloodstream and you are not wrong; call them the mail and you are not wrong; call them the treasury and you are still not wrong. They carry trace everything — heat from the backs of lickers, charge from the pedestals, the stripped tithe from the weirs — and the intakes sip them in and the outfalls give them back, silver in, silver out, changed by exactly one load, the world breathing through its smallest citizens. Wade into a shoal and it parts and closes around you and you feel a million small inquiries against your skin: a census of you, taken constantly, remembered nowhere — or remembered everywhere, which in this place may be the same statement. They are the only citizens who never hold still, and the dawn is the only exception: kilometers of silver orienting at once, the water itself seeming to turn toward the light. At the stair-stepped basins you can watch the locks hand them down level by level, rivers of them descending the steps like water falling, which, in every sense that matters here, they are.

The striders have the surface, the sky-side of the world, and they are the only citizens who touch it. Their feet write dimples that the water forgets instantly, so that their whole literature is a continuous erasure, and in dry hours their ticking is a faint local rain. When a rain column stands up and walks the plain, the flotillas raft out to meet it — the only ones who go to weather — because the rain is a delivery, and they are the receiving dock. The film they farm is one molecule deep and it is enough, because enough is the local unit of abundance.

The kites have the air, and they read it the way lickers read seams. All day they ride the radiator thermals, and in the afternoons they work the skirts of the walking rain columns, and you have come to believe — you cannot prove it, and the kites could, and have not been asked — that this is why the thunder here is always distant: the storms are sipped of their potential, bled gently, before they can ever break overhead. At dusk they come home in slow streams and settle shoulder to shoulder along the pylons, thousands to a structure, the order of the roost being the order of arrival, never disputed, each bird — each not-bird — running a fine inspection along the vanes of its neighbor. It behaves exactly like preening. It is perhaps exactly maintenance. The soft servo chorus of the settling lasts a few minutes and is the loudest sound of the day, and then the pylons stand hung with amber and green marker lights, a city on every structure, quiet.

The mist has its own people. In the radiator forests, if you look for the slowest thing in sight, you will find the fin-climbers — and they will have been there all along, an arm's reach away, drinking. They are slow the way sloths are slow, if sloths were made of patience and alloy, and they climb the warm fins and sip the condensate before it can drip, and they are the reason — or the tenders of the reason — that the forest's ticking is arrhythmic and yet statistically constant, a ticker reporting a number that never changes. They are the statisticians of the mist. If they stopped, the sound of the world would change, and the world, you are certain, would notice.

Overhead, the canopy has its folk too: the runners, long bead-bodied things that travel the cable catenaries and never in their lives touch water or floor. At crossings they transfer with a reach you always half-miss, and after one has passed, the cable sings faintly and the condensate lies along it a little more evenly, and that is all you will ever know of their work. Gantry country is a canopy the way a rainforest has a canopy, tenanted by creatures for whom the floor is a rumor. You imagine this. They have not said so.

The below has its citizens as well, or perhaps its singular citizen. You will never see the hospitals, though they must exist: once you watched a wader stand at a pedestal for an entire day, motionless, while the queue flowed around it without comment, and by morning it was in its round again — or it was gone and the census was somehow unchanged, and you could not tell which, and there was no one to ask who would answer. And once — once only — a hatch irised open near your camp and something many-armed came up out of the floor, unhurried, and trued the lip of a weir with a single pass of a palm like a trowel, and every wader in sight turned to watch: the only thing besides the sunrise you have ever seen them all watch together. Then it descended, and the hatch became a seam became a plate, and by evening you could not find the place. You have stopped expecting a second time. The world keeps its plumbers, or its priests, under the floor, where it keeps everything it does not need you to see.

And the horizon keeps a few of its citizens theoretical. There are shapes out near the superstructures, silhouettes that carry weather on their backs, and you walked toward them once for three days and the distance did not close. Either they are that large, or they keep their own counsel at their own remove. Whether they are the largest animals or the smallest structures is a distinction the horizon declines to draw. You turned back. The plain is big enough to hold mysteries the size of mountains, and it does.

And between the plates, in the seams, there is the whisper — the gray, single-celled, dull simplicity, the only unmade thing in a made world, unless it too is made. The waders crop it; the lickers seal over it; it never spreads and it never dies, cropped as fast as it grows, the world's only crop. Stay long enough and you will come to suspect the arrangement is not incidental. A perfectly balanced world would have nothing outside itself to tend, and tending is the entire occupation of everything here — so the world keeps one small living thing, one gray smear of the old chaos, and feeds it and trims it and organizes ten thousand citizens around its keeping. Call it the garden. Call it the grave of the wild world, carefully kept. Call it the point. The water declines to clarify.

Watch them long enough and the strangeness reorganizes itself. Two waders will mirror each other's bill-dips in perfect alternation for an hour, and it behaves exactly like courtship, and nothing comes of it, and nothing was ever going to come of it, and nothing about it is diminished. Kite pairs loop the same long loop above the radiators, and it behaves exactly like play, with no winner and no score. The noon stillness on the licker islands behaves exactly like siesta, though nothing here is tired. The night slowing behaves exactly like sleep, though nothing here needs to wake. It is a whole ecology with the stakes removed, and you expected that to feel hollow, and instead it feels serene, and the serenity is harder to sit with than hollowness would be — because nothing here is pretending, and nothing here is waiting for the real thing to begin. The rituals are not rehearsals. The rituals are the life.

There is no fear here. Clap your hands: heads turn, regard, return. Nothing flinches, because nothing has ever needed to; fear is a tax levied by scarcity and death, and both of those have been abolished. You are the only animal in this world that has ever been afraid, and your fear thins by the week, like an accent you are losing. You are also the only animal here that is aging — the sole carrier of the past tense in a world of continuous present — and perhaps that is what they see when they turn their visors toward you: the one walking novelty, the one unbalanced account, the only entry in the ledger with a date on it.

The day has its liturgy, and they keep it. Before dawn, the fog and the slow and the soft clicking — some of it the plates ticking as they flex, though nothing ever cools, and some of it, you have come to believe, conversation among citizens you have not met, ledgers the world keeps to itself. Then the light, and the facing: every wader and licker and kite, every raft of striders aligning, every shoal orienting at once, the lickers pausing mid-seam, a few breaths of absolute corporate stillness while the world confirms itself to itself. Calibration, roll-call, grace — the place declines the distinction. Then the rounds resume, and they are the same rounds, and the dusk brings the kites home and the chorus, and the night brings the slow, and the shoals keep turning in the dark like thoughts the world is thinking, and nothing is gained and nothing is spent.

You will stop counting and start recognizing. The marker lights are constellations, and you will name the lickers of your parish after stars, and the names will hold, because the lights never change. And there will be one wader — you know it by a single lamp set greener than the rest of its kind — whose congregation's ground overlaps your camp, and each day, at no fixed hour, it steps out of the loose hundreds and crosses to you and presents the port beneath its bill and waits, patient as a pedestal, and then steps away. A daily appointment with no clock behind it. Once, you touched the port with a fingertip: warmth, the ghost of charge, and beneath it a readiness with no timer on it. A held door. You withdrew your hand. Nothing insisted.

That is the offer, you think, the same one the world makes to everything it contains: give what you carry, take what you need, be accounted for. You have not accepted. You have noticed — it took you longer than it should have — that you have not declined, either.

So: who lives here? Not residents, in the end. Residence. The world lives here, once, in ten thousand bodies, and the animals are not its tenants but its living of itself — its grazing, its watching, its patient attendance upon its own morning. The whisper is its garden, or its memory. And you are the only guest, and the hospitality is this: lights brightening ahead of you along the gantries and dimming behind, shoals parting and closing around your knees and giving back warmth, and a port held open under a wader's bill, forever, uninsisted upon.

In the morning the fog comes up white and warm, and everything turns to face the light, and at some point — you could not say when it began — you understood that you had turned to face it too. Nothing asked you to. Nothing here asks. Across the fog a bell sounds: two notes. The answer, once. Attendance taken, everyone present, the count exact — and lately, though you say nothing about it, lately you stand in the count.
