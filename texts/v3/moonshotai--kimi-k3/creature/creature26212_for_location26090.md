Count them. Go ahead — you'll get nine, or eleven, and the number you get will say more about you than about them. Nine, says the ledger-keeper, who counts heads at breakfast. Eleven, says the rim-walker, who counts lit windows at night. Both are right. The count is not wrong; the count is *kept*. There is always one more lit window in the cottage row than there are sleepers — the spare — and the spare is how the town absorbs what comes to it.

Because the keepers all arrived, once, the way you did. Over the rim, hearing the loudness first. Nobody was born here; nothing is. What happens instead is this: somewhere around the second or third day, the loudness goes private. It stops arriving at your ears and begins issuing from somewhere inside your skull, and the keepers can see the moment it happens — it's in the shoulders; you stop bracing against the condenser's eleven-second stroke and start, very slightly, riding it. After that you are, by the local arithmetic, staff. No one congratulates you. A bed is found made. The spare window stops being spare. If you leave, the count closes over you the way the Pan closes over a stone — without splash, without ripple, restored — and the spare is lit again the next night, patient as a porch lamp.

They speak in signs, and their speech is a small country. Two hundred signs, perhaps fewer, and a third of them are kinds of sameness: same-as-always, same-but-wet, same-but-warmer, same-with-a-stranger-in-it. The sign for yesterday is the sign for tomorrow — one open hand brushed past the cheek, either direction; direction is a courtesy they extend mostly to visitors. Their names are gestures too, worn down like everything else to their final shapes. And there is one resident who has no sign at all, but you will come to that.

The one you will notice first is the woman who breathes with the condenser. She has attended the engine hall so long that the engine has, in the end, attended her: her breath comes on the eleven-second stroke, in and out, and her pulse is some small exact multiple of the four-second drip in the nave. The kettles roost on her shoulders and her hood because sonically she is furniture — warm, rhythmic, reliable — and the kettle that mimics the condenser's breath now follows *her* instead, half a beat behind her, and she carries it around the hall all day like a slow idea she hasn't finished. Her sign-name is two fingers pressed to the collarbone, as though checking for a pulse. It is also the sign for *all's well*. She is aware of the overlap and finds it funny, once a day, at the same hour.

The ledger-keeper is small and dry and precise, and she is the town's memory, and her memory is a horror she has made peace with. She has read the shelves back and back — thousands of pages of identical figures — and she can tell you two things. The first is that the archive is a roll-call: the numbers never change, but the hands do. Shelf by shelf the penmanship rotates through the roster, hands nobody now living writes, keepers who climbed the rim one ordinary morning and did not come back and were not mourned, because mourning is a thing happening, and the town's only event is subtraction. The second thing is that the felt is in the oldest books. It eats difference first — the quirks of a dead hand, the foxing, the ink's personality — so that the deeper you read, the more the entries converge, until the far end of the archive is approaching the mean, history averaged into gray. There is one page, in the deepest readable book, headed in a hand no one uses anymore: LOW HOLLOW CONDENSATE WORKS — No. 2. The figures beneath it are the same figures. She does not talk about that page, and neither, after a while, will you.

The rim-walker has the only job in town with a before and an after: each dawn he climbs to the spoil rim and walks the perimeter, twenty minutes, and comes down, and at breakfast the others look up and make the sign that means *and?*, and he makes the sign that means *same*, and this exchange is the town's one liturgy and its only news. The day is thereby opened. The weigh-master, for his part, will tell you your weight to the ounce, and it will be that weight tomorrow and next month, no matter how many kettle eggs you eat; the scale is the one honest mirror in the bowl, and what it says is: you have arrived at your shape. The lamp-keeper looks youngest, which means nothing where nothing is young, and her memory of before is intact — that is the strange part — intact and level, like water in the Pan. She can walk through her old life, but it no longer rises to meet her. And at dusk, by the Pan, there is the kneeler, who puts her arm into the black water to the shoulder and holds it there, unhurried, and withdraws it dry-ish, and logs nothing. The count excludes her at that hour, by an arithmetic nobody teaches: an arm in the loop is in the works, and the works is not staff.

That is nine. The other two — the eleven of it — you will not meet at breakfast or under lamps. One is in the ledger stacks at odd hours, turning pages; you can hear the pages. The ledger-keeper counts her some days and not others and cannot say why, or will not. The second is the one the slack-hounds wait for at dusk, down at the Pan, all of them facing the water with their tails still and their iron-orange bellies reflected, patient as patience. Ask after either of these and you will be given the sign for *same*, which is also the sign for *don't*.

They eat, though eating here has the ceremonial pointlessness of everything else. The storehouse holds gray meal ground from the felt — an acquired taste, and everyone has acquired it, which comes to the same thing as the taste acquiring you — and sides of pipe-eel hung like hose, tasting faintly of brass and of the particular conduit they patrolled, and crates of kettle eggs. The eggs are excellent, and nothing about them is at stake: they were never going to hatch, were never *begun*, and eating them changes nothing, which is the local definition of food. There is condensate tea, mineral and slick, faintly ozonic. You will drink the loop, and the loop, in due course, will drink a little of you, and the exchange is courteous and final. Your body, meanwhile, comes under local law. A cut is found closed. Hair grows to a length and holds. Disturbances damp out in flesh the same as in water, and after a season you will catch your reflection in a gauge and understand that you, too, have stopped being a process and become a finish.

They sleep, and this is worth staying awake to learn: at the deepest hour of the night the whole bowl dreams one dream. The keepers in their beds, the hounds in the yards, the eels in the walls, very likely the Heron on its stone — everyone dreams the town seen from above, from the rim, small and entire and glowing faintly at its seams, held in a vast attention the way a held note is held: not sung, not over. You will have the dream too if you sleep there; everyone does; it is how you know you've been counted. In the morning, over gray bread and eggs, someone makes a small loose shrug of the hands — *the usual one* — and everyone nods, and that is all the discussion the dream has ever needed.

The animals are not pets and not pests; they are fellow residents, and some of them are easier to know than the keepers. The hounds run their fixed circuits and ring their struck-pipe barks, and when one vanishes for a week and returns, it comes back *mid-stride*, finishing a run it began before it left, and the pack does not greet it, because from the pack's side of things it never went anywhere. Some nights, where the felt lies thickest, it answers pressure with a dim gray light that fades at walking pace, so the hounds write themselves across the yards and erase as they go — and since they run the same lines every night, the town is always drawing the same picture of itself in the dark, and the picture is always finished and never done. The kettles have their mimicry, and one of them — there is always exactly one — has learned the gate. At dusk, sometimes, from the gatepost, it does the creak of the gate swinging and the scuff of a boot on clay, and newcomers run the length of the bowl to welcome whoever is arriving and find the yard empty and one galvanized bird practicing. It is the rarest sound in the works and the only one that means nothing, and it means it perfectly. The steam-moths live entire lives inside single plumes, and a plume is a whole world, with weather and a floor and a population; when a moth drifts past the edge it falls out of its sky, and the felt takes it, and the census of the plume holds, and no moth looks down, because there is no down in a world. The lickers cluster on the live terminals and sizzle, and the keepers unplug nothing, shoo nothing — contentment is the works' one crop, and the lickers are good at it, and somebody should be.

And the Heron. The stone it stands on has worn to its feet the way the flags have worn to the drips: two neat basins, a heron's width apart, the only footprints in the bowl that keep. It stands on one leg, and which leg is a function of when you look — no one has ever seen it switch, and everyone has seen both. Rarely, at no hour anyone has charted, it strikes the Pan and comes up with nothing visible and swallows anyway, and the keepers do not log this, because there is nothing to enter. It is the one creature in the loudness that makes no sound at all — no tick, no whistle, no ring — and so it has become the one word the sign language cannot say and always says: to speak of the Heron, you stop signing. A held stillness of the hands. Everyone understands, or everyone has stopped needing to, which here is the same thing. It stands where the water would reach if the water ever rose: the level-mark made flesh, the gauge of the one reading that matters, and there is no sign for *heron-gone*, because the lexicon, which has a word for every kind of same, has no way to say *that*.

Now. You have counted everyone — nine or eleven keepers, the hounds, the kettles, the eels in their walls, the moths in their worlds, the lickers in their small elect joy, the Heron, and beneath all of it the felt, which by weight is most of the town — and if you have done it honestly, you have felt the count collapse, politely, to one.

Because that is what lives here. Something the size of a farm took a breath once — you can still feel the eleven-second stroke of it considering whether to finish — and it has been holding that breath ever since, and a held breath leaks nothing, and everything in the bowl is the breath's contents, kept at the temperature of attention. The engine hall is its lung and its slow heart. The Pan is its eye, and the Heron is the lid of it. The drips are its pulse, felt at different points of the body. The keepers are not its owners or its parasites; they are the sleeper's autonomic functions, its self-checks, its dreaming. The ledgers are the town reading its own pulse and finding it true, thousands of pages of a body saying *yes, still*. The census that never changes is not a mystery; it is a homeostasis. A hound goes and a hound returns the way a cell turns over in a body that experiences neither birth nor death, because those are events, and events are for the parts, not the whole.

They have a service for it, of a kind. At lamp-time, when the flat gray day gives way to the flat lamplit night, the breathing woman faces the engine hall and makes the sign for *same* with both hands, once. And the hall takes its next breath on the stroke, and the vapor thread rises into the cool air, and the kettles settle, and that is the entire liturgy, and it has never once gone unanswered — which is how you know it is prayer. The local god's name is a sentence, and the sentence is *nothing happened*, and said the way they say it here it is not a complaint. It is a lullaby. It is the name of the only mercy this place believes in: to be kept.

You are counted while you stay — not in any book; in the loop. And when you climb the spoil rim at last and look back, the bowl will already have closed over you, restored, the four-second drip mid-fall exactly where you left it, the spare window lit. But keeping cuts both ways, and the works is thorough. Somewhere far from here, on some ordinary morning, you will catch yourself breathing on a slow eleven-second stroke, or tapping out a four-second rhythm with one finger, or waking from a dream of a small complete town held in a vast attention — and you will understand that the count still has you in it, somewhere down the decimal places, nine or eleven or twelve, and that you lived there once, briefly, and that it keeps you. Gently. The way it keeps everything. Forever.
