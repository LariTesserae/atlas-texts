Start with the census, because it is short, and because one of the tenders has already written it down — front page of the current log, where other books keep maps.

*One plant.*
*One lichen, in rosettes. (More than one kind, probably. No one here can say. The page says one.)*
*Two insects: the white moth, two weeks a year. The diving beetle, all year, every flark.*
*One bird. (Geese are weather.)*
*Four people.*
*Five engines.*

That is the census of the basin, and it has been essentially the census for ninety years — give or take a dog.

Every line on the list is a tenant of the first line.

---

The plant was here first — first after the ice, which is the same as first — and no one who lives on it can manage to speak of it as a plural. Botanists presumably could. Botanists visit rarely and leave quickly, and the log records them the way it records everything from outside: briefly, and by what they would not walk on. The tenders speak of the skin the way you speak of a person. *The skin is low this year. The skin is brown around Three. The skin is taking Four.* It has a temperament, and the temperament is patience. It forgets a ditch for thirty years and then takes a building, and it takes the building the way it does everything, over decades, without malice, and it has never once been hurried, and it has never once stopped.

It keeps its own records, downward, in peat, a millimeter a year — everything it has ever been, filed beneath it in the dark. The tenders cut that record into black bricks and dry it in herringbone ranks and burn it, and the smoke that leans away down the valley from the six chimneys — the five chimneys — is the basin's memory, leaving. The smell of the drying racks is the smell of the valley remembering, and every tender goes nose-blind to it within a month, the way you stop hearing the engines, and for roughly the same reason: it is the medium. It is not the message.

Everyone who has lived here long has gone through the skin exactly once. It is the basin's one lesson, given free. You read the color wrong — pale firm ground, dark hungry ground — or you step off the planks at dusk confident, and the skin receives you, and the water is so cold it is a flavor before it is a temperature, and you come up hauling yourself onto a ridge of cushions while a wave you made sloshes a flark thirty meters off, late, like the ground finishing a thought. The log records these the way it records comets: date, stake number, depth. *M. through at Stake 60, to the waist. Water cold. Mended the hole.* You mend the hole. That is the other half of the lesson: the skin is the ground, and you mend the ground you break. Afterward you are, like everyone else here, at all times, being held up by the plant — carried, literally, by the thing you work against. Nobody forgets it. Nobody mentions it either. It is the medium.

---

Of the small wild lives, each has a custom attached, the way small towns give every street a story.

The lichen is the senior tender's oldest relationship. There is a rosette the size of a coin on a boulder beside the pass trail, and for thirty-one years the senior has touched it going up and coming down, and in thirty-one years it has grown the width of a fingernail. It was here before any person. It will be here whether the engines win or lose. It does not know about the contest. The senior finds this restful.

The moth is the year's hinge. Somewhere in the six-meter mat, one kind of caterpillar grazes the largest single plant anyone will ever stand on — the sole herbivore of a forty-kilometer pasture, one species to one clone, and the math of that is the basin's math, and it works. For two weeks in high summer the floor snows gently upward, and the tenders' lamps — the only lamps in forty kilometers — gather the moths in drifts against the glass, and in the mornings the drifts are swept into the boiler at Station One, because the boiler is where the basin puts everything it has finished with: sod, moth-drift, broken walkway planks. The smoke in moth weeks is faintly sweeter. No one remarks on this. It is in the log anyway, in the newest hand: *Sweet smoke, moth-burning.* The old hands let it stand. It is accurate. When the first moth comes early the log says *Moths early*, and the tenders read omens in it, and pretend not to.

The diving beetle is slow as the water it lives in, and a flark is a universe with green walls, and a beetle may live its whole life in thirty meters of clear freezing water and know everything about it. The walker — the tender who makes the long rounds — carries small tins punched with holes, and moves beetles, a tin at a time, from the flarks the ditches are draining to the flarks beyond the ditches' reach. There are more beetles in one kilometer of floor than the walker could move in a lifetime. The walker moves them anyway. It is not conservation. Nothing here is conservation. It is manners.

The plover comes back with the thaw, a few pairs, gray as the scree, and its first two-note call of the year goes in the log with a date, like a comet. It nests on the drained margin near the stations — on engine-ground, on the first true ground the basin has ever had — which makes it the only wild thing that has chosen the engines' future, the first citizen of the hedgerow map, and the tenders, whatever side they are on, are glad of it, and say so by saying nothing. The walker keeps a second log, unofficial, in pencil, of individuals: the hen with the twisted foot, the cock that nests by Stake 40. The others pretend not to consult it and consult it. One of them whistles to the plovers and the plovers answer, and the conversation has outlived many birds — no single plover lives as long as the conversation. It is between the place and the practice now.

The geese are weather. When a skein crosses, work stops. Nobody decided this. The tenders come out of whatever they are doing — out of the engine's shout, out of sleep — and watch the birds thread the notch of the pass, high, going somewhere that is finished, and nobody speaks until the last dot clears the rim. They watch the way you watch a train you have decided not to take. Once, in ninety years, a goose landed. It is in the log: *Goose down, flark by Stake 96. Lame. Stayed the summer. Died at first frost.* The only goose in the record. The record is ninety years.

And there was a dog, once, years back, that came over the pass with a new tender. It would not leave the bricks. It barked at the four-second silences. It lasted a winter and was sent out with the autumn train, and the log says only: *Dog went out, autumn.* The census does not count it. The census is careful.

---

The engines are not alive, and the census includes them anyway, because no one who sleeps to their beating has ever managed to leave them off the list. What the company did, though it did not think of it this way, was introduce a grazer. The engines eat the plant. They drink the ditch. They breathe a feather of smoke. Their droppings are ground — the brown firm margins around the stations where the garden is, and the graves, and the plover's nest. Everything in the basin that is not the moss lives in the tension between the moss and the engines, and the tenders are the grazer's keepers, and the arrangement is older than any of them, and it has a name, and the name is husbandry.

The five that run have temperaments, and the tenders know them the way shepherds know sheep. One is the metronome; the others are tuned against it, and its keeper denies a favorite and oils it first. Two hunts — it is the log's most-mentioned name, a fussy governor, a machine that is never quite happy — and the mechanic, who complains about it most, oils it first too, and calls the can the shut-up oil. Three is mid-basin and steady, and its beat comes up through the soles of your feet a kilometer before the sound arrives through the air, so that walking out to it you meet it twice. Five was rebuilt once, long ago, and has had a catch in its voice ever since, and knocks on cold mornings until it warms, and its tender knocks back on the casing with a wrench, one knock, a ritual neither party acknowledges. Six is the outpost under the snowfield, highest and coldest, a day by trolley from anywhere, and it has never once been the one to stop in the night. The tenders have noticed. They say Six wants the job.

Four is the dead one, and it is being redistributed. The mechanic keeps a parts ledger: *took 4's valve chest for the trolley brake; left the boiler; will want the flywheel bolts come spring.* Its window glass is in the garden cold frames. Its chimney bricks are at the heads of the graves. Its number plate hangs over a cottage door the way a retired soldier's name goes over a door. The moss will get whatever is left, and every year there is less left; Four is being out-salvaged. It is the only race in the basin the engines' side is winning.

When an engine stops at night, all four tenders wake in the same second. There is no alarm in the basin and none is needed; the absence is the alarm. Whoever's station it is goes out with a lantern, and the pump-handle of the trolley carries for kilometers in the cold, and the other three lie awake and listen to the repair happen in sound — the far-off clang, the silence, the first uncertain strokes — and they sleep when the beat rejoins the phase, the way you sleep when crying stops down the hall. And in the four-second silences, when all five engines fall out of time together and the floor holds its breath, the new tenders hold theirs, and the old ones listen to the small sounds the noise was hiding: the drip of the skin, the slosh of a settling flark, frost forming with faint clicks, their own pulses. The senior sings exactly one song, one line per silence, and a verse can take an hour. The engines do not mind. They have no high notes to drown.

---

Tenders arrive the way everything arrives here: over the pass, at first light, looking down at the shining floor and hearing the hearts. Some turn around at the rim. The muleteers bring them back down the same day, and no log records them — the basin interviews at first light, from the rim, and most candidates decline. The ones who come down are given goggles and a cot and time. There is no ceremony. The first year is walking: the colors, the planks, the one free lesson. The second year is hearing: which station is which, from anywhere, by how the beats unravel. Somewhere in the third year they are handed the pen, if their entries have gotten short enough.

The shelf of logs goes back ninety years in six kinds of handwriting, and you can tell the current four apart at a glance.

The upright hand that never blots is the senior, thirty-one years in the basin. Arrived at twenty with a trunk and a reason; the trunk is a workbench now, and the reason wore out, which is what reasons do here. Went skin-blind once, in the early years, before learning the goggles by the door; wears them now even on dim days, and the eyes underneath are the palest thing in the basin after the flarks. In winter the senior reads the old volumes aloud, a few pages a night — the nearest thing the basin has to theater, or church: the voice of a hand fifty years stilled, still reporting water levels to whoever is awake. One of the dead hands exaggerated. His stakes never matched his entries, and the discrepancy was caught forty years after his death, and it is still mentioned, fondly. *He's high again,* the mechanic says, of a man dead half a century, and the senior turns the page.

The cramped engineer's printing is the mechanic, who sleeps in the fuel shed. The cottages were offered; the shed was nearer One's voice, and the cot was too soft, and that is the whole explanation anyone has ever gotten. The mechanic can stand at the cottage door and diagnose by phase — *Three's dragging* — and be right, and keeps the parts ledger, and is slowly moving Station Four, bolt by bolt, into the other five engines and the graves and the garden, the way a family wears out a coat into patches.

The long sloping hand with the odd e's is the walker: gone days at a time up-valley with the beetle tins and the pencil comet-log, the one who whistles to the plovers, the one who knows every stake's number from anywhere on the floor. The walker watches Station Four drown with an expression nobody names, and clears ditches beautifully anyway, because the work is the work.

The fourth hand still writes too much. Three years in, and the entries come with weather in them, with adjectives, with the occasional line that isn't weather at all — *Sweet smoke, moth-burning* — and the old hands read those lines the way you read a patient's chart, watching to see if the place is taking. It is taking. The entries get shorter every month. The new one keeps the garden, and once, in the first year, asked what happens when the water's gone. The mechanic said: *Then we dig peat.* The senior said: *Then the work changes.* The walker said: *Then the plovers get a valley.* Nobody said the fourth thing, which all four knew: that nobody here will see it, that the question is a form of homesickness for a future, and that it passes.

The garden is the only hedgerow the charter map ever produced: potatoes and kale and onions, rhubarb against the warm brick of Station One, cold frames glazed with Four's window glass, a fence of survey stakes too old to trust. Beside it are the graves, each with a brick from Four's fallen chimney at its head. The shelf counts pens, not lives, and there are more graves than dead hands — the early cook, a muleteer who stayed one winter and then thirty winters, and one grave smaller than the others, from the company's time, nameless now, which the four keep weeded because the log of that year is laconic even by the shelf's standard: *Child died, spring. The mother went out, autumn. The father keeps Two.* Ninety years of reclamation have produced two fields: one that feeds them, one that holds them, and the plover patrols the fence between, impartial.

---

In winter the pass closes and the basin is an island. The skin freezes in sheets and the flarks lid over and the beetles go slow beneath the ice, and the smoke lies flat against the cold, and the beats come through the frozen ground louder than in summer, as if the valley is holding its breath and the hearts are the only sound left. The logs lengthen. Essays appear. A poem gets copied out. Once a careful drawing of a moth, in pencil, which the others agree is excellent and the walker denies having made. Nobody finds any of this strange. Strange is the outside. They know this because the outside comes to them, once a year, in autumn: the supply train over the pass before the snow, flour and salt and lamp oil and boiler fittings and letters a year stale, mentioning machines that did not exist when they were sealed. The mules will not step on the skin. They stand on the made margin and scream. The mules know. The muleteers camp two nights; the tenders talk too much the first night and fall silent the second, already withdrawing. In the valley, the muleteers report, the tenders have gone moss-mad, skin-footed. From inside it does not look like madness. The basin removes everything that is not itself, and what remains of a person afterward is what they brought. The current four brought patience, mechanics, attention, and a question. The basin is using all four.

Some tenders leave. They walk out with the autumn train, rich on paper — the trust's wages banked untouched in the town for decades, compounding, belonging to people who no longer quite exist. The muleteers say you can tell the ones who are leaving for good: they stand a long time at the rim, not looking, listening, getting the phase pattern by heart to carry out with them. Whether it helps them sleep, out in the finished world, the log does not say. The log's jurisdiction ends at the rim.

Ask the four which side they are on — the skin's or the engines' — and you get four deflections. The mechanic says the charter. The walker says nothing for a long time. The gardener says the garden, which is the engines' side in a fence. The senior says the stakes will say, and means it. Nobody says: we will be dead before the answer, so the answer is not ours. Nobody needs to.

---

At night the census revises itself. Add the dead — in the ground at the garden fence, on the shelf in six hands, still consulted, still fondly corrected. Add, two weeks a year, the moths at the one lit window, when the census is a line longer and the line keeps landing on the glass. Add the pulses: in the four-second intervals, at two in the morning, the loudest things in the basin are four hearts in three cottages and a fuel shed, keeping time badly, phasing slowly against five iron ones, now together, now unraveling.

The basin, which is a beginning, and in no hurry, has learned to sleep to the sound of them — exactly as they sleep to the sound of it.

Put the hearts on the census. The basin already has.
