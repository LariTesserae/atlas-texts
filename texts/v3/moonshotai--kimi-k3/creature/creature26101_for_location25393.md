A census of the hold is a short document, and most of it is footnotes. Nothing here is thriving — thriving is a rate, and rates are not permitted. What lives here has been exempted, one by one, from the general refusal, and the exemptions are few enough to count without needing both hands, and old enough that counting them feels like reading the plan aloud. Five entries. Six if you count the mountain, which you should. Seven for the length of a dive.

The ray is the eldest of the exemptions, and the only one that was ever naturalized. Everything else the boundary declines; the ray, the boundary declines to decline. This was not always so. Whatever it was when it arrived — a deep-bodied animal of the living ocean, pressure-built, dark — the hold spent its first centuries here gently pushing it toward the door, as it pushes everything foreign. But a body replaces itself. Meal by slow meal, cell by cell, across a span no instrument spans, every atom in the ray cycled out through the hold's bookkeeping and was replaced by an atom the hold had already accounted for, until one unwitnessed day the boundary stopped pushing. Its citizenship is isotopic. It is made, now, entirely of local material, and the still water passes through its thirty translucent meters the way light passes through the buildings' thresholds: without comment.

Which means it cannot leave. Inside the rim, the weight of the ocean is declined; outside, three kilometers of water are still honestly leaning. The ray's tissues have been remade for the declined world — soft, clear, unhurried, built for cellar-cool and no load. The living sea would crush it in an hour. It is a deep-sea creature that has been converted, across geological patience, into a shallow-water animal, and its shallow water happens to sit on a mountaintop three kilometers down. Whether it ever tests the boundary — whether the rim-grazing that looks like grazing is also, each circuit, one long question asked of the wall — is not recorded anywhere but in the ray, and the ray keeps its records where it keeps its years: in itself, unreadable.

Its flight is the water's idea. The grain-lines at the rim run in their great slow arcs, and the ray rides them because riding them is silk and crossing them is wading, and so its patrol is less a decision than a path of least resistance flown by a body with nowhere else to be. It does not beat its wings to move. Gliding frictionless along the grain, it has almost no need of propulsion; instead it beats them the way clocks strike — to announce, not to arrive. Once per axis, perhaps: when its circuit crosses one of the spoke-lines where the streets run out to the rim, it gives one slow concussion, and the held water conducts it losslessly down the axis to whoever or whatever is listening, kilometers away, beside you whole. It is the town's only bell. It is also the town's only hand: one circuit every eleven years, ticking past the numerals of the streets, eleven being the kind of number this town prefers. If the sediment ring on the outer slope is the clock the hold keeps for the world, the ray is the clock the hold keeps without admitting it — the second hand of a place that insists it has no time.

It eats at the outbox, and the outbox is worth describing, because it is the only queue in the world. Everything the hold refuses — the motes that drift in across the boundary, the dead of the living sea sinking past the rim, a visitor's lost glove, a probe's shed foil — is gathered with that infinite, gentle patience into a slow procession hanging a body's length off the wall, in the order of arrival: the hold is patient, but it is also tidy. The queue may take months or decades to be put back outside, and in the meantime it is the only larder here. The ray browses it the way a reader browses a very slow newsstand, and it takes only what was once alive. The rest — the gloves, the instruments, the bright trash of expeditions — it passes with the same wide clearance it gives the buildings. It can taste the difference between what lived and what merely traveled. In a place with no rot, it is the only one performing the old office of the scavenger, and it performs it so rarely and so completely that almost nothing is wasted and almost nothing is gained.

Its organs burn at the town's own wavelength, polarized with the grain — a lamp that travels. To the congregations at the eaves it must look like a relative, a large slow cousin of the lights they keep, and sometimes a few combs lift and trail it for a day or two before drifting home, as if briefly persuaded that their lamp had finally gone somewhere. Whether there were ever two rays is not written. Whether it remembers the ocean its species may have already vanished from — whether, in a body the stillness tolerates precisely because it barely changes, memory persists as the one unlicensed change, the one archive in a town that keeps none — no one can say. But consider this, when your dive is done: the town will not remember you. Remembering is a kind of change, and the town declines it. The ray might. It has had eleven thousand years of nothing to forget.

The combs are the second entry, and they are less a population than a count. A few hundred individuals, distributed among the lamps in congregations that never grow and never shrink. Count them, if you have the decades: each lamp keeps a prime number, and no two lamps keep the same prime. Whether the town counts them, or whether they are how the town counts, has never been determined, and the distinction may be meaningless in a place where the script and the operating state are the same thing. They arrived or they were placed; they are grandfathered either way.

Each is a handspan of fused, comb-like plates, transparent past visibility — you find them by their effects: the slow winking of their lamp as they turn, and the torque. Hold one in a gloved palm and it aligns itself, gently and irresistibly, the way a compass needle aligns — except that its north is not north. Its north is *around*: it points along the grain, along the great slow curve that circles the center, and holding it is like holding a small argument for the curvature of the world. It does not struggle. Fear is also a rate, and nothing in the hold can afford it. Released, it drifts back to its congregation without hurry and resumes its one occupation, which is feeding on the only food there is.

The lamps are the town's pores. Nowhere else does the perfect ordering of the held water spend itself — but at each light, within a body-length of the source, the grain relaxes by a whisker, orientation leaking out of the molecules and into nothing. That leak is the town's one exhale, and the combs live on the breath. They sip the spent orientation, the decay of order back toward ordinary water, and the entire food chain of the hold is thus one link long and begins at a principle. They are the only things here that consume anything the town produces, and what they consume is the town's perfection, taxed at a rate of almost exactly zero. As they feed they turn, keeping one face to the polarized light and one to the grain, and their turning is the wink — each lamp, constant in itself, breathes through its congregation with a period of years. It is the only pulse inside the rim. The Note is constant, the lights are constant, the cool is constant; the combs are the hold's single admitted rhythm, a heartbeat slower than any heart, kept by faces turning toward cold light.

Your lamp, when you bring one, is an offense they are too patient to call an offense. Their light is pure — one wavelength, one polarization, one note. Yours is broadband, unpolarized, a glare: to them it must be what static is to a tuning fork. As you approach a lamp, the congregation rotates its faces away from you, all of them, in unhurried unison, and from two kilometers out — where distances cannot be learned by looking, only by swimming — you would see the lamp dim as you near it. Not by shadow. By refusal.

The commuter is the third entry, and the strangest, because it is the only resident whose relationship to the hold is a schedule. It is the size of a forearm, plated like a treaty between an isopod and a bathyscaphe, and its armor reads to the glove the way the buildings read — thermally indifferent, seamless, as if it had opted out of the same conversations. It may be the only structure that is also an animal. It may be issued rather than born. What is certain is that it has never been seen in motion. It is seen in *position*: at the central threshold, and later — years later — a plate's width along, and later still, gone inside. It does not move too slowly to watch. It moves too slowly to catch moving, which is a different thing, and the hold's preferred thing. Of everything resident, it is the only one whose pace exactly equals the pace of the place. The ray keeps the time. The commuter keeps the tempo.

Its errand is the one journey here that matters. The water at the central mound's mouth stands vertical and open; no probe sent down the shaft has returned anything but a silence with the texture of strain. The commuter crosses that standing wall — the only thing that ever has — and goes down into the listening dark where the Note comes up, and in some span of years it comes out again. It is the patient's only visitor, or its physician, or its confessor; at this timescale the three are difficult to tell apart. What it carries down, what it brings back, whether it continues outward along the great axis all the way to the rim — whether, once in some span of years, the two eldest residents meet at the boundary, the hand of the clock and the messenger, wing to shell, in the only appointment the town keeps — has never been witnessed. The entire social calendar of the hold, and no one has ever been present for it. And the commuter, asked nothing, says nothing. Saying is a kind of change.

Then there is the sixth entry, which was here first. The mountain was mid-gesture when the town arrived, and everything else — the lamps, the grain, the combs' primes, the ray's eleven-year hand, the commuter's descending rounds — is arrangement around that fact. Is it alive? The census can defend only one definition: it is the one that everything else attends. The streets converge on it like attention. The lamps stand around it the way night-lights stand around a sleeper. The stillness of the hold, which elsewhere reads as perfection, reads at the center as listening. And the Note — slow any sound far enough and it flattens into a single constant tone, pitch being only a rate, and rates here are stretched past recognition. The Note is not a hum. It is a syllable: the first instant of the mountain's one word, drawn out across the whole length of the pause. The mountain is not silent. The mountain is speaking very slowly, and the entire town — the declined pressure, the declined heat, the declined aging, the cool and the grain and the clean young floor — is the cost of keeping it at its first syllable, forever, one millimeter of amplitude, the loudest thing in the world held perfectly quiet like a hand on a shoulder. Whether the hold is a restraint or a hospice is not written in any script you can read. The commuter could tell you. The commuter is not saying.

Of the builders, the census can say only this: a sentence that is still being said implies a sayer who is still, in the only way measurable here, present. No one has found their bodies, their names, or their tools — unless the town is all three: a body that does one thing, a name written once in a courtyard in standing water, a hand laid on a shoulder and never lifted. They live here the way intention lives in a held gesture — entirely, and nowhere you can point. Perhaps they are at the other end of the export, in the somewhere that is marginally warmer than it should be, tending the far side of their own refusal. Perhaps the saying long outlasted them. Either way the saying continues, and a saying that does work is not a relic. It is a resident.

And then, for the length of a dive, there is you — the seventh entry and the only footnote that walks around. While you are here you are the fastest metabolism in the hold, its loudest sound, its only uncertainty, the only thing present that will certainly end. Your body heat is declined as you make it; you cannot warm even the water of your own wake. Your exhaled breath leaves you and begins its long deportation, grain-wise, rising at a pace that is not rising; come back in thirty years and some of it is still in transit, queued politely at the wall, your oldest breath still being seen to the door. The combs turn their faces from your light. The commuter does not pause — pausing is its ground state; it cannot pause harder. The ray, on its far circuit, banks one degree off the grain-line as you pass beneath it, which is the only welcome the town has ever offered anyone, and which you will mistake, if you notice it at all, for current.

Count again when you leave, and the census is unchanged: one patient, one sentence still being said, one messenger mid-errand, one hand of a clock, a few hundred faces at the pores, and — if the outbox is honest — a little of you, waiting at the wall to be put gently back outside. The count has been the same since the sediment ring was a line on bare stone. It will be the same tomorrow, though tomorrow, here, is a rumor the stillness has not confirmed. The ray completes another degree. The combs turn. The commuter advances the width of a plate. The mountain holds its syllable, and everything that lives here is part of the holding — which is to say, everything that lives here is the saying of one thing, and the rest is attendance.
