*Nothing here was hired. Everyone here works.*

**The Staff**

There are no locals. There is, however, a full staff.

To live in the Kettle is to keep at least two addresses — a feeding floor and a sleeping floor — and to commute between them on a schedule older than your species. The animals here are not tame. They are something stranger: punctual. Their appetites have been synchronized to the building's day — the staggered figs, the two hours of sun, the evening hatch, the storm's standing appointment — until the line between a creature and its shift has worn thin. Elsewhere, animals hold territories. Here they hold shifts, and the shifts hold them.

There is no one to name them, so take the names the way the valley would assign them: by trade. The Kettle is not sentimental about job titles.

**The Condenser Crew**

The rim keeps the early shift.

At first light the tussock tips are stood upon by fog beetles, black as punctuation, rumps raised to the wind off the canyon. The mist beads on their backs and runs down to their mouths. Each beetle drinks its own small sky. The condenser has condensers, and they are the same shape as the place.

The giant lobelias hold a cup of water at their hearts, and the cups are stocked. In each one, a rustle of seed-shrimp, copper-colored, no bigger than rice. Their eggs arrived as weather — up the Stack from the Broth, lofted with the steam, rained out onto the cold rim. The water clock starts here, and it starts already carrying passengers.

A small dark nectar-bird works the lobelia spikes before the sun clears the wall, frost on its crown feathers, paid in nectar and water both. And the groundsels' lagging — the trunks wrapped in their own dead leaves — is housing. Between the dead leaves and the bark live a mouse and a quiet guild of beetles, tenants in the insulation of the heating pipes, kept warm all night by the plant's thrift.

Mid-morning, when the mist sea stirs and begins to rise in columns, the hawks clock in. They find the columns first and ride them without a wingbeat, inspecting. The elevators open at ten. The hawks are always waiting when they do.

**The Bench Hands**

The organ pipes are occupied. Between the basalt columns, in the joints where cooling's geometry left slots, lives a lizard flat as a bookmark. It works the Lighting: when the sun reaches the benches it slides out onto the lip, spreads itself, takes two hours of the only direct light the walls ever get, and files itself away again. The only right angles in the Kettle have a tenant shaped to match them.

Behind the falls, two trades share one curtain wall. The swifts hold the wing trade — out at dawn through the water, dry to wet to dry, skimming the mist sea to drink on the fly, then up at dusk to work the hatch when the mayflies rise off the warm river in columns. The other trade belongs to the glow-worms, who bead the wet rock beside the nests with silk snares hung in the spray, each line dotted with glue, each larva lit at the tail. The swift takes the fliers. The snare takes the drifters. One address, two shifts, no disputes on record.

The bench goats descend during the Lighting to lick the mineral seeps — small, slate-grey, rubber-hooved, moving down routes no hand ever cut but that generations of hooves have polished to a shine. The kids learn the timetable before they are weaned. They are the day shift of the salt economy: in a valley where the rain leaches everything, salt comes up the cliffs on four hooves or not at all. And when the slot canyons sound their note before the storm — the hum too low for the ear — the goats are already climbing. The rain finds them above the waterline.

The cat keeps the goats' schedule from the shadow side. It has no name; there is no one to give it one. Smoke-colored, small, patient. You will not see the cat. You will see the goats keeping the cat's hours.

**The Weep Tenantry**

Below the dew-point contour, the rule is that everything wet is occupied — and that includes the occupants.

The epiphyte mats are tenements. A single mat, thicker than the branch that holds it, houses a thumbnail crab that has never once touched the ground, a frog no bigger than the crab, three kinds of beetle, and a centipede the length of a finger that is, by default, the landlord.

The slow folivore hangs in the fern curtains and goes about on a schedule so unhurried it counts as architecture. Its fur grows algae. Moths live in the pelt. Once a week it descends one particular liana to one particular root, deposits its dung — the moths riding down with it to lay their eggs — and climbs back into the mist. The slow freight run. Weekly. Never late.

The dripfrogs keep stranger hours. Each male holds a territory of one drip — one overhang, one interval — and sets his call to it, calling in the gap just after his drip's tick, so that his voice and his roof keep time together. At night the whole Weep is a slow switchboard: one call, and then, a long time later, an answer from across the canyon. Business is conducted at the speed of patience.

The monkeys run the fig calendar. The figs fruit on staggered schedules all year, and the troop knows the stagger — which tree, which week, which liana corridor gets there fastest. The troop's memory is the canteen menu, and it is never wrong by more than a day.

On the wet wall beside the falls, something is coming up: the climbing fish, thumb-long, inching up the glazed rock on fins fused into a sucker, grazing the algal paint as it goes. Everything else in the Weep is moving down. It is the only freight on the up line. More on it at the Broth.

And on the walls works the groundskeeper: a broad, flat snail mowing the algal film in slow swathes, on a route a month long, its trail a tended lawn behind it. Nothing else in the Kettle leaves anything looking so *maintained*.

**The Staff of the Works**

The side canyon of hot water has the oldest staff of all.

The senior partners are the mats. Rust-red at the heads, where the water comes out at eighty degrees; emerald a step down; chalk white below that; amber where the sulfur comes in. Each color a guild, each guild taking its cut of the chemistry and passing the water on. They were working before the walls finished standing around them.

Below them the line is staffed in stages, by temperature, the way the pools are staged. In the algal paint at fifty degrees graze the larvae of the hot fly, which lives its entire adult life in a single morning. A small black snail rasps the forty-degree steps. A beetle patrols the pools that would blister a hand. At the amber line, a pale crab picks over what the upper shifts have left. The water changes hands all the way down the staircase. Every hand is attached to an appetite.

The spider of the vents has learned the building's pulse. It strings its web across the steam line and keeps its appointments by the breath: mending while the breath is held, striking on the release, when the pulse flings stunned flies against the silk. It has never once been caught out by the rhythm. Nothing here has.

The stair bird works the pools on scaled feet, bobbing at rims that would scald anything else, picking flies off the foam lids in the lull between bulge and settle.

And in the warm travertine at the stair's foot, the egg-burier keeps her nursery. She tests the ground with the heat-sense of her tongue, digs where the sand stands at blood-warmth plus a degree, lays, covers, and walks away. She does not sit on her eggs. The building does. The chicks hatch feathered and ready, dig themselves up out of the warm floor, and clock in — the only crew that arrives fully trained.

**The Floor Hands**

The crocodiles keep to the cool inflows, but they know the whole river by temperature the way you know your own house in the dark. The old bulls carry a rime of travertine on their scutes — mineral stripes for long service. When the slots hum before the storm, the bulls answer from the inflows with a bellow too low to hear; you know it by the water standing up in shivering fur above their backs. They are the customs house of the lower river. Whatever drifts down past the inflows is inspected once, briefly.

The herons wade the hot shallows on thick-scaled legs, unhurried, fishing pools that would scald anything else. They can afford the patience; they have no competition. Every heron learns the river's thermal map the same way — once, young, by being burned — and never misreads it again.

At dusk the wired river comes on shift. The electric fish work the warm margins, each one spelling itself in small, precise pulses, and the shallows fill with careful sentences. To wade in is to be read aloud. The jungle drinks from the edges with a certain care, and the care is correct.

The canteen never closes because the kitchen never misses a delivery. The fig wasps — small enough to be overlooked, indispensable enough to close the place — emerge on time inside each ripening fig and fly the staggered schedule, tree to tree, week to week, carrying the pollen that keeps the whole valley in fruit. The bats and monkeys and birds are the customers. The wasps are the reason there are customers.

The fruit bats run the valley's best logistics: up from the floor at dusk, four hundred vertical meters, climbing empty; down again at first light, gliding full and silent over the mist, letting the gradient do the hauling. Up light, down loaded. The building approves.

The cicadas are the short shift's siren. They spend their youth counting in the dark and their adult lives counting to two — two hours of sun-slot, two hours of drone felt in the sternum more than heard. When the drone cuts, the floor takes note: one hour to the storm. Their silence is the most useful sound on the floor.

And when the storm punches in, the storm shift hires daily: toads that spend the dry hours shut tight as valves in the litter open with the first warm drops, call, breed in the thousand temporary rivers, and leave their tadpoles racing the evaporation on a deadline measured in days. A plain brown bird follows the ant columns through the litter, eating whatever flushes. It keeps the ants' schedule. The ants keep the floor's.

**Export**

The Broth has a shipping department, though nothing it ships ever booked passage.

At the salt rim, brine flies move in black lace along the waterline, and a pale wader picks through them step by step. That is the local traffic. The long-haul freight goes up the Stack.

The steaming shallows are full of small, patient things — resting eggs, cysts, spores, algal dust — and the plume lifts them all day. What enters the Broth becomes weather, and the weather is not empty. Some of it rains out on the rim, back up the wind, into the cups of the giant lobelias, where the seed-shrimp hatch and begin their small rustling again. The condenser's tenants are the lake's grandchildren. The water clock carries passengers in both directions.

There is exactly one return ticket, and the climbing fish holds it. Born in the river, washed down to the Broth as larvae, it grows a season in the warm shallows and then comes back — out of the terminal lake, up the braids, up the wet margins of the falls, through four hundred meters of Weep, on a sucker made of fins. *What reaches the Broth does not come back, except as weather.* The fish is the exception the valley permits — perhaps because it comes back improved, returning as food for herons after being raised by the lake.

The vultures ride the Stack's thermal all day, the auditors of the whole operation. Nothing stays on the books long at these temperatures. Sanitation is quick, and thorough, and has the best view in the valley.

**The Night Watch**

When the basalt benches begin their slow ticking, the gecko comes out on the organ pipes to lick the night's condensate off the cooling stone, and to take the moths that settle on it for the warmth. The benches tick. The gecko keeps the minutes.

The moths it eats are the night shift of the salt economy. While the goats walk four hundred meters to lick the seeps by day, the tear moths work the dark — drinking from the eyes of sleeping herons, sleeping crocodiles, sleeping goats bedded on the benches, collecting drop by drop what the day shift climbed for. Salt is never free in the Kettle. It is only transferred.

Over the river, the fishing bats trawl the margins where the electric sentences are thickest. The dripfrogs hold their intervals: one call, and a long time later, an answer. The mist refills past the benches. The vents breathe on alone.

And below all of it — beneath the Works, in the hot dark of the deep water — works the crew no one has ever seen. Their reports arrive at the surface as color: rust, emerald, chalk, amber. As the breath of the vents. As the heat the whole valley is stacked around. They were there before the canyon was cut, before the plateau finished rising. The river did not discover them so much as grow down toward them. The Kettle did not hire them; it grew around them, and it has been running their shift ever since.

**The Terms of Tenancy**

Everything has a floor. Most things have two.

Everything works a shift. Nothing works two.

Below the Weep, everything is occupied — including the occupants.

Salt is never given. It is transferred.

The dead are processed the same day. Nothing stays on the books.

The schedule is not taught. It is inherited — learned once: by a burn, by a missed hatch, by a storm read correctly.

Nothing here is tame. Everything is on time.

---

Stand on a bench at dusk, when the shifts change — the swifts coming in through their falls, the bats rising past them the other way, the stone beginning to tick, the frogs finding their intervals — and you will understand that the strangest thing about the Kettle is not that it runs. It is that it is *staffed*. No hand cut a step. No one draws a wage. And there has never once been a vacancy.

Nothing here was hired. Everyone here works — and no one, not even the oldest crew in the hot dark below, has ever been late.
