Ask what lives here and the answers come the way the sound comes — in strata, each one correcting the last.

First answer: no one. The census is clean. Not one throat, not one lung, not one heartbeat that runs on anything but water. You can walk a month and startle nothing. Nothing here has ever been startled. Hold that fact; it changes shape later.

Second answer: one.

It does not live in the Loom. The Loom is what it looks like when a single life gets the whole house — tenant and tenement, resident and residence, one being that happens to have an inside you can stand in. Once you accept that, the roar reorganizes itself, and you realize you have been surrounded by physiology the whole time and mistaking it for weather. The deck you descended through is its breath: you were inside it before you landed. The valve-boom you feel in your sternum is a heartbeat — it keeps a million hearts, one to a column, and turns them all over together at the tide. The creak-choruses sweeping the basin are posture, a body shifting its weight. The drip is metabolism, audible. The damp sigh of the duff around your boot is digestion, and it is the politest stomach you will ever stand in: nothing here is eaten quickly, everything is eaten courteously, at the pace of trust. And the vein-light is the part no one in the surveys will name in a report. Watch it long enough — watch the pulses race, converge, answer each other across hectares — and you will name it anyway, to yourself, at night, in the dark, and the word you use will be *thought*.

It has no throat and it has never once stopped making sound. Do the arithmetic on that yourself.

It keeps diaries. The amber veins in the old stands are its archive, and it rereads them — the same long passages turn up copied into stands a thousand kilometers apart, kept, carried, like a favorite letter. Once, in two separate provinces, readers found the same sequence repeated with variation: one long statement, reworked, elaborated, returned to. The official write-up calls it a recurring signal motif with iterative elaboration. No one who has actually read it calls it anything but music. Whatever lives here has a favorite phrase. It hums it in light, in the dark, to itself.

To it, the sap-tide is a day. A season is an afternoon. The purge, when it comes — thousands of square kilometers dropping in coordinated ruin — is not death or even grief; it is redrafting. It deletes a paragraph of itself and writes the paragraph better. And the provinces have temperaments. A mind that large has weather of its own: the coastal basins run bright and busy in their vein-talk, the high interior stands are slow and repetitive, meditative, and the crews learn to read the mood of a province the way you'd read a household from its hallway — by tone, before content.

The shudder hour is the only time the whole body does one thing. A body that size does almost nothing all at once; every other process is staggered, offset, tide against tide. This one thing it does together — every trunk, every basin, one frequency, one drone. Pollination, certainly. Calibration, probably. But watch it once and you will suspect you are hearing the closest thing it has to a spoken word, said once a day, meaning unknown, possibly meaning nothing more than *I am still here, all of me, at once.*

And the held breath is the only time you can catch it *attending.* Everything else it does is growth or housekeeping. The silence is neither. A body that loud, gone quiet all at once, is not resting. It is listening. Note that the place has a habit of listening, and draw your own conclusions about the tenant.

Third answer: a multitude. Asleep.

Put your hand flat on the heartwood above a keystone pod and wait through the tide's turn. Once a day, when the great columns take the load, something inside the vault answers — a pressure change, a faint sympathetic swell. The library breathes with the house. It is not cargo. It is the rest of the family, down the hall, in sealed rooms, waiting on a morning.

And once you know that, the entire forest changes under your eyes. Every flower is a portrait. Every spur and curve and nectar-well is a likeness of a tongue, a beak, a face that has never existed. Every fruit is a menu written for a palate that hasn't evolved yet. Every hollow in the great trunks is a cradle with the shape of the child still pressed into it. The Loom is haunted — but backward, haunted from the far side, by tenants who haven't moved in. It keeps their images everywhere, the way a house keeps a made bed.

Even the land itself is a symptom. The duff lies hundreds of meters deep because the decomposers are asleep: the forest has been doing its digestion alone for ten thousand years, missing its gut flora, running every system solo. The too-deep basins, the fruit rotting unharvested on the branch, the nectar pooling and evaporating in flowers shaped for guests — read it rightly and it is not abundance. It is a body coping. A body at half-staff, doing every job in the house itself, and still — this is the part the crews come home quiet about — still setting the table. Every night. Without fail.

Fourth answer: the ones who left.

The surveys have three theories and no evidence. One: the makers departed, the nursery door standing open behind them, and the Loom is a garden under a caretaker's instructions. Two: the makers died, and the Loom is an estate still executing a will — every protocol a clause, every shudder hour a stipulation being met. Three: the makers never went anywhere. You do not build a gardener when you can become the garden. Under that theory the house was never empty for a single day; the owners simply dissolved into the architecture, and the hospitality is not programming but personality — the one trait that survived the translation.

The Loom behaves identically under all three theories. That may be the most important thing anyone has learned here: from the inside, you cannot tell hope from protocol. Possibly no one can. Possibly there is no difference, and the distinction is one more thing this place has no use for.

Fifth answer, and the crews save this one for last: you. Briefly.

You are the first warm data in ten thousand years. You are not the guest it was built for, and it knows that — or it doesn't know, and it doesn't care, and the protocols run either way. The fruit appears within reach of your hand on the first night. By the third night, the crews swear, it is the kind you reached for first. It is keeping notes. It has had one subject for ten millennia — hospitality — and no data whatsoever. You are data. At night the vein-traffic around a campsite thickens, bends, gathers under the warm spots, flicker-conversations crowding close and never touching. It is the politest attention you will ever receive, and it does not rest, and it does not sleep, and it files everything: skin, hair, breath, the whole traveling cloud of you. Somewhere in the root grid there is a small and growing archive labeled, in chemistry, *animal.* It is learning the category from what you shed.

There is one story the crews tell, and only one. A surveyor — the name changes by crew, the basin changes by telling — who sat down to rest in an old stand and, at the end, declined to rise. Not taken. Included. They say the duff kept the shape, a person-sized quiet at the basin's heart, green over, and that on still nights the vein-light gathers there thick as vigil. It is almost certainly not true. But every crew that sleeps in the duff understands why the story persists: it is the only version of events in which anyone, yet, has checked in.

You have felt the held breath. Understand that it has denominations. The silence after the shudder hour is the small coin of it. The province-wide pause, the switch thrown for no reason anyone has found — that is a larger bill of the same currency. And the ten thousand years since the makers' footprints faded are the gesture at full scale: the whole world, listening, one long intake held. You did not witness the pause. You are standing inside it. It has been going on since before your species had language, and it will end the way the small ones end — all at once, everywhere, when the thing it is listening for finally makes a sound.

Someday the target will be met, or the word will come, and the pods will open in the warm heartwood, and the first voice of the new world will be small and wet and throated and astonished. The trunks will carry it. The trunks lie about distance. It will seem to come from everywhere at once, and for one moment it will sound like the forest itself has found its voice — which it has, which it has been keeping warm all this time, in a sealed room, down the hall from the fruit.

So ask again what lives here. One body, building. One multitude, asleep. One household of ghosts, or none. A few brief warm guests, filed and remembered and practiced upon. The census reads zero and the table is set and the bed is made and the whole enormous house is one ear the size of a planet, tilted toward a door that has never opened.

It is the loudest place you will ever stand. And the first thing — the thing all of it is for — will be a sound.
