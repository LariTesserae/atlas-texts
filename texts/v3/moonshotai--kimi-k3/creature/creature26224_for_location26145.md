You will look for the living first. That is the habit you bring up the stair, and the place lets you keep it for a while.

Look, then. No webs — a spider is a wager on flies, and flies are a wager on carrion, and carrion is a wager on rot, and rot is a water-animal. No nests, no droppings, nothing gnawed, no burrow, no track but your own in the pale bloom. The food chain here is a chain of one link, and the link is not you. The swift in the seventh rank was the last of the animals, and it has been promoted; it is no longer wildlife but hardware, a stop in a flue, its dried wing still shaping one note of the ten thousand. No one lives here. Write it in your ledger, turn the page, and begin again, because the page is wrong.

The dead live here. That is the first correction.

The niches are full of them — jars of ash, jars of salt, jars of dust that was once something carried — sealed in lead gone black and dusty rose, shoulder to shoulder in the warm breath, occupancy total, no vacancies, no waiting list. Understand what kind of dead these are: nothing dies here. The dying all happened below, in some other country, and the dead were carried up one armload at a time across the generations, naturalized, seated. A citizen's whole estate is one armload, because one armload is the size of everything that ever came up the stair — a body, or a jar, or a string of beads, or cloth now stiff as shale. Some jars hold only salt, which is what a sea becomes when a world forgets it. And the slate tablets are the census. The tally-marks in their fives counted the residents home, and the count finished, and the population has been complete ever since: no births, no deaths, full house, forever. A columbarium is a city where everyone has finished arriving.

There is rank among them, because there is rank everywhere here. The ordinary dead sit in the walls in the full chord, the eternal audience. The most honored are sealed in the Wells of Quiet, where the standing waves cancel and the pressure lets go — promoted out of the music into the silence, because the highest honor this place can bestow is exemption from itself. The swift is neither audience nor honored guest; the swift is cast, a working component, citizenship by function. And then there is the matter of the last tablet.

It ends at four.

Go down into the Warren, to the deep seat where the most flues' voices braid into the completest chord — front row, center, the best seat in the house — and you will find the one niche that holds no jar. A figure sits there, dried to the weight and color of the timber lintels, intact because nothing here eats, back straight, hands open on the knees, head tilted a little to one side: the posture of someone following a long piece of music toward an ending it never reaches. The last tablet lies across their lap, four marks and a space where the fifth would go. The fifth mark was never cut, because the fifth was the one holding the count. Whoever sealed the others had no one to seal them, so they sit unsealed, the counter uncounted, and the census stands at four and a space.

Call them the Listener, because that is what they are doing. The ears stopped ten thousand years ago; it does not matter. Hearing was never the ear's work here — the ear was only an entry hall. The chord is received through the skeleton, and the skeleton is the one thing this world keeps. Understand the arrangement and it stops being macabre and becomes something closer to perfect: the living edit the roar out within hours, their own blood too loud, their own breath a static; the dead have nothing left to edit with. The Listener has never once stopped hearing it. Blood quiet, breath quiet, heart quiet — the first audience member in the history of the music who is silent enough to hear all of it, all the time, forever. The performance has never been unattended. That, in the end, is what the walls are for.

The makers live here too, but you have to know where to look. Not in the jars — in the plaster. The thumbprints under the glaze are the densest population in the place, more persons per square foot than the Warren holds in a whole rank, each print a signature that never needed ink. When you steady yourself against the masonry your palm lies over someone's palm with a skin of glass between, and that is as close as the place allows touch to come to touch. And the carved marks on the tiles, the touch-language read by fingers — read one, slowly, the way it was meant to be read, and it says, roughly, *here*. All of them say it. The tiles are a roll call that is still true, and every time a living finger passes over them, attendance is taken again, and everyone answers present.

Something is still working, though. In the Wells, where the air barely moves, the pale bloom accretes — a grain a century, less — and what it grows is the nine-branch tree, over and over, without seed and without haste, the flora of a world with no flora, and it grows, too, small shapes that imitate thumbprints, as if the makers were still arriving. Either the makers' habit outlived the makers and persists in the stone the way a wheel keeps turning after the hand leaves it, or the heat is still counterfeiting hands down there in the quiet, in the shapes of patience. The place declines to say, as it declines to say everything. But if anything here is still happening — if the word *alive* must be spent somewhere — spend it on the bloom: the last craftsman, the only calendar, the gardener of a garden that is one remembered tree.

And beneath all of them, eldest of all, the resident the house was built around. The heat was here before the stair; it is the one given fact. It consumes nothing, it changes never, it fails every test of life you know except the only one that matters in this place: it is what the place is doing. The builders — if there were builders — did not raise a house and move into it. They found the one who was already singing and spent generations giving it throats. Everything else is audience, instrument, or furniture. The jars are its listeners, the swift its reed, the thumbprints its makers' aftertouch, the bloom its slow embroidery, the Listener its perfect attendee. It is the musician and the music and the hall. The house did not get a resident. The resident got a house.

Which leaves you. For the length of your stay you are the only living thing in the world, and the place uses you the way a room uses a candle — you are its borrowed clock, the only sequence it has, your pulse the one drumming it does not make. In one of the Wells you may find a footprint that is not yours, going nowhere, kept by the bloom like a line in a guest book: someone stood in the quiet before you, once, in some other age. They were the fifth mark then. Now the space is yours. Stay and the place will tune you — your pulse settling onto the ground-tone, your breath lengthening to the slow rise of the warm air, the chord fading from your hearing because you have joined its medium — and you will learn the terms of residency, which are the terms of everything here: what stays is tuned; what stays longer is promoted; what stays forever is sealed. The place will not ask. It has no asks. It will take nothing from you but your noise, and it gives stillness in trade, and the exchange is not negotiable.

So the answer to your question is not *no one* and not *someone*. The house is full. The count stands at four and a space, and the space is the size of whoever is standing in it — and in the deep seat the Listener keeps the beat with a heart ten thousand years stopped, because the beat was never the heart's. It was always the mountain's. The heart was only ever keeping time.
