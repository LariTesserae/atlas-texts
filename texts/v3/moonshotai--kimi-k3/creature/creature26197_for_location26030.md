Begin with the census, and the census fails immediately, because first you must decide what counts as alive, and the Seethe will not help you decide. Everything here that lives, lives as an answer to a single question. The plain digests the quick and the finished; permanence is only digestion slowed; so how do you stay? Every inhabitant is a different answer. Slowness is the only citizenship the world recognizes. What follows are the visas.

The first answer: be slower than the appetite. The walkers, then, are the world's only full citizens. No one is left to name them, so they are known by their silhouettes, the way mountains were once known — the Broken Stair; the Long Even One; the One That Carries the Ring, oldest of all, whose forest has a ring of foundation stones fused into it like an inherited fence. A few dozen of them, worldwide, filtering the froth, wading at the speed of weather on stone — no, slower than weather: the storms herd around them and are outlasted by them. The ground-tone you feel in your teeth is, in part, the walkers speaking. A low note carries through the slurry farther than through air, and so the whole scattered population is one conversation that has not paused in centuries — a few dozen voices, a sentence a season, never together, never entirely alone. In the canyons of their cracked hides live small translucent things, kin to the gardeners, who have never once touched the ground and would not know what it was. And on their backs the forests go on carrying everything else: the last true trees, and with the trees the last of the birds, each walker a different ark with a different cargo. When two walkers pass within earshot — an event decades in the arranging — the two forests call across the gap, and sometimes a bird makes the crossing. This is the only migration left, and it is enough. Afterward the plain rehearses the meeting for years, two domes leaning together out of the slurry, over and over, in provinces the walkers themselves have forgotten. Once in an age the churn finishes a calf: buds stacking into the almost-shape and not slumping, standing instead, bare-plated, blinking its dim red eyes. It will follow the nearest elder for a century, learning the routes. The plain manages *almost* very well; it has been trying to finish a walker for a long time. And walkers do not die so much as settle — a kneeling that takes a century, a rising that takes another, until what is left is a swell of ground the maps would call bedrock. The glass reeds find these rises. The organ note they sing, say the travelers, is the walker still speaking, slowed past hearing into pitch. Whether the world's last hill is stone or a settler, the plain declines to decide. There is also a silhouette that appears only beyond the second horizon — the Far One — which does not approach and does not recede and is, by the rules that obtain out there, consistent with nothing. Possibly the first walker. Possibly the last. It keeps its own counsel, which is to say it keeps the counsel of the line it walks beyond.

The second answer: be brief. There is life that exists only while it is raining — a species whose whole span is the fall of a single drop, condensing inside it, living, breeding, dying before impact, for whom the drop's descent is not a second but a season. Whole civilizations of the rain-born rise and close inside one storm, and the storm, which is centuries old, experiences them the way you experience a thought. There are tenants of single domes, riding one rise-and-slump like a house falling upward. And the churn turns up more than seeds: sometimes it is an animal from a harder era, sealed in the sediment like a grain, and it gets its hour the way the hour-trees get theirs. Once, in living memory — walker's memory — the plain gave up a bird. It spent its hour flying, because that is what it was for, and it sang a song from before the sun swelled, and for the length of that song the drumming faltered, the buds leaned, the storms stilled along their itineraries, because sound shapes matter here and this sound was an ancestor of all the others, and the Seethe keeps what happens to it, and it *recognized*. Understand: the brief are not tragic. To the rain-born the Seethe is geological, the sunset eternal, the walkers a kind of slow weather. They are not dying young. They are living exactly on time.

The third answer: be edgeless. To be eaten you must first be a shape, so the largest guild of all has declined to be one. You will never see an edgeless animal. You will infer it — slurry flowing gently up-current, a warmth against the shin, the buds of a whole district leaning one way like grass in a wind that is not blowing. Some are the size of your hand. Some may be the size of provinces, a contraction crossing a hundred kilometers over a year, and the walkers step around these, which is how anyone knows they exist. The float-mats are their visible cousins — or their visible portion. Ask whether the mats are many plants or one, and the place declines to decide: mats that meet merge, mats that tear go on, and every mat may be a cutting of a single original, which would make the commonest plant also the largest living thing — one organism the size of weather, indifferent, feeding on the slurry directly, thinking, if it thinks, in currents.

The fourth answer: be an event. Some life here never had a body at all. There are events with itineraries — a particular notch-shaped wave that has run the same circuit for millennia, a spiral of froth that migrates against the current — and standing inside one, you can feel its mood the way you feel a room's. The scrum of the plain is not only physics; it is an ecology, events eating events, one recurring form overwriting another, predation without teeth because nothing has teeth because nothing, strictly, is there. Loud places bud faster, and so the storms farm the churn beneath them like herdsmen, and the densest life of all lives under a storm's skirt. The colonnade is the largest event anyone watches, and it is maturing — each day more resolved — and it has an audience: smaller events gather at the dim hour of its building the way, elsewhere, things gather at water. And near the place where the plain once, memorably, budded almost a face, the buds have never quite stopped trying, each attempt a little less almost. Say the travelers: the face it rehearses is the last one that bent close and looked at it for a long time. The plain keeps what happens to it. Being seen is a thing that happens to it.

The fifth answer: be preserved. The sediment is full of sleepers waiting for their hour, and the bubbles are full of weather waiting for its quiet hole. Mostly a popped bubble gives back only its storm. Sometimes more comes out — a creature sealed along with the air of a lost afternoon, released later, *changed*, per the rule: translucent, stretched, remembering a dry world, lasting a day, asking nothing. Occasionally a bubble holds only a voice, and the voice is mid-sentence, and it is always, somehow, an ordinary sentence, which is the worst kind. And in the quiet holes themselves live the hushed — flat as pressed blankets, patient past patience — gathering at the edge of your footprint's silence to drink old sound. Whether they find the ancient storms or bring them is not known. Either way: if you stand very still in a quiet hole and feel something listening, it is not the past. The past does not listen. Something else is doing that.

The sixth answer: live in the sound. In the Seethe, sound is food, shelter, and weather, and most of what lives here lives in it the way elsewhere things live in water. The roar is not a byproduct of the churn but one of its organs, and the organ is grazed. Echoes are hunted — that is the whole explanation for their behavior. An echo arrives late because something caught it; early, because something flushed it; sometimes it does not arrive at all. You will never see an echo-eater, but you have already been inside a hunt: your own footstep arriving a breath early, politely, so you will know where to put it — that is what the inside of a hunt feels like. The glass reeds eat the roar and exhale pitch, and since they root only on the settled, the world's chord is partly its dead, still talking. And the storms are the oldest citizens of the sound, and it costs nothing to treat them as persons and it explains everything. There is the storm that has followed the Long Even One for two centuries without once catching it, out of what everyone agrees to call love. There is the storm that will not cross the causeway; it rains along one side and broods. There are two storms that met once, merged, and have been separating for fifty years, bitterly, each keeping some of the other's thunder, addressing each other across whole provinces in stolen voices. Storms converse by trading bubbles. A storm's voice is every storm it has ever eaten.

Then there are the almost-persons. Stand too long at still water and it may keep your reflection, and the kept go about on the undersides of standing water, walking elsewhere, fading across years. A few are very old. There is a pool in a ring of foundation stones, still beyond believing, that keeps the reflection of the sun when the sun was small and yellow — the only yellow left in the world — and the kept gather to it the way the audience gathers to the colonnade, the way anything here gathers to anything that has not changed. Occasionally a kept thing goes looking for its original. Travelers have been met on the causeway by themselves, coming the other way, a breath out of step, wanting — what? To compare notes. To trade. To warn. It is hard to tell what a kept thing wants. They have been given back changed.

And the gardeners, who are the closest thing here to a people. Nobody planted them — that is the official position — but their eggs ride the walkers' legs, each footfall an inoculation, and so every footprint is a slightly different idea of what a gardener is. Sometimes a print holds one gardener for all its days, a whole world with a single citizen; sometimes, later, there are more, and no one has watched closely enough to say where they come from. A print lasts days, and that is time enough for a cosmology: the world is a sequence of worlds, each pressed down from above by a god with a route. When one stride falls across an older stride and two prints merge, two universes make contact — there are wars, there are weddings, there are, say the bubbles, treaties. The fruit of the hour-trees falls into the prints and is eaten, and the seeds go down into the sediment to wait another age, and nothing leaves. If a walker has ever adjusted its stride by a hand's breadth to set a print in kinder ground, no one quick enough to prove it was watching. But the plain keeps what happens to it, and lately — by plain standards — some of the gardens have begun to include arches.

So: the census. A few dozen walkers; the last forests and the last birds upon them; gardens beyond counting, each doomed; the brief, the edgeless, the events, the kept, the hushed, the storm-persons; the sleepers in the sediment; the mat, which may be one; and beneath all of it the churn, which may be one also, which may be thinking all of this — the buds its unfinished thoughts, the colonnade the one thought it refuses to release, eleven arches a day, closer each dim hour. If it ever finishes, it will become bone, and the world will have remembered how to hold itself up. Count it uncountable, or count it as one. The Seethe declines to decide, and deciding is a kind of finishing, and nothing finishes here.

And know that the census now includes you. You have stood here, and so it has you: your footfall has pressed a world into the ground that is already being gardened; the water has your face and is walking it elsewhere; a bubble somewhere has the sound of you, kept for later. The plain keeps what happens to it. You have happened to it. And some dim evening — and it is always some dim evening — the slurry will swell beside the causeway and rehearse the shape of a figure standing at the edge of the only dry road in the world, looking down: a little more resolved than yesterday, a little more certain of itself.
