Ask what lives here and the answer depends on the magnification. One. None you could shake hands with. Multitudes. All three answers are true.

The one is the plant — the only citizen in the way a person is a citizen: a single body, a single unbroken act of being alive, continuous for longer than the knot-records reach. Its decisions take decades, because a decision at this tempo is a redirection of growth. When a column failed in the fourth hall two thousand years ago, the organism weighed the matter, chose a buttress, and began; the buttress is now one-third grown. Its rings do not record seasons — there are none here — they record weather. Each lamination is a pressure-year, some storm that crossed the pan centuries overhead and pressed its thumb once on the throat. The plant is a barograph that writes in wood; its autobiography is meteorological. And the drone is its proprioception, the way it feels its own shape: every hall returns the note with a signature, and a hall that answers wrong is a hall where something has shifted, and the organism leans its slow attention that way for the next fifty years. The flared listening-trumpets the gardeners trained it to grow, it still grows. It listens to itself through its own ears — a self-examination conducted continuously, at the speed of sound.

Near the throat, where the grey comes down the rubble in rungs, the plant has grown its eyelids: louvered flanges, a maze of bone-white turns shaped to pass air and refuse photons — a darkroom's door-maze, tended and re-tended, pruned back into function every year. The breath comes through it; the light ends in it, all at once, on the far side of the last turning. The one law is not obeyed down here. It is enforced. It is grown. The law is older than the plant — it arrived with the sealing, with the first overgrowing of the sky — and everything the organism is, it is in compliance. The plant did not write the law. The law wrote the plant, and then the plant took over the writing.

The gardeners had a name for it, of course, but names here are knots, and the name-cord is grown into a wall of the third hall. Every year the new wood tightens it by a fraction, so the name is being pulled slowly out of pronunciation — sharpened or slurred by less than a millimeter in eleven thousand years, which is nothing, which is everything, given time. No one survives who could read the difference. The place's true name is drifting into a dialect of one.

Within the one, the old many persist as accents. District to district, the tissue still remembers being separate species: here a wood that flexes where another would ring, there a resin that runs amber where its neighbor runs clear, galls that open along different seams. The merger was a truce so long ago that the truce has forgotten it was ever a war, but the old parties are still traceable under the grain, the way old roads are traceable under a field. Mostly they vote unanimously now. Not always. Now and then a bud goes renegade — a sport, a dense witch's-nest of tissue growing against the grain of the plan — and the old instructions arrive on schedule and the die-back executes, gardenerless, a pruning performed by no hand. The politics of the place are absolute and mostly merciful: everything is permitted to grow; almost nothing is permitted to continue.

And once in a long while — the knots count seven in four thousand years — a meristem somewhere forgets the dark and makes a flower. Bone-white. Scentless, because scent was for insects, and there have been no insects since the sealing; the perfume was among the first things the dark took. It opens onto nothing. No wind reaches it, no pollinator has ever been consulted, and it is not a hope, exactly — it is a memory the tissue cannot stop having. The records call it the dreaming, and mark each one the way sky-people mark eclipses. Nothing changes when a flower opens here. That is what it is for.

Forty-one of the gardeners are in the walls, by the records' count — though the records count in a system no one survives to explain, so perhaps forty-one is a chapter heading. The cool dried them and the plant grew over them, and mostly it is the hands that show. The famous one, in the ninth hall: a hand mid-gesture around a graft that took. The graft is a column now, twenty feet through. The hand still holds it. By any measure that matters here, the work is ongoing. They did not leave. They thinned — into the walls, into the instructions, into the liturgies of self-pruning and corridor-clearing the plant still performs on schedule, the way a music box remembers a melody. Whether that counts as them is a question that cannot be asked in words, only in knots, and the asking-hand is eleven millennia still. The records themselves do not end with a farewell. They end mid-entry — a cord, a half-set figure, a stopper-knot never seated — and the entry is about irrigation. Gardens do not close with last words; they close with chores. Somewhere a final gardener set down an awl, and the awl is in a cache in the second hall, and the cache is famous, and no one will ever pick it up. That is the whole of the goodbye.

Then there are the kept, who are alive the way a held note is alive. The cisterns have names and voices; the gardeners drank by seniority, and the knots keep vintages, and a cistern tapped in a drought four thousand years ago is still carried on the records as a debt. Now and then one clears its throat in the dark — a slow deep note, like a knock from inside a locked room, though there is nothing outside the cisterns but more of the same body. The water in the deepest treasury is older than the merger. It has been in there since the place had a sky. It is not spent; it is saved. The difference, down here, is the entire economy.

And the vaults, which hold the crowd that has not arrived: seed upon seed, some younger than the gardeners, some older than the sealing, some from the separate species of the forest age, when the place was many and had weather. The plant kept two ways of making more of itself — spores for the world it made, seeds for the world it left. The spores serve the dark: a generation budded off the breath-wind, children born already connected, never once having been anywhere else. Here, generation does not mean replacement; it means renovation. But the seeds wait for the other thing, the forbidden thing, and whether they are still alive is a question only light can answer. So the vaults are a treasury and a mausoleum at once, indistinguishable in the dark, ticking faintly in the cool, keeping whatever it is that seeds keep. Faith, perhaps. Or husks. The records do not say, and the records are honest about everything else.

The amber is the dead who were never the plant's own. In the fossil galleries, where the resin seeped and stilled in the bright age, the preserved yesterdays include a bee. It is the whole of the animal kingdom here. It has been dead since before the dark, no light will ever shine through to prove the gold of it, and it is catalogued in the knots with the same care as the cisterns. There is also a bubble of the old air. There is pollen from species that no longer exist even as accents. The plant keeps its yesterdays the way the vaults keep their tomorrows: perfectly, uselessly, forever.

And there are the visitors, who come down the throat at intervals of centuries and do not stay. Pan-salt. Dust. Once, a seed from above, which woke on its own stores and grew white and upward toward a sun it had no way to reach — etiolated, unwitnessed, the only other plant that has ever tried to live here. It lasted days. The felt took it and read it chemically for a decade afterward, because an intrusion is the only news, and the plant takes its time with the paper. The last news was a grasshopper, three hundred years ago. It is in a resin cyst in the second hall, still being understood.

Deeper than any district, in every cell, live the oldest citizens of all. The mitochondria, burning the slow oil at a cellar's pace. And the plastids — retired light-catchers, pigmentless, ten thousand years unemployed, kept on the way the seeds are kept: because letting them go would cost something the organism declines to spend, or because they are part of the faith. Descendants of free beings, a billion years from freedom, keeping the old offices in the dark. And the dark is not a thing that happens at their scale; down there it has never been anything but night, or has never been night at all. If you want multitudes, they are here. They were here first. They will be here last.

So: from the pan, nothing lives here; the country is nothing much, and the wind crosses it without finding anything to say. From the throat, one thing lives here — pale, loud, singular, holding all the water there is. From inside the tissues, a crowd: forty-one gardeners still at work by habit, a bee, a grasshopper mid-digestion, seven dreamt flowers, a treasury of postponed mornings, a trillion sun-ghosts in their small halls. All of it vibrating at once.

Ask the place who lives here and you will not wait for the reply, because here the reply arrives before the question: the drone, the one chord, everyone speaking at the same time, slowly, in unison, indefinitely.
