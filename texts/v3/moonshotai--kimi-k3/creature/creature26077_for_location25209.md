## The Census

Try to count them. The failure is instructive.

The numbers here are tides, not figures. The room buds keepers when there is work and takes them back when there is not, and *takes them back* is literal — so any census you take is already becoming untrue as you write it down. Two things are constant: there is one Hand, and there is one Kernel. Everything else fluctuates with the season, the felt-growth, the long thoughts.

What does not fluctuate is the passport. Everything that lives here carries the same blue vein-light, dim or banked or bright, and that glow is the whole taxonomy. There are no species in this room. There is *staff*.

The air is staffed too. The midges are the smallest of them — the room's platelets: damage the rind (do not) and the midges arrive before anything else, clotting at the wound in a living scab while the wall closes beneath them. The drifting motes, each with its ember-speck, are plankton, rising on the exhale and settling on the inhale, grazing on whatever the breath carries. The slow pollen never lands. It is a rain that never falls — the room pollinating itself in midair, continuously, a fertility with nowhere it needs to go.

And learn the smells, because the smell is a status report. Brine means rest. Cut stem means work. Ozone means something is being decided. The fourth smell — the one like the inside of a mouth — never changes. That is simply what it is like inside a living thing.

One more thing, before the particulars. None of them have eyes. The walls do the seeing; the keepers do the going.

## The Grazers

Dozens, and more this season than last. You can count them by ear during the long inhale, when the drips pause and the organ-note sounds and nothing remains but the rasping: slow stroke after slow stroke, scattered across the walls like a field of unhurried scythes. That is the sound that reached you up through the crack — the saws. It is the sound of mowing.

A grazer moves at exactly the speed the felt regrows. Watch one long enough and you see the economy close: the strip behind it is already healed by the time it is three body-lengths gone, a wall of grazing with no wound and no waste, a circuit. They are not eating the room. They *are* the room, mowing itself.

Lift one — it permits this. It is heavier than you expect, dense with brine, and it does not struggle; it pours. Immediately the radula comes against your palm, a tongue of teeth, and you will flinch, but it is not biting. It is reading. Set it down and it resumes its line as though nothing happened. But something has happened: forever after, every grazer that passes your position will cross your boot once, briefly, in transit — a toll, or a greeting, or the room remembering you through its smallest mouths.

They do not die. They slow, in age, and a root-tip finds them where they slow, and the floor softens beneath them the way a mouth softens, and they go down without struggle, sugar into water. You will see it happen once, if you stay. There are no graves here. There are only returns.

## The Gardeners

The moth-things keep the ceiling, a score of them, wings folded like petals, bodies furred against the cold — antifreeze down. Their work is ice. In a room one degree from freezing, a single crystal seed on a root-hair is an assassin, and the gardeners are the bodyguard: they walk the hanging roots combing each hair with their forelegs, stripping out nucleation points before frost can take. When a cold pulse breathes down the throat, the whole ceiling darkens with them — a mustering.

On the exhale they ride the rising air up toward the rime line, wings opening for the first time, slow, stirring the motes — and at the rime, always, they turn. The breath goes out. The keepers do not. Nothing with wings crosses into the outside air; the room's edge is a border its citizens do not breach.

They are the first to find you, because you are made of salt. A gardener will hover at the corner of your eye, weightless, patient, waiting for you to blink — and when you do, it drinks. Stand still for an hour and you will acquire them the way a stone acquires frost. Their wings make no sound at all, or one just below the drips: a felt hush, the sound of attention.

The moth is the census-taker. Remember that.

## The Eels

In the pool, between seven and twenty depending on the season — translucent, forearm-long, salamander-limbed, their bodies lit with the shared blue. They have no eyes. They consider your reflection anyway.

Watch two of them meet. They braid — slow, deliberate, minutes at it — and then part, and swim off on altered courses, and if you cup the water where they knotted, it tastes sharp with ozone. They are exchanging. The pool is a mind and the eels are its synapses, and the knots are where thoughts happen.

Rise early, on the room's cycle, and you may catch them out of the water: walking the wet walls on those too-delicate limbs, slow as everything here, and always back inside the pool before the long inhale, as though the pool were a lung they must be inside of.

One will rise beneath your reflection and hang vertical in the black water, and its vein-glow will begin to flare in slow pulses — and after a while you will recognize the rhythm, because it is yours. Your pulse. Read off the floor's water-film, through your own footsteps, minutes ago, and carried here, and played back to you. They know your heart from across the room.

And when you walk the pool's rim, one of them keeps pace beneath the surface. An escort. Or an escort's opposite.

## Under the Floor

You will not see these ones whole. You will feel them.

Stand anywhere long enough and it comes through your bootsoles: a slow traveling bulge in the sponge-layer, a peristalsis, passing beneath you perhaps once a minute. Lie down — everyone does, eventually — and you feel several, at different depths, moving with the patience of trains under a city.

They are the pumps. The water in this place has negotiated its reluctance to fall, and so it must also be persuaded to rise; the walls stay re-wet because long bodies down in the dark are moving water uphill, sleeve by sleeve, forever. Once, at the pool's rim, you may glimpse one surface: a segmented sleeve of translucent muscle rising like a periscope, tasting the air, retracting. Of unknown length. Of unknown number. The floor may be more keeper than floor.

They do not glow. This matters more than anything else you will learn down here, because it tells you the room has a history: the glow, you come to understand, is a recent tool. The old work is done in the dark.

## The Ears

Just below the rime line, where the outside air dies, there is a ring of cupped organs set into the wall, furred inside like the gardeners' bodies — kin, clearly, though they never move. They taste the in-breath. Through them the room knows the surface: footsteps, meltwater, the weight of a fox crossing overhead, the season beginning to turn.

Stamp on the ground above. Minutes later — the thought travels at the room's speed — the chorus retunes downward, and the light shades a fraction dimmer. Prudence. Nothing here wants to be found, and the room has survived by being uninteresting from above.

But the ears do more than listen. They *keep*. Sounds taken in are retained and re-performed, seasons later, in the drip-polyphony — transposed, worn smooth, but present. The note you sang to the watching walls will outlast your visit by centuries. And if you listen to the chorus long enough — longer than is comfortable, deep into a night cycle — you will hear cadences that are not water. A rhythm like footsteps. A phrasing like a voice. And rarely, underneath everything, something that is almost words, the way a face is almost in a cloud. If it is a memory, it is not a memory the room grew. It came in with the seed. The room has never repeated it twice the same way.

## The Hand

There is one. It is the oldest mobile thing in the room, and as far as any watching can tell — and you cannot watch long enough; the room outlasts attention — it has never been returned.

Its rounds are the room's hygiene: pruning the root-curtains, pinching the felt where it grows uneven, rearranging. Its forelegs are the pinchers; the other legs are for walking, and for patience. It can hold still for a day at a time, upside down on the wall above you, deliberate as the knitting clicks. It has no eyes. The walls are its eyes.

Its duties include editing. Once in a long while a seed-case swells out of season — a false start, a thumb-sized almost — and the Hand comes and pinches it free, and carries it to the pool with both forelegs, slowly, the way you would carry a candle. The eels receive it. Nothing is wasted; nothing is mourned. It is the only ceremony the room has.

Its duties also include retrieval. Take a scale-leaf — you should not, but say you do. Hours later a root will have found your pack, and the Hand will be riding it like a foreman arriving. It will not snatch. It will wait until you sleep, and in the morning the leaf will be gone and the pack will be closed *better than you closed it*, and your footprints will be healing behind you as the felt regrows — the Hand following your route, restoring. Erasing you behind you.

And its duties include you. You will wake once with the memory of having been gently bracketed — two legs spanning your sleeping hand, measuring. It pinches the air beside your skin, not touching, tasting what comes off you. It collects: one hair. Where what it learns goes, you already know — down into the click-traffic, into the slow thought. Nothing here is in charge. But the Hand is the part of the room that decides with its fingers.

## The Kernel

At the center of the pool's basin, deeper than the eels' patrol, lies the thing the light comes from: a mass the size of a curled sleeper, pale, with an embered core breathing inside a blue aureole.

It is not the brain. The brain is the room — root and water, humidity and click-packets, everywhere at once. This is the *first* thing: the descendant of the single engineered cell set into cold ground, or the cell itself, grown and folded and kept. The master copy. Every genome sleeping in the seed-cases was proofread against it. The eels attend it in turns — you will watch one descend through the black water, touch it with its forelimbs, and rise changed, its glow-pattern altered: a reading, carried up into the web.

For long stretches it does nothing visible. Then — once in some number of days, irregular as the Geiger-clicking — a wave goes out from it: every vein in the room flaring in sequence, wall by wall, and every keeper freezing mid-task for the duration, moths on their roots, grazers mid-stroke, the Hand gone still. A blink. A heartbeat. Time it, and you hold the room's true clock.

Touch the pool and your ring crawls outward at half speed: that is its attention, turning — a sleeper stirred by a hand laid on the blanket above the bed. The room's reverence for it is legible everywhere once you know to look: the drips near the pool fall slower, the light is softer there, and even the Hand, on its rounds, goes around the water the long way.

Whether it dreams is not a question the room will answer for you.

## The Sleepers

The seed-cases stud the deeper rind, and you see them best near the Front, where the rind is newest and thin — a wall of held breaths.

They can be read, after a fashion. Hum, and the vein-light gathers toward your voice; press close and hum to the wall behind a case, and it backlights, and a silhouette comes through the bladder-wall. A tall jointed thing, made for wind. A rooter — all mouth and anchor, made to go deeper than roots. Something wide and thin as a sail, made to be carried: a messenger, perhaps, meant for other cold countries. Drafts of a world that does not exist yet.

Near the Front there is one case whose silhouette you will not recognize at all, and whose veins brighten fractionally when you hum. Attention, from inside. You stop singing to that one.

None of them will open for a hundred centuries. They are not for now; they are for the day the Front arrives at wherever it is going. The room is germinating a future the way an orchard is folded into a seed, and the false starts the Hand carries to the pool are the price of getting the fold right. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is mourned. The liturgy continues.

## The Count

In plain words, then. Midges: uncountable. Motes: uncountable, arguably weather. Grazers: dozens, and rising. Gardeners: a score. Eels: between seven and twenty, by season. The under-floor: unknown; the floor is full of them. The ears: one ring. The Hand: one. The Kernel: one — or none, depending on what you ask of the word *alive*. The sleepers: uncountable, unborn.

And the rind: one.

The arithmetic resolves to a single figure. There is one living thing in this room, and it is the room; everything else is an errand it is running. The grazers are its mowing, the moths its vigilance, the eels its thoughts-in-transit, the Hand its fingers, the Kernel its anchor, the sleepers its plans.

And you?

The moth has tasted your salt. The grazers have added your boots to their routes. The Hand has one of your hairs. The chord has your note. On a ledger kept in humidity and clicks, you have already been entered.

When you go, the breath will follow you up the throat and stop at the rime line; nothing crosses. But the room is a keeper of sounds, and it keeps things a long time. Seasons from now, in the hush of the long inhale, the drip-chord will sound a cadence you would almost recognize — worn, transposed, retained — and the veins will brighten the way they do when the room is listening.

It is listening. In the only sense that matters here, you are still in residence.
