You will not see them at first. From the rim there is only the tarn, and from the shore path only the landing: nine stone huts, smoke, goat pens, drying racks, a soapstone grease pot by the water steps. Forty-odd souls — the tally counts them, along with everything else. It is only when you notice that half the village is missing at any given hour, and that nobody is looking for them, that you understand: they are below. That is where the people of this place live, in the sense that matters. The landing is where they sleep. The lower village is their address.

They name things plainly and are named plainly. Each household keeps the name of its house under the water — the mill, the ash house, the red house, the priest's house, the smith's — and introductions are made that way: *she is of the mill; he is ash house.* The drowned buildings are not their history. They are where they are from, present tense, and when the roof of your house needs weeding you go down and weed it.

The lake makes its own kind of body. A good diver is made slow: slow heart, slow hands, slow speech, as if the cold had edited out everything quick. Mostly this has meant the women — they hold the cold longer, the village has observed, the way it observes the delta — but the lake cares only for the slowness, and there are men whose counts are long, and they dive. The grease goes on before dawn, and it goes on by another's hands; no one greases her own back, so every dive begins with being touched, and nobody has ever said why, and everybody knows. Then the belt, the two flat stones, the bronze knife, and the breath — the last breath taken like a tool being picked up. Every diver knows her count the way you know the length of your own stride, and it is entered in the tally next to her name, and it changes across a life, and the change is news. By sixty, a long-count woman has spent a full year of her life below, minute by counted minute. The village respects both her ages.

Down she goes, and the cold narrows her mind to a point — and then, if she is good, the point opens again into a green room of work. Sight is the weakest sense down there; the world is readable at arm's length and no farther, so the village is kept at hand's reach. The hedges are handrails: a diver swims the lanes with one palm on their clipped tops, and the tops are flat because palms have expected them to be flat for two hundred years. Knots are tied by shape, not feel, because the fingers go numb first. Above her, on the raft or at the steps, a tender counts aloud, and the count comes through the water not as sound exactly but as something remembered, and it is the clock. When the count reaches her number she goes up. Before that, what sends her up is the gray — that is what they call the edge of consciousness, because that is what it looks like, the green going to gray at the edges like dusk entering a room. No one argues with the gray. The village is old because of this.

The knock-code is small on purpose; under cold water you do not want a large vocabulary. One knock, *here*; two, *look at this*; three, *up, now* — and anyone may knock three, even a child on her first season, and a whole team of elders will surface for it without a question asked, because the one who sees the gray in someone else's hands is the one who speaks. Four slow knocks means *help*, which is almost never heard and never once ignored. There is one more sound, and it is not in the code: a single soft tap of stone on stone, too gentle to be a knock, a vocabulary of one word meaning *I see you; be easy.* The tap is for those who live below but do not dive. It is given to the old one under the altar, and it is given to the dead.

The work is the hedges, the weirs, the withies, the mussel beds, the garden rows under their patient tethered floats — and the work is governed by the tally, a slate at the landing and a recitation held in three memories, which is the closest thing this place has to a government. Everything is on it: which hedge section is due, which weir panel is sound, which mussel bed is resting, which willow stool is in which year of its rotation, the otter takes, the delta's advance, the souls, the gone-up. Every craft is held by three hands — an old hand, a working hand, a learning hand — because the lake can take one and the slope can take one, and a thing known by only two people is a thing the village considers already half lost. There are shore hands too, and they are not lesser: the goats, the hay, the cheese, the smoke, the creel of trade that goes down the creek. The lake is fed by the snowfields, but the village is fed by the shore, and everyone says so. What goes down-valley is baskets — water-willow takes a bend like nothing grown in air — and dried charr, and mussel shell, so that the valley fastens its good clothes with the lake. What comes up is salt, grain, iron. Iron is for the shore. Below is bronze and stone and bone, and the bronze never leaves: knives wear down to slivers across the centuries and are recast at the smith's little furnace, so that all the metal in the valley is one family, and the bell has cousins at every belt.

A childhood here is an apprenticeship in cold conducted so gradually that it feels like play, because it is play. Toddlers in the summer shallows, grease from the start. The count learned as a song before it is learned as numbers. The game called dipper — walking the bottom with a stone in each fist, head under, working an imaginary lane. And then, around twelve, the finding of the stones. It takes a summer at the gravel bars of the delta, test-knocking, pair after pair, until two flat stones speak true together and suit the hand. You do not make your stones; you find them, and their voice is yours, and a diver working across the bowl is known by her knock before she is known by her flicker. After the stones, the first descent to the family's own house, a hand on the lintel, one knock: *here.* And when her count is long enough, the tower — down past the arch, into the green dim, to lay a palm flat on the bell. Then she is entered in the tally as a full diver: her name, her house, her count. The tally is the only certificate there is.

The village marries out more than in — forty souls must — and a newcomer is shore-married first, sometimes for years, while the breath is built. The marriage below comes the day they first reach the door of the spouse's house and knock once on the lintel, and after that the union is counted entire. Some never make the depth. The tally has a place for them too; the lake is not for everyone, and nobody here pretends otherwise. There is play that is not training: ungreased evening swims in high summer, and in the drumming dusks of the spawn, when the meadows are closed to work, young divers go down anyway and lie at the meadow's edge on the chara and let the charr drum them, the thrum coming up through the turf into the ribs. It is the only music below that is not a message. No ticket but a breath.

One day a diver says, of an ordinary morning, that yesterday's was her last breath below, and it is entered in the tally like rain, and she becomes a shore hand of the watching kind — a counter, a tender of the grease pot, a teacher of knots, a keeper of the tally. When a diver dies — in the bed, on the slope, and three times in living memory below — the village goes down to the chapel wall and knocks three for them: *up, now.* It is the only other use of that call, and everyone hears it for what it is: permission. After that the word for the dead is *gone up*. The pair of stones is split — one to the grave under the pines above the flood line, one set on the boundary stone of the family's plot in the plaza — and the plaza's corners carry generations of half-pairs, and the children learn the voices of the gone along with the living, and sometimes, working nearby, a diver drifts over and gives a grandmother's stone the soft tap. *I see you; be easy.*

The other citizens keep their own schedules. The old one under the altar takes the evening crayfish procession one by one, as it has since before anyone's counting, and in the drumming weeks it is gone, and the tally reads *the old one, out*, and in due time the slate is amended: *the old one, present.* Whether it is the same fish each year — whether it is one fish at all, or an office held in succession — is not asked, and the not-asking is the nearest thing this village has to prayer. The otters have their holt by the outflow lip, and their share of every weir, and when a weir is emptied the last fish is left: the share made deliberate. Their kits and the village's children learn the water in the same weeks, and regard each other across it without comment. The dippers nest behind the inflow fall and under the landing eaves and walk the lanes at hand's depth, the only other citizens who treat the ceiling as no ceiling. The heron takes what it takes and is given nothing and asked nothing; the heron is weather. That is the great sorting, the one axiom under the tally: the world divides into the scheduled and the weather, and the heron is weather, and the gray is weather, but the delta is not, because it is measured, and what is measured can be borne. The family of the swallowed house — the low house, they are still called, keeping the name of a building under the silt — swims the delta front each spring after spate, notes its pace against the boundary stones, moves the garden floats that much upslope. The lower lane will go under in a century or two. It is on the tally. The gardens are already moving. This is a people that grieves in advance, mildly, for masonry, and plants for grandchildren as a matter of course.

From seal to spate, the lake rests from them. No one goes below; the ice is the ceiling and the closed sign both, and the village turns to weaving and mending and the midwinter recitation of the tally entire, three voices in turn, an evening long, the children corrected gently and exactly. The ice booms, and in deep winter a child may lay an ear to it and hear the lake ticking away down there in the gray-green dark, and the old women say: *it is counting.* Then the melt, the spate, the bell waking and ringing itself for weeks, and the first dive: the audit read out, the rope hauled by two pairs of hands — the longest count still diving and the shortest, the old and the new together, because the note is big and the rope is heavy and because that is who the bell belongs to — and the green note rolling up through the ceiling and out across the cirque.

Ask them whether the chapel is still a chapel and they will say it is still the chapel. Ask whether the old one is holy and they will say it is old. Ask why they clip hedges for houses no one will live in again, and if you are lucky you will get a wet arm lifted toward a dipper walking the lower lane, going about its small work as though the water were weather — which to the dipper it is. *The dippers do,* they say, and that is the whole answer you will get, and in the end it is the whole answer there is. Nothing here was founded. It was only never stopped. The tending is not a tribute to the past; the past is simply where the work is.

And on the first working morning after the bell, the divers go down one to each corner of the work, and the knocks come up through the ceiling like a roll being called: *here,* from the weirs. *Here,* from the gardens. *Here,* from the chapel wall. The tenders answer, the count begins, the hedges are clipped, the weirs hold, the children at the gravel bars are finding their voices stone by stone, and under the altar slab in the green dark the old one shifts its fins.

Present, says the tally. Present, present, present.
