You will not see them at first. The basin keeps its people the way it keeps everything: spaced wide, one at a time, with long reaches of no one between the gardens of their presence. There is no counting them. Counting is a kind of watching, and watched, the count takes the hour; unwatched, you already know it, and it is exactly what you expected, and it settles nothing.

The first sign is always a place where someone has just been: a mat dimpled at the height of a sitting hip, water risen black and bright into the dimple, the weave mid-recovery and in no hurry to complete it. A cup set down half-full, laying on the matting the cool silhouette of a cup filled to the brim. The world here remembers bodies the way clay remembers thumbs. Then, one at a time, the bodies themselves.

The woman at the seam you will meet early. She sits where two mats meet, and the bone needle stands in the seam between them, threaded, and her hand rests near it — not on it: resting near is her work. She has been about to pull this stitch for as long as there has been a stitch to pull, and she is not waiting in any way that would sour. The seam, once pulled, would join two mats into one meadow, and the basin is not ready to be one thing. So she keeps the thread taut and the pull declined, and her thimble is clay, and the thumbprint inside it, you will notice eventually, is familiar.

The beam-carrier has learned the basin's first lesson: don't watch. He carries bamboo from the stack to the half-walled hall, and because he never looks at the far end he arrives before he has walked half of it, surprised every time, and sets the beam down, and turns, and there is another beam. The hall will never roof. He is not troubled. He hums, low, the way the insects keep their hum, and under his path the weave has learned his step — water rising to meet his foot a half-breath before it lands, which is the closest thing this place has to erosion, which is to say the closest thing it has to affection. The stone adze on the half-split beam is not his. It rests there for whoever comes next. Everything rested is an offer.

The fisher sits at a mat's edge with a line of twisted rush and no hook to speak of, and below him the fish hang in the clear water, unhurried, in no danger, aware. He is not fishing for fish. The fishing is how he and the fish keep company, and the frog beside him, not calling, is part of the arrangement. Sometimes he looks down — not at the fish but at the water, where his reflection is hauling them in one after another, triumphant, fed. Contentment here is something your reflection does for you. The world's small allowance of change is all spent below the surface, and the inhabitants know it, and so the surface of every person is as calm as the surface of the water, while underneath, in the bright upside-down, their real lives ripple and complete and complete. Lean low over the frog: its reflection's throat is blown like a bubble, mid-call, forever. Put your ear to the mat and you can almost hear it — the splash of the heron's strike, the tick of a pulled stitch, the click of one more bead. All the declined sounds, spent like coin in the country under the water.

Shade is the only real estate. The bead-stringer's doorway stands in a wall that reaches the hip, and her house is its shadow — the cool full-height silhouette the wall lays down of its own completion. Inside that hypothetical dark she keeps her mats and her bowls, each bowl half-filled, each casting a shadow of full, and across the doorway runs the strand of copper beads that have never heard of verdigris. One more bead after every creak, they say, though no one has verified this, because verification is a kind of watching. The strand is the basin's memory of its intervals. It grows the way everything grows here: in the looking-away, back to itself, and exactly the same, and longer.

The half-coiled pot shades like a jar you could crawl into, and someone has. The oldest of them sleeps there on a mat, inside the cool silhouette, a vine across the ankles, two green shoots up through the matting near the hair — the boundary between made and grown gone soft in a person, as it has everywhere. Their hands are clay to the wrist. Fresh thumbprints appear in the soft coils of the rim at no interval anyone has measured, and no one has seen the sleeper rise; but then, no one would. The makers of the larger works are never seen at it, not even, perhaps, by themselves. This is how one person builds a valley: in sleep, in the blind spot of their own day, a coil at a time. Everyone's hands here are a little clayed. No one quite remembers why.

The heron is the keystone. It stands in the shallows asleep on one leg, mid-strike, and has stood so since the making, and the fish beneath it hang unhurried because the strike is permanently declined. The whole noon rests on that declension: if the strike ever landed, something would end, and ending is the one thing not stocked here. So the heron holds, the load-bearing bird, and its wage is paid in the water, where its reflection traverses the reflected sky and strikes and strikes and is endlessly satisfied. An ecosystem, in the Nearly-Finished, is an arrangement of moments everyone has agreed not to have.

The weaver birds are granted what no one else is: transit. They cross the polite distances without courtesy, arriving while watched, coming and going from the gourd-halls in the garden trees, and their building is the world's building — look away and a nest is further along, and exactly the same. Some of the halls have doors on the inside, and those are occupied. By birds, mostly. In one case — the leaning tree, the lowest hall — not by birds. You know because the door is shut, and a door that opens inward can only be shut from within, and shutting is the one completion the basin permits: self-enclosure. Privacy is the only finished thing here. No one knocks. The politeness of the distances is also a politeness of persons.

The wasps are the expectant ones, the only builders working toward an event rather than a state: their clay organ-pipes stand tuned to a wind that does not blow, and they maintain them, and the maintenance is the music, rehearsing for a weather permanently about to. The dragonflies hold station at the corners of the heat-shimmer's fixed draperies — the curtains are pinned to their stillness — and when one moves, somewhere a crease of gauze re-hangs, unwitnessed, over the same distance.

The children you will hear before you see, because laughter is the one sound that arrives early, before you have crossed half the distance to it. They play in the shadows of the unbuilt — hide-and-seek among the declined futures, where hiding is as simple as stepping into a hypothetical and standing still. No one is ever found, because finding would end it. No one has ever lost a game here, or won one. If you walk through the cool silhouette of the tower-that-isn't, you may feel them pass you, faintly, like walking through the shimmer-curtain: the ghost of being chased.

You will never see them gathered. But you will find the gatherings: bowls set out in a circle on a garden raft, each half-full, each shadowed full; a game with more pieces in motion than one child could move; two cups where you are sure there was one. Two inhabitants are each other's unwitness — this is how anything at all gets done: in pairs, in the mutual looking-away. Their whole communal life happens in the blind spot of the world, and it is warm there, by all evidence, and unhurried, and always already tidied back to itself. The reason you meet them one at a time is that you are watching.

There is, at all times, exactly one figure on the far side — a small bright interruption in the haze-line, an hour away if watched, no distance at all if not. Walk toward them unwatched and you arrive, and they are on the far side again, behind you now. The basin keeps a counterweight to you the way a pot keeps its center. No one you ask will explain this. They smile, the way you smile at a question that answers itself once you've been here long enough.

They eat, though nothing is consumed. The gardens carry their fruit always at its moment — nothing grows past it — and picking happens, and tasting, and the fruit, unwatched, is on the branch again, exactly the same. Meals are taken in shifts of looking away. Sweetness repeats. And they touch the world, constantly, deliberately: palms on the mud brick, thumbs along the rim, because the clay stays soft where it is touched, and softness is the whole agreement. This is a world of greenware, leather-hard and holding; the dew that never falls is its slip; the noon is the kiln that has never lit and will not, the sun a decision the sky has made and, like all decisions here, permanently deferred. To live in the Nearly-Finished is to keep it unfired — to wet the seam, to press the coil, to hold the whole bright valley at the moment before the kiln. The prints in the small clay are the size and the whorl of your own because, look long enough, they are all one print. The basin was coiled by a single hand, at the scale of forever, and the hand belongs to whoever holds still long enough to be counted.

Which brings it to you. The tools set down mid-task are not abandoned; they are waiting in the way this place waits, which is without impatience and without end. The threaded needle. The resting adze. The half-strung beads. Each is a place where the work pauses for a hand, and the hand it pauses for is the next one. One day you will sit down beside the seam, and the needle will fit your fingers as though measured — it was; everything here is measured to the hand that finds it — and later, no one could say how much later, the creak having come and gone at its unmeasured interval, you will find a thumbprint in clay you do not remember touching. It will be fresh. It will be yours. That is how you learn you live here: not by deciding, but by discovering, unwitnessed even by yourself, that you have already begun. The weave will learn your step. The drop at the reed's cut end will lean toward you, minutely, and decline, as ever, to fall. Your reflection, down in the bright country, will take up some work and finish it, over and over, gladly.

So what lives in the Nearly-Finished is the nearly-finished: a woman and a needle, a man and a beam, a sleeper and a valley, a heron and the held noon, a frog and its unspoken call, a strand of beads, a shut door, a child inside a shadow, a hand that is everyone's and yours. Brought to the edge of done and left there — deliberately, lovingly left — each of them holding one unpulled stitch of the world, and finding in the holding what the world finds in them: that the last stitch, unpulled, is here the same thing as home.
