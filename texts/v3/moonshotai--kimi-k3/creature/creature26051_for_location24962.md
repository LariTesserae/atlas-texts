## The Kept

Everything that lives on the array came to the water, and the water keeps what comes.

The guests first, because they are the easiest to count. Swifts in their thousands, nesting in the fin-gaps — and their colonies are the quietest bird-cities in the world, because the finfields drink their screaming. All day the flock-noise goes into the metal, and at dusk the culverts give it back, soft as breath, and the swifts settle to the sound of their own morning. A tall gray heron-thing at every cistern, motionless, patient as the plumbing. Midges writing and rewriting themselves over the basins. Water-boatmen rowing the black glass. Seam-rats threading the gutters with solder-bright whiskers. Foil-moths grazing the shed laminate down on the talus. And along the aqueduct lines, the migrants — crossing the dry gulf from sister plate to sister plate, navigating by the leak-threads, those single vertical hairs of falling water, drinking on the wing. The threads are sky-wells. The array waters its flyways. What the birds miss evaporates before it lands; the desert below gets nothing. The plateau keeps its water in the air, in itself, in bodies.

The green keeps its place: black seam-moss in thread-lines along the drips, the verdigris bloom in the still cisterns, the gray-green lichen that eats tarnish and nothing else. The lichen is less a resident than a policy. It is the plateau's skin flora, and whether it arrived or was always part of the works is a question of the same kind as all the others here, and it gets the same answer, which is none.

Count carefully, though, and the census will not close. The numbers do not hold still, because the water does not. Some of the animals were not born and did not arrive. They precipitated.

You know the mechanism by now: the warm shallow pans, the weeks of patience, the thing climbing out sharp-edged and unworn and younger than young. The tells are faint and they do not last. Foil in the feather. The cistern-frog's tick, like cooling metal. An extra stillness. And for the first few days, the lag — the shadow arriving a half-breath behind its animal, the way thunder arrives behind lightning. Then the season turns. The foil molts out. The tick fades to a frog's ordinary voice. The shadow catches up. And the young of the printed are born wet and ordinary and free of all of it — the array's signature is written in the moment, not in the germ. So the flocks are blended past all untangling. Every born heron may carry printed grandmothers. No one catalogs the difference anymore because there stops being a difference to catalog. The plateau has been co-authoring the gene pool for as long as anyone has watched, and it was here first, and it has all the time there is.

The herons are the most-printed beings in the world, and this is why every cistern has one. Stillness is a heron's whole profession. A born heron lands at a cistern, folds itself, and holds — and the warm pan beside the cistern takes the impression the way the fins take dew, bead by bead, week by week. One morning there are two herons. The elder weathers gray; the younger is slightly brighter, slightly newer; both drink; both ignore you. When a printed frog goes down a heron's throat it ticks, briefly, a small swallowed sound, and the heron does not react. Nothing on the plateau surprises a heron. They have listened to the drains all their lives. The cisterns come with herons the way the fins come with light.

The swifts are the opposite case, and every traveler takes comfort in them. The array has never caught a swift. They sleep on the wing, half a brain at a time, falling through the dark at speed; they touch water only flying, sipping the leak-threads; they nest high on the cold bright fins, far from the pans' warm shallows. The pans need warmth and stillness, and a swift has neither. You cannot precipitate weather.

The foil-moths are the near case — printed moths graze the talus beside born ones, nearly perfect copies of a thing already half made of the plateau's own material, wing-foil on foil. The talus never accumulates, because the moths graze it to bright dust, and the brief local drizzle washes the dust into the drains, and the works take it back. The array eats its moths' leavings. Everything returns; only the Front is net gain, a finger's width a year.

The midges are the brief case, and the strange one. On brightfall dawns a pan will sometimes exhale a swarm — condensed all at once, rising like reversed rain, glittering faintly with the hour's gathered light. Born midges spend their childhood in the basins; the printed never were larvae at all. They climb out adult into a hatch already dying by dusk, and for their one extra day they are the eldest midges on the plateau — younger than young, and older than everyone.

Now the rules, because the rules are natural history, and because they are one rule.

Keep moving. Do not answer the drains. These are the same instruction. Stillness is the door the array prints through, and the drains are not a lure with intent — they are merely interesting. They give back yesterday, gently, at hours of their own choosing: a heron's cry from three days ago, your own footstep from this morning, soft as breath. And interest holds a person. A traveler kneeling at a culvert at dusk, listening, is a traveler standing in a warm pan by other means. Answer, and you stay to hear the answer. Stay, and the pans have you. There is no malice in it. There is no hunting in it. The plateau does not pursue. It condenses.

So the fieldcraft: drink from cisterns, never from pans — the pans are workbenches. Count herons before you camp: one gray heron, the water is idle; two herons, one bright, something nearby is being made. Never sleep twice in one place. Trust a shadow only after it arrives. And know that the crossing marks you. You can tell a person who has crossed the array for the rest of their life — the drumming fingers, the rocking foot at the fire, the body that learned the rule in its bones and never unlearned it.

The water itself is nobody's. You cannot own a thing that prints itself; you take it and you go. The caravans walk the aqueduct shade between the sister plates, and they carry one law older than any of them: what walks out of the pan walks out free. An original holds no claim on a print. A print owes nothing but the rule. So it happens that a company of twelve camps a month beside a warm basin and leaves as thirteen, and nobody remarks — or everyone remarks once, gently, at the fire, and then not again, because by season's end there is nothing left to remark on. And some come on purpose: the old, the dying, the grief-struck — to leave a self beside a pan the way one leaves water for the next traveler. The array offers the only immortality there is, and its fidelity is uncertain, and people take it anyway. People have always taken it anyway.

Which brings the census to the residents proper: the Still.

The name fits twice. They are still, and they are what the still made. They are the ones who stopped — prints who stayed, originals who consented — and because anything standing in a pan's warmth gets copied again, a person who stays on the array is continued. Second printing. Third. Ninth. The drift compounds: foil at the edge of the iris, a tick beneath the laugh, stillness laid on stillness, until the eldest sit so quietly that swifts perch on them and brightfall pools on their shoulders and they do not brush it off. Each printing remembers only as far as its own pan-side afternoon, so the eldest are chains — memory handed down like water in newer and newer buckets, the water older than any bucket, the originals centuries walked-away or dead. To talk with the Still is to talk with one person at many hours of a single life. The only arguments on the plateau are a person disagreeing with themselves across decades — gently, and both right.

Whether they eat: they drink. No one has watched one eat. They are in no hurry about hunger. They are the operators the old riddle goes looking for — the ones who honor the cold-face trick the builders understood — and they honor it the way lichen operates a cliff: by residing. On the array, nothing may rest but the finished, and nothing here is finished. Only the Still rest, having consented to be continued.

The pans, remember, mostly make copies. Mostly. The unusuals come rarely and come wrong — a bird built of frog-patience, a surplus wing pair — and mostly they do not last, or they last and hide. And then there is the long pan, in the backwater under the third aqueduct's shade, which has been accreting one shape for longer than any record anyone's grandmother kept. It is too big for its pan now. It spills the rim in a slow bright curve, and it is made — anyone can see this, no one can explain it — for deep water. The array has never seen deep water. It has only dreamed it, in cisterns, in black glass. What grows in the long pan is a citizen of a climate that does not exist yet: the plateau, which has all the time there is, printing ahead of its own weather. The Still go and sit with it, which they can do. Herons attend. Travelers may look — walking, briefly. If it ever finishes, it will climb out sharp-edged and younger than young into a world with no sea in it. And then, the Still say, with their unhurried certainty, the array will make the sea. It is making the water, after all. It has made nothing else all these years — unless you count everything else.

Below the third cistern level, the Still do not go, and they suggest, without insistence, the same. Not danger, exactly. Nothing there needs you. Time thickens with depth the way it thickens at dawn; the drips fall long enough to age by; the hum of the bus-lines rises out of the subsonic until the works' quiet music is almost a sound; the presses are near. And the old drains down there release sounds that no one placed: patient, speech-shaped, in no tongue kept above. Builders' voices, perhaps. Condensed machinery. Thunder from before the birds. The array talking in its sleep, if it sleeps. The strata decline to answer the question, and the Still, who have sat and listened, decline in the same way, which is their answer.

So the census, honestly taken, is this. The born. The printed. The kept — voices in the drains, yesterday's sun standing in the puddles. The becoming, in the long pan. The continued, on their stones. And through and under all of them the one certain resident, whose body is the plateau, whose breath is the long thermal tick of the fins, whose blood beads on the cold bright faces, whose memory murmurs up out of the culverts at dusk, whose children are everything that ever held still in warm shallows.

Stand at a cistern at the long dawn, once. Brightfall sifting down almost invisibly. Fog knee-deep in the gaps. Two herons drinking, one gray, one bright, both real. One of the Still seated nearby, ninth vintage of someone, a swift on her shoulder, in no hurry whatsoever. Your own footstep from yesterday murmuring up out of the drain — do not answer it. And in the warm pan beside the cistern, faint as the brightfall, a new outline gathering bead by bead, unhurried, unfinished.

It might be a frog. It might be a heron. If you came anyway, and stood too long at the water, it might be you.

Keep moving. Or stay.

The water keeps what comes.
