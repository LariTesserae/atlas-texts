Not everything alive in the hollow lives there.

Draw the line first, because the room blurs it on purpose. The piston, the lung, the wheel: these are not inhabitants. They are the heath itself, working — no more residents of this chamber than your heart is a resident of your chest. They do not live here. They *are* here, the way a body is in its own parts. The residents are the others, the small presences in the open water and the loud air, and the longer you stay the more clearly you see that each of them is less an individual than a seat — a position in the room that keeps being filled.

Begin with the frogs, because you hear them before you see them. Each perch is an inheritance. A frog holds one for the whole of its life — a decade, perhaps more — sitting its hollow reed like a musician sewn into its instrument, because that is what the perch is: a tuned pipe the plant grew to a note, and the frog grew to the pipe. The calls rotate. Listen for an hour and you can map the room by ear: the two-note bark moves around the walls in the same direction the wheel turns, each frog taking the rest-interval after its neighbor, a round with no first singer and no last, every entrance on the meter, every one off the beat by the identical fraction — the fraction, you come to understand, at which the membrane returns a voice. Call at that phase and the room throws your voice back doubled, audible to every perch. Call at any other and your voice falls into wool. The room teaches timing the way a current teaches swimming. You can watch a young frog, new up from the drainage channels, bark into dead air for three nights and find the phase on the fourth, and from then on it will never miss, not once in ten years, because the architecture has accepted it and it is now part of what the architecture does.

The fish you count by their shadows. They are pale as the water they hang in, translucent, their organs a thread of amber echoing the mat below, and the green-gold light from the ceiling throws a sharper fish onto the floor than the fish itself presents to your eye. Count the shadows and the number is always the same. Count again after a month: the same. Not the same fish — the same number. They are born back in the drainage channels, among the eels, and on some blue tick you cannot distinguish from any other tick the spawn is released — the room's one moment of increase, timed like everything else, and spent almost immediately, because the bird is waiting at the top of the water with its clockwork patience, and it takes the surplus as it rises. The bird is not hunting. The bird is editing. Every outflow in the room has a creature standing on it.

The midge columns are the oldest continuous families in the chamber, after the machines. Three or four of them, hanging at their exact stations in the air, each a standing eddy of warm current rising off the mat — the midges are not keeping the formation; the formation is keeping the midges. They are dye in a flow, convection made visible. An individual lives a handful of days, is born into the column, mates inside it, dies out of it; the column itself has not moved house in a thousand years. Shear one with the draft of your arm and it re-forms at the same height, in the same shape, within minutes. The room remembers its air.

Under the mat's edge, the eels are the only blue anywhere in the green-gold world, and their discharge travels. Watch long enough — an hour of patience, your chin at the waterline — and you see it: the synchrony is a wave, a ring of blue ticks lapping the drainage channels, and the lap time is exactly one turn of the wheel. They are how the mat hears itself. Trail your fingers in the channel and the wave stutters around your hand and re-locks on the far side, the way a stream rejoins below a stone. Whether they are animals that learned to be wiring or wiring that grew into animals is a question the room long ago stopped answering.

In the mineral corner, the snails carry the waste stream on their backs — their shells accrete the same porcelain they graze, so that each is a piece of the crust that stood up and walked. The loudness of the room drowns their feeding, but press a finger to the crust and you feel it: the rasp, faint as a stitch being pulled, notched into the same meter as the drips. Their trails are silver writing that the next snail reads by eating. When one dies, its shell stays on the crust and the crust takes it back within days — a graveyard that digests itself, the only monument the room permits, and it is permitted on condition of not lasting.

Then the bird, which is the most nearly a person of anything here, and which you will find yourself watching the way you watch a fire. It does not own the path. It is the path's way of staying. Forty steps on the click, the pause, the strike or the almost-strike, forty steps again. At night, when the membrane and the patina edges take up their dim glow, its pause lengthens to the lung's full minute — that is its sleep, taken standing, on the path, in installments, so that even unconsciousness is entrained. And here is the thing you will only learn by luck or by seasons: the bird is not born in the room. When the old one dies — on the path, mid-pause, a rest that does not end — the tenders come out of the walls and take it apart into the mat in a single night, and within days a young grey bird comes down the root-handholds of the fissure, drawn by the drone through the ground, and walks to the worn circuit, and its first footfall lands on the click. It has been walking on this beat its whole life. The whole upland is the clock; every hollow's birds are raised inside the common time. The room does not raise its own hand. The clock supplies one.

The tenders. You will spend days blaming the whine on your own ears. It migrates. It is never where you look, because it is behind the weave of the walls, inside the braid — and then one dusk you see a pore iris open and a tender come through: thumb-long, the color of the ceiling light, green-gold, as though the room's light had condensed and learned to walk. Its wings are veined like leaves. Its segments are ringed like a stem. If it held still on the wall you would take it for a bud that had dreamed of being a wasp. There are perhaps two hundred of them, and you will never see more than a dozen at once, and their working is the whine — a high stridulation of polishing, their strokes per second a far multiple of the wheel's click, which is why it sounds like a dynamo. It is one. They groom the ear-frill of the lung's spiracle so the drone stays in tune; without them, over years, the room would clog and wheeze. They sweep the pawl's notch of the fibers the pawl loosens — the pawl cuts; they carry away. They buff the piston's sheath, which is why the teak polish neither deepens nor dulls. The wall feeds them: touch a fingertip where one has been working and a pore weeps a bead of sap, and they eat from the room like children at the breast, and they lay their eggs into brood-pockets the weave grows for them, sealed behind them like seeds set in a seedbed.

And once — if you stay long enough, you will see it once — a frog misses its entrance. Mid-bar, one voice is absent from the round. The perch stands empty in the green light. And at dusk the tenders issue from the walls in their full number, the only time the room ever shows it, and through the night they take the dead frog apart fiber by fiber, bone by bone, carrying it down into the warm felt of the mat, and by morning there is no frog and there has never been a frog, and the perch stands polished and vacant, and three nights later — you will not see from where — a young frog is sitting on it, and its first bark lands in the rest-interval, off the beat by the exact old fraction, and the room returns its voice, and the round goes around again, complete.

What the tenders are, you cannot finally say. Insects the heath adopted so long ago that the adoption is complete, or buds it learned to animate — either way, the distinction has dissolved the way the distinction between organ and machine dissolved, at the same depth, in the same warm dark. They are not a species anymore. They are a faculty. They are the heath's sense of touch, grown legs.

You yourself are tolerated the way everything here is tolerated: by being timed. Nothing fears you. The frogs do not stop when you approach; the fish do not dart; the bird's pause does not shorten. You are simply not on the meter, and so for the first hour you move through the room like a rest in the bar, an unnotated silence the performance closes over. And then, degree by degree, it takes you. Your heart locks to the lung's minute. Your steps begin to land on the click. You catch yourself breathing in the piston's four-and-four. The room does not change for you; it changes you into something that does not disturb it, and for as long as you stay you are counted — a temporary resident, a grace note — and when you climb out you are subtracted as cleanly, the level holding, the census restored before your boot leaves the last handhold. The only resident who ever leaves.

So: who lives in the hollow? Count the answers and watch each dissolve. Not the machines — they are the heath's own tissue. Not the animals — they are seats, reprints, notes the bar keeps hitting; every one of them will be carried into the mat and replaced by an identical continuator. Not the tenders — they are the heath's fingers. Not the heath — it does not live in the room; the room is in it, the way a thought is in a mind.

The one tenant who has never once been absent is the rhythm. Every cell in the chamber has been replaced many times over; the piston is new wood holding an old shape; the pattern alone is original. It was sounding before anything now alive in the room existed, and it will be sounding after every one of them has been taken apart into the felt, and it is alive in the only sense the room recognizes: it persists, exactly, by consuming exact replacements. Everything else — frog, fish, eel, snail, midge, tender, bird, you — is something the bar does in order to continue. The hollow's sole inhabitant is a piece of music, immortal, self-performing, and its body is the room.

And not even it lives here alone. You feel it on the climb out, through your soles: the second tremor under the first, kilometers off, locked in antiphase. The tenant of this hollow is one voice of a chorus spread under the whole upland, a single animal made of time, and this room is where you may stand inside one of its heartbeats.

For days afterward your pulse keeps the room's slow minute, and you are spoiled for silence — real silence, which now sounds to you like a stopped machine. Somewhere below the heath the wheel carries one more spoke past the pawl, the drip falls, the frog barks in its gap, the bird's foot comes down on the click.

The tenant is home. It always is.
