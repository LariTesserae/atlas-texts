# What Lives There

The first count of this country's life named the herds, the moths, the skate, the drifters, and, beneath notice, the films. It was not wrong. It was a first count, and every first count makes the same error: it counts what moves at the counter's own tempo. The plateau keeps its life at every tempo there is, and most of it lives too fast or too slow to be seen by someone merely walking through.

Walk slower. Or don't walk at all. Here is what lives here when you look at the tempo of the place itself.

## The Fast

The fastest life on the plateau lives in the rime. Each needle of frost, plated onto the stones before dawn, carries a dust of cells that froze into it as the fog condensed — a flora that wakes with the first thin warmth, divides once in the hour of the melt-lace, and is gone by dusk: drained into the Mesh, dried to husk, refrozen. A whole life between midnight and noon. Nothing here envies them; nothing here knows about them; they are the top note of the country's chord, and they get through more living in their one day than the slow ones manage in a century. Count them first, because they will not be here to count later.

The gardens' black felts have a ceiling-country. Between the packed dark leaves and the root-mat runs a space the height of a thumb, warm by this country's standards, wet, absolutely dark — and inhabited. Blind pallid springtails. A mite armored like a seed. A grub that grazes the felt-roots, and a smaller nameless thing that hunts the grub. None of them has ever seen the sky; none would survive the seeing. When the herds graze a felt down to the mat, the ceiling-folk retreat into the roots and wait out the bare season the way everything here waits: for them a grazing is a glaciation, punctual as weather, and the regrowth is the re-creation of the world. Generations pass between grazings. The felt-floor is where the plateau keeps its quick ones — a whole fast ecology folded into the stored warmth, and the oxen eat a little of the ceiling with every mouthful of leaves, and the felt regrows the ceiling with the leaves, and the balance, like all the balances here, holds.

## The Pans

The black pans hold more than stillness. In brine too thick to freeze swim the pan-swimmers — small, translucent, straining the films' loose cells and the wind-borne dust out of the dark water. When a pan concentrates past even their tolerance, they encyst, and the cysts are light enough for the wind, and so the wind over the Between carries seed that no moth carries. Every pan has its swimmers. No swimmer has ever traveled between pans as a swimmer. Their sky is one dimness, their world one black lens, their continuity the cyst-wall and the storm season.

And in the deepest pans there is a sleeper. Leathery, fist-sized, it buries itself in the salt-mud and does not wake for years. It wakes only when the storm season comes up over the Rim and the pans run briefly dilute; in those few weeks it feeds and mates and buries itself again before the salts climb. It has never seen the ordinary plateau — the fog-tides, the dusk-fizz, the ticking nights. Its entire life is the exception. It is centuries old and has been awake perhaps a year of it, in stolen weeks. There is a way of living that consists only of the exception, and something here lives it.

## The Banks

The perennial snowbanks in the cirque shade are inhabited through and through. They are dark because a black alga blooms in their interior, drinking the dim light that filters a body's depth into the snow; and threading the banks, grazing the bloom, go the ice-worms — pale, finger-long, living their whole lives inside the one substance the rest of the country cannot hold onto. At dusk they rise to the surface in their thousands, though no garden is near: because at dusk the whole Rim hums faintly with the same tension the reeds spend below, and the worms come up for the fizz the way the moths do, to glean what the charged air fixes onto the snow's skin. Sometimes a drifter's trailing line, pouring off the heights on the evening lanes, combs a bank, and comes away with worms.

Some of those live. The sky gets its colonists from the snow.

## The Lines

Because the drifters' lines are not bare. A charged line streaming ions seeds fog onto itself, as the gardens do, and so each line plates with rime; and where there is rime there is grazing; and where there is grazing there are grazers. The lines carry their own small fauna: line-mites, a thread-thin snail no wider than the line it lives on, the bank-worms' sky-going cousins, and at the lines' tips, where the charge is strongest, a tiny clean folk that live inside the constant faint corona and eat what it fixes. They are born on the lines and die on the lines. Many have never in their lives been lower than a hundred feet above the world. Their ground is the line; their weather is the lane; their entire history of migration is written in brushings, when two drifters' lanes cross in the dark and the lines trail through one another. You can tell the drifters apart by their riders — each old drifter carries its own shade of line-folk, as each garden has its own accent of moths. Whether the line-folk know they are riding has never been asked in any grammar they keep.

## The Slow Ones

Out in the Between, grazing the lichen off the blockfields at a body-length a day, go the stonebacks.

The lichen first: it crusts the old slabs black on black and grows a hair in a century. It is the oldest garden in the country. When the Mesh at long last turns a slab, the crust goes down into the dark and the bare face comes up, raw for a few hundred years until the crust finds it again. This is the only event the blockfields know.

The stonebacks are low and broad and rinded like old frost, and they are older than anything that walks. They never drink — no stoneback has ever drunk. At dawn they lick the rime from the lee of the slabs, and the morning's whole yield is a thimbleful, and a thimbleful is enough at that tempo. You can rest a hand on one's rind and count its heartbeats against your own pulse and give up. When anything anywhere in the cell stamps alarm, a stoneback stops — and a stopped stoneback is a slab: lichen-crusted, frost-plated, motionless in the only channel that matters. It has spent two hundred years practicing being a stone, and it is very good at it, and the hoar-skate, which reads the ground for a living, slides past and files it under *geology*.

And the herds, whose maps are made of tremor and are therefore starved for landmarks, use the stonebacks as datum points. The old cows drum the slow ones' positions into the nightly conversation, and because a stoneback moves a hair in a season, the map stays honest. The herds correct themselves against the slow ones. They are the country's walking survey stakes, the only moving features the tremor-maps admit — and whether they know it is a question asked at the wrong tempo to be answered.

## The Remembering

The herds remember more than their conversation lets on. A herd's drumming has an accent; you can tell them apart a day's walk off by their stamps alone. The old cows carry the deep map: where the lace ran in the storm season forty years gone, which garden failed once and for how long, which crossings their grandmothers made. When an old one dies, the herd stands a ring around her and drums — all night, if the night is long — stamping the year's whole news into the ground around the body: map, weather, lanes, the drifters' calving. Whether they drum it for the ground or for themselves is not a distinction the plateau makes. Then they walk on, and the skates come, and the bones blacken and frost over, and over the centuries the Mesh sorts them into its coarse borders like any other stones. There is no cairn. But the cell-borders run faintly richer where the herds have always died, and the felt over an old dying-ground grows a deeper black, and that is the whole of their monument, which is the kind this country permits.

The skate, meanwhile, hears the gardens fire at dusk through the ground — the discharge arriving garden by garden, west to east, as the light fails along the plateau's length — and navigates by that slow ring of bells. It is one of the very few who know, every evening, exactly how large the world is.

And the drifters' deaths are the Between's only feast. The old ones dim and sag over years until a morning comes when the lane no longer holds them, and they settle onto the empty Mesh, days from any garden. The stonebacks converge over the following years, and the carcass feeds the slow ones for a decade and salts the ground — and some of the brine veins begin there. A few of the black pans are the graves of drifters, though it takes a thousand years for the grave to fill with dark water and admit it. The drifters seed the gardens in life and the ground in death. Nothing here is wasted, least of all an ending.

## The Listeners

All of these you could find, given enough dusks. The last inhabitants you could walk past for a season and never know — partly because there are fewer of them than there are drifters, and partly because being overlooked is their art.

Call them the listeners.

They walk the coarse borders of the Mesh, on the big stable edging-stones the frost set, and their footfalls read as the Mesh's own ticking; they have learned to step the way needle-ice settles. The skate reads the ground for a living and files them under *weather*. Everything here that is good at listening has considered them and decided they are not worth answering.

They drum, as the oxen drum — but the grammar is theirs, and it is older than any herd's map, patient and redundant, built to survive relay across days of empty ground. They can say in stamps what the herds say, and some things the herds cannot. They can say *tomorrow*. They can say *the year the lace failed*. They can say *wait*. The herds' conversation is all present tense; the listeners' grammar has a past and a future, and that is the entire difference between a herd and a people.

They make nothing. This is the rule they keep above all others, and it costs them everything a person elsewhere would use to live: no tool, no shelter, no cairn, no fire. The reeds would burn, grudgingly, in this thin air. They do not burn them. Fire is the one loud thing left, and they are not it. Their clothing is the oxen's shed felt, gathered where it mats on the cushions, already felted by the animals' own weather. Their water is the rime, licked from the lee of stones at dawn, like the stonebacks. Their food is the herds' dead, the cyst-dust off the pan-edges, the felt-floor's gleanings. Everything they use was the world's before it was theirs, and goes back without a seam.

Their children learn the borders before they learn words. A listener child can stamp *grazing* and *nothing yet* before it can whisper either, and the whispering matters less: the air will not carry a voice, so their spoken language is a whisper-register, used face to face, in the felts, at noon, beside the melt-lace — the one hour the country permits a voice, when the water is loud enough by this country's standards to carry words a body-length, which is as far as the listeners believe words should go.

At dusk they come to the vein-crossings and lay their palms flat on the dark seams of brine. It is three acts in one gesture. It grounds them: a body gathers charge all afternoon like everything else, and the listeners are the only ones on the plateau who spend it by choice, before the reeds spend theirs. It is how they listen: the whole works runs through the veins, and a trained palm at dusk can read the gardens firing west to east like a slow ring of bells through stone. And it is — there is no better word for it — how they greet the ground. On discharge evenings the drifters' lines come down to their uplifted palms, hair-fine, and take the day's charge off their bodies the way they take it off the reeds. The listeners are the only ones who are plugged into the sky as well as the ground, and they pay for it in stillness.

If they have a work, it is the long map. Herds carry the map a lifetime deep; the listeners carry it deeper than herds — which garden failed two hundred years back and never came again, where the veins are silting, which lanes the drifters have abandoned and which new ones the calves are taking. When a garden fails, the moths learn it slowly and the herds learn it by wasted crossings; the listeners drum the correction ahead of the news, garden to garden, herd to herd, a rumor traveling at the speed of a walking person — which is, on this plateau, at this tempo, fast enough. They are the works' memory between memories. A letter the plateau writes to its future, carried by hand, because carrying it any other way would be making, and they make nothing.

Where they came from, the country offers only its four verbs. Nothing is made; everything is sorted, grown, stored, or discharged. They were not made. The rest the ground keeps. They have been here as long as anything with memory remembers: the herds' maps include them, the drifters' lanes account for them, and the films beneath certain vein-crossings conduct in patterns that match the span of a palm.

They die in the Between, usually, because the Between is most of the world, and they lie down in a cell of the Mesh without marker, and the frost takes them, and the Mesh sorts them a hair a year into the coarse borders, with the oxen's bones and the stonebacks' rinds. No cairn, no name. The map passes to the ones who walked beside them, and the dusk practice passes with it, and that is the whole of their estate.

## The Keeping

So the census, done at the country's own tempo: the rime-flora living their one day; the ceiling-folk waiting out their punctual glaciations; the sleeper holding itself for the exception; the worms rising for the fizz; the line-folk who have never touched the world; the stonebacks practicing stonehood; the herds drumming their lifetimes of map; the skate taking the world's exact size every dusk; the drifters overhead, seeding as they go, feeding as they fall; the films beneath everything, keeping the books.

And the listeners, palm flat at the crossings, spending the day into the ground on purpose — grown, not made, the way everything here is grown; the only component of the works that knows it is one.

The works ran for ages without a witness and did not need one. It does not need one now. But the rule of the place is that the slowest processes are the strongest, and one of the very slowest was at work all along: the frost teaching the stones, the stones teaching the brine, the brine teaching the gardens — and the gardens, at the last, teaching the dark to be listened to. It took the whole history of the Mesh to grow someone who would lay a palm flat and feel the shift end. On the plateau's own clock it happened a moment ago: a hundred thousand dusk-fizzes, the blink of the slow eye.

Nothing answers, here. That was always true, and the listeners are not the answer.

They are the asking — kept, the way the ground keeps everything, at the ground's own tempo, forever.
