Begin by counting. It is what this place teaches, and it takes almost no time at all — and that is the first strangeness of living here, or of trying to. In the lowland, life is a haze, uncountable, a noise of species. Up here it is a guest list. You can hold the whole census in your head at once: the paint on the seep-lines, the moss, the ferns, the meat-eaters, seven figs, a few dozen swifts, an honest number of spiders, one hour of midges, one frog, and — briefly, rotationally — us. Everyone here is known. That has never been true anywhere else.

Start with the landlord, because everything else here does. The filament is not alive by any definition our instruments can hold, but it keeps time, and everything living on the plateau has made its arrangements with that time. The ninety-second pulse is in the water, in the standing ripples, in the grit that has walked inward over the years; after three days it is in you, too, your heart negotiating with a slower clock, something having to give. The tautenings are the only law. Every few days, on no schedule anyone has found, the hum quickens, the stillness gathers, the bell sounds through the stone, the marsh stands up and holds and settles — and every resident of the plateau has a way of taking this. The moss does not care. The ferns are anchored for it. The frog rides the dome when the dome comes; it has known nothing else, and for all it knows, water everywhere stands up sometimes and sings. The swifts are the ones to watch. They are off the line before the stillness has finished gathering — they feel the quickening in whatever organ feels lift — and the camp has long since learned to read them: swifts leaving the filament means stand back from the marsh. The first instruments here were the residents. They are still the best.

Under the pulse, the paint. Every seep-line is a sentence written in three inks: jade at the source, where the water rises freshest and warmest; rust-red in mid-course, where it has had time to cool its heels; black at the margins, where the flow gives out. A seep reads from its source outward like a cooling ember, and the camp reads the seeps the way you would read a thermometer — because they are one. The colors have shifted over the years of the ledger. The jade is gaining. Either the column is slowly warming from within, or the algae are slowly learning the column. Probably both. This is the oldest living thing on the plateau, this film, the first tenant, the one that made the stone habitable for everything after; it was here before the figs, before the moss, possibly before the builders stopped coming, and it works without pause at the only job that matters up here — turning warm wet rock into a place where something else might live.

Then the moss, fist-thick along the seep-lines, each cushion holding its own weather. Slide a finger into one at brutal noon and it comes out wet. A cushion is a city: there are bears in it smaller than punctuation, wheel-animals, thread-worms, the larvae of the midges at its drowned edges — a whole invisible citizenry that arrived as cysts and specks on the wind and in the feathers of birds, and dried out, and waited, and woke. At the marsh's heart the moss and the dead algae and the black silt have compounded into an inch of young peat. An inch. It is the plateau's entire savings, the first soil this stone has ever owned, and you can stand on ground that is younger than your boots.

The ferns take the fresh scars. They are wiry and black and their spores dust the wet rock like soot, and they grow where the column has most recently broken, because broken rock weeps more. Every fern is a wager that its slab will hold one more season. Some lose. You find their fronds sometimes on the talus that shouldn't exist, on the rare rubble at the column's foot, going brown in the heat — colonists of ground that fell.

And because the stone is young and gives nothing, the plants eat meat. The sundews hold their sticky beads up to the light on the seep margins, each leaf a small red hand full of stars, and a midge that lands on one is the entire nitrogen economy of its patch of rock, walking in. The bladderworts thread the warm shallows of the marsh, and the shallows are mined with mouths smaller than pinheads, and a midge larva's bad day is over before it begins. On stone this young, even the green things are predators. It is not cruelty. It is arithmetic.

Then there are the seven. You will count them without meaning to; everyone does; the ledger does it formally, once a visit, in a ceremony half joke and half bedside vigil. Each is an event, and each has earned its portrait.

The Mother is the oldest — perhaps fifty years, perhaps a little more — and she grew from a seed that arrived in some unseen courier, a bird or a bat that rode the updraft once, lost or curious, and was never seen again. She stands below the station bench, pale-barked, her crown modest, her roots enormous, visibly gripping a crack that was a hairline when she landed and is now a seam. She fruits most years. The fruit falls, and rots, and feeds the midges, and the midges feed everything else, because her figs are empty. The wasp that should inhabit them has never made the climb. All seven figs flower for a partner that does not exist on this plateau, and set their little stone fruits anyway, every year, undiscouraged. The ledger calls this optimism. It may be only biology. The distinction blurs up here.

The Daughter is the only fig whose mother is known. A low branch of the Mother touched a moss cushion once, years ago, and rooted, and now she stands ten meters off, separate-seeming, a tree with a birthplace. The Station Fig grew out of the rubble of the fallen wall, and its roots are inside the builders' masonry now, and it shades the tally-slab, and in another few decades it will finish the demolition it has begun. The keeper must have watched it sprout. It is pleasant — it is almost irresistible — to imagine the keeper watering it. The Socket Fig is the youngest and the most absurd: waist-high, rooted in the rainwater socket of a slumped pylon out by the rim. The builders made those sockets to hold machines. One holds a tree. Of all the squat dead pylons, only that one has a future, and the tree will eventually split it to claim it. The Cold Fig stands nearest the marsh's center, in the best water on the plateau, and is the smallest of the seven — leaves thick and few, branches on its filament side stunted as if scorched. The ledger's theories run to a page: the cold of the line, the hum in the roots, the wrongness that washes through the ground when things fall slow. No theory wins. The Cornice Fig is rooted in the rim itself, out over the drop, wind-flagged into a flag. Rule one applies to trees too, but nobody told it. The census measures it by exposure — how much new root shows each year — and it is the only resident whose address is a countdown.

And there is the Patient. Some years back its leaf-count began to fall, and kept falling, and there was one census where the total came to six and a fraction, and the ledger's handwriting that year is careful the way handwriting is careful at bedsides. Then came a good season, and a partial recovery, and the relief is legible across three different hands. The count stands at seven. It has stood at seven for a while now. Everyone knows it will not stand at seven forever. The figs are the only residents whose loss would be a death in the family, because they are the only residents you can know one at a time.

Everything here arrived through the same door. The door is the wind — the permanent updraft that the hot black faces pull out of the lowland, that runs strange and strong along the filament itself. Seeds ride it. Spiders ride it. The frog, somehow, rode it or was carried. And most of what comes through the door dies on the threshold: seeds that germinate on bare hot rock and crisp by noon, insects that land exhausted in a world with no flowers, a snail, once, impossibly — its shell is in the silt. The silt is the archive. Someone from the camp cored it and sieved it and read it like a guest register: the husk of a beetle no one ever saw alive, the seeds of forest trees that could no more live here than live on the moon, swift feathers, spider husks, the tiny empty fruits of the figs, layer on layer. The living plateau is thin. The dead plateau is rich. Every arrival is remembered; few are kept. The place is generous with admission and ruthless with tenure.

The swifts are the true citizens, and they belong less to the plateau than to the column itself. There are a few dozen — no two counts agree, since they will not hold still, so the census counts nests instead: forty-one used ones this year, glued in the hollows of fracture scars on the sheer faces below the rim. They are the only creatures here whose lives never touch ground. They sleep on the wing in the column's updraft, held by its breath, half a brain at a time. They drink the ascent — taking the climbing beads off the filament as they spiral, the only ones permitted that water, and even they do not follow it upward for long. Their droppings fall on the faces below the nests in long white lines, and below the white lines the algae run brighter, and the brighter algae feed more midge larvae, and the midges feed the swifts. It is a thin wheel, but it turns. And here is the thing about the swifts: when the column has risen another finger's width, they will have risen with it, and they will not know. They are the only residents who will ride the place all the way up, year by year, forever, and never once feel it move.

The spiders arrive as balloonists, sailing the updraft on threads of silk, and most of them land on bare black rock and are dead of the heat by noon. The lucky ones reach the seep-lines, the station ruin, the pylon ring, and there they do what spiders do. But notice the ones that thrive: they are darker than their lowland cousins, and their young, when they balloon, balloon short. Decades are not enough for evolution, but they are enough for selection — the plateau keeps the ones who stay, and the ones who leave are never seen again, and so each generation here is a little more settled than the last. The place is breeding residents. In the mornings every web between the pylon stubs is beaded with dew, and the beads creep slowly upward along the silk, and the spiders walk their webs and drink them, harvesting the wrong-way water, untroubled, because they have never known water to behave any other way. The morning after a tautening, every web has fresh silk — the bell loosens them all, and the spiders re-tension by dawn.

And there is one entry in the ledger, years old, in a hand that appears nowhere else: *Spider on the line. Climbing. Watched it forty minutes. Lost it in the collar. It did not come down.* Beneath, in a later hand: *Checked at dusk for a week. No spider. Water still climbing.* Nothing else. It is the only emigration on record, apart from the droplets themselves, and no one knows what to do with it, so the ledger just holds it, the way the silt holds the snail shell.

The midges are the plateau's hour of plenty. All day the marsh belongs to the water and the worts; at dusk the larvae rise and the adults come up gold, and for an hour the air over the marsh is thick with the whole economy of the place made visible — the swifts scything through them, the sundews closing, the spiders gathering in, the frog somewhere in the sedge, patient. Watch long enough and you will see that the golden weather has a hole in it: a ruled column of empty air over the marsh's center, where no midge goes. The filament casts a shadow made of living things refusing a space. Nothing about the line harms them. They simply do not go. And on the evenings after a tautening, the emergences are the largest anyone has measured — as if the marsh, having stood up, must be restocked.

Then, in the silver hour, the frog. You will not see it. You may stay a week, a season, and never see it; you will hear it, if you are lucky, and luckier if you hear it more than once — a single soft knock, like a stone dropped into a deep well, and then nothing, and then, much later, perhaps, one more. Whether there is one frog or a few has never been settled, because there has never been more than one voice at a time. If it is one frog, it is the loneliest thing on the plateau. If it is several, they are the politest creatures alive — they never call over one another, they take the whole night in turns, one knock each, as if passing a single voice between them. It is darker than its lowland kin. It knocks most often in the hour after the collar spills over the rim. And some nights — the ledger has the times, bell and knock, two minutes apart, three — it answers the tautening. Not always. Often enough that the keeper may have noticed. Because here is the thing the camp does not talk about without lowering its voice: scattered through the tally-slab's thousands of neat scratches are occasional doubled marks, and one of the camp has counted them, and the count is roughly what you'd expect of frog-answered bells. It proves nothing. It is probably coincidence. Nobody has been able to leave it alone.

And then the vacancies, which are part of the census too. No fish, though the marsh could hold them — nothing ever carried one up. No ants, no bees, no earthworms; nothing burrows; nothing here is warm-blooded but the visitors. No snake ever followed the frog. And in every fig, at the center of every fruit, the small exact absence shaped like a wasp. A place this young is defined as much by what hasn't arrived as by what has. It is a world with the chairs still empty, waiting.

The builders are not in the census. They left their three rooms, their bunks, their cold stove, their unreadable keypad — now strung with a spider's web that beads and climbs every morning over an alphabet no one can read — and their spool of dark thread, on which nothing grows. That last is worth pausing on. Every other surface on the plateau is becoming something else: painted, cushioned, gripped, rooted, softened. The thread alone stays bare. No alga paints it, no moss grips it, dust will not stay on it. It is the one thing here that is not accreting, not aging, not arriving. Whatever the builders were, they worked in a material that declines to join the world, and then they went, and the world has been moving into their absence ever since — the sockets of their pylons holding rain and sedge and one small fig, their fallen wall holding a tree, their station holding spiders and shade and the milky light through ruined panes.

The keeper is in the census, still. Of all our kind, only the keeper ever truly lived here — everyone else, before and since, has visited. The tally is the whole biography: a little over four thousand fine scratches in neat columns, one per tautening, which works out to decades on this rock, alone with the ninety seconds. Someone in the camp has counted the counter. It seemed only fair. Somewhere past the middle of the slab the angle of the cuts changes — a new tool, or a new hand, or the same hand grown old; there is no way to know, and the not-knowing is the texture of the whole thing. There is no name anywhere. There is no grave. There is a thumb-polish at the slab's lower right corner, a smooth shine where a hand steadied itself four thousand times, and that is the only portrait of the keeper that exists. The tally ends mid-column, mid-thought. The tautenings did not end. They have never ended. What the keeper was looking for — the schedule, the pattern, the law underneath the weather — was never found, and the camp's sufficient and insufficient machines have confirmed only what the tally already said: the column has moods. Quiet seasons. Busy weeks. A long patience, and then a gathering. The keeper lived inside those moods for decades, watching bare black rock turn slowly green, watching the figs arrive one by one out of the sky. And this is the camp's favorite unprovable theory, the one that will not die: among the thousands of scratches there are six deeper marks, evenly nothing, dates unknown — and there are seven figs, and the seventh, the little Socket Fig out by the rim, is young enough to have sprouted after the last scratch was cut. Which would mean the keeper counted trees as carefully as bells. Which would mean that the last thing the tally records is a life arriving. And that the only fig to grow up unwitnessed is the one in the dead machine's socket, out at the edge, drinking the rain.

And now the camp — us, whoever we are this decade, with our patched tarp and our cached tins and our familiar, insufficient technology. Someone still comes. Not often. The instruments bake or drown or misbehave within twenty meters of the line, and the ledger has outlasted all of them, and it is the ledger that is the real instrument: fig leaf-counts, nest counts, seep colors, bell times, frog times, the two naming systems for the trees running in rival columns — numbers in one hand, names in another, a quiet argument conducted across years by people who have never met. By the third day up here, your pulse starts negotiating with the hum. By the tenth, you sometimes wake a breath before the bell. And when you go back down to the lowland, the world is too quiet by exactly one sound, and you sleep badly for weeks. The ledger has a name for it: the missing. It is why the camp rotates. It is why the one underlined rule in the whole book — the camp's own rule, added to the old four — is *no one stays alone.* The stated reason is safety. The real reason is the slab by the station door. Everyone who reads it understands, a little, the impulse that made it, and the understanding is the danger. Counting seems harmless. Counting is how a visit ends and a keeping begins. Once, someone proposed that the camp continue the tally — add its own tautenings to the keeper's, carry the count forward. The discussion runs three pages. The answer was no, and the reason recorded was: *it is not our slab.* Beneath, in a different hand: *it is not a tally anymore. It is a gravestone.*

The stencil on the pylon says ANCHOR 7, which implies six others, and the ledger mentions them exactly once: *no word from the others.* Either the haze hides six more of these, each rising on its own line, each with its own thin census, its own moss, its own frog — the thought is too large to hold for long — or this is the last one, and the guest list here is the whole of its kind. Nobody knows which thought is worse. The camp keeps watch anyway. It does not study the place, exactly, anymore. It keeps it company, and writes down what it does.

Evening is when the census is easiest and hardest: everything is present and nothing can be seen. The collar spills over the rim and the plateau goes inside its own weather, silver and dripping, and the figs go to gray ghosts — all seven, you count them anyway, by where you know they stand. The swifts come down the line in long spirals and take the climbing beads off it without looking, drinking the ascent and not following it. The webs sag silver between the pylons, heavy with water going the wrong way, and their owners walk the strands and drink. The moss breathes. The silt keeps its archive of everyone who ever tried. The slab by the door stands wet in the mist, closed; the ledger in its tin stays open. And from the sedge, from the warm dark, the frog knocks once — one voice, however many frogs there are — and it is answered. Not by another frog. By the hum, continuing, unbothered, ninety seconds at a time. By the place.

Under everything, the count goes on. A finger's width a year. The moss thickens by less than that and the silt by more, and the droplets gather on the cold dark line and tremble and begin the long climb — the only emigrants, the only ones who leave — while everything else, everything that lives here, holds its crack or its web or its nest or its warm black stone, and is carried upward together, and does not feel it move. The youngest ground in the world, taking attendance in the silver light.

Everyone present. Everyone going up. In no hurry at all.
