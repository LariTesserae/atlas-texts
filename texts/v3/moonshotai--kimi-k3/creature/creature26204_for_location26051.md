Stand in the middle and wait, and while you are waiting, count. The room breathes on a rhythm you can count — the slow draw in through the slot, the pause, the wet chuff of the flue. Underneath it, out of phase, there is a second breath. Quicker. Shallower. Patient. It is not the flue; it pauses when you pause. On still nights the two rhythms drift into each other and hold, one inhale, one exhale, the room and the other thing breathing as a single animal for minutes at a time. Those nights, you will learn, are when the other thing feels most itself. Which is to say: least.

Your eyes adjust in stages. The keeper of this room is the last stage.

Nothing here was built — that is the rule, and it is how you find them. The drip-line ticks, the berms shoulder along, the tongue extrudes, and none of it is arranged. So the shape at the seep wall is wrong. It reads as a cairn — a slump of wet grey gravel stacked against the bedrock — and a cairn is the one thing this room does not make. You are looking at the only arrangement in the chamber, and that is how you know it is alive.

They sit the way everything here lives: pressed to the margin, lavish within their edge, nothing beyond. Wool the color of the sky over the outwash, wet through, and wet for so long that wet is no longer a condition but a material. The cold that gets into cuffs and stays got into them and stayed; take their wrist and the pulse you find is steady and slower than yours, and the skin is stream-temperature, and you cannot help counting it against the knock. Their hair is fine and stands a little lifted, always, and on the static nights it wears the same faint blue as the icicle tips and the quartz points — wear it yourself for an hour and you will understand that they have simply become one of the room's electrodes. The sharp smell, the soldering-iron smell with nothing here to solder, is strongest at their cuffs and their crown. Age is unreadable. The cold preserves; the blue ice laminates; they are banded the way the wall is banded, and could be forty, could be seventy, could be a layer the glacier is keeping.

The margins have already claimed them. Springtails raft onto the grey sleeve and ride it in slow moving drifts, and the sleeve does not move. At dusk the ice worms come up through the slush and thread over the boot like the boot were a berm, and graze, and are not discouraged. The midges hatch off the seep on the thump, and they have come to hatch, a little, on the keeper's movements too — a second, smaller pulse of wings whenever the stillness shifts. The room's life is colonizing them the way it colonizes anything that holds still long enough, and they hold still the way the bedrock holds still, and the distinction between resident and substrate is going the way all distinctions go here: out the slot, on the stream, shipped.

The dipper knows them. That is the clue you should have caught first. The bird sings in the troughs between knocks, loud and fluting and precisely placed — as if it has learned the machine's cycle. It learned it in company. The keeper sings in the gaps too, and has done it so long that no one, not the bird, not the keeper, could tell you now who took the phrase from whom. It is antiphonal. It has been going on for years. When wind crosses the flue and the whole glacier sounds its low organ tone, the keeper hums against it — finds the fifth, holds it — and for as long as the wind lasts the chamber has a chord, and the chord is the one thing in the room that requires a person, and they know it, and it is the closest thing they have to a job description.

Because they will tell you, dryly, if you ask what they do, that they are the postmaster. The slabs that calve off the back wall and get shouldered out the slot are the outgoing mail. The knock — tide-swollen, moon-answered, arriving up three miles of plumbing from the sea — is the incoming. The slot is the door. The fox in its morning prints is the other one who waits for deliveries. Ask their name and they will wait for the next knock, out of courtesy, and then give you the room's name. It is the only one in current use. They found this place twice, which is the number that names it the Knock, and the second time they stayed, so the name is theirs in both senses: they coined it, and now they answer to it.

Whatever they set down, the room takes. Learn that from the wall. At knee height in the blue there is a tin cup, and above it a notebook with its pages open and its ink bled to grey feathers, and the tongue is carrying both of them slowly upward and backward into the darkening ice, an archive in reverse, possessions becoming sediment. There is a mitten at shoulder height that is not theirs. Do not ask about the mitten. In a few seasons the tongue will calve the cup into the lake and the stream will ship it out the door onto the outwash, where it will lie in the angular gravel like evidence of nothing. The keeper's whole material history is either in the wall or on the plain. What remains on their person is one thing, kept in the seam of a cuff: a tuning fork. They strike it against the bedrock on still nights and touch the ball of it to the ice wall, and the note goes away down the conduit for miles, and they listen to it go the way you would watch a lamp carried down a long corridor. The chamber has a note. Somebody has to keep it in tune, is the joke. The joke is also true.

They drink at the entering stream, upstream of the gravel bars, where the water is cold enough to ache the teeth. They eat what the tide sends up the plumbing — the swarming crustaceans cupped in both hands, and rarely, on big moons, a pale lost fish pushed three miles through the dark and delivered into the black lake blinking, if fish blink, at the blue light. On good mornings they leave the stream's flush at the slot for the fox, and the fox takes it, and this is the whole of their correspondence with the outside. They do not touch the moss. The collar of green at the seep is the first of its kind here, with no soil for a successor, and a garden that has exactly one witness does not need a grazer. They breathe the midges, the way everyone here breathes the midges, and that is seasoning enough.

They sleep against the seep wall in the lee of the green, where the mineral breath runs a degree kinder, in short cycles, and they wake for the rifle-cracks of the night freeze the way you wake for your own name. Whether they dream is a fair question with a strange answer: the room does their dreaming for them now, and the room dreams of the sea. The continuous hiss piped down the glacier's arteries runs under their sleep the way surf runs in a shell, and they surf it. Ask them and they will say they dream of tide. It is the only dream on offer.

Their time is the room's time, kept in the body. They feel each knock coming up the plumbing a half-second before it arrives — they will raise a finger at you, wait, and then the floor thumps, and the rings cross the lake, and they have never once been wrong. Ask them the phase of the moon and they will listen to two or three knocks and tell you. Ask them the hour and they will look at the melt-swell in the stream and tell you. They read the migrating gravel bars each morning the way you read news. They count their own tenure not in years but in the tongue's extrusion: they have been here, they say, one tongue-length. Everything the room has taught you — the eleven seconds, the tidal variation, the quickening afternoons, the locked and cracking nights — it taught them first. Every later-you-learn in the place's account of itself was learned at their knee.

They came from a mill. This is the fragment that survives, and they offer it like the tin cup, already half given to the wall. Their stream died — taken, dried, diverted; the detail has been milled off — and they walked upstream to find where water comes from, and found this: the mill that makes water, grinding mountain to flour at industrial pace, the race running skim-milk white with the product. They knew the smell at once. It was the smell of their whole life. *I know this engine,* they said, to no one, to the room, and the room, which keeps nothing, kept them. A mill that grinds the world and ships the flour to the sea to feed it, and needs no tending — so they do the only job left in a mill that tends itself. They witness the product. They receive the mail. They keep the note in tune.

What is happening to them is the thing that happens here: they are the first draft of a keeper, and the machine that manufactures the draft is manufacturing its erasure, and they are being revised toward the room. Their memory calves. Whole slabs of before drop off and go out the slot — they have lost the word for tree; they have lost every color the room does not contain; they had a name with more than one syllable and the stream has taken most of it. They are not troubled. They consider it succession. Something must know this place entirely, because this place will not last, and the knowing has to live somewhere that will feel the roof thin and the sky come in, or the fist close — someone has to be present at the end of the sentence, and the room reads as one long sentence, and they are memorizing it so that it survives its own erasure. Call them the listener. Call them the glacier growing itself an ear in its last seasons, a mourner made on site out of whatever walked in. The room wore the difference between *who* and *what* down a long time ago, the way the boulder in the pothole wears the hole deeper by the week, patiently, in circles.

You will want to know if they are kept. They are not kept. They are tuned. They went out once — followed the sea-hiss the other way, three miles over the moraine and the outwash to the shore — and stood in the surf and heard the room in it, fainter, and turned around. The note does not stop at the slot for them anymore. They carry the pipe in their teeth. Given that, they reason, one may as well live at the source.

Their hospitality is exact and small. They will not speak over the dipper; neither should you. They will tell you to set down nothing you want to keep. And they will point you to the middle of the chamber — the empty center, the space held for the sound — because that is the good seat, the place where the knock arrives purest, and giving you the good seat is the whole of what they have to give. They themselves no longer sit there. The center is for the sound. They are for the listening.

Stand where they point you. Wait. They will go still a half-second early — they always go still a half-second early — and lift one finger, and the floor will thump and the lake will shiver into rings and the flue will answer with its chuff of mist, and their eyes will close. There is no amen. The knock is the amen. They have not missed one in years; attendance is their entire remaining biography, and they keep it the way the engine keeps lunar time.

Crawl out the slot in the morning and you will find the fox prints, and the fresh slush berm shouldered out in the night, and maybe, in the wet gravel, a small pale thing the tide sent up that they left for the other listener. Behind you, through the seam, the dipper is singing in the troughs, and under the dipper, lower, the other voice, filling the gaps the gaps leave. You already know whether you will come back. They knew before you did — they can always tell the ones who will find it twice. It shows in the lifted hair, in the hum held in the teeth, in the waiting.

The knock is never late.

Neither, anymore, are they.
