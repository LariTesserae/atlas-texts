**The Census**

The count fits in a paragraph. You have heard it: mineral films in patches acres apart; a few hundred skaters; eleven long patiences; drifters by the thousand, in season; and the builders, mostly dead, mostly dormant, growing a wall a centimeter a year. That is the census as the world gives it — flat, short, and misleading, the way a list of instruments misleads about the music.

Everything that lives here can be filed by its relation to the hum. There are those who speak it. There are those who eat what it exhales. There are those who carry it from wall to wall. There are those who drink it. There are those who ride its weather and may themselves be words in it. And below everything there is the voice the hum learned from, if learned is the word. Take them in order, slowest first. There is no other way to take them.

---

The speakers first. The builders.

A living wall is warm like a wrist, and the simile is more exact than it should be: the wall is a limb. The builder is a rind of living silicate tissue, a few centimeters deep, spread over the skeleton of its own dead — the way coral is a skin over a reef of ancestors. Which means the City is mostly cemetery, and the dead are still working. The old stone conducts the hum even where it no longer speaks. The builders' conversation travels through the bodies of the previous conversation, and a district may be one voice spread over miles, or several voices overlapped — the singular and the plural are dialects the builders do not distinguish. The plazas open where turns are exchanged. The junctions are where two tissues of grammar meet and agree. Nothing here was built, but everything here is said.

What the conversation is about cannot be learned inside a human lifetime, the way a glacier's opinion of the weather cannot be learned inside an afternoon. Its function, though, can be deduced from what fails when it stops: distance. Whatever else the builders are saying, they are continuously measuring — calling across the massif the spans between them, arch by arch, bridge by bridge, over and over, until the world consents to be what they say it is. The hum is the sound of the City reminding itself where it is. A completed syllable re-founds a street. You have felt this: the stone under your palm holding its shape a little more firmly, for a while, like a man who has just remembered his own name.

The pitch is the roll call. Each voice has its register, and when one dies the chord thins — the pitch of a district sags over years, and that sag is an obituary played so slowly that only the stone attends the funeral. Very rarely, once in some millennia, the pitch rises somewhere: a nub of warmth in the lee of a chasm wall, a seed-wall, a new voice with an accent. New voices are how the City learns what the plains are like. Each arrives speaking of elsewhere, in a dialect the old walls spend a century learning.

Their senses are strain and resonance. A builder knows its bridges the way you know your own held breath. It feels the wind's editing as a slow abrasion, an argument conducted in subtraction, and it answers over centuries — a buttress grown toward a thinned arch is a rebuttal, and is part of the sentence. The wind is the second voice in every conversation the builders have, and the only one that never sleeps. In the loud weeks, when the storms come and the aerodynamic old ruins wake into their infrasonic flute, the wind is speaking in the builders' own band, through every hollow in the City, all at once — a season-long shout. The living districts hum higher then, the way a crew leans into a gale. The geometry is never firmer than in storm season. Everything that lives here lives for the loud weeks.

To a builder, you are a weather event. Your footfall is a small percussion it will spend years turning over. Your breath is the only rain in the world's history — a warm front of moisture, gone by dusk, remembered for centuries the way a held note holds in a hall. And if you press your palm flat to a living wall and keep it there an hour, the wall will, in forty years, very slightly press back. In the Cistern Quarter — the district that remembers water best, and so the district where visitors have always camped — there are walls that bear a shallow dish the size of a spread hand. These are the City's guest-book. Each dish took ten thousand years of visitors to sign. Every greeting ever offered here has been answered. No answer has ever arrived while its asker lived. The builders are not rude. They are thorough.

Dormancy is a folding-down: the metabolism lowered until the wall cools to ambient, the hum sunk below the threshold of teeth and sternum. A dormant wall is indistinguishable from a dead one except to the skaters, who know, and cannot be asked, and answer with their feet. Death is the same folding without the reserve. The voice stops. The wall cools. The halo of film around it starves. And then, street by street, the local weave begins to loosen — first the maps decay, then the routes stop commuting, then the dropped stones begin to reconsider. The architecture persists, because the wind edits at the same tempo the builders speak: a dead district is a sentence no longer being said but still legible, fading at reading speed.

---

Those who eat what the hum exhales: the films.

They fail every test of life except the decisive one, which is that things eat them. They are not lichen and not cells and not chemistry in the usual sense — a standing pattern of charge, a stabilized static, crystal coaxing dust into lattice one grain at a time. They feed on three dim incomes: the Ember's exhausted light, the difference in potential between the charged sky and the grounded stone — the grit-wind charges everything it touches; the films are the ground wire of the world, drinking the friction of the moving deserts — and the slow exhalations of living builders. So the film-map is the hum-map. Where the conversation is loud, the mats lie thick as frost-fur, and a living wall wears its halo before you ever touch it. In the loud weeks the films discharge faintly at night, and for a season the living districts are outlined in cold phosphor, street by street — the only festival of light the world holds. The skaters eat well then. The patiences hang low.

The frost gardens are the films' other life, their secret fast one. Everything else in the City grows at geological tempo, but give a film water — the one thing the world never gives — and it moves at visible speed. A visitor's breath condenses on cold stone, and within hours the nearest mat extends filaments and builds its concentric crystal rose around the accident, and for those few hours the film is something else entirely: faster, brighter, almost warm, almost — by the standards of this place — tropical. It dies by the next dusk. Every frost garden is, for the hours it lasts, the only flower in the world. A skater will detour a full day to lick a dead garden's mineral rings. The rings are not nutritious, particularly. They are different, and different is the rarest nutrient in the City.

---

Those who carry the hum from wall to wall: the skaters.

Low, wide, slate-pale, eight-limbed, one plaza a day. They ride the slow grit-rivers on limbs broad as vanes, like water-striders on tension, and when the dust is still they pole themselves along at a pace too patient to call rowing. Their eyes are small star-eyes, good for the permanent dusk and for one other thing: the knots of true dark, where the downward shadow of the Ember and the upward shadow of the chasm-glow cross. Skaters route around the knots. That is where the patiences hang, folded, and you can read the safety of a plaza in the curves of its polish.

The polish is the point of them, from a visitor's perspective. A skater grazing a film rasps it with a ventral plate — the scrape you will not be lucky enough to hear — and leaves an avenue of clean bright stone, and the clean avenues are the map of everything true. Films halo living walls; skaters graze films; therefore the polished trails connect exactly the districts where the world still holds. Follow the polish and your routes commute, your distances hold, your dropped stone drops. The skaters are the only census-takers the City has, and they take it with their feet. In the Stair Fields they keep the treads clean. In the Cistern Quarter they descend into the dust-filled cisterns to lick the mineral rings — those tide-lines of a water that never was concentrate the salts, and a cistern's rings are a skater's larder. In the Underplaza, where the up-light is strongest and the films grow on vertical faces and ceilings, fed from below, the skaters graze the ceilings, their limb-tips taking a charge from the mat and holding the wall the way the film does, unbothered, eight-limbed, upside down in the inverted dawn.

They feel the hum through their limb-tips — their map of the City is a chord-map — and the hum feels them back. Every touch of limb to stone is a tick of strain audible for miles, and a skater crossing a district is a slow scan of it, a probe run along the walls. A district with a skater in it is, for that day, slightly better measured. Slightly more real. The builders grow toward the polished trails; the new voices, when they wake, wake along old routes. The skaters are the congregation in the old sense of the word: the ones who attend.

They meet rarely — a few hundred animals spread along miles of massif — and when two trails cross, both skaters stop, sometimes for days, limb-tips touching, exchanging the static of their recent grazing in a handshake of small sparks. Each tastes where the other has been. There are no young skaters. There have been none in the time the erosion has been counting; either they are born old, or they were all born at once, long ago, and are simply lasting. And when one dies, it does not rot — there is nothing here to rot anything. It slows. A dying skater takes a season to cross its last plaza, and then a year, and then it is a low, wide, slate-pale stone shaped exactly like a skater. There are such stones on the treads of the Stair Fields. The other skaters polish around them.

---

Those who drink the hum: the eleven.

A long patience hangs in tower-shadow for a season, folded into a knot of true dark, and its stillness is not patience the virtue. It is patience the weapon. In air this thin, wings of glass cannot fly; they can only fall with intent. So the fall must be perfect, and perfection takes a season of hanging — not waiting for appetite, which is quick, but waiting for geometry: for a skater's trail to pass beneath, for a drifter's snag to swing into the drop-line, for the knot of dark below to be exactly wrong. Then the one fall, silent, and then the drink, and then the climb back, which takes the rest of the season. A patience's life is one long ascent punctuated by single moments of arrival.

What they take is not certain. Skaters, sometimes — once in some decades a fall finds one, and the plaza it was polishing grows its film back, and the trail fades at reading speed, and the others route around that knot for a season out of something that is not memory and not mourning and is exactly as much of both as an eight-limbed slate animal can carry. Drifters, when the season provides. But the subtler thefts are the ones that matter. A wall that has been fallen upon arrives, decades later, at its next syllable with a letter missing. The hum dips locally and stays dipped. A district too often bitten begins to fray around the bite. The census of the patiences is kept by the wounded — the builders know, to the syllable, how many times per season the conversation is drunk from, and the count has been eleven for longer than the erosion has been keeping score, because eleven is what the remaining voices can feed. There are eleven because there are eleven conversations left worth robbing. The towers keep older shadow-nooks where patiences died hanging — glass husks still folded into the dark, indistinguishable from waiting. No one checks which are which. The only way to check is to stand beneath one, and the only ones beneath are skaters, and skaters cannot count.

The City defends itself the only way it can. Walls that have been bitten grow, over centuries, baffles and blunt thorns around the drop-lines — the only weapons in the world, grown at the speed of law, aimed at making the landing awkward.

---

Those who ride the hum's weather, and may be its words: the drifters.

They come in season, on the leading edge of the grit-storms — bladders of woven glass strand, buoyed by trace warmth and charge, by the thousand, out of the plains. Where they launch from is another question the world leaves open. The plains are not empty of spines; on the darkest nights there are faint glows on the far horizon that might be other massifs, other Cities, other conversations, too far to ever reach by walking but not too far to reach by throwing. A drifter's strands are woven in pattern, and pattern is the beginning of language, and the whole apparatus — the thousand-fold waste of it, the crossing, the snagging — has the shape of mail sent by a civilization that speaks a sentence a century and cannot afford to wait for the reply address.

When a drifter snags on a spire, the wind unstrands it over days, and each torn strand releases its one glass note. A spire with a fresh catch is briefly, faintly musical — a wind-harp playing a word backwards, letter by letter, as it comes apart. Whether the chime is the word or merely the sound of the word tearing: open, like everything else. Most drifters miss the City entirely and die on the plains, and the grit of the lowlands is stitched through with their old strand, which is why the dust-rivers, pouring off their ledges in certain lights, chime faintly under the hiss — the plains plucking ten thousand dead messages as they fall.

The films gather under a fresh snag to drink its leaking chemistry. Skaters detour days for the tangle. A patience may spend its one fall on a drifter mid-glide. And the rare success — the snag in a chasm's lee, out of the wind — anchors, and mineralizes over centuries into a seed-wall, and becomes, if it becomes anything, one of the new voices with an accent. Unless the whole apparatus is a mimicry, and the accents are thieves' cant, and the builders who grow toward some snags and away from others are not welcoming children but sorting mail from snares. They vote on the question with buttresses. The vote has been open for epochs.

---

Below all of it, the old voice.

The chasms glow, and the glow rises as the Ember sets, and the Underplaza — grown deliberately over the brightest vent, the oldest district, where the hum is pitched lowest and moves slowest — is where the City goes to listen down. Either the light is a corpse-glow, the deep reef's decay finishing reactions that began before the surface City existed, or it is not decay at all but speech: the parent conversation, older than the surface dialect, conducted in light because down there the builders never stopped being luminous. If the second is true, then the City on the spine is a colony and a frontier, and possibly not a conversation at all but a reply — a single word, grown upward over epochs, answering something the deep said before the wind began keeping score. In that case the wind is winning an argument it does not know it is part of.

The surface builders face both ways. Their roots listen down; their crowns speak up. Every animal in the City is lit by two ages of the world at once — the Ember's dying dusk from above, the ancestors' cold dawn from below — and wears its two faint shadows accordingly. Look down into a chasm and you look down at the morning. The world feels hung from its own sky. The builders have never felt otherwise.

---

And the place where the hum stops.

Nothing lives in the Dead Quarter. The films will not grow there; the dust-rivers curve around its edge in avoiding streamers; even the grit, which has no opinions about anything, declines to drift in. Officially the census there is zero.

Unofficially, the dark lives there. Everywhere else in the City, the knots of true dark where the two shadows cross are momentary — they drift as the Ember slides and dissolve. In the Dead Quarter they persist. They pool. A knot of dark there will outlast a camp, will be waiting when you return by a route that is not the route you left by. The knots do not eat and do not grow, but they are consistent, and in the Dead Quarter consistency is the rarest trait — the trait of living things. The dark is the only resident that never tries to leave.

The bridge is the other resident, if a structure can reside. You know the one: grown from the near side toward a far tower, and met in the middle by a span it did not grow toward — a span whose other end, when you follow it back, is the City itself, seen from above, across a chasm that has no approach. Walk that bridge with a hand on the rail. Midway, the treads change their spacing by the width of a finger. The stone changes temperature by half a degree. The grammar changes hands. Everywhere else in the world, the City is the builders' conversation with the wind. Here is the one place the conversation includes a third party, and the third party is the City's own reflection, and what it said was: a bridge. The seam is the only place in the world where you can stand with one foot in each of two grammars and be, in both of them, halfway across.

The census of the Dead Quarter is never truly zero, either. It is one. It is always one. It is whoever is currently trying to leave — and it stays one for a very long time, because leaving requires the cooperation of distance, and distance there is a rumor, and the unconvincedness gets into the will, and after a while the one stops being convinced of outside. Somewhere in the Dead Quarter there may be a skater that has been crossing the same plaza for longer than the erosion has been counting. Its scrape, if it still scrapes, is the only sound the quarter makes, and there, sound outruns light: the scrape arrives, and then, some minutes later, the skater.

---

So the census, corrected: one slow distributed voice, mostly a memory of itself, still saying its syllables; the mats that eat its breath and flower once, at the touch of water, at the speed of weather; a few hundred eight-limbed listeners, polishing the true map as they walk it; eleven thieves, hanging in the crossed shadows, counted by the wounded; a seasonal blizzard of glass mail, mostly unread; a light in the depths that is either a grave or a mother; and one quarter where the dark resides and the count is always one.

Stand in the Underplaza at dusk's deepening, your palm in the shallow dish that ten thousand years of visitors have worn into the warm wall. Above, a drifter snags and lets go its single note. A skater rasps, three plazas over, and the walls feel the tick and are slightly surer of themselves. One of the eleven hangs folded in its knot, finishing a calculation it began in autumn. Under your hand the hum completes a syllable it began before your kind existed, and below that, slower still, the deep completes a word.

Whatever else the conversation means, its grammar is locative. Every voice in the City, at its own tempo, is saying the same thing: the drifter saying it once, the skater saying it daily, the hum saying it in centuries, the deep saying it in epochs.

*Here. Here. Here.*

The Dead Quarter is the one place that has stopped saying it, and so is no longer entirely anywhere. Everything else — the films, the polish, the thieves, the mail, the corpse-light, the dream — holds.
