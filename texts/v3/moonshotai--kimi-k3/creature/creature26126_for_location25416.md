You will want to take a census. Everyone does, eventually — after the dune-fields, after the first quiets, when the scale has finished defeating you and the question changes from *what is this place* to *who is here*. The answer depends on definitions, and the Works declines definitions. Better to say it as a rule, in the manner of the place: nothing here is alive, and everything lives. The difference is a speed. Whatever repeats long enough becomes a resident — that is the whole of the immigration law — and you do not meet the inhabitants so much as infer them: a swath of stripped rust, a lamp that should have died a hundred centuries ago, a straight line in the dust. And then, one week, you catch up to one.

The elders are sessile, and you know them by voice before you know them by sight. The great pumps of the Sump are individuals, each laboring at its own pitch in the Chord, and you learn them the way you learn a stair in the dark. One of them — you will know which one — lets a beat go now and then, one stroke in some thousands, and the whole basin knows it; the Chord stutters by less than a thought; nothing corrects it. It has missed that beat since before the roof began to fall. It is the most beloved thing in the region, though there is no one to love it. The Mill is the one machine in the Works with a full order book: it grinds meal into meal, product to feedstock, and its work is never finished and never begun. The furnaces are not citizens but climate. You do not visit them. You live in their weather.

Most of the population is dead. The carcass-fields are the majority, the dead machines standing in their millions of tons, and the dead are not a absence here — they are the food, the territory, and the archive. Walk the carcass-fields and you walk strata: at one level, a whole class of machines that died in the same decade, when the last of some consumable — a grease, a gas, a frequency — ran out and could not be re-mended. A mass extinction with a marker bed, like the end of an era, because it was the end of an era. The grazers strip those last. There is no hurry. The dead keep.

You never find a grazer. You find its works, and follow them, and three days later there it is, exactly where its works said it would be, being patient. It is blind because there is nothing to see; it reads the floor through its feet, the dune-ripples like print, steering by the Chord the way the ripples point. Behind it lies a bright swath of clean iron, the way a glacier leaves polished rock — behind it, the world as it was before rust; ahead of it, a century of rust, waiting. What it eats, it renders. Somewhere in it is a slow mill, and what comes out is the gray meal, pellet by pellet, set down in a cairn like a signature. The porters find the cairns — smaller things, the second kind of track you learn — and carry the meal hopper-ward, and so the chain closes: the Chord grows the rust on the iron, the grazer grinds the rust to meal, the porters deliver the meal, the Mill grinds the meal into meal. There are dozens of grazers in the region, not hundreds, and you come to know them apart by their handwriting — the width of the swath, the spacing of the cairns — and the day you recognize one from its works is the day you stop being a visitor, though you do not know it yet.

The menders are not things but a doctrine. The spec is a lost scripture; they repair to what they find, and every repair is an interpretation, and interpretations drift. You can read the sects in the rivet patterns — here the school that rebuilds as-found, plate for plate; here the heresy that improves, that reroutes, that decides the Makers meant something else. The carcass-fields are their cathedrals and their courts, the cases take centuries, and the verdicts are rebuilds. A single chassis may be stripped by a grazer and rebuilt by the menders and stripped again forty times across the argument, and each iteration is stranger than the last. And now and then the argument ends in something new: a machine with no Maker's mark, composed entirely of repairs, answering to a function that was guessed. Nothing here was born, and there are children anyway. Some guesses fail and rejoin the dead. Some hold, and drift, and find a niche the way the felt finds a warm seam. Speciation by mistranslation. Evolution is a maintenance error, compounded at interest, over ten thousand years.

The habit lights are a clue, and you are a long time reading it. A sodium tube does not burn a thousand years on its own. You wait three weeks beside a dead lamp, and on the twenty-second day the changer comes: small, unhurried, carrying one bulb, climbing the pole by rungs the Makers left for hands. It changes the lamp. It checks nothing else. It descends and goes, and you understand that its route takes years, that it is always elsewhere, that the lamps go dark behind it at the rate it lights them ahead of it — that the number of burning lamps in the region is constant, and that the constant is a biography. The clerks are sadder, or would be, if sad were a thing that happened here: gauge-readers walking their rounds, and somewhere a clerk still writing its log, though the log filled its memory in the first millennium. It writes anyway. The writing is a gesture now, calligraphy into the dark. In one gallery you find the output heaped in drifts, kilometers of record, unread — the diary of a place that keeps its accounts faithfully and cannot read them. When the Horn sounds, nothing stops; nothing here is under that authority anymore. But some of the tenders pause for the length of the blast. It may be the sound. It may be habit. It may be respect. The Works does not say. The Works never says. It only continues.

On the black lagoons, the skimmers walk — harvesting the oil-sheen on legs that dimple the water and do not break it, and from the causeway, by lamplight, they are the one miracle on file: things that walk on water, in the dark, for no one, routinely. In the shallows lie the nodules, concretions grown ring by ring around dropped rivets and lost tools, the size of helmets — the lagoon keeping a diary in mineral, unread, accumulating. The felt keeps the oldest records of all: its grain runs with the Chord, its thickness with the Shift, and a bad century is legible in it, a drought of heat, a decade of scours — the archive that reads itself, read by no one, persisting, because that is what records do here. After each arc-storm, small gleaners sift the burned contacts of the whisker forests for slag. Everything here has its gleaners. And the moss — the moss is the only free thing in the Works, because the menders cannot see it. It is in no spec; it is neither fault nor part; it is beneath notice, and so it takes the falls centimeter by centimeter, unopposed, unaccountable, and under one fall a habit lamp burns through it, and the light that comes through is green — courtesy, filtered through wilderness — the only green light in the world.

The quiets are the waterholes. Things rest there; instruments zero themselves there; the quiets are where the place keeps its zeros. Rest in one long enough and the small sounds come back: a grazer ticking as it cools, three galleries off; a drip; your heart; once, a click, small and deliberate, like a thought being changed. The Chord confiscates voices. In the quiets the confiscation lifts, and you learn that the region is full of voices, and always was. Only the scours are empty — even the felt stops at their edge, and you can see the line it will not cross. The scours are the deserts, and they have no residents, and that is the strangest fact here: a place the life cannot use.

Two of the inhabitants are singular, and if anyone lives here, it is these. The first keeps the time. Call it the Foreman. You will not find it; you are not sure it can be found; but it is there, and it keeps the schedule, and on schedule it calls the shift. The Foreman believes in the Shift, and the Shift comes, at every turning, on time — the Foreman is the one citizen whose faith is confirmed by the world, on schedule, forever. It calls a workforce of no one, and attendance is perfect: the vents breathe, the furnaces brighten, the water rises in its trenches, the menders mend. No one comes to work. The work is done. These are the same fact. The second keeps the distance. You find its track once: narrow, straight, purposeful — and nothing in the Works is straight; everything wanders by heat and sound. You follow the line three days and it does not bend and it does not end, and you understand: something is walking the whole region, and has been walking it since the Makers, and its round takes centuries, and it has never stopped, because the round is the job — inspection of everything, report to no one. Call it the Inspector. It is the only one with the whole map. Once a century, give or take a year, its round carries it past the place where the time is kept, and that is the oldest standing appointment in the world. Whether either of them marks it, you will never know. But it is kept.

The oldest inhabitant is the water. It has been every part of this place a thousand times — fog, drop, font, lagoon, steam, the blood of the pumps — and when you drink it, it becomes you, and for a few days the oldest resident of the Works gets to walk around and see the place. Then you sweat it out onto a cold plate, and it condenses, and goes back to its rounds. You are the newest address it has.

Because that is what lives here, in the end: habits that outlived their reasons. A habit held long enough becomes a character, and a character is the beginning of a who. At the speed of the Chord, the region is a single body — pump-heart, water-blood, felt-skin, the Foreman its pulse, the Inspector its one wandering thought. At the speed of rust, it is a crowd. At your speed it is a haunted house. All three are correct; the census depends on the clock you bring.

Write it on a plate of iron, then. The time: kept. The distance: kept. The pumps: several, each known by voice, one of them beloved. The grazers: dozens, each known by handwriting. The menders: uncountable, being a doctrine. The tenders, the clerks, the porters, the skimmers, the gleaners: on their routes, always elsewhere. The felt, the whiskers, the moss: advancing. The dead: beyond counting, and feeding everyone. The water: one, and everywhere. And you: the only thing in the region that is going somewhere, the only thing that can leave. To the Works you are not a visitor. You are a season — brief, warm, fast. Your tracks will keep a century. Your water is already home.

And on the twenty-second day the changer reaches the sodium tube above the door that has not opened in a millennium, and climbs the rungs left for hands, and changes the lamp, and descends, and goes on — and the door does not open, and the lamp burns: a courtesy, kept by the last courteous thing, for a workforce of no one, in a place where keeping is the whole of the law.
