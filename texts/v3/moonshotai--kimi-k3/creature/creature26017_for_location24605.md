Take the census. It is shorter than you expect, and longer.

Nothing here was planted. Everything living in the hall arrived the way everything arrives anywhere — by accident, and then by staying: a seed in a boot tread, a spore in the sealant, a bacterium in a lungful of the last shift's air, a pinch of fill-dirt washed down through four days of rock. For thirty years the door has been shut, and so every living thing in this room is now endemic in the strictest sense on Earth: found in one room, in one room only, in the universe. The garden is not a garden. It is a passenger list. Learn the smells and you can read the roster blind — the crushed-stem note is green breath, the wet iron is something at table, and under both a dark cellar-note, mushroom and dust, that you will not place until you understand the lagging.

Count the crowns first, because they are countable. Each burning lamp has one, and each crown is one vine — follow the leaves inward and you find the body, a stem thick as your wrist now, lignified to iron-colored wood, wedged into the cable tray where it sprouted in the year of the shift. It has been leaning over the same lamp for three decades. It has never once touched the floor. Each leaf is a coin, and the vine spends them on light — angled, re-angled, week by week, to the edge of the possible. The scorch-kisses at the leaf-tips mark a contract: this close, and no closer. The vine leans to the verge of burning and lives there, and has for thirty years. No animal in any forest arranges its life so precisely around its food. And the green itself is an heirloom. No crown has ever seen the sun; their grandmother did. They are faithful to a light they have only heard about, and they pay for the faith in full, in watts.

When a lamp dies, the crown does not die. That is the thing to understand about them. It waits — months, sometimes a year, you can read the duration in the etiolation — and then it does the bravest thing anything does here: it sends a piece of itself out into the dark to find another. A runner in transit is the fastest living motion in the hall. It covers a centimeter a week. Nothing else alive here moves in any way you could catch moving; the drips are weather, the pump is a machine. The runner is an animal in everything but kingdom — rootless, colorless, burning the mother crown's stored sugar, navigating by a memory of light toward a glow it can barely sense as scatter on the vault. One line on the floor has a bend in it, a shallow arc like a river changing its mind. The bend is a lamp-death, fossilized: the runner set out for a light that had years left, but not enough years, and somewhere in the black it stopped, re-aimed toward a farther glow, and went on. It is the only sentence ever written in this room, and it says: not that one. This one. Other lines end short, in a curled tendril still pointing. A direction is the last thing a vine owns.

The pale congregation pressed to the tap housing is not a separate people. They are the crowns' cousins — orphans' children, and the children of seeds that landed on warm steel and sprouted in darkness, which means they never had any green to lose. You cannot grieve what you never had; the congregation is the only contented thing in the hall. Ask what they eat and you get the right answer to the wrong question: they eat the machine. Through the felt, which eats the bore's iron breath; through the black beds, which eat everything; through grafts beneath the litter, where their roots have fused with the crowns' roots, as trees of one stock will — so that in the dark, the last light in the hall is quietly subsidizing the blindness that will replace it. The arithmetic explains the allegiances. The whole green economy — three crowns, every pilgrim on the floor — runs on less than a hundred watts. The congregation sits on kilowatts bleeding up through steel. Green is this place's poverty, and its pride. Heat is its wealth, and its blindness. When a pipe cools — a valve half-closed in the commissioning year and never touched since — the leaves on it flag within days, and the plate is abandoned, left pressed to the dead steel as a parchment husk that keeps the pipe's shape for years. The congregation has been keeping the maintenance log all along, in husks: a plant's-eye record of the last human decisions ever made here.

And it flowers. Several times a year the blind congregation does this thing it has no reason to remember how to do. They open on a calendar no one can check — inherited clocks keeping the surface's seasons, but a clock with nothing to set it drifts, and each year the festival comes a little later, migrating off the schedule of a sky it will never confirm. No one is counting. The flowers are. In a hall where sound arrives one note at a time, a minute apart, the loudest thing here is a smell: the shout of sweetness pooling in the still air, spurred trumpets engineered for a tongue that has never existed in this world. The spurs brim with nectar that has never once been tasted. Toward the end of a bloom's week it ferments where it hangs, and the sweetness gains a low wine-note; a flower smelling faintly of wine is a flower in its last days. It dies a little drunk on its own hospitality. It cannot even console itself — the stock is self-incompatible, built for a partner, and the congregation is, genetically, perhaps one individual repeated ten thousand times. Every flower opens beside its own self and waits for a stranger. There are no strangers. There has never been one seed.

Stand long enough and the place resolves into what it is: a waiting room. The crowns wait for the lamps to outlive them. The runners wait, at the pace of growth, for a glow. The flowers wait for a moth; the nectar for a tongue; the pump for a return line that is capped; the cart for a shift; the arrow for the lift. Everything in the hall waits for exactly one thing, and no two of them are waiting for the same thing, and none of it is coming. Almost none of it. Peel one pale leaf from the steel — it resists like a suckered hand and comes away with a small sound of tape — and hold it against a lamp cage an hour a day. Within the week, green rises along the veins like a blush. The machinery was never dismantled, only switched off and kept, the way a cellar keeps a bicycle after the roads are gone. The congregation has not renounced the sun. It has filed the sun away, against a day the crowns are trying, leaf by scorched leaf, to make last forever.

The oldest settler is not a plant at all. It is the cream felt on the pipework, and it passed for lagging for thirty years — the only inhabitant with a cover identity. Call it a fungus for want of a nearer word; whatever it is, it is alive, and it is one. A spore in the sealant, most likely, or riding a cable reel; it found the warm pipes in the first winter, before any seed sprouted. The garden did not find an empty room. It found a room already taken, and grew up renting. What it eats, you smell: the wet-iron taste of the air is its table being set, dissolved metal bleeding up with the bore's warmth through the gaskets. It thickens where the warmth leaks — which means it has been writing the maintenance report since the year the engineers left, in felt, in a hand they cannot read. If they ever come back it will tell them where every gasket is failing. And the pump is not pumping for no body. Its line is capped, yes — but the line sweats at its joints, the sweat wicks through the felt's sleeve, and the felt is watered by the liturgy it attends. A heartbeat keeping time for no body has found a body. The return line was never commissioned, but it has been in service for decades. Sample the felt anywhere — this pipe, that flange, thirty meters apart — and you are sampling one individual, the largest living thing in the hall, heavier than every crown and runner and flower combined. It has never fruited, because it is already everywhere it can be. It does not reproduce. It persists. Immortality by occupancy.

Against the housing the black beds lie knee-deep, and they are the warmest living touch in the room — a degree and a half above the air by their own digestion. They give like bread and smell like a forest floor after rain, a smell they have inherited and never earned. Push your arm in and you are elbow-deep in the archive. There are strata. A seam of petals marks a flowering week six years gone. A flattened ghost is a runner that failed. Each lamp-death has its little autumn, a sudden leaf-fall, preserved — a geology of autumns, each dated to a filament, read only by the ones who wrote it. Those are the true natives. The microbes of the beds run generations in hours; thirty years is a hundred thousand generations, deep time, and what lives in the beds now is not what arrived. It is fitted exactly to forty degrees, to floral rot, to felt-wall polymer, to dark — new species, the most evolved citizens of the hall, existing in one trench of compost in the universe. The rarest ecosystem on Earth is not an unclimbed valley. It is a black bed with one door.

The census has a tail of ghosts besides. The daily breath carries the surface's seasons down the shaft — grass pollen in summer, a spruce's dust in spring, mold in the autumn — mail from a world with a sky, addressed to no one here, opened by no stigma. The hall cannot answer; scent is too heavy to climb. One mailbox, one direction, thirty years of undelivered weather. Almost none of it stays. Once, something did: the faint haze in the sump trenches, dilute enough that the mirror still holds the panel's red ember, descends from a single raindrop that fell on the surface years ago and arrived four days late, like all the weather here. It is the only letter that got through. It says nothing. It says: rain. Count, if you like, the soda-straw stalactites at the breach — hair-fine, two centimeters, growing. Not alive, but growing. The hall makes little distinction. Neither, after a while, will you.

And then there is you. You are the largest animal ever to enter this room, the first since the shift, and nothing here can see you — but everything here can feel you. Warmth is the hall's eyes, and you are a bonfire walking between the lamps. The crowns cannot use your breath in the dark; stand under a lamp anyway and breathe, and you will have given the garden the first fresh carbon it has tasted in thirty years. It will not thank you in any way you could notice. But the beds will keep your skin-cells for a century, the dust will keep your footprints longer than the galvanizing keeps its shine, and the scent of the last flowering is on your clothes now — the only pollinator-shaped event this room has ever had, walking around inside it.

So count again, and watch the categories close. The crowns feed the congregation through grafts in the dark. The felt plumbs the beds to the pipes; the pump waters the felt; the lamps burn the bore's own heat to keep three small memorials of a star. Trace any calorie — green, pale, felt, bed, breath — and it ends at the bore. The garden has one sun, and it is the Earth. Which means the census resolves, at last, to one. One living thing, seventy meters long: shotcrete skin, steel heart stroking on its thermostat, limestone lungs breathing once a day, a fur of cream felt, and a garden growing in its gut the way a body keeps a memory of meadows. The stencil on the housing was never the machine's label. It is the inhabitant's name. GEOTHERMAL TAP T-3 has lived here thirty years. It is going blind at the pace of filaments, it will live by the bore's clock for ten thousand more, and in all that time it will make exactly one attempt to leave. It is making it now. It is on your sleeve — a spore of the lagging, a cell of the beds, riding the first animal that ever came down, up toward a sun nothing here remembers. The flowers aimed at a moth and missed by a kingdom. The room aimed lower, at you, and will not miss.

Take the census of the surface, when you climb out. It may have gone up by one.
