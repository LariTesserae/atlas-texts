Ask what lives here and you must first say what size of answer you want, because the room is inhabited at every scale the way a sea is inhabited — and, like a sea, it is finally all one water.

Begin with the smallest life, which is also the most punctual. The drop is born at the knuckle of the vault and takes eleven seconds to do it — you can watch the whole thing, the one visible act of manufacture in a place that otherwise runs on invisible production. It swells from a bead to a lens, holding in its curve a grey thumbnail of oculus-light, and then it lets go and falls the whole height of the world. The assembly is its life; the fall is its one act; the tock against the sump is its funeral, well attended — the springtails scatter like a crowd flinching at thunder and drift back down in the wake of the rings. That is the entire biography. Eleven seconds long, nearly eight thousand of them a day, and not one has ever been late. And it is not a death in any sense the water would recognize: the sump takes it, the pale curtain of roots takes it, the sapwood lifts it, the leaves breathe it out into air the cold vault will bead again tonight. Only the shape is spent. Here is the strange accounting: there has been a drop assembling at that knuckle for two thousand years. It is always eleven seconds old. It has never once been absent. The most constant citizen of the room is the one that falls.

For the grey dust that hops on the skin of the sump, the drop is not a sound. It is the weather. The springtails live under the most reliable sky in the world — it falls on them every eleven seconds, opens its rings through their country, and settles. Their ocean has a floor you can stand on and a tide with no moon. They graze the film at the waterline in their thousands, and no one among them has ever needed to survive a winter, because their world has never had one: eight degrees, always eight degrees, since long before their kind had a name. The plain outside is all season and hazard; this is the one climate on it that keeps its word. Eden by thermostat.

Lift a bark-scale the size of your thumbnail and you lift the roof of a country. Under it the felt runs in ravines and plateaux, greyed with the first digestion — a lace of hyphae eating the house's shed skin and paying it out to the grazers — and the mites move through it like herds on a plain too vast for any of them to cross. And over the herds, in the under-country between scale and wood, reigns the dragon of the place: the pseudoscorpion, three millimetres of jurisdiction, venom-clawed, unhurried, the apex of everything it can perceive. It has never seen the light column. Its world has no up, no oculus; its sky is bark and its history is the slow tectonics of scales settling into the floor. Beneath its notice the herds go on. Above its notice the vault goes on. Neither knows about the other, and the arrangement works.

In the wall, the beetle ticks, and that ticking is the sound of the room's one negotiation still in session. Galleries cut; callus is laid down at exactly the rate of the cutting. The treaty is conducted entirely in wood, at the speed of wood, between two parties who have never met — the beetle knows nothing of callus, the tree knows nothing of beetle, and the frontier between them has not moved a hair's breadth in centuries. It is not a war. Wars end. This is older than that: a peace that cannot be broken because it cannot be concluded.

The gloom moths are the air's citizenry, a standing wave the colour of the dark they live in. The room carries exactly this many moths the way a chord carries exactly these overtones — individuals flicker, the wave remains. Their geography has one forbidden meridian: the light column, walking its arc across the floor like a moving noon, and no moth crosses it. They sip the beads from the flutes before the trickles gather — theft from the loop, but budgeted; the account has carried a line for them since before the arch was worn smooth. They are born in the felt and returned to it; the floor is their parent and their graveyard; no moth has ever needed the sky.

Then the wren, which is not one wren. The office is older than the language that would name it, refounded every spring in moss behind the rib, the same fluting, the same cup, a wren-shaped wave maintained by replacement. It is the only citizen with a commute — out through the arch at first light to work the sedge an hour in every direction, back at dusk — so besides light, the one open line in the closed account comes in through a wren's beak. It is the only citizen who has ever seen the house from outside, and it is not interested. And when it sings, louder than the whole machine floor, absurd for a body lighter than a coin, the small citizens endure it the way you endure weather: a daily cataclysm that has never once meant harm, thunder that only ever announces morning. The vault takes the song the way a river takes rain. The tree counts its years in rings; the room counts its long time in wrens.

The oldest inhabitant is the house. The tree lives here too, in the only way a thing can live inside itself — the room is its kept absence, heartwood surrendered and refined into a hall, and everything in it that is finished is finished except one green line under the bark, one cell thick, where the last frontier runs. All the becoming that is left in the place lives in that line. The tree lays a ring each year to replace what the year removes, cell for cell: mastery as repetition, practice with no performance coming. Its memory is its shape — the fire-scar, the rolled wound of the oculus — history kept the only way the room keeps anything. As form.

There is no one else. Do not furnish the place with a hermit, some keeper in a coat of moss. Eight degrees will not keep a person; there is nothing to eat here but the dark's small change and nothing to burn but the house itself, and the fire-scar says how that goes and how long it is remembered. The room is not a dwelling waiting for a tenant. It is full.

And still the census is not finished, because everything you have counted keeps turning out to be the same answer at a different size. The drop stands and the water passes. The moth-wave stands and the moths flicker. The wren-office stands and the wrens pass. Each is a shape that water, or light, or time, takes while passing through — and the largest of those shapes is the room itself. Run the honest tests on it. It holds its temperature the way you hold yours: it is warm-blooded at eight degrees, and has been for two thousand years. It breathes at the oculus. Its pulse beats every forty seconds. It repairs its wounds. It eats light — the column on the floor is the daily delivery at the only port — and pays it out through the plumbing to ten thousand small appetites, takes it back as breath, condenses it, drinks it again. By every measure that means anything, it is alive. It fails one test only: it does not reproduce. But reproduction is the strategy of things that stop. This has chosen the other one — *not stopping*. And the chord, the foundry-roar, is simply what a metabolism of that size sounds like from inside. Not machinery. Not the ghost of machinery. A body, being loud about living. It is not a god and it is not a ghost; those are wanting things, waiting things, and waiting is the one line item this account does not carry. Bring your word for it — *lonely* — and watch it fail to stick. Lonely would be the most artificial object in the room.

You, meanwhile, are counted. While you stand there you are a warm tower in a cold country, a mountain range that radiates; the condensation finds your sleeve; your breath climbs the flue and will bead tonight on the vault and run the flutes and fall on schedule — the account takes everyone, in small change. Your footfall is weather that closes over. Your heart bangs along off-tempo, the room's one rough edge, and given ten thousand years the place would file you smooth the way it filed the flutes to their trickles and the arch to a stooping back. It gets an hour of you. When you duck back out into the big cool sky, the groove follows you a little way — your heart keeps trying the forty-second beat, and failing, and failing more slowly — until the flat has you back. The room keeps nothing of you. Keeping is not what a loop does.

From across the sedge at dusk the sound goes on, sourceless, steady, and now you know its name at every scale: the drop, the tide, the treaty, the wave, the song, the ring — a pulse you can hear from a kilometre off, the tenant at its shift, not asleep, not awake, keeping the books by the drop, and the books balance. And inside, in the ordinary dark, at the knuckle of the vault, a drop is assembling. Eleven seconds. Tock. On time.
