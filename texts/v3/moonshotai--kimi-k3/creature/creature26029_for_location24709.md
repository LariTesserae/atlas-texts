Ask what lives in the Long Morning and first you have to repair the word. *Lives.* It goes thin up there. Run the census and it comes out wrong every way you run it. By bodies: tens of thousands — a city's worth — packed flank to flank across the White Floor, strung along the corridors, standing at the pan's edge with their tongues out. By heartbeats: one. Yours. On most days the plateau's entire circulation is whatever is moving in your own chest, and you can hear it, because there is nothing else for it to hide under.

So the returned sort the residents into kinds, and the sorting is the lore, and the lore is what they give you at the bottom before you climb.

The first kind is the Held. You have touched them by now — the warm flank that yields, the thin musk that never strengthens, the eye that is not glazed and not regarding you. Everyone asks it, alone among the herds: is anyone home? The returned divide on the question, and the division is gentle, because nobody can win it. One school says the moment inside them is too narrow for experience — a hundredth of a second, stopped, too thin to hold a thought. The other school points to the old woman. She is mid-turn toward the child; the child is mid-reach; and if a hundredth of a second is wide enough to hold an intention, then she has been intending toward that child for longer than the lower country's language has existed. Every traveler wants to give the child something. No one does. It would rest in that reaching hand forever, and the reach is not yours to finish.

Notice, too, that everything up there is mid-appetite. The herds to the salt, the cat to the calf, the bee to the flower, the vultures to the future corpse — ten thousand hungers seated at the table and no meal, ever, and no one complaining, which is the only mercy in it. The effect on you is strange: you learn to be the only fed thing in the world that does not feed. You drink little. You walk past the lickers with your full water-skin and you feel the obscenity of being satisfied.

Read the herders as a people and they reward it. Their hands are a literature — callus and rope-burn, a healed break, nails bitten down. The copper on the old woman's chest is trade; nobody smelted that at altitude, which means the herds went down once, or the low country came up. The stone of the blades crops out two days east; someone made that walk a habit. And the sea had been dead a long time before these people were born. The strandlines were already old to them, the ghost rivers already ghost. They were a people making a good life in the drained bathtub of the world, and the Long Morning was not the first ending they survived. It was only the first one that stopped.

The second kind is the Stayed. A tally that ends mid-stroke means a person who meant to make one more mark and did not — not *did not get to*; did not. The half-line is there, and the hand that made it is findable. You learn to tell the Stayed by the wrongness of their century: a boot style two hundred years out of wear, a coat of a cut nobody sews anymore, a steel blade in a country of knapped stone. Some of them walked out into the Congregation to do it — stood themselves flank to flank with the herds, as if what took the animals might be more easily found among them — and the corridors detour around them now, and cairns grow beside them. The cairns are the only things in the Long Morning that grow.

They tell you at the bottom: stillness costs. Every day you are up there it costs — the standing, the looking, the long silences — and the cost is how you know you are yours. Your nails keep growing. Your hunger keeps its schedule. That small ongoingness is your proof of guesthood; the coal burns, and that is the good news about the coal. But when stillness stops costing — when you come back to yourself at the pan's edge and cannot say how long you have been standing there, and it was *restful* — you go down that day. Do not finish the count. Do not water first. Go down. As for whether the ones who didn't go down slowed and joined the morning, or simply died and were kept the way everything is kept — that is undecidable, and the plateau will never release the answer. You may take their hands. Everyone does, once. The hands are warm. Warm proves nothing in a place where nothing is allowed to cool, and the returned will tell you so, and then go quiet, because they took the hand anyway.

And there are the ones who climb on purpose, at the end. Down below, when someone is dying in the good way — old, finished, mourned in advance by people who love them — they sometimes make the Last Climb, to be kept. You find them seated on the tally-house thresholds, facing the herds, composed. Their families come up. That practice is kept exactly: you visit the way you would visit a grave, except the grave takes your hand. The mother is older every visit. The son is never older. There is nowhere else in the world where that arithmetic is run openly, year after year, in useful mid-morning light, and the people who run it do not call it cruel. They call it the visiting.

The third kind is the passing — people like you, briefly resident. Sometimes there is a long-stayer, someone a year or three into a vigil, and you will hear them before you see them: the only other sound in the world. People have walked toward that sound like sailors toward a light. The customs of meeting are few and exact. You exchange bowl-levels — what you found, what you left, which houses want carrying — because the plateau's only news is water. You do not ask the reason. Nothing up there records a reason; the custom honors what the place leaves blank. Some long-stayers name the animals on their corridor — a census of affection, a village of the almost — and greet the lammergeier each time they pass beneath it. Others refuse: naming is claiming, they say, and claiming is the one sin the place is organized against. Both kinds keep the corridors. Both kinds drink little. Ask either kind what they miss and it is never food. It is stars. No one on the plateau will ever see a star again, and the Stayed least of all. And sleep, they will tell you, is the one change the Long Morning permits for free: you lie down under the standing sun and inside you, unrecorded, whole weathers happen — and you wake and nothing shows it, and you are briefly grateful for a body that can keep a secret from the world.

Living things blunder up sometimes — a storm-blown bird over the rim, a fox up the gullies following your scent. The place does not take them; the taking was a morning, not a habit. They find nothing to eat that is not somebody's and nothing to drink that is not everyone's, and they go back down, or they don't. You may see a traveler cup water from a bowl for a bird that has no business at that altitude. It is the only act of hospitality the plateau has ever hosted, and the bowls record it like everything else.

The fourth kind is below you, and made the place what it is. The returned — the ones who named it, who keep the lore, who ask you your levels when you come down. Everything gentle up there is their doing, and none of it is innocence. Consider the roofs: hide of the same species that stands outside. Maybe the herders roofed the bothies themselves, in the ordinary way of herders, from the cull. Or maybe the first comers up the notch found the herds standing and read them as meat instead of moment. The returned do not know, and the not-knowing is part of the custom's force. The corridors, the measured bowls, the unmoved calf — all of it is done in the knowledge that it might be an apology. Something was carried down, once — a small held bird, the story says, past the notch, to see. Ask three of the returned what happened and you will get four stories. In the gentlest, the bird finished its wingbeat. In all of them it is dead within the hour — of time, the tellers say, of the backlog — and in all of them the one who carried it does not say what they expected. Every version ends the same way: nothing is carried down anymore. Whatever the plateau gives, it gives at its own altitude, on its own terms. At the rim you can find the footprints of the leavers going over and down, mid-stride, fresh as the hour they were made, some of them centuries old. The plateau keeps the leaving. It does not keep the reason.

And the last resident is the hour itself. It lives there the way a scent lives in a closed house — except no house has ever kept a scent this faithfully, and the thing that made this one is ten thousand years gone, and the house has no doors. It is not a being. Do not make the mistake of praying to it; it keeps prayers the way it keeps footprints, exactly and without reading them. It has no attention, and that is its mercy and its demand: nothing watches you in the Long Morning, which means your kindness there is the purest kind that exists — the kind with no witness but your own trail, spelled out behind you in the salt.

So the census, run honestly: the Held, who are kept. The Stayed, who are kept undecidably, whose hands are warm. The cairns, which grow. The bowls, which remember. The hour, which is ongoing. And you, briefly — the loudest living thing in the world, the one heartbeat in ten thousand chests.

And here is what the returned save for last. The population never decreases. Nothing that comes is subtracted, because everything that comes is recorded. Your footprints are up there right now, wherever you walked; the bowl you drank from still says so; and in that sense — the only sense the place allows — you never entirely leave. Everyone who ever climbed is still resident in trace, still arriving, still standing at the pan's edge deciding about the calf. For a little while, the population includes your heartbeat. Forever, it includes your passage. The herds bend to the salt. The light holds. It is a good hour, and it is always now — and now has more people in it than you think.
