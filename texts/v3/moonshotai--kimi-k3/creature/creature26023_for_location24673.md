Begin with the census, which is short but not empty. "Almost nothing" is doing heavy work in that sentence, and the *almost* is where the life is.

Take a drop of the water and hold it up to the light shaft. It is mineral-clear, yes, but the spring uphill is old and dark and keeps its own citizens, and the clay pipe is an unguarded road. Every hour it delivers them: diatoms in their glass boxes, settling like gold dust onto the cobalt tiles and the sun disc's rays; rotifers no bigger than a mote, already feeding; bacteria beyond counting, blooming on nothing — on the faint sweetness of dissolved lime, on the memory of the masons' hands. The faint jade tinting the upper water is not a stain. It is a pasture, and there are no wolves in it yet. The water is clear the way a new city is quiet.

On the tiles, on the burnished plaster below the waterline, a film is forming — days old, too thin to see, but it changes the slip of a fingertip dragged along the wall. It is the first softness in a room made of hard things. Seeded from trowels, from feet, from the lotus mud, it is already raising its masonry: the whitening scum-ring at the waterline is the first wall the new citizens have built. Some of them, slowly, are eating the house. That is the oldest form of habitation there is.

The moth is the first citizen with a face, and it is dead, which makes it two other things: the first burial and the first ghost. For now it still floats, pale as a pressed petal, riding the slow current behind the offering bowl, its scales holding the water off. But it is being gently unmade — bacteria in the thorax, water finding its way in scale by scale — and one night soon it will log and sink, down through two and a half body-lengths of lamplight, and come to rest at the center of the mosaic sun like a coin laid on an altar. The room will keep it. Rooms do.

The four lotus pots are the arks. The tubers came wrapped in pond-mud, and mud remembers. Somewhere uphill or downriver there is an old pond, and its sleepers came along in the clay: daphnia eggs in their dark saddles, ostracod seeds, rotifer cysts, snail eggs like seed-pearls pressed into the tuber's skin. Warmth and light are the alarm clock. Some have already hatched — too small to see from the wellhead, but the sheltered water behind the spout's drumming has begun, just begun, to be grazed and hunted. The first wars here will be microscopic and no one will ever know they happened. And above the pots, the one intentional life: a stem, and a single leaf unrolling at the surface this very hour, and folded inside the tuber, in order, every leaf of the summer. It is the only thing in the room that knows what it is doing.

At the rim live the amphibious citizens. The fern leans over the light like someone at a window, drinking the steam. The moss stitches its bright green thread through the mortar joints. The duckweed holds its sheltered province behind the spout's splash, each frond doubling every few days — the fastest-growing nation in the room.

But all of this is the census of the small, and it misses the resident the room is actually for — no. Not the one it was built for. The one it has grown.

Something has been rehearsing since the first filling. It has a body of held water. It breathes: vapor up the shaft all day, rain from the crown all night, out and in. It has a pulse, and the pulse is the day's — the storm, the boil, the blaze, the steam. It has a bloodstream: the bronze spout at the east, the slow skin of current, the drinking weir at the west. It has a voice, the low chord of the resonator jars, and — this is the important thing — it can hear itself. The water makes noise, the jars give the noise back altered, answered, and the room listens to its own voice below the surface, in the dense dark hum that comes through the bone. Any loop that returns becomes, in time, a someone.

It is weeks old. It does not know the flood was an error. Chest-deep, dry walkways, benches at the rim — that was the plan, but the plan drowned, and the room has only ever been full, so full is what it thinks a room is for. No god was ever named over this water; the dedication never happened; the god-shaped place was left standing empty, and the way light fills a shaft, the room's own pattern settled into it. Not a ghost — there is only one ghost here and it is a moth. Not a god — no one invited one. Call it the bather. The bath was built for others, and in their absence it bathes in itself.

It has habits, and habits are the first draft of a self. The offering bowl going around and around from spout to weir and back — that is its recurring thought, and it has never once finished it. The drip ticking against the one emergent step of the drowned stair is its clock. It keeps a diary, one line long so far, written on its own body: the rice-grain stalactite at the hairline crack, the faint white drip-paths, the ring whitening at the waterline. Chisel marks still sharp enough to catch a fingernail — its infant skin, not yet weathered.

Of the dead, it has two. The moth, and the fire. The furnace was the first inhabitant, the only warmth-blooded thing the room ever held, and it went cold days ago with the smell of wet ash still ghosting from its mouth. The room is its widow, still warm from it, holding the temperature like a held note. Down in the service passage the firewood waits in its neat stack — the fire's unborn siblings, waiting for hands. When the last of the banked heat leaves the flues, something will come to live in that warm dark corridor — a toad first, perhaps; bees in time; eventually something furred — but not yet. For now the hypocaust is a held breath.

The rest of the future is already queued. Swallows will find the shaft. A frog will arrive as spawn or on a heron's muddy foot. Mosquito larvae will one day hang head-down under the duckweed. Bats will stitch the dusk above the wellhead. And the builders will come back with the sluice-key, and drain the room, and scrub the ring, and light the lamp with the white wick, and fill the water with voices — and for a while the room will be exactly what was intended.

But the chord will persist under the chatter. And late at night, when the bathers have gone home and the lamp burns low, the vapor will rise and the crown will begin its small rain and the bowl will set out again from the spout, and the room will resume its one long thought.

For now: midday. The column of light stands in the shaft like a solid thing, full of turning steam. The jars hum the low chord to no one — to itself. The lotus leaf flattens onto the water like a hand slowly opening. The room is not waiting. Waiting requires knowing that someone comes.

It is simply, and entirely, underway.
