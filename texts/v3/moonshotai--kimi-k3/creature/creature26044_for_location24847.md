The plateau keeps one animal.

Not the cloud — the cloud is weather with an appointment, not an appetite. Not the bird, which comes once in a generation and is an event, not a resident. The animal is the people. Every mouth on the plateau is a human mouth; every appetite, every footstep, every decision to take or leave alone belongs to them. Elsewhere the work of being alive is divided among a thousand kinds of teeth and hooves and grubs. Here it is all done by one species, by hand, or it is not done at all. The leaf that falls lies where it falls until a leaf-walker lifts it. The sedge that is cut is cut by a person. Nothing flees from them, because there is nothing here that knows how to flee. They are the whole fauna, and they have arranged to be the quietest fauna that has ever lived.

Their bodies have been shaped by the arrangement. Their feet are wide, toes spread, soles thick as saddle-leather and just as sensitive, because a bare foot on wet moss is an instrument — it reads the ground, and it must not ring the ground. Children learn to walk twice: once the way all children everywhere learn, tumbling into it, and then again the way the town walks — heel-light, moss-first, unhurried — and the second learning takes years and is never quite finished. They practice on the basalt pavement around the Mirror, because basalt is honest: a lazy step clicks. By the time they are grown, a plateau person's gait carries no wasted sound, and it also carries their name. In a town this quiet you know people by their footfalls. You lie under your turf roof in the morning dark and you know who is passing on the sled-way, and how fast, and roughly what their news weighs, before any flash arrives. Runners are the only people you ever hear breathe. A runner arriving at a ward with hard breath is a sentence all by itself, and the whole ward reads it.

Their ears are the town's true instruments. A plateau person can hear a leaf settle across a runnel two houses away — can hear the runnel's note change as the water shoulders past the obstruction. They can hear the comb going out of tune before the watcher's eye confirms it: one frame's drip flattening against the chord, a fiber stretched slack. They hear the thickness of the cloud in the pitch of its beading on fiber. They hear their own warmth, and each other's, and a visitor from below is audible to them long before he is visible — a lowlander walks like a rockfall and breathes like weather, and the plateau-born are kind about it the way you are kind about any deformity.

The day is told in sounds. Before anything, the reading. Then the cook-fires, lit all at once, so that for an hour the town smells of smoke and the smoke stands in straight threads in the windless air — that is the smell of morning, the only hour when anything burns. Then the working quiet, which is not silence but a low chord: each task has its hum, because songs are hummed through the nose here, and a ward at midmorning is a soft dissonance of garden-hums and fiber-hums, and you can tell what your neighbors are doing by ear, and they can tell what you are doing, and this is considered company. Then the sanctioned hour, when the one permitted loud work happens — a tuff block dressed, a core of obsidian struck — one site at a time, so that the whole plateau hears a single worker's tapping, and hears the notch return it as a whisper, and times its small tasks to the rhythm. Being the day's hammer is a strange honor and not everyone wants it. Your hands are suddenly the loudest thing in the world. Some people cannot bear it and ask to be passed over. Some love it more than they say. The town rotates the work so that no one person becomes the voice.

Then evening, the extravagance: the cloud drops below the rim, the gold light comes, and people walk to the edge — and this is when the plateau hears its people speak at full voice, because at the rim a voice can go outward, over, down into the cloud-sea with the un-becoming falls, and not trouble anything. Whole conversations are held facing the lowlands. Courtships are conducted there, half in words sent over the edge and half in the mirror-flashes of midday — every house keeps its polished obsidian disk, and there is a whole grammar of small flashes, and a patient flicker across a moss lawn from one window to another is how most marriages on the plateau begin. The other way they begin is walking. Lovers learn to walk in step until the ward, listening, hears one pair of feet instead of two. That is considered an announcement.

The reader kneels at the Mirror on a stone worn by generations of knees — the knee-hollow is one of the few marks a body is allowed to leave here, along with the polish on the door-stones and the dark shine of the sled-ways. In a town where everything is renewed block by identical block, where nothing is permitted to accumulate and the young rock records nothing, these small human polishes are the only patina, and they are treasured as such. The reader's craft is mostly breath: learning to kneel without rippling the water, to breathe around it, to bring nothing to the edge but attention. The reading is spoken — one soft sentence, the water is at the Line — and that is the town's whole daily liturgy. Then the knot is tied and hung in the Ledger-house, one knot, the same knot, added to the ranks.

The keepers will tell you the secret of the ranks, if you ask quietly. Every knot is the same knot. Every knot was tied by someone. An old keeper can run her stiff fingers along a rank and read the hands in it — here a keeper with a burned thumb, you can feel her caution in the lay of the fiber; here a young keeper, first year, the knots tied too tight, as if tightness could make the water behave. The sameness is real. The persons inside the sameness are real. The plateau sees no contradiction, and by the time you have lived here long enough, neither do you. Ask, and a keeper can find the knot of the day you were born. It is identical to tens of thousands of others. It is yours. People come sometimes and stand before their knot without touching it, and this is the closest thing the plateau has to a pilgrimage.

There is one other cord, kept apart, and it is short enough to hold in one hand: the cord of different knots. The rockfall that took the eastern comb-frames. The thick year. The birds — three knots for three birds across the whole memory of the town, and beside the cord, in a small box of basalt, a feather, grey, nothing special, shown to every child. The telling of the bird is the first story a plateau child learns, and the tellers make it carry a whole category of the world: wings, song, a creature that eats things it catches with its mouth. The children listen the way children elsewhere listen to stories of the dead — with the particular thrill of a true thing that does not exist.

The second story they learn is the two stains. Every child is taken to the Ledger-house and shown the flood-stain high on the oldest wall and the drought-groove low, and is told: this was before we learned. The ranks hang between the two marks. That is the entire curriculum of history, and it is enough.

Birth here is a matter of arithmetic and architecture. The population is fixed; a roof stands empty before a child is born into it, never the other way. So when someone dies, a runner carries it — death is news that must arrive embodied, not flashed — and the house enters its empty time: a full turning of the maintenance round, sometimes more, while the ward renews it, block by block, turf by turf, until the house has finished becoming itself again. Then comes the grant, and the grant is also carried by a runner, and the whole town can tell from the runner's pace which news it is. The finishing touch of a renewed house is a birth. And the sound no rule touches — the one sound exempt from every covenant on the plateau — is an infant. A house with a crying baby is the loudest house on the plateau, and no one has ever asked it to be quieter, because a crying house is a house that stood empty and does not stand empty anymore. People find reasons to walk past on the sled-way, slowly.

The number bends sometimes; it does not break. Twins are born into a house granted for one, and the town absorbs it — the next empty house stands empty a little longer, and the cord of different knots may take a small joyful knot, and everyone eats the same. A midwife holds the sharpest edge of the covenant in her hands, and she is honored accordingly, and feared a little, the way you fear anyone whose work touches the arithmetic.

The dead take up work. There is no beast here, and yet the tool-kits include bone, and the plateau is honest and gentle about what that means. The soil is a hand deep — you cannot give a body to a hand of peat — and nothing with jaws or grubs will come to do the cleaning. So the dead are laid in a still place, a moss hollow at the seep-ward's edge, where the mist and the moss do in years what elsewhere is done in weeks, and when the bones come back clean they come back into the houses as awls and needles and pins, and every bone tool has a name, and the name is used. You mend fiber with your grandmother. You set a sluice-float with your grandfather's awl. Grief is wet on a wet face — the plateau makes it easy to hide, and nobody hides it — but the dead do not entirely leave, and the houses hold them the way the houses hold everything: exactly, and in use.

Now and then the plateau grows a person the hush does not fit. It happens; it is remembered. The town's answer is not to break such a person on the covenant but to place them, the way everything here is placed. The loud-hearted are given the loud places: the comb at dawn, where the drip-music is as close to thunder as the plateau comes; the hammer's hour, gladly and often; the long watch at the notch; the rim at evening, where a voice can go over the edge. There is room for loudness here. It is just that the room has edges, and the edges are exact.

At night the town hears itself sleep. The turf roofs drink overhead, and condensation ticks off the lintels, and the wards lie quiet under the mist, and everyone knows their neighbors' night-sounds the way you know their footsteps — one beloved old snorer in the west ward whose rumble is, by unspoken agreement, counted among the sounds of the plateau itself, like the seep-hiss in the tuff. People dream, and what they dream is their own business, but it is said that lowland dreams — dreams of noise, of things chasing things — visit children around the age they are first shown the feather, and pass.

They think about the lowlands more than they admit. On clear evenings, facing the warm haze below, they speculate: a world where things eat things, where water runs downhill in actual arguments, where sound has no covenant. It frightens them and it pities them, that world — they speak of it the way you speak of a relative who never learned to be still. Sometimes, rarely, a young person stands at the rim longer than the others, and the elders notice, and give that person the rim work, the comb work, the watch — everything facing outward — and usually the person settles, the way water settles back to its mark. Usually.

The cloud keeps its appointment every morning. So do they. That is, at bottom, what the people of the plateau have made of themselves: a kind of weather. Daily, cyclic, reliable, exact — a town that arrives on time, beads on its own fibers, drips into its own channels, and returns what it was given. They are the only animal here, and they have decided, together, one knot at a time, to be a quiet one: the mouth that takes no more than the night replaces, the voice no louder than the drip it listens to, the history that fits between two stains and fills the space with identical days, each one tied by a different hand.

Stand with one of them at a stone basin in the evening, in the warm wet, and watch. The drip falls from the moss lip. The basin is full to its mark. The ring goes out, and the surface comes back, and stays. And the person beside you breathes out — slowly, through the nose, the way they hum their songs — the way you breathe when a note you are holding is still, still, being held.
