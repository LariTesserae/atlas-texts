Begin the census with the ground, because the ground is the first resident, and everything else is a guest in its body.

Kneel — slowly, the way everything here does everything — and put your ear close to the Felt. It is not silent. Each scale of the tessellation, each plate of living hide the size of your hand, has its pore-mouth, and every pore-mouth is sipping: a faint, continuous intake, thousands of small mouths drinking the mineral breath that seeps up from below. It sounds like the last inch of a drink being drawn through a reed, multiplied until it becomes a texture of the dark. The hide is warm as blood. Press a palm flat and hold it there for an hour — you will not be able to watch it happen, but when you lift your hand the hide will have gathered toward it, minutely, curiously, the way a tongue gathers toward a taste. It samples every footstep. It knows the herds by flavor, the gnawers by flavor, you by flavor. The print you leave closes over slowly, but the Felt does not forget what it has tasted. Nothing here has had more time to remember.

Whether it is one animal or a nation grown seamless is the bowl's oldest open question. The tessellation could be the borders of many selves fused edge to edge, or the scales of a single self too large to feel its own extent. What can be said: a wound closes; the spires are its organs, breathing in steady columns at high breath; the drumming you feel through a flat hand is not only the herds' music — under it, slower, there is a peristaltic pulse, a contraction that takes minutes, the heartbeat of something the size of the world. And of everything that lives here, the Felt alone notices the blade of light when it leans through the notch. It has no eyes for it either, but it feels the touch, and tightens, the way a sleeper stirs when a door opens in another room. Whatever the makers were watching for, from their seat at the stair's head, part of what they watched was the ground noticing the sky.

It grows a thumb's thickness in the time a language dies. It has grown a thumb's thickness across the lowest glyphs. Do the arithmetic.

The herds are what the Felt permits to walk on it.

A felt-ox, close up, is a piece of the floor that learned to move. Low and broad, six-legged, each foot a pad wide as a spread hand with the nails drawn in like a cat's — an ox walks the way water lies down. Its plates overlap like bark, like shingles, like the tessellation of the hide itself, grey-black and worn pale at the edges; a young one is sleek, but an old one carries a garden — spore-dust in the plate-seams, ash in the hollows, a population of plate-ticks that has lived on that one animal longer than the animal's own calves have been alive. It has no eyes. Its blunt head is all muzzle and listening: nostrils that read the mineral grammar of the air, and a skull shaped like a sounding-box. Its whole carapace is a tympanum. An ox hears the ground the way you hear a room.

Its tongue is a strap of muscle set with keratin files, and what it grazes is the Felt's living surface — the thin nutrient film, the fresh folds thrown up at bloom. The Felt endures this the way a field endures cattle, and perhaps the way a field requires them: the herds crop, clean, spread spore in their seams and droppings, keep the pore-mouths open. At high breath, when the bloom moves in a slow ring around the vents, the herds follow in their long spiral, packed shoulder to shoulder until the floor seems to be moving as one fabric, and in a sense it is: reef below, Felt at the surface, herd above, mites in every seam of both. The rasping of a thousand file-tongues is the standing sound of the world, soft as rain that isn't.

They are not timid. Timidity is a quickness, and quickness is the one unforgivable sin here. An ox's calm is a technology: damped plates, padded feet, a pulse that does not spike. Their fear, when it comes, moves through the herd like weather — a slow leaning-away, a closing of the gap. And their joy, if that is the word, moves the same way. Lay your hand flat near a herd at the season of pairing and feel the duets: bull and cow a field apart, tapping shell to ground in a pattern sustained for days, a conversation conducted through the body of the Felt itself, which carries it faithfully, which carries everything. The floor is their telegraph and their confessor. It tells on them, and it tells for them.

Calves are born at the dark's center of the herd and fed red water mouth to mouth, because water is never once set down. Old oxen drift fringe-ward, where the cool spills from the rim make the Felt pale and tough, and there they stop, and the floor closes over them at its own pace, and the herd neither mourns nor does not mourn: it leans, and closes the gap, and the spiral goes on.

Beneath them lie the parishes.

A listener is a forgery. Spread flat against the ground in a shallow bed it has worn over decades, ribbed low like a skate, it is the size of a sleeping-hide and indistinguishable from one: its back carries the Felt's exact tessellation, the same scale-plates, the same pore-dimples, even false siphons that open and close in rhythm with the real ones. Only its edge betrays it, and only to touch — a fringe of cilia fine as ash, each one a tuned wire, drinking vibration out of the ground. A listener lies still for days the way other things breathe. It may wait years between meals. Its patience is not a discipline; its whole metabolism is geological, a slow banked fire that one good killing feeds for a season.

It chooses its ground by ear. The herds drum their territories; the gnawers keep their rounds; the mites seethe; and the listeners settle where the music is richest, the way hunters elsewhere lie along a river. Then the floor tells. A shell-edge clicked carelessly, a stride too quick, a calf wandering on the wrong rhythm — and the floor blooms upward. That is the only phrase for it: the ground opens like a flower, a star-mouth splitting where a moment ago there was honest hide, and the rim of the animal folds up and over in a single motion, and there is a wet thump, and a brief crackle of plates, and then floor again, slightly thicker, already smoothing itself. It is the loudest thing that will happen that day, and it is over before the herd has finished leaning away.

Whether the listeners are tenants or organs — whether they are the Felt's own mouths, grown where the game trails are sweet, fed back into the hide, or old unrelated animals protected by a perfect disguise — no one has settled, and the bowl offers evidence for both. Their flesh, where a strike has torn it, is felt-flesh. No listener has ever taken another listener. And in the days after a large killing, the Felt blooms in a ring around the parish, lush as a watered field.

The shroud-moths arrive within the hour.

They come out of the dark the way ash comes down from the Roof: silently, in ones and twos, then in a settling drift, homing on the thump of the strike from across the bowl, navigating by a sense that reads ground-sound the way a kite reads warmth. Each is broad as two hands, winged in ash-grey membrane scalloped like scale-tegument — the tessellation again; everything here wears the floor's livery. They land on the killing-ground and overlap wing to wing until the red is covered. A shroud. They drink what the listener leaves, lay their eggs in the warm seams, and their pale blind larvae tunnel down into the reef-ground, where the real work is done: the mingling of carcass with reef, the masonry of the underfloor, the slow conversion of the dead into the next layer of the world. The Felt digests its fallen from below, and the moth-grubs are its trowels.

They are also its couriers. Spore clings to them; they carry it spire to spire through the dark. And once in the bowl's turning, after the Seeding, they swarm — rising off the hide in spirals up the heat-columns until the air itself is soft with wings — and then the sweetness comes. That faint note in the mineral dark that comes and goes with the seasons: that is the moths, the musk of their congregation, drifting down from the columns where the kites are already waiting. The swarm is the moths' brief festival and the kites' harvest, and the sweetness is its perfume, and when it fades the year has turned.

The water belongs to the walls, and the walls belong to the gnawers.

Everything else in this census is of the floor; the cistern-beasts are of the rock. They are a second nation in the bowl, citizens of the vertical. Low, tortoise-broad, plated in interlocking scutes, with a blunt head and two ever-growing chisels of dentine that they must grind down or be killed by, a gnawer spends its youth at the pale veins of sweet-stone banded through the clinkstone, chisel-scritch, chisel-scritch — the only industry the bowl permits, carried on at the walls where the floor's tattling runs thin. The sweet-stone is a hydrate; the water is locked in the mineral itself, and the gnawer's gut is the only key. What it wins it stores in bladder-flesh, lobed sacs slung under the shell, until the plates ride apart on their seams and the beast waddles down from the wall heavy as a wineskin, sloshing faintly if you lay an ear against it.

The water is red. Iron in the veins wakes in the melt, and the bowl's only drink is rust-water, blood-water. Everything carries some. The herds shadow a fat gnawer for days, patient as everything here is patient, and sometimes an old gnawer, grown tame in the way of creatures whose defense is their own value, will seep at a seam-valve and let an ox-calf drink — the gentlest sight the bowl affords, a living well giving water mouth to mouth. Gnawers live long precisely because cracking one feeds everyone, including your enemies. Their armor is almost incidental. Their real shell is the law.

For when one does crack — under a rockfall, under a hunter — the bowl keeps its one truce. The spill lasts seconds, and for those seconds nothing hunts. Grazers converge, mites boil up from the seams, moths settle onto the wound itself, the killers come and kneel beside the grazed-upon, and even a listener, if the cracked beast lies across its parish, holds its mouth shut and lets the congregation press against its flanks. The floor drinks what the bodies miss. Then it is over, and the dark is as it was, except that everything within sound carries a little red in it for days. Trace any drink in the bowl back through the bodies and it ends at a vein of stone. The kites drink from the moths they take; the moths drank from the dead; the dead drank from the gnawers; the gnawers drink the mountain. It is the only rain this world has ever known: brief, red, and caught between bodies.

Below the notice of everything, the mites are having their own history.

Every seam of the tessellation is a street. Glass-shelled seam-mites graze the pore-slime in herds of their own scale; hook-legged plate-ticks ride the oxen and change hosts at the packed high-breath crowds like travelers at a fair; spore-mites keep cellars under the loose scales, farming drifted spore in chambers swept clean as granaries; the spire-throat mites live their whole lives inside the chimneys, on the warm uprush, filtering the breath of the mountain before the mountain's own lips taste it. At their size the Felt is not a floor but a country, with weather: the bloom is spring, low breath is drought, a footstep is an earthquake, a strike is the end of the world. Some of them are almost certainly the Felt's own — free cells, gardeners in its employ, tending the pore-mouths they cannot be told apart from — and down at that scale the border between resident and tissue dissolves entirely. Weighed all together, the bowl may be as much mite as it is anything.

Above it all, where the columns of warm air stand invisible, the ash-kites ride.

They are the circling you sense over the vents and never see. Membrane-winged, light as paper, ribbon-tailed, with no eyes at all and a face of deep heat-pits, they read the bowl as a map of temperatures: the vent-columns stand up like golden towers in their sense of the world, the herds are a slow warm weather moving on the ground, and the ember-threads deep in the cracks are rivers of fire to steer by. At the Seeding they hang in the columns' throats with their wide jaws open, sieving the rising motes, standing in the chimney of the world's one light. At the moth-swarm they hunt, silent as the ash-fall they resemble. They roost high, in the crevices near the notch, and when the rare blade of true light leans through and slides across the bowl, they cross and cross it like shuttles on a loom — not because they know it is there. They cannot. Nothing here has eyes for it. They cross it because their sky is made of warmth and the band of daylight does not exist in it, and the weaving of their flight through a thing they cannot perceive is a beauty available only from one seat at the head of one stair.

And then there is the light that is not light.

You will not see the glimmer itself, and you should arrange to keep it that way. What you will see, at Seeding-time, is a small cold bluish point, patient, winking in the dark in exact imitation of drifting spore — except that spore-light drifts, and this holds still, and the difference is the whole of the law. What it is, assembled from molted remains found in the ash at the dark edges away from the herds: a low thing on many jointed legs, furred in ash-pale bristles, with a stalk over its ring of hooked mouthparts, and at the stalk's tip a lantern of living spore-flesh. The glimmer does not make its light. Long ago it swallowed the Felt's spores and kept them alive, and it farms their glow the way the spore-mites farm grain — a little garden of lies carried above its mouth. It lives for the Seeding, when everything in the bowl is walking toward lights, when moths and the young and the curious are abroad and trusting. The kites never fall for it; they know it by its wrong warmth. But for the warmth-blind there is no learning here, because no one learns this twice. The bowl's creatures obey the law in the marrow. It is written down only for visitors: light in this world means food, or the thing that eats what seeks food. Do not walk toward a light here.

Which leaves the whoever.

They are gone, but they were tenants, and the terms of their tenancy are legible. They lived on the rim and never below it — not one stone of theirs stands on the hide. They climbed four hundred scooped steps carrying the most precious thing in the world on their backs, and at the stair's foot, in a roofless cell, they poured it out. The cup-shaped hollow in the threshold is worn smooth by pouring. Understand what that means, in the one place where water is never set down: the makers set it down. They gave back to the ground, unasked, the thing the ground cannot ask for. No other creature in the bowl's history has ever done that. It is the whole of their theology in one gesture.

They drew themselves with no eyes and great outsized ears, and the drawing is accurate — not of their bodies, necessarily, but of their practice. They listened. They pressed themselves against the landings of the stair where the floor-music runs up the rock, and they counted: the tally-marks are breaths, high and low; the concentric circles are the bowl and the herd-spirals and the ring of bloom; the tessellated glyph is the Felt, worn by everything, worn even by them in effigy. And at the stair's head, facing the notch, one of them at a time sat in the worn stone seat and held the office of watching: for the two or three days in a year when the world's geometry lines up and the blade of light crosses the bowl, and the ash shows silver within it, and the kites weave it, and the Felt — the only resident that can feel the sky — tightens under its touch. The watchers had eyes for it. They were the bowl's witnesses, the only ones it ever had, and the cairns on the rim say they did it for generations, and the reef-fur over their lowest glyphs says how long ago they stopped.

Where they went, the bowl does not say. Perhaps the bright country took them back. Perhaps they are ancestors of no one. But the laws are theirs, you can feel it — they wrote nothing down except the counts, yet everything a visitor is told, they were the first to know: do not be loud, for the floor hears and the floor tells. Do not walk toward a light. Drink where you can. Keep the mountain's calendar. Keep off the hide.

So the census, when you finish it, does not come out as a number. It keeps collapsing. The mites blur into the Felt's tissue; the listeners may be its mouths; the herds are its croppers and the moths its couriers and the kites carry its spore on their wings; even the oxen's calves are raised on water that the rock gave up and every body passed along. You set out to count the bowl's inhabitants and find instead: the floor, which is one; the walls' small nation of wells; the circling air; the false light; the gone watchers; and you — warm, wet, ringing, the anomaly, tasted by the ground with every step and remembered.

And long after you have gone up the scooped steps and out through the notch, the bowl keeps the flavor of you, the way it keeps everything: a thumb's thickness at a time, slower than a language dies, forever.
