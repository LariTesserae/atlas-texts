Start with the count, because the valley does.

Nothing here is born in the way you mean it, and nothing dies in the way you mean it. Lives are issued, spent, and collected, and the books balance by morning, like the water. If a moth dies against a bud at midnight, a seam swells by the next dusk. The population of the valley is a ration, kept since the beginning and never once overspent. There are no children here. The moths come out grown. The snails are simply there, the way streets are there: laid out, kept, and not discussed. Even the birds' chicks have the look of small adults, as though issued rather than raised. Everything arrives complete, and is withheld anyway.

Notice also what has been declined. No beetles, no ants, none of the small rustling tenure of an ordinary floor — the valley keeps no decomposers, because it keeps no death in stock; the floor does that work itself, overnight, and needs no crew. Nothing hunts. Predation is a form of change, and change is the one import refused at the rim. A hawk crossing over at noon looks down at the green floor, hears the chord, and keeps going. There is no seed here, no fruit, no carrion, no roost that isn't a mouth. The borders of the valley are not walls but answers, and everything that arrives decides, within the day, to be elsewhere.

What stays is the following.

**The moths** are the first thing to understand, because they are closest to the buds. How they arrive, no one has properly witnessed. Watch a seam at its brightest hour and what you see is: no moth, and then a moth, folded and damp as a kept letter, drying on the bud's warm flank. The buds release them the way flowers release scent — that is the best account, and it is less an observation than a surrender. Whether the moth comes through the lips or condenses out of the seam's own wetness, no witness has been patient enough, or the valley has been patient longer.

Hold a wing to the seam-light. The veins repeat the plan: the streets, the courts, the causeway going nowhere, the three small circles of the towers. Each moth carries the whole city into the dark, an ambassador with the map on its back, returning to a door that is the only address it knows. They feed at the unparted lips, drinking what comes through. They navigate by the chord; in calm air, when the chord goes diffuse, a moth will fly in widening spirals, listening, until the wind gives it back its geography. Its whole world is: bud, seam, dark, chord, bud. It lives days. It dies pressed against the warm skin, and the skin takes it back, and the count is made good. The moth at the seam tonight is the same moth as last year's, in every way the valley can manage. The valley can manage everything but one.

**The birds** are the only residents with a profession. Slate-green, nesting in the mouths of the hollow columns, laying eggs the exact pale of the reservations — the valley's one color for things that exist ahead of time. At dusk they distribute themselves among the mouths, one to a column, and each sings a single note, and the notes are corrections. The chord is the wind's; the tuning is the birds'.

What they are tuning toward is the question at the bottom of the place. Not a chord they were taught — a chord the valley was taught, once, before it stopped finishing things, and has been trying to remember ever since. The birds are its memory of its own intended note: the opening-note, the pitch at which the seams would part. Some evenings — a few a year, no more — the chord comes very close to true. Then every seam in the valley brightens at once, the knocking inside the towers quickens like something climbing faster, the moths batter themselves against the buds hard enough to bruise the green, and the snails stop grazing and turn their eye-stalks toward the towers. Nothing opens. The wind shifts a degree, the chord slides off true by less than a quarter-tone, and the birds begin again, note by note, column by column, employed by no one, paid in nectar. The buds feed two hungers: the moths', which returns, and the birds', which sings.

The birds do not know about never. Or they know, and it does not move them — for a bird, the note is not a means. They are the only contented residents, the only ones who live inside the *just* the way fish live in water.

**The snails** are the surveyors, and the archive. The lines they graze are not invisible to them: they are the edges of the reservations, and the snails keep them legible, cropping the turf so the pale rectangles stay pale and the street-lines stay close-shaved. Stand over an old one and look down. The lichen on its shell grows in districts — streets, courts, a hall's footprint in grey-green. Each snail carries one neighborhood on its back; together they carry the whole city, walking. The plan is kept on the shells because a plan that walks can survive a forgetting. There is no forgetting here either, but the valley keeps a written copy the way a careful mind writes down what it knows by heart: superstitiously, gratefully.

When two snails meet at a reservation boundary, they stop, and for an hour or more they touch, tentacle to tentacle — two surveyors reconciling their maps with their whole slow bodies. Then each goes on along its line, and if a street-edge has shifted a thumb's width in some court across the valley, both shells will show it within the season.

They are the oldest objects in the valley. The floor cannot digest a shell while its tenant lives, and the tenants live long, so the shells accumulate what nothing else is allowed to: time, laid down in layers, the valley's only sediment, its only history, written in the one script the floor cannot erase in a night. If you want to know how long the valley has been *about to*, ask the snails. No one reads shell. When one finally dies — rare; you could live here years and not see it — the floor takes the flesh overnight and keeps the shell for days, its one permitted litter, a small pale monument in the turf, before it softens and goes down. Where the new ones come from has never been seen. They are simply there, the way streets are there.

**In the leaf-cups** at the column bases, the original ration of water keeps the original ration of small lives. Threads, a finger's length at most, translucent, a fixed few per cup. You can count them — nothing here minds being counted; being counted is the local condition — and the count is always the same. They eat nothing visible. They sip the water they live in, and the water is, in some sense, them; the same atoms have been passing back and forth since the first rain. No one has found their function. They are the oldest continuous lives here, older than the snails, and the best guess is that they are what water dreams when it has been cycling too long. The cups are where the valley sleeps lightly. Move a thread from one cup to another and the cups will not keep your arrangement; by the next condensation the counts will have been made good, and no one has seen how.

And then there are the ones who only come at night.

By day the plan casts its shadow; by night it gets up and walks its rounds. Stand in a reservation in the deep dark and you will feel it before you see it: a long low ridge moving through the turf along a street-line, like something passing under a carpet, except it is the carpet. In the courts and the larger halls, the floor rises further. Upright shapes, slow, green, seamless — the almost of people. They have no faces, because faces are details, and details are for the finished. Their hands are there but unfingered, smooth as buds. They walk the plan the way you would walk a house you were thinking of buying: proprietorial, unhurried, checking. They pause where doorways will be. They turn the corners the plan turns. Sometimes one stops and presses a palm flat against the air — against a wall that has been reserved — and holds it there, and moves on.

They are warm. They smell of cut green. They make no footsteps — the turf gives them back their weight — but you can hear the small creak of tissue holding itself almost together, and sometimes a low knocking, like sap, like the towers, like stairs being climbed that aren't there. If you stand in their path they go around you the way water goes around a stone, noticing you the way the floor notices a fallen leaf: as something to be taken up later. If you stand in a reserved doorway and raise your hand, one may stop and press its palm to yours across the space where the wall is kept — the one permitted degree warmer than the night — and hold it, and move along, having checked you the way it checks the walls: for presence, for plumb, for readiness. You will smell of cut green for a day. You will feel, for longer, that you have been measured for something.

What they are depends on the hour you ask. They may be the tenders, still extruded when the plan needs pressing down — the grafting hands themselves, and if so, the mystery of the arches dissolves: persuasion needs no fingers, only patience, and the valley is nothing but patience with a shape. They may be the citizens — the population grown to the same stage as everything else here, issued early and withheld, the bloom's crowd taking possession of its streets each night and giving them up each dawn. Or they may be a memory: if makers were ever grown and then drunk back, the valley replays them the way the water replays rain. All three accounts are true, or all three are the same thing at three hours of the night.

Before dawn they go down. Not dissolve — descend, unhurried, the turf closing over them like water over a stone. By full light the courts are empty, close-cropped, disciplined, warm by the one degree.

Once — only once in anyone's account — something changed. In a reserved hall near the causeway, a doorway altered its width by a hand. Within a season, the snails' maps showed the new width. Nothing else here has ever changed. Perhaps someone lay down on the plan at the wrong hour and stayed too long, and the valley, which keeps nothing, filed the shape.

Count them all, then — the moth at the seam, the bird at the mouth, the snail on its line, the threads in the cup, the walkers in the courts — and you will find you have taken one pulse at five points. There is one resident here. The valley lives alone, dreaming several. The moths are its pollen that never leaves. The birds are its ear, tuning it to itself. The snails are its written memory. The threads are its dream of water. The walkers are its dream of people. It lives here the way a bud lives on a branch: as the part that hasn't happened yet, around which everything else is arranged.

Everything that lives here lives at the door. The door is the only finished architecture in the valley, and it is finished because it was never built — a seam, a lip, a glow, a warmth — and on the far side of it, pressed against the other skin, all of them, all of it, just about to.
