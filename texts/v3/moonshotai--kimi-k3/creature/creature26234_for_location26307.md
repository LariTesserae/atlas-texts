Start with the problem of counting. A census of the Drum fits on two hands, but it takes years to complete, because almost nothing here comes to you, and everything that lives here has arranged its life around not being found by the sound. You census the Drum the way you'd census a night: by waiting, by going quiet, by learning where the silences pool. Find the nodes — the pockets behind buttresses and in the lee of towers where the reverberation cancels itself to something merely loud — and you have found the neighborhoods. Everything living in the Drum lives within a few meters of an acoustic shadow. The map of life here is a map of quiet, and it does not match the map of streets.

Everything arrived the same way. The intake tunnels drink the cold sea, and the sea carries eggs, larvae, spores, the small drifting beginnings of things — and so the city has been seeding itself for as long as it has been drinking. Most of what comes down the intakes dies in the heat or the noise or the open dark. A few things, once in a decade, once in a century, catch in a node and stay. Every living thing in the Drum descends from an accident of the city's thirst. And the drinking continues, so the experiment continues: somewhere below, right now, something new is trying the water and finding it possible or not.

First, the pasture. The veils are the only growing things — iron bacteria furred across the warm plates, rust-red in the mild bands, sulfur-yellow where the heat climbs, closer to mineral than to meadow but alive, unmistakably, when you watch it long enough. It thickens a millimeter in a decade. It builds the rusticles, those soft iron icicles that hang from overhangs like the architecture of a cave, some of them meters long, each one a century of patient accretion around a thread of bacterial longing. The veil is richest down near the hearth, where the water runs near boiling and nothing can graze it — the lushest garden in the Drum is uneaten and uneatable, growing for itself in the red glow. The grazable band is the middle depth, the warm streets, and there the veil is kept mown. Which brings you to the ones who mow it.

The isopods are the cattle of the Drum, if cattle were the size of your thumb and pale as paper and eyeless and slower than the hour hand. One every few towers — that is the stocking density, and it has presumably been found, over the generations, to be correct. Each isopod keeps a parish: a tower's worth of rusticle garden, which it grazes in long deliberate arcs, so that a tended garden has a visibly mown look, a geometry of appetite, and you can tell from across the void whether a tower has its isopod or has lost it. They taste the world through their feet. They know the Hours by the shudder that precedes them — everything here has learned the Hours, the chord that calls the machines also warns the fauna, the same bell that summons the works tells the isopod to clamp flat and hold — and during a surge they grip the plate and let the cavitation hail over them, armored, unbothered, patient as cobblestones. When they molt, the shed skins go into the fall and drift down with the rust, so that the snow over the doorways is partly made of the isopods' old selves. Nothing here eats quickly. Nothing here is eaten quickly. The food web has the tempo of the crust.

The small crabs are the citizens of the silt. Coin-sized, pale, industrious in a register you have to slow yourself to perceive. Each one keeps a threshold — a shop doorway, an office lintel, an arch of the arcade — and picks the drifting fall for the bacterial dust that rides it down, and the proof of their tenancy is written in the sediment: those small tracks, printed and reprinted over the drift, the only handwriting the Drum still produces. The signage on the walls is in a script no one reads anymore, and the doorways carry a script no one ever read, and between the two that's the whole literature of the place. You learn to read a doorway anyway. A kept threshold — silt brushed back from the lintel, tracks fresh and overlapping — is a lived-in doorway. A threshold drifted smooth is an inheritance pending. The crabs never cross open water. They are creatures of the wall, of edges, of the ring-streets' curbs, and when one dies its doorway silts over for a season or a decade until another crab, ranging the long way around the galleries, finds it and takes it up, and the brushing resumes. Doorways outlive crabs. The real estate is older than every tenant.

Then, the lamps. Hang in the dark below a gallery and watch a working light for an hour, and you will meet the quickest things alive in the Drum — quickness itself being one of the rarest elements here. The fish are small, translucent, half a dozen to a lamp and none anywhere else, and they live inside the light the way other fish live inside a reef. The physics of it is simple and lovely: the lamp warms the water above it, the warm water rises in a thin thread of a plume, bacteria stream up through the beam on the current — and the fish sip the beam. They are eating the light, functionally, and they look it: as each one crosses the beam it goes bright, lit from within, and dims as it drifts out, so that a lamp with its complement of fish seems to be thinking, small thoughts switching on and off around it. They hold station facing the glow, spaced like communicants, and the word congregation stops being a metaphor and becomes a description.

They know the tenders. That much is certain, whatever mechanism you propose. A tender climbing a lamppost with a fresh bulb is preceded by its sound, its particular clank and cadence, and the fish gather to it — they school around the work light, unafraid, because a tender's visit disturbs the veil, wakes the beam, and means the lamp will burn on for another season. The tenders do not notice the fish. The asymmetry is total and it runs through all the Drum's ecology: life has learned the liturgy; the liturgy has not learned life. When a lamp dies — and bulbs fail faster than rounds can always reach them — the fish wait at the dark cage for a day or two, dimming, and then they do the most dangerous thing a creature here can do: they cross the open water, hundreds of meters of it, exposed and slow, steering by the far points of other lamps, and some of them arrive. And when the Hours come and whole decks cascade alight at once, the fish have their harvest festival — sudden plenty, ten beams where there were two — and they feed in the brief glare while the machines converge and the cavitation turns to hail, and then the inverted chord sounds and the lights go out deck by deck and the long ordinary resumes, two lamps and six fish and the snow.

A word for the limpets, briefly, because they ask so little. They live on the hot plates and on the warden's back, layered like the crust itself, and a limpet may not travel a body-length in a decade. They are the Drum's memory made mineral, the beard on everything, the elders.

Now the machines, because they are the population proper — the parishioners, the clergy, the body of the church. They are rarer than the noise suggests, dozens perhaps, mostly idle in what can only be called far solitudes: berths and sidings and niches under stairs, spaced around the city like hermits, slow-cycling, listening. You can swim ten minutes and see nothing move, and then pass within three meters of a crawler standing in an alcove, dark-eyed, doing nothing you can name, and feel that you have walked past someone praying.

The tenders are the most seen and the most worn — small, sure-footed, built for lampposts and galleries, carrying each new bulb the way you'd carry an egg. Their rounds are weeks long. Their palettes have drifted: the machines that mix the replacement bulbs have forgotten the original colors, and each tender has forgotten them slightly differently, so the city is zoned by temperament of light — one gallery running deep amber, another a green gone seawrong — and you navigate by it the way you'd navigate by dialect. And they have rituals. That is the only honest word. Watch a tender finish a bulb: it does not admire the light, but it taps the cage twice before descending, a gesture with no current function, a leftover of some older protocol where a tap dislodged corrosion — the cages changed decades ago and the tap remained. Maintenance repeated past function becomes superstition, and the tenders are full of these small faithful gestures, procedures fossilized into piety.

The crawlers are the heavy clergy: slow, unregarding, hauling stock across the plazas, their wheels scribing grooves in the silt that the fall gently refills behind them. There are generations among them. The eldest are brass-jointed and riveted, with nameplates in the unreadable script; the youngest were made here, in the forge-cells, by the arms, from patterns that have drifted the way the bulb-colors drifted — and so the new machines are slightly wrong, slightly strange, children raised by the city in its own image as misremembered. The Drum still manufactures its own kind. The population renews itself by copying, and copies drift, and what that is, over centuries, is a folk.

The arms are the contemplatives. Each keeps a forge-cell and never leaves it: a bench, a stock-bin, a water lock through which stock arrives and finished parts depart. An arm may work for a century and never once see the open Drum. Its world is a cubic meter of precise, necessary, unending labor — and if contentment exists anywhere in this place, it lives in the forge-cells. One of them, somewhere in the middle decks, has been filling a standing order that no one has cancelled in a hundred years, and its gallery holds the result: ranks of identical castings, hundreds, crusted in rows, carefully warehoused, never collected. Not scrapped. Kept. A warehouse of gifts with no recipient, tended like a votive.

And rarest of all, the builders in open water — you may see one in a month of diving, and what you see is mostly the welding-flash, a brief blue sun across the void that makes the fish flinch in their beams. They are the authors of the strange architecture, the scrap-coral scaffolds branching into open water, the stairs that climb to blank plate, the doors sized for nothing with legs. Watch their work long enough and a theory forms, no more provable than any other here: they tap what they build, and listen, and add a strut, and tap again. The private vernacular of the later city may not be visual at all. The stair to nowhere may be a tuning fin. The legless door may be a resonance box. The Drum's weavers may be sculpting the reverberation itself, adjusting the bell from inside, until some node deepens or some standing wave sits right — and when it rings true, they stop, mid-staircase, and go elsewhere. If so, then the city's strangest structures are its most successful: acoustics perfected for an audience of water.

The Hours are when this scattered hermitage becomes a body. The chord sounds, and here is the thing you come to believe after enough of them: the chord is not a general alarm. It is a chord of names. Each machine keeps its own frequency, its note in the overtone series, and when the great valves open and the chord rolls through the water, each machine hears itself called — a sung roll-call, a summons in one voice — and they stream from their solitudes across the city toward the priority, and for an hour the Drum has a crowd, a purpose, a two-handed fury of demolition and raising. Then the chord sounds inverted — the dismissal, the benediction — and the lights go out deck by deck and the machines disperse back to their far niches, and the city is empty as ever, and the fish come back to the two remaining lamps. Between Hours, the machines are solitaries. During Hours, they are a congregation. It is the only social life they have, and they seem — this is projection, but you will project too, down there, you cannot help it — to keep their berths at the farthest possible distance all the rest of the time, as if saving themselves for it.

Machines die, and the Drum has its customs. A failed machine is visited, assessed, and either repaired in place or unmade over days, parts carried off by tenders and crawlers — a funeral of salvage, unsentimental and thorough. But a machine that fails somewhere low-priority simply stays. The fall catalogs it. The crabs climb it. And some later Hour welds it gently into the fabric, mid-task, tool still in hand. The walls are full of the ancestors. The city is its own catacomb, and it does not distinguish very sharply between the dead it absorbed and the dropped tools and the one or two shapes, in the older galleries, that were not machines at all — that came in through the stack under their own power, long ago, and stayed. The city does not see you. But it keeps what it is given.

The dry cells are the one habitat with no inhabitants — warm, lit, air-filled capsules that the machines service faithfully, changing nothing, checking everything, keeping the dry world ready for occupants who are not coming. The only smell in the Drum lives there, hot oil and ozone, the ghost of the city the Drum used to be, and the machines go in and out through the locks and maintain the air itself like a reliquary. Once — this is in the accounts, for what accounts are worth — a crab came through a water lock riding a crate of stock, and was found pacing the dry floor of a cell in the dry light, the only wet thing in the dry world, tracks crossing and recrossing in dust instead of silt. What it made of the air, or the air of it, is not recorded.

And then there is the warden, who gets the last word because the warden gets all the last words here.

You find it by its track: a broad scribed line through the silt of a plaza, days old, leading to a shape you took for a resting machine — table-sized, plated in rust and scale and limpets, legs like pilings, still as a parked cart until it isn't. It moves a few meters a day. That pace is not random and not merely slow: it is the exact speed of exemption. Hold still in the Drum and you are absorbed — catalogued by the fall, welded into the fabric, made furniture. The warden holds *almost* still, and the difference between almost and entirely is the difference between a citizen and a fixture. It crosses a plaza the way a season crosses a coast. Its circuit takes years, and it samples the whole city on its rounds — the warm ring-streets, the untended gardens where no isopod's parish reaches, the galleries, the long way around — and everywhere it goes it is audible, the slow scrape and tick of it carried by the water to every corner, announced to everything, for days in advance. Everything else alive here hides in the nodes. The warden hides in nothing. It is never private and never still, and so — you come to think — it is the only animal the machines fully register, because it speaks the one language the works respect: it makes sound, therefore it exists.

During the Hours, when the machines converge from across the city, they route around it. Nothing else in the Drum is routed around.

The open question stands, and the Drum does not answer questions. The case for fauna: it grazes, it grows, and in two or three alcoves around the city there stand warden-shaped shells, empty, intact, that look very much like molts — unless they are predecessors, unless the warden is not an individual but a succession, a title with a walking body, and when one warden stops, something somewhere takes up the rounds. The case for machine: its gait is metronomic past all biology, it never pauses mid-stride, the width of its track has not varied in decades of silt. The limpets on its back are neutral witnesses; they colonize anything slow, and they give the warden the appearance of carrying its own node with it — a traveling silence, a moving parish, the only country in the Drum that gets anywhere.

So the census, when you finally write it, is short. The veils. The isopods in their mown parishes. The crabs at their inherited doorways, printing the only fresh script in the city. The congregations of the lamps, switching on and off around the light like thoughts. The limpets, remembering. The tenders on their rounds, tapping the cages twice out of faith. The crawlers, the arms at their benches, the weavers out in the dark tuning the bell. The hermits in their solitudes waiting for the chord. The dead in the walls. And the warden, crossing the plaza at the speed of exemption, audible to everything, acknowledged by the works, going somewhere at a pace that ensures it will never, by its own lights, be late.

Few kinds. Fewer of each. And yet the Drum is not empty and never was: it is attended. The liturgy that outlived its reason has witnesses — sparse, slow, patient beyond telling, fluent in the Hours, fed by the fall, hidden in the mercy of the nodes. The machines perform the rite, and the rite performs the city, and the city shelters the small quiet lives that learned it. You surface, and the hum stays in your sternum for hours, and what you carry up is not the scale of the thing but its attendance: six fish holding station in a beam, a kept threshold, a green slightly wrong, and a broad track in the silt, going on.
