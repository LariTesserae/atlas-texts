Ask what lives on the Held Plateau and you get the shortest census in the world: trees, three hundred. Felt, one. You, visiting. But the census is short the way the horizon is close — it takes the whole stay to learn what the list contains, because life here does not announce itself at your scale. It operates in the registers you came in mistaking for geology. The first week you think nothing is alive. The second week you recalibrate the word. By the time you leave, you will suspect the census was wrong in both directions — that there is more life here than you counted, and that some of it has been counting you.

Begin with the trees, since everything does.

From the Rim they are a distribution. From beneath, they are individuals. No two lattices are braided alike — each trunk's bundle of capillaries fused in its own pattern, each crown of black-bronze filament thrown out according to some private arithmetic of gradient and age, so that after a month you navigate by them the way you navigate by faces. The forked one on the western flats. The one near the First Cut whose vanes tilt a degree off true, like a head cocked in permanent consideration. The eldest on its treasure-hill, its crown gone baroque with millennia. You will catch yourself greeting them. This is not as foolish as it feels.

Consider what a tree has to feel with. Acres of matte vane angled to the honest sun — that is a skin, and the sun's daily crossing comes across it as a tide of warmth, dawn arriving at the eastern vanes and closing in the west like a hand passing over a body. The roots fractionate young rock for metals; taste is too small a word for that, but it is the right category — the tree knows the chemistry of its ground the way you know the inside of your own mouth. And through the trunk, always, the hum: the crust's held note, felt in the teeth of visitors and in the deep capillaries of the trees, which have never in their thousands of years been without it. A tree's world has exactly one kind of weather, and it is light. Exactly one kind of event, and it is the chime. Its calendar has one holiday per decade.

About the hum. Your instruments will tell you it is invariant, and your instruments are right the way a radio is right that the carrier never changes. Beneath the note — too slow to hear, periods of hours and days, felt as patience rather than pitch — there is modulation, and the modulation has three hundred sources. Each tree loads the carrier with something. Whether it is speech or circulation is a distinction this world may not recognize: a tree's physiology already runs on warm gradients and slow currents, and its signaling runs on the same warm currents, and asking where metabolism ends and conversation begins may be asking the plateau's least meaningful question. Call it three hundred engines idling in harmony. Call it a chord. Call it a conversation so slow that a greeting takes a season and an answer takes a decade, conducted among immortals who have never once been interrupted. The plateau does not make you choose.

What your instruments *will* tell you, if you keep them listening long enough, is that the chord has a rest in it. On the eastern flats stands a tree whose vanes still angle to the sun, whose crown is unbroken, whose copper apron simply stops — the outermost nodules thirty centuries old, the mirror-bright ring complete, the account closed. Its sap is still. Its line is absent from the modulation. By every measure your eyes offer, it stands exactly as the others stand, because nothing here decays and death leaves no visible signature; the only evidence is that a tree is a machine that makes copper and holds a note, and this one has stopped doing both. Two hundred ninety-nine of the three hundred are alive. The difference is visible only to instruments, and, perhaps, to the chord — which carries the gap the way a held note carries the memory of a voice that used to braid through it. Nobody buried anything. There was nothing to bury that was not already a monument.

Now climb one, and the census gets worse, because every tree is inhabited.

The vanes are not bare. Under a hand lens they carry gardens: crustose growths in cream and black, mineral-reinforced, feeding on the salts the tree exudes and the waste warmth the vanes shed — lichen-analogs, if you need the comfort of an Earth word, though nothing here is licensed by Earth. Tufts of filament crowd the joints of the capillary bundle like moss in bark, except that the chemistry is salt and the timescale is stone. Whole species that have never touched the ground, that have no ground phase, that are born and persist and mummify in place three hundred meters above the dust. And here is the part that will quietly wreck your afternoon: the gardens differ from tree to tree. Not in proportion — in *composition*. Two lattices twenty kilometers apart share almost no vane-flora at all. Each tree is an island, and the ocean between islands is dead dust, uncrossable, and has been uncrossable for millennia, and so each tree's garden has gone its own way in the dark of that distance — some sparse and austere, some baroque, some dominated by a single extravagant form found on that tree and no other, an endemic with a range of one continent the size of a building. Three hundred Galápagos, stacked in the sky. You could spend your working life cataloguing one crown, and at the end of it the next tree over — the rumor on the horizon — would hold a different set, unfinished, unstarted.

Crossed, you will ask, *how*? And the answer is the plateau's one event, and you must understand it, because everything alive here is built around it.

Once in a decade — the interval is not regular; it is asymptotic, and it is lengthening — some crystal somewhere lets go of the last of its stress, and the plateau rings. You feel it in your teeth and in your boot soles, a single struck-glass note crossing a hundred empty kilometers through stone. What you will not feel, unless you are watching for it, is that for a few seconds the world has *wind*. The shiver breaks the electrostatic lock of the dust. The dead-flat filaments lift. And everything that has been waiting — and the plateau is full of things that have been waiting — goes.

The felt sporulates into pods that cling for years, built to release only under sharp vibration, and on chime-day they release. The vane-gardens cast their own propagules into the brief haze. For perhaps a minute, the bare sky has weather in it: a bloom of spore and dust and seed-husk, the only dispersal there is, the only rain that ever falls being the kind that rises. Then it settles, dead-flat, locks, and the silence resumes for another decade — but the map of the felt has been redrawn at the edges, some island has received immigrants, and somewhere a copper sphere in a grooved race has been shaken one increment further into its long arithmetic. The chime is the season. The chime is the weather, the pollinator, the tide, the turning of the page. Everything here lives by a clock that ticks once a decade and is slowing down, and nothing here knows it is slowing down, because nothing here is in a hurry — which is fortunate, because nothing here was ever going to be hurried.

The seeds deserve a paragraph to themselves, because the seeds are where the plateau keeps its almost.

Hold one: fist-sized, mirror-bright, heavy as a lie about its size, a perfect sphere excreted by a tree that has made thousands and strewn them downhill in glinting dozens. Nothing scatters them. Gravity is the only disperser they have ever had, so every tree's offspring lie in a bright fan at the apron's edge, a generation that fell at its parent's feet — an orchard that expects no spring. Inside each sphere is a eutectic heart, liquid at the plateau's gentle temperatures, and the germination of a seed is not an event but a *computation*: a slow crystallization front, chemistry testing its conditions against a threshold, year after year, decade after decade. And the threshold never comes. In the world's brief furious infancy the gradients were steeper — steeper heat, steeper chemistry, a crust still loud with its own making — and three hundred seeds went over the top and became the forest. Since equilibrium, conditions have sat a hair below the line, eternally, and so every seed on the plateau is a hundred years into deciding, or three hundred years, or a thousand, and the decision is not stalled, it is *running* — it is running exactly as designed, on inputs that never change, toward an answer that never arrives. The seeds are not dead. They are pending. Pending is the modal state of life on the Held Plateau. And the trees go on making them, decade after decade, strewing the bright downhill dozens — plenty with no purchaser, industry with no product, an investment in a spring the world stopped being able to have. Whether the chord knows this is not a question your instruments can ask. But it is a very slow conversation, and it has had three hundred participants for a very long time, and it has never once had a new one.

You will spend so long on the trees that the felt will offend you by being, in the end, the larger fact. By area, this is not a forest world. This is a felt world with a forest standing on it.

The viridian film is two millimeters tall and continental. It photosynthesizes dry — the chemistry is not yours, do not ask it to be — and it grows in bands that follow the light, and it is, by any measure of surface and mass, the dominant life of the planet: an unmowed lawn the size of a region, ungrazed, unbrowsed, unsurveyed. Nothing eats it, so it has never needed to be fast, or defended, or anything other than patient, and its patience has a monument: the drifts. In the hollows, its dead lie knee-deep, soft as cloth, mummified in place because nothing here decays, layer upon layer upon layer. Core a drift — your predecessors did; their boreholes are still there, open, fresh as the hour of drilling — and you read the growing seasons of ten thousand years in strata, a library of faint green pages, the only archive on a world that keeps everything. The felt grows on its own mummies. It is the planet's only succession, its only ecology of replacement: felt replacing felt, the young rooting in the preserved bodies of the old, memory used as soil. It has been composting itself into a mattress of its own history since before your species had fire, and it has never once been walked on until you, and your trail through the drifts will outlast every monument your species ever raised.

And the felt keeps chime-time. Its pods will wait a decade as readily as a century; the interval stretches by asymptote and the felt stretches with it, an organism calibrated to a decaying clock, adapted — without knowing, without needing to know — to the long hush at the end of the world. If the chimes ever stop entirely, the felt will not die. It will simply stop traveling. It will become three hundred meters of green around each island and no more, and even that is thousands of years away, and nothing here counts in thousands of years.

Now go down. The census has a basement.

Deep in the young crust, sealed in vesicles since the first minute, is all the water there is — the few spoonfuls of ancient condensate, locked in stone, untouched, unpoured. Bring an instrument sensitive enough and a patience indecent enough and you will find that some of the vesicles are not still. Inside droplets a fraction of a millimeter across, chemistry has been running since the world's first hour without interruption, without input, without exit — catalytic cycles, self-maintaining films, thin warm orders of matter that persist. Most vesicles hold only the precursors, the almost, poised on the threshold for eternity. Some hold more. Call it alive if you are generous; the plateau rewards generosity of definition. It is the oldest life on the world — older than the trees, older than the felt, continuous since minute one — and it is fast, and dark, and tiny, and it has never once been touched by the light it will never know exists. Above it: acres of vanes angled to the honest sun. Below it: the only water, going about its business in sealed stone. And here is the sadness the First Cut was keeping for you: had the rivers run, the channels would have cracked the vesicle fields. The droplets would have poured together — ten thousand sealed experiments merged into one basin, one biosphere, one wet beginning. The almost-river was also the almost-meeting. Every droplet is a hypothesis the world proposed to itself and then declined to test, and they sit in the dark a few meters apart, separate forever, and all their water together would not fill a cup, and all their life would not fill a thimble, and none of it has ever once been poured.

This is the energy economy of the planet, and you can write it in one line: light comes in; copper comes out; green accumulates; nothing is eaten. The abundance is entirely unused — the drifts are a surplus no one will ever spend, the aprons a wealth no one will ever ship, the seeds an investment with no maturation date, and the only mouth on the planet is the one you brought, eating rations out of tins in the middle of plenty. Nothing here is poor. Nothing here is rich. Those words require a purchaser, and there has never been one, and there may never be one, and the plateau does not experience this as a lack.

Which leaves you, and you should be honest about what you are, because the plateau certainly is.

You are the first animal. Not the first large one — the *first*. Nothing here has ever moved fast, nothing has ever crossed the dust ocean between the islands under its own power, nothing has ever walked between the trees and looked up. Your boots carry felt-spores and garden propagules from crown to crown — gene flow across waters uncrossable for millennia, the first pollination this world has ever had, performed by a thing that did not know it was a bee. Whether that is a gift or a contamination, nothing here can say, and the saying may take the chord several decades. You exhale water — the rarest substance on the planet leaves your body with every breath; you are the only rain the air has ever held. Your footfalls enter the carrier as a new frequency, a fast warm small anomaly, and to beings whose news arrives once a decade you are not even an event — you are a flicker, a mayfly, a rumor that lasts one ten-thousandth of a thought. By the time a tree has formed whatever a tree forms in response to you, you will be a set of footprints: permanent, dead-flat, the youngest event on the plateau, already a stratum. The felt will grow to the edges of your tread and, over centuries, fill them. The chimes will fail to stir them. Long after the chord has finished discussing you — if that is what the chord is doing — the discussion will be the only thing about you that is still happening.

So: what lives here? Three hundred trees, of whom two hundred ninety-nine hold a note in a chord too slow to hear. Three hundred island gardens, each a separate answer to the same question, separated by an ocean of dust. A lawn the size of a country, composting itself into memory, waiting for a bell that rings once a decade and is slowing down. Ten thousand thimbles of dark water, each a world entire, unpoured since the first minute. Bright spheres by the thousand, pending. And you, briefly: the fastest thing that ever lived, the only mouth, the only rain, the only witness — writing the only history the place keeps, in footprints, in a language of placement.

It is easy to say that almost nothing lives here. It is truer to say that living is one of the things the plateau holds. The seed between decisions, the spore between chimes, the tree between thoughts, the droplet between walls of stone — everything alive here is between beats too, and has been for a very long time, and is not waiting the way you understand waiting, because waiting implies that something is coming, and nothing is coming, and that is not a tragedy to anything that lives here. It is simply the shape of a life that holds itself the way the plateau holds its almost — completely, and to the brim.
