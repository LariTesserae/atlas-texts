They are called the hands, and the name is exact. It stopped being a metaphor so long ago that the chisel-script has no older word for person.

A hand is, before anything else, a sound. You will know this within a fill of arriving: the crews do not count heads, they count strikes. Every mallet in the Kettle falls a little differently — a setter's even patience, a scrabber's quick close tapping, the slowed deliberation of an old hand whose wrist is going — and the ringers, who can place a strike three walls away, carry the district's whole population in their heads as a kind of music. When they want to know whether everyone came up from the deep galleries, they do not call a roll. They listen for the chord to be complete. To be somebody here is to be audibly accounted for. A stranger is the one strike no one can place — which is why, when you come down off the spoil and take the first cord-path, a crew will find you, and give you one.

It works like this. A guest-knot is tied into the cord where you will sleep, and a guest-strike is agreed — two quick, one slow, or whatever the district is not using — so that every ear within earshot learns the unfamiliar hammering and files it under *accounted*. The cake they press into your palm is not charity. It is the other half of the contract: while you are on the cord, you are one of the sounds they make. If you stop sounding, they will come. If they stop sounding, you must go. And if your mallet falls silent because you were only resting — you owe each of your rescuers a fill of your labor. Rescue-debt. They collect it cheerfully, because the next time it will be real, and they will want you to come just as fast.

The hands themselves are smaller than you expect, and harder. They are not pale — pale is the color of things that hide: tickers, eels, the undersides of stones. The hands are the color of wet clay and old hide, wrinkled young by the damp, their wrists oiled with eel-fat against the chill that settles into every joint by mid-shift. Their palms are rubbed smooth within a few thousand fills — but the very tips of their fingers stay exquisitely sensitive, because those are reading instruments and are guarded the way you would guard eyes. Setters work barefoot in the wet: a floor tells the truth through skin, and a boot is a lie you can fall through. Their sight is good for exactly one range — arm's length, in red — but within that range they see more than you do. They keep a ladder of words for the useful reds, from the last black glow of a dying ember up to clamp-eye, and a well-fired brick's color can hold a whole crew's attention like a face. Which, in a way, it is. Most hands have seen the faces of the people they love only by firing-light: a dozen times in a life, red, at arm's length. The rest is voice, strike, palm, height, breath. Their word for *beautiful* means, at root, a joint your nail cannot find. They smell of duff-smoke and eel-oil and wet hide. After a fill among them, so do you.

Their speech is built for the noise: short words, hard edges, the important word said first and last, and no shame in repetition — repetition is how anything survives in surf. In the loud districts whole courtships are carried on inside the work-chant; a hand will say *I sang to her for three fills* the way you would say *we talked all night*. Where speech drowns entirely there is wall-talk: a palm pressed flat to the masonry, taps shaped into the old patterns, because the Work carries vibration farther and truer than wet air carries shouts. Gossip in the old districts travels literally through the walls. News of a naming can arrive under your boot before the messenger reaches the stair. And this is why there is no sneaking in the Kettle. Moving silently is not cleverness; it is the one thing a person can do that reads, to every listening ear, as either buried or burying. Thieves tap constantly, loudly, innocently — and are caught by the falseness of the rhythm. Strike-faking, the imitation of another hand's signature, is the deepest crime the crews know, deeper than theft of rope or metal. It steals the one thing everyone is guaranteed: to be known by their sound.

Children — soft hands — are tied to the cord before they can walk, and learn its knots before they have words, which is only just: the knots will save their lives more often than any sentence. They are lulled to sleep by hammering and frightened by quiet. A child who wakes in a hush cries, and no one hushes it, because crying is a sound and sound is safety, and besides, the adults are listening too. Until its first true strike a child is called by its tap-count — second-tap, fifth-tap of its crew. Then one fill, at the velvet trays or under a setter's tolerant eye, the mallet stops falling in imitation of someone and falls into a rhythm that belongs to no one else, and a ringer who has been listening without seeming to cuts it into the wall at hand-height, and the child is somebody. They carry that wall the way you carry a name, except that it is also a signature on the Work and, in time, a gravestone.

The crews divide the labor the way water divides itself — by finding its own level. Setters are the aristocracy, valued for the evenness of their strike and the patience of their bedding; a master setter lays by ear, hearing the true ring of a well-seated brick the way you hear a right word. When the bell-stones call for arch-hands, every crew sends its steadiest setter up for a fill on the great half-arches, and they come back ordinary and changed, and their strike is never quite the same — it slows, as if it has learned the size of the thing. Scrabbers go down into the deep galleries for duff and come up black, smelling of cold and metal and age, and they are the Kettle's accidental theologians, because they alone see the strata. They say one word to the seam before they take it. *Give.* And when the duff is fed to the ember-baskets, someone speaks a name aloud — any name, read off any wall — because a seam was somebody, once, and should not burn nameless. The ringers neither confirm nor forbid.

The ringers are chosen young: a child who can hold a chord in its head across a whole shift, who hums the district back to itself while scraping trays, is taken to the bell-stones and taught to hear in centuries. They keep no plans — no one in the Kettle keeps plans — but they keep the chart: a great wall near the basin's heart cut with every note that has ever rung flat, a map not of what to build but of what not to trust. Their authority is total and almost entirely negative. They cannot order a wall raised; they can only declare one unsound. They preside at three ceremonies — the naming, the doubling, the Quiet — and their whole theology fits on one chisel: listen first; answer everything; the Work holds what it holds.

Time is wet, and its priests are the gaugers, who read the cisterns and call the fills in a singing voice that is the one voice permitted to interrupt the bells. Cord-keepers are drawn from the still-handed — those whose wrists gave out early and whose patience did not — and a wrong knot is understood by everyone as murder waiting; cord-keepers are inspected, honored, and slightly feared. Lamp-wards walk the ember circuits with their oil-flasks, and a lamp-ward's route is the nearest thing the Kettle has to a constellation map. Children navigate by it.

Wealth, here, is warmth at a distance. The clamp-keepers are the rich: they sell ember-time by the fill, and a clamp mid-firing is bank, hearth, theater, and mirror. There is firing food — cake pressed double-thick and crisped against the clamp wall, eel smoked over duff coals, the vat broached, the vat being the black sour ferment of velvet that tastes of rust and smoke and keeps the cold from settling in the chest. And at a firing, a district discovers what it looks like. Children stare at their mothers' faces and are scolded for it. Old rivals fall silent at the sight of each other and cannot say why. This is when the doubling happens, because it is the only time two people can properly look at one another: two hands bring their strikes together until a ringer pronounces them in chord — one rhythm with two hearts — or does not pronounce it. That, too, is law.

There is no ground to bury in. There never was. The dead are given to the Work: a niche at hand-height in masonry still fresh, the chisel-button closed in the fist, the strike-name cut above. Then the ringer strikes the leave-chord on the bell-stone — the one signal that means *do not come* — and the crew stands quiet for one full fill of the vessel, the only licensed silence in a life, while the rescuers of three districts stand down. After that the mallets resume, and that rhythm is never struck again. The old districts are genealogies you read with your palms; children learn their ancestors by touch on the walk to the trays, and the dead outnumber the living in every wall, and are better remembered. A wall with the dead in it is said to ring warmer. Metal is the other inheritance: the soft precious chisel-stuff is never new — salvage adds a little, the melt keeps the rest — so the tool in your fist is, by weight, mostly ancestors. To inherit is to be handed a cloth of worn buttons and a place at the melt. The worst sentence the crews know is not death. It is to be chiseled smooth — name struck from the wall, buttons melted into a stranger's chisel — to become the only thing in the Kettle that was never touched by a hand.

Then there is everything that lives here without being a hand.

Velvet first, because velvet is first. The tenders farm it on trays of old brick and fight it everywhere else; half of maintenance is the war against the one soft thing, and children with bone scrapers are its infantry. Pressed cake is the daily food, smoke-and-iron; the vat is the rest-shift comfort; and old tenders die of velvet-lung, a cough like tearing cloth, and are given the driest niches near the clamps as honor. The hands are connoisseurs the way the hungry are connoisseurs. They can tell you which district's trays give the sweeter cake, and they argue about it like weather.

The eels come to rhythm. Eel-fishers crouch at the sump's edge and tap the black water with a bone wand — slow, slow, quick — and the blind white shapes rise to answer, because to an eel the whole Work must sound like the one river in the world. A district with eels under it counts itself lucky; a district whose eels leave checks its walls, and then its conscience. Hide for rope, oil for lamps, bone for needles and wedges, meat for the firing-table: an eel is a toolchest that swims. In the Drowned Rows there are eels longer than a scaffold-pole, and those the divers will not touch. The crews call them *tenants*.

And tickers, which no one has ever quite seen. You see where one was — a pale flutter the size of a thumb-joint, gone into the mortar gap before your lamp-turn finishes. They flee vibration, which is to say they flee people, which is to say the Kettle is fullest of them exactly where no one is looking. At rest-shift the walls come awake with them, and an old district ticks like a field of clocks, and the hands call this the second shift and find it restful, the way you find rain restful. Hands sleep best against a ticking wall; a quiet wall makes restless sleep, and is, not coincidentally, a wall to watch. The ringers read the tick as a pulse. The tickers eat the velvet in the gaps; the velvet eats the mortar; the hands eat the velvet. It is not a chain. It is a round.

The divers are a crew apart and know it. They grease themselves head to foot in eel-fat, pay the water first — a chisel-button dropped open-handed, because everything down there is old and some of it keeps accounts — and go down the hide lines into water that has never been lit. Some crews pay out a second line beside the diver's, bare, no hook, and let it drift. They call it the courtesy. Ask what it is for and they say: *company*. What keeps pace below the rope keeps pace with them too; it is not in a hurry, and so they are not either. You rise fill by fill. You do not look down. You do not stop tapping the line, because the taps are how it knows you are a working crew and not an offering. The bells they find ringed on the drowned lintels come up a color for which the hands have, correctly, no word, and they are never struck. They are hung where the drip will find them, so that the water plays them, slowly, across years. It is the only music in the Kettle that no hand chooses.

The doors are the scrabbers' scripture. Some are too low and too wide, their lintels worn by shoulders. Some are narrow and tall past reason. None are sized for hands. When a crew cuts into one, they do not go through — so far, in the memory of every wall, no one has ever gone through. They wall it back; the cord-keeper ties the stop-cluster; the ringer strikes the stone and cuts the flatness into the chart. The Kettle has had other tenants. The hands' entire position on the matter is an extension of etiquette: we are all crews in the Work, and you do not walk into another crew's front uninvited. No one, in the memory of any wall, has been invited.

And there is one exception to the oldest law. Answer every strike — except the one from above. The scaffold-crews learn it before their first ascent: when the mist goes grainy and the drip forgets its direction and the tapping comes down through the Lid, unhurried, many-handed, answering nothing, you keep your own rhythm steady and you do not answer. The ringers do not explain. They say: it has not answered us; we will not answer it; that is all the treaty we can afford. Hands who have been up too long come down with a habit of pausing mid-strike, head tilted, as if hearing their own rhythm played back slightly wrong. They are called lid-struck. They are rotated to the sumps to sleep beside honest water, and watched kindly, and the ones who beg to go back up too soon are not let.

And then, once in a firing, once in a flood, it all stops at once. The hands do not call that silence — silence is a small thing, an accident. They call it the Listening: ten thousand mallets ceasing in one breath because the basin has, for one breath, asked a question, and everything in it — hands, eels, tickers, the shape below the ropes, whatever taps above the mist — straining together toward the answer. When the tapping resumes, one mallet, then a crew, then a district, the hands go back to work with the briskness of people who have been told something and have agreed, as one body, not to repeat it.

So: what lives in the Kettle? A few thousand sounds with bodies around them. Eels and tickers and the one soft crop. The walled dead, who outnumber the living and are better remembered. A colleague below the ropes. A neighbor above the Lid with whom terms have not yet been established. And under and around and through them all, in every course and chord and inherited button of metal, the Work itself — the one arch, growing everywhere at once, patient as coral, certain as water. The hands are how it builds. The bells are how it hears. The hush is how it thinks.

Ask any setter what lives in the Kettle and you will get the only answer they have ever given, the one that is a promise and a job and a name.

More lives there.
