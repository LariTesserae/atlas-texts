Ask who lives here and the hollow answers with a question of its own: at what hour? The census is a different document at dawn, at noon, at dusk, in the dew-dark, in the one wet week of a rain-year, and the place files all of them. This is the whole roll: the citizens, the officeholder, the tenant, the tenantry, the sleepers, the rooted, the guests — and the dead, who have not exactly resigned.

Begin with the nine. Everything else here does.

The tortoises are the hollow's citizens in the old sense: they hold property, they keep the waterworks, they outlast the record. Their shells have bleached to the color of the walls — the light here pales everything it keeps — and their eyes are the black of the well-eye. Each maintains, at the shaded base of the wall, her own seep-hollow: a basin the size of a cupped hand, deepened a grain a year by forefeet grown smoother than the stones they work. A hollow outlives its digger. It is passed down, occupied, deepened again; the nine wells along the wall are older than the nine tortoises at them. What passes down here is not knowledge but addresses.

Once in a generation the census changes by one: a hatchling in a rain-year, digging out of the warm silt against the south wall, or — rarer, and never explained — a walker come down the ramp from the plain, a young tortoise with plain-dust still on her, arriving like an heir who did not know she had inherited. The grooves on the ramp are the signatures of those arrivals, some of them centuries apart. The century before this one kept seven. The slump-face suggests the number has never passed thirteen nor fallen below five. The bowl regulates.

The dawn queue at the well-eye is the oldest institution here, and no one has found its charter. The order is not age, not size, not the order of the hollows along the wall. It has survived deaths, which is the strange part. When one of the nine dies — and they die rarely, and are found stopped, and in time join the pale coins in the silt — the queue does not close over her place. It keeps her hour. The one who drank after her still waits out the full length of her drinking before lowering his head, and has done so for decades, as if her turn were held in trust. No one has explained this, and no one here is looking for the explanation.

Then the officeholder. The anvil-bird is singular by design: there is one, the office has one holder, the holder changes and the office does not. Crested, deliberate, gray as the debitage it makes, it performs the hollow's only permitted violence at its favored stones: the double *tock* — first note the blow, second note the stone letting go. Its trade is removal, like everything made here. It strikes flakes from cores gathered at the slump-face, and sometimes digs from the fill a flake struck long before its own hatching and re-strikes it, so that the newest edge in the hollow may be the oldest work. With the flakes it levers snails from their shells and splits the hard pods of the well-bush. The scatters at the anvils — emptied nacre shells bleaching to paper, pod-husks, the gray dust of reduction — are middens and archives both.

Whether the office is taught remains an open question. No one has seen a lesson. What is seen: a young bird, some years, watching from the horizontal tree, which is the office's lookout and its only perch. Then one season the old holder is gone and the young one is at the stones, striking badly, then less badly. Perhaps the stones teach. Give a bird these anvils, these snails, these pods, and the craft condenses out of the place itself — the workshop training its workman. The eldest tortoise has outlived two holders of the office. She has watched three learn. If anyone here knows whether teaching happens, it is she, and she is not telling.

The owl is the tenant. Its doorway — a burrow dug by a tortoise long since filed in the slump-face — opens at dusk like all the tenements, and the owl stands in it blinking, letting the light go out of the day. Then it does the one thing no other resident does: it leaves. Over the rim on soft wings, out across the plain, to hunt the night there and return before the first tortoise wakes. It is the only citizen whose work is elsewhere, the only one exempt from the ramp. Its castings at the burrow-mouth are the hollow's sole import: the small skulls and fur of plain-things that will never fall in on their own. Read the owl's pellets and you read the outside. It smuggles the plain into the record, a bone at a time.

Now the tenantry, who make the census a matter of timing. The silt-bees keep their bores in the wall-face, each tunnel the width of a straw, and the humming of the wall at dusk is them going on shift, and at dawn coming off. They work the dew-hours, when the well-bush opens its folded leaves and puts out its brief pale flowers, which smell, impossibly, of cold water. In rain-years, when the tree flowers too, the bees have their jubilee; in the long dry runs they keep to the bush's ration and wait, as everything here waits.

Below the bee-line, the eleven pits. Eleven is not exact; it is eleven the way the seep is a drop a breath — the place's manner of saying *this many, no faster*. At the bottom of each cone waits a larva, jaws like forceps, flicking grains at whatever the rim lets fall. For the rim is a provider: the plain's wind throws small lives over the lip, and what falls in stays, and some of it lands in a pit. When a twelfth pit opens within a handspan of an eleventh, the silt settles the dispute; there is afterward one larger larva. The adults — pale, gauzy, risen at dusk like the pits' own ghosts, which they are — last a few nights, mate over the well-eye, lay into the silt, and are gone. The pits are the children. The beautiful ones are nearly nothing.

The beetles work the water shift. All night they stand along the rim, head-down, dark beads on the lip of the world, drinking the dew off their own backs; at dawn they descend the flutes like a reversed rain, and by full light they have shut their wing-cases — the click is one of the sounds the listening-ground reports — and are under the stones, done. The gleaners among them never climb; they work the floor itself, and what they find on it is their own affair. The floor is for crossing, but they make a living of it.

And the snails, the night-scribes. Nacreous — their shells keep a tarnished pearl — they come out with the dark and climb the flutes to graze the lichen-strokes, each snail bound its whole life to a single stroke, a handspan of wall, reading it with its mouth and trimming it into legibility. Behind them, the silver: wet trails catching what little light the night offers, so that by moon-dark the walls are covered in a fresh edition, written in a language of appetite, sentence over sentence, no two nights the same text. The silver never crosses the glassy patch; the snails turn at its edge, and why they turn is one of the books the hollow does not open. By noon the edition is burned to salt-film and the walls are blank paper again. The emptied shells — levered at the anvils, dropped, bleached — go into the silt like pages torn out and filed. The bowl keeps them.

Then there are the residents who are mostly not here. The shield-shrimps spend their tenure as eggs in the dry silt, waiting out the years between rains with a patience even the tortoises do not attempt. When the skin of water comes they hatch within hours, and for one week the floor is busier than the plain above has ever been: they swim on their backs, ancient faces turned down, as if reading the silt for the date, and the anvil-bird gorges, and the owl takes them two at a strike, and the tortoises ignore them entirely — drinking standing, the queue dissolved, all licenses granted. The week's purpose, insofar as a week can have one, is to put the next waiting into the mud. Then the drying returns them to the census of the sleepers.

The rooted are the slowest clocks. The tree has never stood; standing is not among its customs. It grows sideways out of its crevice, flag-formed, casting the floor's one sideways shadow, and it leafs only in rain-years — the leaves wait folded in the bud for years at a time, patience being the local currency, minted in several denominations. The week the shrimps swim, the tree greens and flowers, the bees riot, the pods set; then it stands bare again, a gray flag over its own shadow, for as long as it must. Its crevice sits on the seep's seam; it drinks from the same slow agreement that keeps the well-eye. If the tortoises are the citizens, the tree is the founding document.

The well-bush keeps the seep's shade and folds its leaves like closed hands all day, opening them at dusk to the dew. The lichen-strokes in the flutes grow a hair a year; a stroke the length of your forearm predates the tortoises, predates perhaps the tree. The flutes were written by rain; the lichen copies the manuscript stroke by stroke; the snails annotate it nightly; noon burns the annotations. It is the one book here that is always being written and never advances.

Under the overhang, in the two handsbreadths of shade that are the last of the old cave's dark, lives — some say — the cave's remaining citizen: a small white thing, blind, with no use for the light-well's one abundance, holding a citizenship the cave itself resigned when the roof let go. You will not see it. The tortoises drink around it. It never crosses into the light, so it never enters any census — and so the census, however careful, is always off by one.

The guests do not count, and count anyway. The swifts come down at noon on the hollow's one breath, the only fast things, stitching their thin screams through the bright, and are gone. Speed here is lent, not held; they return it when they leave. And there are the midges of the dew-hour, whose entire lives fit inside the hour everyone else merely visits — the smallest denomination of the local currency, spent at once.

Under everything, the second census: the slump-face layers with their drowned pit-dimples, their flakes at many depths, their pale coins of shell. Nothing resigns from this place; it is promoted into the record. The eldest tortoise will one day stop at her hollow's edge, and the queue will keep her hour, and the rain-year floods will carry her down through the fill a grain at a time until she lies at a depth that someone, someday, will read as history.

Add the roll: nine citizens, one office, one tenant, eleven pits, the bees in their hundreds, the beetles, the night-scribes by the handful, the sleepers beyond counting, the rooted three, the guests, the blind keeper, the dead in their layers. The sum is one. What lives here is the keeping itself — a single long life that wears tortoise and bird and snail the way a day wears its hours, that pulses at a drop a breath, that breathes once daily at dawn and noon, that keeps its population the way it keeps its water: in time, not in place.

Come at dusk, when the burrow-mouths open like eyes and the wall begins to hum and the first snail lays down the first stroke of the night's edition. Stand still in your doubled shadow. You will feel it — the hollow noticing you back, entering you in the record: a thing that fell in.
