The first citizen of the kettle is the floor.

It is one body. Ask the house-layers and they will tell you what their grandparents told them: the felt came here the way everything here came, on a mountain's hide, in the years when the buried ice was still sweating itself hollow and the floor below was nothing but wet gravel breathing mist. A sleeper wallowed in the new wet, and where it wallowed, the waxen nap first took. From that halo it walked itself outward across every handspan of the room and partway up the walls, and it has been walking ever since — into footprints, which it closes inside a week; over dropped awls, which it takes inside a month; through dead huts, which it digests inside a year. On its surface the floor has a memory exactly that long. Beneath its surface it keeps everything it has ever eaten, in the order it ate it, compacting down toward the old dirty ice that is the true floor of the world. The floor forgets. The ice remembers. Everything the people do is a choice about which of the two to give a thing to.

The columns are the floor's organs — its way of breathing and weeping at once. Each hollow stem wicks the seep upward through itself, rimes the wet cold along its length, and melts that rime again where its crown reaches the warmer air pooled high in the room, so that the water falls back in single drops: a rain the size of a room, one drop at a time, every column keeping its own interval in the dark. The people hang leather buckets beneath the granted stems and drink the room's rain; children learn the half-dozen sweet columns whose rime may be licked, the way children elsewhere learn the locations of fruit trees. The standing forest is infrastructure and may be cut only by grant; the deadfall is frame and fuel and fiber. And the drip is more than water. It is the map. In a lightless room, each column's rhythm is a landmark, and you cross the kettle by its rain — the quick nervous drip near the seep's eye, the slow metronome of the sweet column, the double-beat of the forked stem that marks the turn toward Three-Plate's ground. A column harvested is a landmark gone; the house-layers weigh that loss against the need for timber, and grant accordingly.

The floor has moods, and they run on the seep. When the deep water swells, the threads — the seep's fingers winding through the felt — quicken first, and the blind white fish ride up out of the rock, and for a season everyone fishes by feel: a bare arm laid in a thread, still as a column, waiting for the touch-and-take. The catch sets the year's light, because fish oil is lamp oil, and lamp oil is counted the way other peoples count grain. When the seep pulses deeper still, it brings up the bloom — the faint cold luminescence that lives somewhere below — and the floor filters it and shines: the bright water, two nights of dim blue with the columns standing in it like reeds in a moonlight that has forgotten the moon. On those nights the nap brightens where it is pressed, so that for once the room remembers your steps in light behind you, fading. Children chase their own shining trails. Lovers walk the intervals by sight, drunk on seeing. And sometimes, on no schedule anyone has ever read, the floor crawls: a swarm night, the whole felt surface rippling with small pale bodies rising to some summons of the deep water, and the town turns out with trays and scoops the glut and fills the ice-caches, and the children are excused from stillness practice, because abundance here is rare and total and must be met with both hands.

Of the floor's ordinary traffic, the most important are the small herd: forearm-long, ringed, soft as the nap they rasp, pale as everything, slow as everything. They follow warmth the way everything here follows warmth, and so they graze the melt-halos, and so hunting them is mostly a matter of knowing where they will be. Their flesh is sweet; their fat burns poorly but feeds well. The people shear the floor itself, too, in rotation, taking the nap the way a shepherd takes a fleece and leaving the underlayer, and the sheared felt is fulled into hut panels and wicks and clothing. The one fresh thing in the diet is halo-nap — the thick new growth flushing up out of a mountain's crushed ellipse, sulfurous and faintly sweet, eaten in the first days of a wallow. Color barely exists down here: the world's palette is pale, dark, the amber of lamp-flame, the blue of the bloom, and copper-green on the oldest needles. The people have more words for grades of warmth than for all colors together.

There is one citizen of the floor that the people mark but do not eat. The pale trap lies flush with the felt, the size of a sleeping-hide, still for weeks, its mouth a seam. Its sign is a patch of floor too tidy — the nap leaning faintly inward, the small traffic absent — and the house-layers ring such patches with gravel that the sole can read, and children learn trap-sign before they learn the drip-map. It culls the herd. Its flesh is bitter. In its own time the floor takes it back, as the floor takes everything: the unmakers — the pale worms, the rising fringe of the felt itself — close over every kill-site like slow water, and within a season there is only a hummock, and then only floor.

And then there are the emigrants — the groomers, thumbnail-pale and quick as thought on a warm flank, who long ago left the floor for a better address: the mountains.

To understand the great ones, begin with what lives on them. Everything else here does.

A great one is a house that walks. Low, broad, dome-backed, its hide armored in horn-sheathed plates that overlap in courses like a roof, it comes down the gravel slips of the rim in a controlled avalanche, plates clashing — the one great sanctioned noise of the room, the festival bell of the year, discussed in whispers for days after. Between the plates run warm creases of softer skin, tufted with coarse hair, and in the creases the animal's own breath-steam and the room's rime freeze into wedges of ice, and in the warm damp of the creases, spores of the floor sprout: every mountain carries a field on its back, and farmers upon the field. The groomers tend the crease-gardens and are harvested in turn — a felt paddle run along a crease comes up bearing a smear that fries sweet, and crease-picking is a child's first food-work.
