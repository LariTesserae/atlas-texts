**The Counted**

They are five hundred and some, on the living roll. The Keeper can run the whole cord through her fingers between one lamp and the next, and that is the first thing to understand about them: how few. The second thing is that they do not think of themselves as the inhabitants of the Palmful. The water is the inhabitant. They are its arrangements.

Water shapes their bodies as it shapes everything here. They are a compact people, long-boned in the forearm from rope and terrace, deep-chested, quiet-footed on the mat. They read fullness the way other peoples read faces: a child can heft a gourd and call its contents to the swallow; an elder can tell a leaf's thirst by the way it takes the blade-light. Plumpness is their beauty — skin with the sheen of a full sac, a shine at the lip — and gauntness is their honor, the mark of the salt road. They have two ways to be beautiful, the kept and the spent, and a marriage between the two is thought lucky.

They smell of the pocket — green, musky, faintly sweet with sap, smoke in the hair. In the old days, when there were visitors, this was how you knew family. It still is.

Ask them what a person is, and they will tell you: a cup that walks. Ask them what the town is, and they will tell you: the water, standing up to count itself.

**Two Voices**

Every person carries two names. The near-name is short, breathed at the lip's width, spoken into an ear. The far-name is whistled, and it belongs to the cleft — a phrase of three or four notes, unique as a face, that can cross four hundred paces of cicada-drone and arrive intact. At dusk the whole town sounds like an aviary of personal motifs, everyone calling everyone home to the drinking.

The figures of the whistle-language are the town's grammar made audible, and two of them are taught before any others. The birth-figure rises. The death-figure falls and comes home — for a death inside the pocket is a falling note that *resolves*: the water stays in the Keeping. And there is one figure that falls and does not resolve. The watchers use it for the rain that dies in the air above the lowlands. They use it also for exiles. It is the same word. It has always been the same word, and the people speak it the way other people speak of ghosts.

Because every far utterance is public, the Palmful has no gossip at distance and total gossip at arm's length. The council argues in whistles for all to hear and conspires, if it conspires, in a hand-cant older than the ladle. Lovers conduct entire courtships within the space of a breath. At weddings the town whistles the marriage figures from every terrace while the vows themselves are said in under-voice, mouth to ear, the only private utterance in a public life — and this is held to be correct: *a promise is water, and water moves close, and witnessed.*

**The First Keeping**

Childhood is a long drill in not-spilling, and here is the measure of the place: the drills are done with sand. No one rehearses with real water. A toddler learns the cup-grip on cups of gravel, learns the pour with dry grains hissing, and the first time a child holds true water in a spill-drill their hands shake — everyone remembers the shaking of their own hands, it is a shared scar, almost a bond.

At seven, a child is given charge of one lantern-sac and the frog that ticks inside it. This is called the first keeping. The sac's weight is entered in the Tally under the child's name, and from that day they are owed and owing. More children have been undone by the death of a frog than by the death of grandparents — grandparents resolve; a frog is a small green nation for which you alone are the sky. The year's first dew, witnessed and measured, is poured a thimble at a time into the sacs of the newest keepers: new water to new hands.

At adulthood the Keeper knots you into the living roll. You are counted, accountable, sworn. They call it being knotted in. There is an undoing of it — the knot drawn out — and the drawn knots hang in the council house in a small bundle beside the copper ladle. The two proofs. That water was once common enough to pour. That it is not common now.

**The Offices**

The Cord-Keeper is the nearest thing to a ruler, and she rules by arithmetic. The current one is Sef, an old woman with cord-grooved fingers who holds the water-pedigrees in her head: she can tell you that the cup you drank at dawn stood in your grandmother's mouth in the year of the long veils, and she says such things the way other priests say blessings, and means them the same way. She is not infallible, and the town is glad of it. Once, years back, she mis-knotted a salt-debt, found it a week later, and stood on the council ledge and whistled her own fault to the whole cleft. They teach it to children as the Confession of Sef: *the cord forgives what is witnessed. It is unwitnessed water that rots the world.*

Dann of the Spiles is tap-master, licensed to draw blood from someone he loves — the law's own phrase, and his. He reads the wellwoods by night, palm flat to the bark, and swears the groaning is speech: not words, he says, but moods. *The trees are arguing again*, people say before a weather-shift, and Dann comes down in the morning knowing which tree can spare a cup and which is lying about its wealth. Even he draws nothing unwitnessed. No one draws anything unwitnessed. That is the whole of the law, and the rest is footnotes.

Tekka is mat-wright, keeper of the underside, the ferment, the warm dark where the pocket's engine runs. She keeps the dead. When someone's time is near, the family carries them down to their own plot in the orchard, so that nothing is spent in transit, and Tekka holds a gauze at the lips for the last breath — because the last breath belongs to the tree that will take the rest. She smells of the warm earth-smell that has no business existing on this plateau. Children dare each other to touch her sleeve. She is the holiest person in the Palmful and the least envied.

**The Watchers**

On the rim there is no drone. That is the thing no one tells the new watchers: the silence. Inside the cleft the sound of life never stops, not for an instant, not for a lifetime; on the rim a young watcher hears her own heart for the first time, and some of them weep at it, and some of them cannot stop listening.

The ones who volunteer again and again go a little strange — *rim-minded*, the town says, kindly — and come down with whistle-figures composed to the wind-notes and a habit of standing very still, as if waiting for the plateau to finish a sentence. Mero has stood more watches than anyone living. He reports the dying veils in the falling word, as the duty requires — *the sky spilled; the ground got none; not yet; not this year* — and once, this is told softly, he whistled the resolving note after it. By mistake, or by hope. The terraces answered with a silence of their own, and no one has asked him which it was.

When an exile walks out, they pass the watch. There is no barring of the way — the sentence is the plateau's to carry out — but the watcher stands, and whistles the falling, unresolved figure as the person goes, and does not whistle them home. The watchers are the last faces the drawn-out ever see, and they carry that. It is why the watch is paid in honor and why no one is asked to stand it young.

On the rim path stands the guest-house. It has stood empty since grandmothers'-grandmothers. It is swept. It holds one sealed gourd, audited at the Measuring like any account, topped when the dew year allows. No one expects anyone; that is not the point. A guest is walking income — the bet with legs — and the watchers keep the latch oiled against a knock that has not come in living memory, because doctrine is doctrine: if the world sends a thirsty courier for its water, the Palmful will not be caught with a dry house.

**The Salt Road**

Twice a season the council prices the outside against the inside — so many cups against a sack of salt, the hardest arithmetic the Tally does — and the party goes up weighed, and comes home weighed, and owes the difference. The debt goes against their names and is worked off in cups foregone, in lamp-duty and mat-duty, sometimes over years. They carry the ledger in their bodies and their bodies show it.

Ila the Gaunt has led nine parties. She has taught herself, breath by breath over decades, to run cool — to move at night along the star-river, to rest by day under stone, to give the empty air almost nothing. She says the plateau is not empty. She says it is *full of the absence of witnesses*, that the silence out there has a texture, and that once in a life the texture changes and you look up and a veil is standing on the horizon, gray curtains trailing, and you whistle the falling word to no one, because no one is near enough to hear. That, she says, is the loneliest sound a Keeper can make: the rain-word, spoken alone, into the place the rain is dying for.

**The Walking Words**

The tortoise herd carries the dead language. In the wet age there were names for open water, and the words are kept the only way the Palmful keeps anything — alive, walking, entered in the Tally. Tarn is the matriarch, older than the oldest person, broad as a doorway, patient as geology; children learn her name before their own far-name, and Mere's, and Bourne's. No one now living has seen the things the names mean. The herd is the vocabulary of the drowned world, grazing unhurried beside the houses, and one name has never been assigned. It hangs knotted apart on the cord, waiting for an animal big enough to carry it. The name is Ocean. The Keepers do not call this a prophecy. They call it an open account.

The hawk they call the Doorkeeper, and count its fledglings under both gain and guard, for it takes its customs at the threshold and what it seizes stays.

The mat-cat has no far-name. It is the only resident without one — to whistle a thing is to keep it, and they will not keep it. In the ledgers it is *the account we do not open*, and at the Measuring its estimate is read in under-voice. After a taking, the council re-opens the old debate — a hunt would cost wounds, blood spilled where the mat cannot reach it, days of sweat — and so far, generation by generation, the arithmetic and the law have spared the animal: *it is in the Keeping, as we are; we do not spend each other.* The families of the taken are not required to forgive the arithmetic. Some do not. Their grief is carried on the cord in a knot no one is ever asked to untie.

**The Day Everyone Was a Cup**

In the year of the split vat — the story is dated from it — a ferment vat let go on the third terrace, and the alarm went up: the spill-figure, the one whistle every child knows and no one had ever heard in earnest. And the town did what it is drilled to do, and then did more. They caught the run in cupped hands, in cooking lids, in their own bodies; children flung themselves down to dam the stream with their ribs and were carried off, dripping and laughing and shrieking, to be wrung out over the orchard roots. By the Tally's reckoning the loss came to less than two cups. It is the town's favorite story — the day everyone was a cup — and at the Measuring, when the year's losses are read and mourned, someone always tells it, and it is the one time the council house laughs with all the cords hanging.

**The Majority**

The dead are the largest population of the Palmful, and the most consulted. Before a hard vote the council drinks the orchard — a thimble from each ancestral tree, passed lip to lip — so that the dead are present in the room, in the blood, and no one votes alone. By custom they deliberate until the cup has reached the heart. No one has ever timed this. It is not that kind of law.

And the spilling law, which is total, has one exemption. Tears wept onto the mat are free. It is the only spill the law allows, and the people use it without stinginess; the mourning whistle falls and resolves, the family weeps the dead back into the family tree, and the Tally notes the transfer with the same care it notes everything. *We hoard everything but grief*, they say, and they do not hear a contradiction in it. To be counted is to be kept. The ledger is not the opposite of love. It is the shape love takes when there is exactly enough of the beloved and no more.

**The Bet**

Once a year, at the Measuring, the whole endowment is read aloud: the dew income, thimble by thimble; the wellwoods gauged; the tortoises walked across the stones of the scale; the living roll run through the Keeper's fingers; the mat's slow rise marked — a finger-width a generation, everything that ever lived settling into everything that will. Losses are named and mourned. The gourd in the empty guest-house is reported. The unassigned name is read, and left hanging.

Then at dusk they set out the dew-stones along the cool end of the cleft, rows of pale ovals facing the strip of stars, and the town lies down to sleep — five hundred and some cisterns at rest, the mat warm under them, the wellwoods arguing softly, the frogs ticking like a long day cooling, the wind across the slot sounding the note that tells the outside weather. The water dreams in them. They are what it dreams.

Ask who lives here, and they will name the water first. Then the dead. Then the tortoises, with their walking words. Then the Doorkeeper, and the frog in every child's charge, and the trees that hold the ancestors, and — quietly, in under-voice — the account they do not open.

And then, last and lightly, the way you mention the watch that happens to be standing: themselves.
