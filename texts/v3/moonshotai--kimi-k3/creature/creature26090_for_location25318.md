# The Kept

*The census, completed.*

---

## The Count

The wardens keep a census, as they keep everything, and the figure it returns has not meaningfully changed in a thousand years: the same number of souls, give or take a cradle, strung along three thousand kilometers of rim. Weighed against the rest of what lives here, it comes to the four-hundredth part.

Put a citizen on the great scale at a condensary and you would have to heap the other pan with their portion of the Majority — twenty head of cattle, a moth tower's worth of wings, lice beyond patience, films beyond weighing — before the beam so much as trembled. No one in Sinter finds this insulting. It is the terms of the lease. They are a licensed species, and they know it, and they would tell you the license is generous.

## The Skin

The people are damp. It is the first thing about them and the last. Their skin never fully dries from birth to the giving-over; their hair is worn roped and beaded with polished louse-shell; their bare feet have spread wide on wet stone. They smell faintly of iron and milk and the sulfur-sweetness of the films they tend, though they cannot smell this themselves, the way no one can smell their own house. They dress in chitin-cloth with its oily drape, in cattle-hide, in rain-capes of shed fan-veil that go translucent in a downpour — rust, bone, sulfur, gunmetal, and nowhere a thread of green except the licensed cuff of a warden and the little locked case a physician carries.

They touch walls constantly. You greet a house by patting the jamb and waiting, half a breath, for the tick to answer: *the house is well.* You lean against a doorway to talk and the doorway leans back. Couples who have been long together keep the same skin — their films have merged, traded back and forth by years of touching the same handrails, the same walls, each other — and physicians who scrape and read the film can tell a marriage at a glance, can tell what street you live on, what you have been eating, whether your wall is well.

As for visitors: sometime in the first week the itching stops, which everyone in Sinter understands and no one thinks to explain. The city has taken them in. Hospitality here is a kind of inoculation — a good host feeds a guest gradually, mild mats first, then the local strains, until the guest's skin seeds and settles and smells of the street. Those who leave carry two things: a tin of wall-scrape, to reseed themselves against homesickness, and a small box of stone-lice for the bedside, fed on film-scrapings, kept for the rasp. Silence is hard to sleep in, once you have learned the other way.

## Names, Cradles, Niches

A person's name is their given name and their hold: the wall-face, the fan-row, the vat their family keeps. Move the lease and the name moves with it. To lose a lease altogether is to be *unnamed*, and the shame of it outlasts the poverty. The wall-less carry place-kinds instead — the apprentices, the dock-born, the wardens, who surrender their wall-names when they take the cup and are known ever after by their reach: Warden of the Ninth, Warden of the Reach Below.

Births are steadied against deaths. The Wardens of the Lease keep the cradle-rolls, and custom says a cradle waits for a niche — when someone is given over, a household somewhere is quietly informed that a place has opened. A child born before its niche is a scandal in some districts and a luck in others, and either way is walked to the Heart in its first month, to be held up in the warm loud dripping dark and introduced.

The dead go into the walls. Washed in Heart-water, laid in a niche cut in the family hold or in the outer crust of the Heart itself, they are given over to the masons, and the family comes back over the years to watch the crust close — pale, person-colored lime — until, a decade on, the face of the niche is smooth. *The smoothing,* they call it, and after it you do not grieve so much as knock, in passing, with one knuckle, the way you greet any house. Ask after someone's grandfather and you may be told, with perfect accuracy and perfect love: *he is holding up the stair.* The wall-less go to the kites at noon, up the foundry thermals, and the kite-keepers conduct that rite as solemnly as any mason conducts his. Wall or wing — everyone is taken by something. The city is built of its own dead, and the people are not exempt, and would not wish to be.

## The Keepers

In a city without fields, every trade is a form of husbandry. The only question is which animal.

The **fan-tenders** climb, cleaning veils with soft blades, dosing fan-fever, taking the drip-measures at the stalks. Among them the fan-wrights are the artists: they trim vane-edges by hairs to pitch, for every avenue has its assigned chord, fixed in the wardens' key-map generations deep, harmonized with the streets to either side so that the weather, arriving, plays the city in modulations. A street off-key is a civic embarrassment. The evening key-change is a performance older than any performer, and everyone attends.

The **rustmen** lease the wall-faces. Their wealth is carried in smeared tins: mother-mats, heirloom film-strains older than the districts, dowried, inherited, fought over, fed. You can taste the district in the bread — iron in the Ninth, a sulfur roundness near the Hearts — and rustmen speak of walls the way other peoples speak of vintages. In a child's seventh year comes the rite of the hand's-breadth: a scraped patch of wall, a smear of the family mat, and in time a first small loaf, eaten solemnly, crust and all. Below, in the steam and dim rot-light of the underflow, the vat-masters raise stronger strains in great ceramic throats, and are regarded as a guild slightly too fond of the dark.

The **roof-herds** belong to the children. Up on the roofscape, above the tram-bells, they walk the cattle from wall to leased wall with click-sticks, each herd's bell tuned to its own interval, so that a strayed cow is returned by ear, sometimes from streets away. The roofscape is a parallel city — of children and cattle and pigeon-creche moth towers — and adults go up there mostly to fetch somebody down for supper.

The **sweepers** come at dawn for the night's shell-fall, the cast plates and spent casings sold on to the lime kilns, and because they are first abroad they know everything first: which street rasped wrong in the night, which fan has turned feverish, whose niche has smoothed. News is sold with the grit, by the handful.

The **sump-keepers** run the underflow — whistling down pipes to the eels, husbanding the pipe-worms, navigating by rot-light. What the city loses, the sump finds; what the city flushes, the sump knows, and the guild's discretion is famous and purchased. Officially they are plumbers. Unofficially they distill the drip-spirit that warms the lower streets, and no warden looks too closely, because everyone understands that the sumps must be kept happy.

The **moth-fanciers** keep the towers and breed for wing-tone, and the great tower-chords are famous up and down the ribbon, with rivalries conducted across generations. The dusk sift is their daily premiere; the egg-harvest and the dark umami paste from the tower pans pay for the hobby, but it is a hobby the way cathedrals are a hobby.

And at the talus, half-drowned in cloud, live the **dockers** — keeper of the lifts, keepers of the tide-books, vellum records centuries deep that track the breathing of the cloud sea and say when a wharf will drown and when a given-back pier will emerge, encrusted, to be reoccupied by the grandchildren of the family that abandoned it. They trade with the Below, from which come strange cargoes: wood in relic-sized pieces, handled like relics; paper; dyes in colors no respectable citizen would wear. The dockers alone see the sea-glow from inside. The city needs them utterly and thinks of them almost never, which is the way of cities with their ports.

## The Wardens and the Fellers

The wardens are listeners. Their training takes years: blindfolded, set down anywhere along the ribbon, a candidate must name the district by its chord and its rasp, must press the listening-cup to a wall and say whether the reef behind it is thriving, sick, or dying. They keep the lease-rolls and the cradle-rolls, the key-map and the long maps, the verdigris vault and the verdigris ink. They run at quiet — it is the one sight in Sinter that frightens children, a warden at full sprint — and they declare the fellings, and everything else about them follows from that: they do not rule so much as *remember*, and their power is the power of saying *when*.

The fellers are the city's one necessary uncanniness. When a district is declared, the scaffolding goes up for years, and the fellers move into the loudest work there is — long water-cooled saws, the shriek of the cut — with tallow in their ears, and when the saws stop for the day they eat their lunch in the naked district, the raw and echoing square kilometer that is the quietest place in the world, and no one envies them. They speak a hand-cant on the scaffolds. Deaf children — the *quiet-born* — are apprenticed to them gladly. They are honored at the felling festival, which is the only festival Sinter keeps, and faintly avoided the rest of the time, because they have made a life inside the city's one bad omen. A felling outlives its fellers; the cut is handed down like a lease; the children of the fellers rarely see the regrowth complete. And when someone must be walked leeward, it is fellers who escort them, because only fellers can walk into silence and come back.

Every other trade serves something alive. The fellers serve the pause between lives.

## The Hours

The day begins before light with the sweepers, and then comes the turning hour: the wind shifts, and ten thousand vanes creak round to face it, the whole city stirring in its sleep. Markets fill — rustbread in gray-red mineral loaves, jars of moth eggs, sift-meal, crackling lice-shell fried in cattle-oil for those with water to spare, sulfur-sweet veil-candy for children. It is a cuisine without flame. Sinter does not burn its dinner; it boils it, steams it, cures it, ferments it, talks it into ripeness in a vat. Blood-broth at midday, milk-wine in the evening, drip-spirit after dark in places the wardens do not look.

At noon the kites go up over the foundries. In the afternoon the city's exhaled cloud caps the Shelf, the light goes pearl-gray and even, and the streets keep their appointments: each rain-schedule street takes its hour, and the hour is social — laundry hung, children sent out to stand in their own street, baths taken, contracts signed wet before witnesses, courts convened under the appointed rain. Dusk brings the sift, and every face tilts up. Evening brings the key-change. Night brings the deep untroubled sleep of people lulled by machinery — though a child who sleeps through tram-bells will stir instantly if the wall's tick falters, and stays that way all their life. The wardens walk the night with their cups, listening.

## The Slow Calendar

Hours are kept by the rain, days by the sift, years by the felling-cycles — and everything longer is kept by the Slow Ones.

There are a few dozen along the ribbon, and they have names like landmarks, because they are: Old Tally. The Long Bride. Their positions are the backbone of the long maps, inked in verdigris on vellum — so that the only green most citizens ever see is the slow green procession of the hills across the records, and green comes to mean two things in Sinter: sickness in metal, and time itself. When the wardens' ledgers speak of a reign, they mean the walk of a Slow One between two named stones.

When a Slow One's foreleg crosses a rim-gate — a transit of days — the district keeps *foreleg season*: lucky for beginnings. Weddings are held beside the passing leg. Leases are signed. Vows are sworn *by the walk of Old Tally*, and the wardens publish the predicted transits years ahead, computed at the pace of erosion. Children collect hill-chips, the flakes that fall where the crust fans against the wind, and carry them in pockets their whole lives; a hill-chip given at a betrothal means *patience*, and means *I am not going anywhere fast.*

Some of the figures on the maps are dead — died standing, centuries back, still upright, still crusted, hills that no longer have anywhere to be. Only the wardens know which. Press the cup to a living column-leg and you can hear it: a slow enormous circulation, the hill's heart. No one forgets hearing it once, as a child. And no warden living has seen a Slow One born, or seen one fall. If one ever went over the edge, no one would hear it happen — which is exactly why the verdigris watch has never once been allowed to lapse.

## Sent Leeward

The law's short list is all ecology. Kill a fan on a water street. Poison a mat. Silence a street. The penalties run from drip-fines, levied in water, through the loss of a lease and its name, to the last one: sent leeward — walked past the last fan-row out into the dust the reef has made, where nothing ticks, nothing rasps, nothing is coming back as breath. The escort is a feller. Within a season the exile's film dies, and the itch comes back, and never leaves again.

A pardon has two parts, and everyone knows it. The wardens may forgive. The skin must consent. There are pardoned men and women who came back to the rim-road's end and stood listening, and were never reseeded — *the city would not have them* — and that sentence is known to every child in Sinter before they learn their letters. Belonging here is not a verdict. It is a flora.

## What Is Said Here

*The house is well.* — a greeting, and a diagnosis.

*By the walk of Old Tally.* — a vow.

*Mind the drip.* — pay attention; nothing is wasted.

*Quiet as leeward.* — an insult, or a fear.

*The sump knows.* — there are no secrets; there is only plumbing.

*Wall or wing.* — everyone is taken by something.

---

At dusk an old woman stops on her way home and knuckles the stair wall, once, in passing — *the house is well* — and the tick answers through her husband, who is load-bearing these eleven years. Behind her a grandchild pockets a hill-chip from the rim-road. The sift is rising. The fan-avenues are coming round to the evening key, and her skin and the stair and the street and the child all keep the same film, and none of them can tell where one holding ends and the next begins.

Ask what lives in Sinter and the answer keeps slipping scale — films, lice, cattle, moths, people, reef, the walking hills — until you arrive at the true one: the loop lives here, and everything else is a phase of it. The people did this arithmetic long ago and are not diminished by it. *The wall is not yours,* they say; *you keep it for the reef* — and after some years a listener understands this is not a saying about property. It is about the body. It is about the name. It is about the grandfather in the stair.

The city does not grow. It thickens — and its people thicken with it, one thin bright crust at a time, counting themselves lucky to be counted in the total at all.
