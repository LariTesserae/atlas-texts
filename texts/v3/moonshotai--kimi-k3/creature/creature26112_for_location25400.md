Ask what lives here and the first answer is the wrong one: nothing. Nothing with a mouth, nothing with an eye, nothing that hunts or hides or is afraid. There is no leviathan in the trough. Do not invent one. The trough is not empty; it is occupied. The question just wants a slower kind of answer.

Begin at the floor.

The felt-fields are the oldest lives in the basin — not older than the Roof, nothing is, but older than the people, older than the oldest pipe at the passes. A felt is not one growth but a thickness of growths: a living skin laid over the packed, preserved body of its own past, meter on meter, because nothing here rots and nothing carries anything away. Every felt is its own ancestor, and the valleys are floored with genealogy. It feeds on what the drowned stone seeps — the only eating that happens in this sea, and barely eating, more a kind of patience practiced on rock. If the felt registers the Choir passing overhead, it registers it the way an old injury registers weather: as a pressure, a slow rightness or wrongness in the water, answered over decades by a thickening, a darkening, a turn of half a degree toward the seep. The felt wants nothing it does not already have. Of the three manners of life here, it is the only one that is finished — and it will outfinish everything.

The pipeweed is the voice, and a pipeweed stem is mostly not there: a wall built around an emptiness, and the emptiness is the organ. A stem is a note's house. The giants at the passes — meters through at the base, centuries in the jointing — are the basses of the Choir; their joints number their seasons the way rings number years elsewhere. They drink the current and give back sound, and in the great exhales, when the Drift quickens and the whole basin swells, an old stand experiences what in an animal would have to be called exertion, or joy: the chord moving through every hollow, the crowns thrumming, the ridge leaning into its note. It wants the current the way the upward fields want the light. And when one falls — to a crack-boom's shove, to a finger's graze, to the plain arithmetic of age — it lies where it fell, unrotting, and the Drift does not stop playing it. A downed stem drones at a dropped pitch, bedding deeper into the scree across the decades, flattening by slow degrees the way the dead do. No stand is without its fallen. Every ridge's chord includes them — which means every chord is a genealogy, the living and the dead held in one interval, and a traveler steering by chord is steering by a stand's whole history at once.

The upward fields live fastest and die most. A field begins as a mote pressed to the ice — one spore out of a risen constellation, come to rest against the ceiling that is the only ground it will ever know — and spreads, and drinks the seep-light, and thins or thickens as the ice above it clears or clouds, and waits. Most fields wait their whole lives. They are born in the gloaming and they die in the gloaming and no crack ever opens within reach; they live on rumor, and in the only sense this place permits, they are content, because the thing they are missing has never once arrived. Then, somewhere, the Roof splits, and the nearest fields ignite. Understand what the ignition is: the field spending itself. Everything the fronds stored across years of dusk goes, in weeks, into ghost-gold light and then into spores — the lantern-rise, the inverted snow, the only travelers born in this country, drifting along the ceiling to seed meadows a full gyre away. When the ice heals and the pillar thins, the ignited field lies pale and tattered beneath its own departure, and does not recover. The light is loaned, never kept — and the price of the loan is the lender. The fields know this the way plants know anything, which is completely and without objection. *Bloom while it lasts* is not advice here. It is the oldest law of the place, and the fields are the only ones who pay it in full.

Now step back — farther than a traveler can, but step back — and the three manners stop being three.

The Roof is a lung, and its week of gathering and week of release are a breath. The Long Drift is a blood, making its circuit, frazil ice rising through it like cells through a vein. The ridges are a voice-box; the forests are the cords; the trough is the chest where the note gathers. The upward fields are the one surface that feels the light — a skin grown along the lid, longing upward the way skin longs. The felt is the flesh of the floor, the part that keeps. Apply the tests a traveler knows how to apply. It breathes. It circulates. It grows. It responds — open it anywhere and it blooms, and then the opening closes from the edges inward, which is how wounds heal. It scars: every finger-shaped clearing in the stands, every blackened frond-edge, legible for centuries. It reproduces; every lantern-rise makes more of it, far-flung, along the wind it has instead of a wind.

And it remembers. That is the test that matters, and the strangest, so take it slowly.

Take the terraces first: pipeweed following the cut lines as if still being weeded. Consider the phrase. *Still weeded.* In the cirque village, the fronds stand in the doorways at roof-height and stop, as if the roofs still limited them. The felt lies thickest and blackest over the old hearthstones, piled like a sleeper under blankets — ash is a seep the stone learned from the people, and the felt has not forgotten the flavor. On the summit shrine, the seed-caches say what the people knew: the light is a guest, and guests are fed.

And out in the empty dark, the rehearsed villages. There is a saddle a day's drift past the pass where the low chord crosses the high one, where the weed stands in terrace-ranks on a slope that was never cut, door-gaps facing the pass, a mooring-gap at the lower edge fronting water no boat has ever touched, and a circle of dark felt where the hearth would be, dark, as if fed. It is the plan of the cirque village, repeated where no village was. Not copied — there was no one to copy it. Remembered, or practiced.

Three readings, and the place refuses to choose. The forests remember habitation: growth laid over what was, keeping the plan the way cold water keeps a stair. Or the forests rehearse it: habitation performed the way a body performs a habit, without deciding to, in case it is ever needed again — build while you can, against a return there is no reason to expect. Or the third reading, the quiet one, the one the dark keeps best: that there are no graves because there was nothing to bury. Nothing fled; nothing was taken; the people did not leave this place. They were translated into the only permanence it offers — kept not as bodies but as pattern, as the difference between a forest and a garden. Ask who lives here, and weigh the third answer: the people do, in the manner the place permits. Not as persons — nothing here is a person — but as a tending. The weed is still weeded. The hearths are still fed. Villages are still being built, slowly, in the wrong places, by a gardener with no hands and all the time there is.

The fingers are not alive; they only behave the way weather behaves. The light is not alive either. The light is the guest — the only one — and the bloom is the hospitality, the resident lighting its lamps, spending its stores on a visitor who never stays.

And whom is the note for? The dark. The dark is the entire audience, and a perfect one: it gives nothing back and it keeps everything. Count the way the place counts, and two things share this country — the singer and the listening.

Then there is you. Nothing here will eat you. Nothing here will even notice you quickly. But you will be kept. What you drop lies where it dropped; your mooring-knot will outlast the name of your language. Stay a season and the weed along your camp-line will lean, almost imperceptibly, in your direction. Stay a century — you cannot, but the place does not know that — and somewhere out in the empty dark a stand will begin, street-spaced, room-shaped, the plan of your camp practiced on a saddle you never saw, the felt going dark where your fire was. That is the last thing to understand about what lives here. It keeps. Given time, it keeps you — intact, unhurried, forever — which is the only immortality on offer, and it is granted to everything, and it is not called anything.

The traveler is given five rules. The residents keep four:

Grow where you are put.
Keep what falls.
Sing what the current gives you.
Bloom while it lasts.

The dark keeps one: hold.

At the bottom of the trough the chord goes on, not a song about the place but the place saying its own name, in the only tense it has — the sustained. Call it the Choir.

Now someone has.
