**The people of Vesper**

They are pale, and they are loud, and not one of them is ever entirely dry.

You notice the hands first. In a town where every household trims its frontage daily, hands are the curriculum vitae. A trimmer's hands are gray to the wrist, palms callused to a texture like sinter, nails kept short as seeds. Mat-harvesters carry rust-red to the elbow, a stain no amount of bathing lifts. The seepwrights — the builder-waterers, the ones who grow walls — are rimed white about the knuckles and cuffs, as if frostbit in a world without frost. The fat-renderers are blackened and glossy. Children who gig frogs in the shallows go orange-footed. You can read a stranger's trade across a steam-house before they open their mouth, though they will open it soon enough, and at a volume that will end the mystery anyway.

Every one of them carries a hand-stone: a small copper-edged chisel, worn on a cord at the belt or the neck, part tool, part utensil, part proof of citizenship in the human estate. A child receives theirs at the first lengthening of their name and is buried with it — no, not buried; accreted with it, laid in the seep with the hand-stone in the fist, so that the stone-kin up on the rim grip their tools forever. You eat with your hand-stone. You trim with it. You tap your name with it. A Vesperian without a hand-stone is naked in a way that has nothing to do with clothes.

Clothes are felted mat-fiber and hide, oiled against the wet, dyed in the only palette the basin allows: rust, ochre, bone-white, lamp-black, and the gray of seep-wall felt. The wealthy wear black, because black means lamp-fat, and lamp-fat means light to spare. Hair is oiled back or knotted tight against the damp and pinned with shell combs; the fashion-forward set rime-pearls into the knots. Teeth come mottled, brown-flecked, mineral-etched — basin teeth, the mark of a native, drank in with the water from birth. Visitors from the canal towns have smooth white teeth, and the Vesperians find this faintly obscene, like a person with no callus at all. Smooth is not a compliment here. Smooth is what they call the dead districts.

Because the dead districts are smooth, and smoothness is death, and this brings us to the thing you must understand about these people before anything else makes sense: they have organized their entire inner lives around a single conviction, which is that to finish is to die. A finished thing is a dead thing. They say it the way other peoples say grace. And so they have made themselves, deliberately and with enormous energy, into a people who never complete anything — and who have learned to love the never-completing more than any people anywhere has loved a virtue.

Start with the voice, since the voice is what reaches you first. The people of Vesper keep two voices, the way you might keep two coats. The street voice is a bellow, trained from toddlerhood, pitched to cut the frog-chorus and the chisel-ring; courtesy demands it. To speak softly to someone in the open street is to treat them as deaf, or as furniture. They shout prices, shout gossip, shout condolence, shout tenderly — a Vesperian mother scolds and comforts at the same volume, and a proposal of marriage is audible three terraces away, which is considered correct: joy, like everything else, should be shared with the quarter. The house voice is quieter, but only relatively. Walls here are shared and thin and full of other people's lives, and nobody expects otherwise.

And then, underneath the voices, there is the other language — the one in the stone.

The roar eats speech across a room, but stone carries a knock the way a string carries a pluck, and Vesper discovered this so long ago that the discovery is no longer remembered, only the practice. Every person in the town has a tap-name: a short rhythm, given at birth, drummed on the doorframe lintel by the parents before the spoken name is ever said aloud. You are named twice in Vesper — once in air, once in stone — and the stone-name is in some ways the truer one. Lovers tap it through shared walls at all hours. Children tap goodnight to grandmothers two rooms away and feel the answer come back through the floor. Whole conversations run through the bones of the town, knuckle on slab, chisel-butt on lintel, a soft ceaseless tickering under the roar that visitors never notice and natives cannot sleep without. The deaf — and Vesper produces the deaf in quantity, since a lifetime in the roar wears the ear smooth as everything else — the deaf do not withdraw here. They become the quiet-ear, the elders, and the quiet-ear are the fluent ones, the masters of wall-speech, sitting with a palm flat to the stone like physicians with a stethoscope, reading the house. Babies in Vesper are not sung to sleep. They are drummed to sleep, the cradle-frame tapped in slow patterns, the town's heartbeat handed on.

This is what the noise has made of them. Now consider what the stone has made of them.

The trim is the first duty and the first art. Every household, every day, at the trim-bell that opens each wake, turns out with hand-stones and cuts its frontage back out of the night's new growth. It takes the place of sweeping, of prayer, of morning ablutions, and it is never drudgery — or rather, it is drudgery transfigured, because a facade is a signature. The way you trim is the way you are. Families cut their patterns fresh each day, heraldry renewed every wake, and a master trimmer can walk a street and read aloud who cut what, the way you might read a letter in a familiar hand. There are styles, fashions, quarrels of style conducted in stone across alleyways. To trim someone else's wall is a crime — facade-crime, somewhere between forgery and assault — and boundary feuds between neighbors are settled in the end by the stone itself: the gutter-masters, called in to adjudicate, will core the disputed wall and read the accretion layers like annals, because the stone keeps the true record even when the neighbors don't. The layers do not lie. People lie; the town testifies.

Above the household trimmers stand the seepwrights, and the seepwrights are the aristocracy of competence, because in a town that is irrigated into existence, water-rights are everything. They route the seeps, they grow the walls, they unseal the drains, they hunt the shifting springs, and their council — the gutter-masters — does the town's law, which is conducted, like everything else important, in the great baths, naked and bellowing over the milky water, on the principle that civic business belongs in the civic body. The other great trades arrange themselves around them: the horn-callers up on the tower platforms, chosen young for lung and pitch, their apprentices — called squallers, without cruelty — learning the long moans and stutters that carry news through the fog between districts; the bell-keepers, who tend the water-clocks and strike the hours in a sunless world and so are, in a real sense, the keepers of time itself; the lamp-tenders and fat-renderers, since light is wealth and a house's lamp-count is its public balance sheet, from the twenty-lamp facades of the fat streets down to the single gutter-lamp shared by a poor court; and below these, the wet trades — mat-harvest, eel-weir, shrimp-pan, the roof-gathers who take eggs from the barking bird-colonies, the mud-shod calf-herds wading their placid bellowing boulders through the pens. And at the margin, the bulge-miners, who dig into the swallowed houses of the low districts for heirlooms sealed in the stone — a trade everyone uses and no one respects, tomb-robbery with a market stall.

A new room in Vesper is an act of years. You cannot build one; you must grow one, diverting a seep over a hide-and-bone frame and keeping it wet for seasons, and so a household expecting a child begins watering a room at the first suspicion, and the phrase *we're watering a room* is the standard way of announcing a pregnancy, and everyone understands that rooms, like children, arrive on their own schedule and never quite in the shape you planned. The house a Vesperian lives in is a family diary. Set into the growing wall — the one rough, unsmoothed, deliberately weeping wall that every house keeps — are the little walls: fist-sized crusts of tufa that each child tends in a seep-tray through their childhood, feeding it drips, watching it gain fingernail-widths, and which are mortared into the family's growing wall when the child takes their hand-stone and their second name. A growing wall is thus a genealogy in stone, lumpy and glittering and never done, and to smooth it is the worst luck imaginable, and everyone knows the story of the household that did.

The names grow too. That is the beautiful bureaucratic madness of it. A child is born with a short name — a syllable or two, something that can be shouted across a loud street. At the first hand-stone, a syllable is added. At marriage, another. At grandparenthood, at mastery of a trade, at any passage the family judges worthy, the name lengthens, so that an elder carries a name like a string of beads, and a person's name is their biography, spoken aloud. And here is the thing that makes visitors stare: the name does not stop at death. The dead go to the seep-gardens on the rim, and the stone-heavy water flows over them, and they accrete, and as the figure grows, the name grows — a syllable added each generation by the descendants who come up through the frog-noise to visit, so that the oldest stone-kin have names that take minutes to recite and are recited, in full, in a chant that is somewhere between a genealogy and a song. The archive-keepers hold the longest ones entire. A name in Vesper is never finished either. Of course it isn't. Nothing is.

Marriage is watering a wall together. The rite is exactly that: the two households raise a frame of hide and bent bone between their properties, divert a shared seep over it, and keep it wet through a season of courtship, and every guest at the betrothal pours one cup of stone-heavy water over the frame. When the wall stands, the marriage stands. The marriage wall is never, ever smoothed — by custom it is the roughest wall in the house — and the state of a couple's wall is public knowledge and public comment. Divorce is done with a saw: the wall cut through, each keeping half, and you can see the sawn halves all over town, scarred and weeping, and people will tell you, in a bellow, who kept the better half and why. Widowhood has its own grammar: some survivors keep trimming around the wall as always, and some let the seep run and the wall seal over, and a sealing marriage wall is the town's way of saying *she is grieving, let her*, and no one will intrude on that house until the trim-marks reappear.

What do they eat, what do they drink, how do they pass the wakes? They eat from the springs, mostly — pots sunk in the hot outflow all day, dinners poaching in mineral water, everything soft, everything steaming, everything tasting faintly of the deep earth. Street food is skewers of frog and snail and eel grilled over fat-flame, shrimp-cloud scooped and crisped, mat-cakes pressed and fermented and sour. They drink a sour cultured brew of mat, always scalding hot, because heat is the one free thing in Vesper, and they drink it in steam-houses by the vents, where the betting on the snail heats runs all wake — children race painted snails across wet felt while their elders wager fat-bricks and bellow the odds, the slowest sport in the loudest town, and nobody has ever explained this to anyone's satisfaction. Sweetness barely exists in this world, and the nearest thing — the pale young tips of certain mats, faintly, hauntingly sweet — is children's gleaning, sucked like a memory of sugar. Every hearth keeps a hearth-frog in a warm pot by the cook-spring, fed on swept-up lamp-flies, and its croaking is the house's pulse; a silent hearth-frog is bad luck the way a guttering lamp is bad luck. The drains of every house belong to the drain-crabs, small blind pale things that graze the accretion and keep the house's veins open, and a house with fat crabs is a fortunate house, and killing one is like killing a spider in other worlds — technically allowed, spiritually expensive.

The wakes structure everything. Bells open and close them — trim-bell, bath-bell, the long bells that mark the cycle's turn — and a third of the town is always up, always trimming, cooking, shouting, so the roar never once lapses, and Vesperians sleep through it like weather, like a heartbeat, like their own blood. This has a consequence they only discover when they travel: a Vesperian abroad cannot sleep. The canal towns are quieter, and the quiet lies on them like a stone on the chest, and they lie awake in the dark listening to the terrible nothing, homesick for a sound they spent their whole lives not hearing. They come home and stand in the street and let the roar rinse through them and weep, sometimes, and are not embarrassed, because everyone has done it.

And yet — the hush-rooms. The deep cellars below the water table where the roar thins to a hum. Silence is the one luxury in Vesper, and it is a complicated luxury, the way a strong drug is complicated. People rent silence by the bell-span for every reason: to think, to weep, to bargain, to couple, to grieve, to go mad a little in a controlled way. Some come out refreshed. Some come out frightened — silence-sick, unable to bear what their own thoughts sound like without the town wrapped around them. Some cannot stay away: the hush-drunk, pitied the way any addict is pitied, and the respectable ones among them become archive-keepers, since the archives are kept in the hush-cellars anyway. That is not an accident, and the Vesperians will tell you why, if you ask, in a bellow, over sour brew: the archives are finished things. Clay tablets, written, done. Finished things belong with the dead, in the quiet, below the water. Completion is a kind of death and must be housed accordingly.

Which brings us to the heresy, because of course there is one. They are called the Smooth — not to their faces — and their sin is finishing. Somewhere in Vesper there are always a few who polish a stone until it shines, who glaze and fire a pot in a secret kiln, who end a song. They meet in rented hush-rooms. They are not hunted; they are regarded with the embarrassed tenderness reserved for the ill, because to a Vesperian the desire for completion is not wickedness but sickness, a death-wish wearing an apron. A polished stone found in the market causes a crowd — people come to touch it the way you'd touch a corpse, curious and appalled — and then someone buys it, because of course someone buys it, and the scandal mutters on for a wake or two. Meanwhile the whole orthodox culture runs the other way: the crafts leave something undone by rule; the bellow-tales in the steam-houses are serials that outlive their tellers, characters aging with their audiences across generations; and in the great baths there is the long song, which has no composer and no ending and has not stopped in living memory, passed voice to voice across the soaking crowds, verses going back hundreds of bell-counted generations, handed off mid-phrase when a singer's wake ends — a song that is, like the town, like the names, like the walls, in a state of permanent arrival.

They fear three things, and they are all the same thing. They fear silence: when a spring shifts and some district's frog-chorus falters, everyone comes out of the houses and stands in the street looking at each other, touching the stone, while the horn-callers put the question across the fog to the neighboring quarters — *are you still singing* — and the children are brought out to hear the absence, because the elders say a child must know what the world sounds like without the singing, must feel the size of the debt. When the chorus returns, the district holds the Answering: lamps doubled, baths flooded, a wake of festival. When it doesn't, the district thins, and the houses seal behind the departing families, and that is how the low districts were made, and the pale bulges with lintels showing like bones are their graveyard. They fear smoothness, which you know. And they fear the smoothing — the illness of the soul in which a person simply goes quiet. It happens. In a town where loneliness is structurally impossible, where privacy is only ever acoustic, where walls are shared and baths are communal and the roar admits no solitude, a person can still withdraw, stop shouting, stop trimming, go smooth inside. The cure is communal and it is not gentle: the sufferer is carried, literally carried, to the baths, surrounded, shouted at, cooked in talk, and a hand-stone is pressed into their palm and their fingers closed around it, because the theory — and it mostly works — is that the hands remember before the heart does.

Death is the town's own process, applied to the town's own people, and no one finds it sad, and somehow, hearing them say so, you believe them. The dead go up to the seep-gardens on the rim, laid out where the stone-heavy water flows, and over the years they accrete into pale kept figures, and the tending of the stone-kin is the tenderest duty the living have: the figures must be trimmed, like facades, or the growth will blur them past recognition. Families teach the cuts. A stone-kin's face is recut every few cycles, gently, with the dead person's own hand-stone laid back in the fist afterward, and a neglected ancestor overgrows and is lost to the stone — a second death, the true one, the only one they really grieve. The oldest stone-kin are vast pearlescent shapes, smoothed by centuries into near-abstraction, tended by whole quarters, ancestors of everyone, with names that take an hour to sing. Sometimes the very old or the very ill go early — walk up to the gardens and lie down in the seep while alive, taking the stone, as they say — and some families honor this and some call it cheating, and the argument is conducted at full volume in the baths and never resolved, because resolving it would be finishing it.

They emigrate too, a few — down the canals to the quieter towns, to lives without the trim, without the roar — and the town has one phrase for this, and it is the same phrase it uses for the illness of withdrawal, and nearly the same tone it uses for death: *gone quiet*. A Vesperian abroad is unmistakable: bellowing, restless, unable to finish a meal, a sentence, a repair, forever reaching to adjust the doorframes of other people's houses, lying awake in the awful silence. The canal folk find them exhausting and hire them anyway, because no one else alive can work stone like a people who spend their whole lives arguing with it.

Stand at the outflow flats at any bell and watch the coracles come in, and the crews will be shouting before you can see them, and the answer will come off the water in a voice gone slightly soft from travel, and the whole dock will roar the news back and forth, and somewhere behind you the trim-bell will ring and a thousand households will turn out with their hand-stones, and a grandmother will tap goodnight through a wall to a child who is technically awake, and the long song will come up off the baths and braid itself into the frogs.

Ask any of them how they are, and they will tell you, at a volume that carries across three terraces: *still rough.* It is a greeting, a boast, a theology, and a diagnosis. The town is growing its people. The people are trimming their town. Neither has the slightest intention of being done with the other, and if you suggested it — if you said the word *finished* to one of them, kindly, over brew — they would look at you the way you might look at someone who had wished them dead.

They are almost finished, all of them. It is the proudest thing they know how to say.
