The city was made for many more, and you will feel that first as a kind of generosity: wide courts, long arcades, whole stair-streets where nothing moves but the light taking its appointed walk across the paving. You can stand in a beam-court at its granted hour and count the people on your fingers. Then you look again. A sleeve flashes from a high window. A face shows briefly at a selenite pane, gold-gray behind the old crystal. Three herders appear inside the tide of pale backs moving up the Fallow Run like stones carried in milk. They were always there. They have been watching you, in fact, since the rim — someone saw your silhouette against the white sky an hour ago, and the news has already crossed three courts by hand, faster than you walked.

They are perhaps a few thousand, and they are the color of their city. Where the light reaches them they weather to tufa-pale; where the felt wraps them they stay the gray-gold of the film. Their teeth are gently grit-worn, because the mineral world gets into everything, the bread, the water, the air, and a Wellborn smile — given freely, there is no custom against it — is always a little dusty at the corners. Their hands are the quick part of them. Children of the Well do hand-work before they can walk; by four, a child's hands have the loose, articulate restlessness of a much older person's elsewhere; by old age the hands are broad and rope-knuckled from seventy years of wringing, and they say more in stillness than most people's voices do at full stretch.

The eyes you will not see for a while. From about the seventh year every Wellborn goes behind the visor — a slit of cleaved selenite bound in felt — and the visor becomes the face. People know each other by the cut of the slit, the smoke of the stone, the particular tilt a lifetime gives the band. A tender's visor is dark against the rim-glare. A pane-gardener's is pale and often pushed up on the forehead, since their work is in the dark. You do not ask to see behind it. Behind it is not for you.

Which means the city stares at its children with a hunger it is not ashamed of. Children go bare-eyed through their early years — the *glare-years*, they call them later, with a nostalgia that is partly envy — and they are the only eyes the Well is permitted to meet. Watch a court at rest hour: every visor angled toward the toddlers, the whole neighborhood drinking the children's open faces like light. When a child is brought their first visor, cut by an elder from the household's pane-offcuts and made a little large, to grow into, there is a small ceremony, and afterward the household grieves, privately and without shame, the closing of a window.

The hand-speech has sizes. There is a broad register for across a court, big sweeping shapes readable at two hundred meters — this is how herders talk across a tide, how tenders talk down the rim-slope, how the whole city reads a transaction in the Beam Court without being able to hear it. There is a close register, small and rapid against the chest, for the person at your shoulder. There is sleeve-talk, signed inside a shared hanging sleeve, which is where the Well keeps its gossip, its courtship, and its small treasons; every adolescent masters it, badly, while every elder pretends not to notice their own fluency. For the cave-dark there is the whispered register, spare and sibilant — and a whisper in the Well dies within an arm's length, which makes this the most private city you will ever visit: every conversation ends one step away, complete, leaving nothing. And below the whisper there is palm-talk, signed into a cupped hand, for the seep-dark, for lovers, for the dying, and for babies, who learn it before anything, riding the chest in the felt sling, read to sleep by fingertips.

They have never heard an echo. Understand what that does to a metaphysics. They have a word for a ghost: it means *a sound that comes back*. When travelers describe a voice returning off a canyon wall, the Wellborn touch the paving, politely, the way you would touch wood. Their dead do not speak. Their dead would never be so rude.

Ask them, eventually, what a person is, and after the conversation has gone around the courts of their grammar for a while, it comes to this: to be is to be seen. Not watched — *witnessed*. The Well is a city that knows itself by sight, and the knowing is not passive; it is a daily, distributed act of keeping each other in existence. This is why nothing here is rude about staring, why a flash answered is simple courtesy, why the worst sentence the Beam Court can pass is not exile but the *unseeing* — for a season, no visor turns toward you, no eye reads your hands; you sign and the court gazes through you, and you walk the bright streets as a sound that does not come back. Most people so sentenced beg, within days, for a beam-debt large enough to work off, any debt, any size, because labor at least is visible. When it is worked off, the court gathers and turns, all at once, and looks. There is no word for that moment. It is the opposite of the word for ghost.

Their day is the basin's day, worn into habit. At the dawn breath-turn the throats exhale and every roof is up, fins set, felt spread, the wringing done at a pace that looks unhurried and misses nothing; water belongs to the roof it condenses on, and a roof is a citizenship — a child becomes a person of their house the morning they first wring felt into the family cistern, and it is marked, quietly, with the child's own cup. Then the mirrors come on, and the flicker begins, and if you learn to read it you can watch the city's mind at work: that beam walking down the eastern wall means the salt-pans traded up an hour; the flash holding on the long court means a wedding's been granted baking-light; the complicated little argument of glints on the north slope is two tenders correcting a drifting pivot. There are people who sit on the rim at midday reading the light-play the way you would read news, and they are considered good company.

Work blooms where the beam lands and stops when it leaves, and no one resents this, any more than you resent the tide. The ovens take the felt-dough in the granted hour — felt is pasture and crop both; the gray-gold film has strains, and the strains have farmers, and the flour of the sweeter films bakes into a dense pale cake that tastes faintly of stone and faintly of honey and is, after three days, something you will crave for years. There is milk, and a sharp cheese cooled in the Root, and herd-meat at the great feasts, and at the wing-blooms — the blooms are weather and manna both — the children sweep the swarmers out of the air with felt nets and there is roasting, salty and quick, in whatever court holds the light. Everything else is eaten cold, or sun-warmed, or slow-cooked by a stone that soaked the beam. Fire is for sentences. A smoke-day is a smell-day; in a city that smells of felt and dust and nothing, one thin column seasons the whole bowl, and everyone breathes the news as they read it.

The crafts are shaped by the absences. No wood: so the masons raise vault on corbelled vault, and the closers — a small, hereditary, slightly uncanny guild — hold the only license in the Well to finish anything, since they alone set capstones and seal the dead. No flowing water: so a third of all labor is the harvest of stillness, fins and felt and wringing. And nothing written: so everything is *done*, and this, more than faith, is the engine of the famous churn. They will take down a sound wall and raise it again so that the hands remember. A skill unpracticed for a generation is extinct; the city is a school that never graduates; the scraping and rebuilding that looks, from the rim, like ten thousand years of useless motion is the Well reading itself aloud so it does not forget how to be a city.

What they write, they knot. Cords of felt-yarn for the day's tallies, cords of grub-silk for what must last: beam-debts, pane-wills, water-rights, marriages. A household's cord-wall is its archive and its face, hung where the pane-light falls, and the cords are read by fingers — in the dark, in the cave, in bed — and the cord-keepers, the nearest thing the Well has to a memory, can read a knot-record the way you read a face and will tell you, handling a marriage cord four generations old, that this knot was tied by someone in a hurry. Here is the Well's strange economy of remembering: property is remembered forever and persons are allowed to go. Debts, rights, panes, and keepers pass down with their cords intact; a person's own cords, the record of their individual owing and holding, are sealed with them in the house at death. The Well forgets its dead on purpose and remembers its debts across centuries. Ask why, and someone will cup their hands — the gesture that means *I*, and *us*, and *the bowl*, and *held water*, all at once — and tip them toward the sky. *We hold. We do not keep.* It is their grace before meals. It is close to their whole theology: nothing is owned, everything is lent — light by the sky, water by the breath, stone by the basin, a life by the house that will take it back.

So a life has its shape in loans and their settlement. Birth: the smoke goes up, and the whisper-name is spoken into the small chest, felt more than heard, the first sound the child knows, and it will be spoken perhaps a few hundred times in a whole life, by very few people, and each time it will die one step away, as it should. Marriage: the bride carries the jar of keepers, and this is not symbol but plumbing — the blind white seed of her cistern goes down into her new roof's water, and the roof's water changes, takes on her house's faint taste, and the two strains mingle and persist, so that a century later a cord-keeper tasting a cistern can read the marriages in it. Roofs marry; keepers remember. The courts grant the wedding a beam-hour, and there is dancing, which you will not hear at all, because Wellborn dance is done through the floor: on the dense flowstone courts the dancers stamp their slow pulse and the slab carries it into the soles and shinbones of everyone standing, thirty people on one stone feeling the same beat in their skeletons, the elders sitting with palms flat on the paving, the herds shifting uneasily in the Runs as the faint drum of it comes up through the ground. It is the loudest thing in the city, and it is silence. Their music is all like this — hummed into an infant's chest, listened to with a hand on the singer's throat, sound with no tail and no memory, existing only in the instant and only within touch. The purest present tense of any music in the world.

Old age is honored in the exact measure it is costly: the elders are the archive, and the archive burns. *Thick-cleaved*, they call the very old, after the panes, and a household's age is given in pane-growth — *three fingers of pane* — since the selenite lays down its layers in the wet dark like slow rings and a family's window is its calendar. Death has its order, and the order is this. First the debts: no one enters the stone owing; the Beam Court convenes, the dead's beam-debts are worked to balance by the household or forgiven in public, and if a great debt of light is settled, the smoke rises for that alongside the death-smoke, two thin sentences over one roof. Then the mourning, which is bare-eyed: the household goes without visors until the closing, eyes red and streaming in the glare, because grief here is required to be *seen*, and the whole court looks, gently and at length, performing its half of the bargain. Then the closers come. The dead is laid in the central room of the house. The capstone — which has stood in that room all along, displayed, withheld, carved and recarved by three hundred years of household hands, the one self-portrait every family makes and never completes — is carried out at last. Into the gap where it will go, someone speaks the dead's whisper-name, once, aloud. The tufa takes it. It does not come back; it is not meant to; that is what names are for here, to be given back. The capstone is mortared home, the eye of the house closes, and the dead go into the stone bare-eyed, visor left behind, because there is no glare where they are going and no one left to hide from.

The closed outnumber the open, many times over. Walk the Closed Rows at rest hour and you will find the living there, sitting on sealed steps, tracing door-seams with two fingers, keeping company. The dead are the city's permanent residents; the living are a minority in their own city, and they would tell you they prefer it — it means the streets are never empty.

And there is the other practice, the still one. For all the churn, the Well reveres stillness with an equal and opposite devotion, and keeps it the way it keeps mirrors: with tenders. Children play at holding shapes on the fins. An adult who can take a fin-shape and hold it for a day — motionless, patient past reason, while the light walks over them — is honored with a sincerity that has no irony in it, and the fin-sitters, as they are simply called, are the city's contemplatives, its standing proof that the churn is chosen. The stalker on the high fins is understood in the same spirit: the old ones are known, named, watched across decades, and left alone. It is the basin's ambassador, they say — the finished world keeping one citizen inside the walls, holding its shape, reminding the unfinished city what it is holding off. Everyone goes down to the seep-pools sometimes, to the true silence, to remember. A few go down too often; the *pool-struck* are treated gently, fed, watched, sat with. And when one of them, usually old, walks down the throat-caves at a breath-turn and does not come up, the household signs the death and the smoke rises thin, and no one calls it anything but a closing.

Which leaves the Answerer. There is always one. It is not an office; no one is appointed; someone simply starts going up to the rim on still nights with a hand-mirror, and after enough years everyone understands who it is now. The dead Sister winks across the dark plains, irregular, untended, a flash thrown by a town with no one in it — and the Answerer finds the thread of light and gives it back. Out of courtesy, they will tell you, and then, if you have been there long enough to be trusted with the rest: a flash unanswered is an existence unwitnessed, and the Sister was their kin, and as long as someone sees its light and answers, it is not entirely closed. They keep a dead city alive by looking at it. Now you understand the chatter — ten thousand mirrors speaking all day, every day, to no one. Not to no one. To each other. To the sky. The city watching itself exist, because to be is to be seen, and the Well has survived ten thousand years on a finished, patient plain by refusing, daily, hourly, to go unwitnessed.

If you go down among them: learn the cupped sign first, and the tilt that means *held, not kept*. Do not raise your voice — it will die a step from your mouth and they will pity you, correctly, as a person shouting at a well that gives nothing back. Accept the guest-light when a court grants it; stand in your beam and let yourself be lit, because the lighting is the greeting, it means *we see you*, and you have never been greeted more completely. Take the guest-cup and drink it to the precise bottom; generosity here is exact, and exactness is its tenderness. If you are shown naked eyes, do not stare and do not look away — you are being given something that a pane takes eighty years to grow.

And when you leave, and the plains take you back, and the nights come down: watch the dark ground behind you. There is no glow; the loan is called in; the whole city is bare-eyed at last in the dark among its herds, all those hidden eyes open at once, the one hour when everyone is naked and no one can see — which is not a paradox to them, but the daily proof that being seen was always a gift and never a condition. If the wind swings a loose mirror and one thread of light reaches you from the ground itself, stop. It is the unfinished city saying the only sentence it has ever needed, the sentence the churn says, the capstones say, the Answerer says across the dark to the dead: *still here.* You are part of the grammar now. Answer it if you can. Remember it if you can't — memory, too, is a kind of mirror.
