A census of the Sunken Stair has to begin below the valley floor. The collapse blocks do not rest tight against each other; between and beneath them are rooms the daylight has never entered, floored with washed-in silt, breathing air at the temperature of deep ground. The city has a basement. It is the oldest part of the city — the part that was here when all of this was ceiling — and it is still inhabited.

Down there live the first citizens, and they are the only ones who were not drawn by the waterworks. A snail the size of a wheat grain keeps the spring throats, a species that occurs in this valley and nowhere else on earth, its whole nation a few hundred meters of wet limestone. Pale crickets with antennae like spinning pick the dark for whatever the dark is given: bat droppings, a drowned leaf, each other. In crevices hang the teardrop egg sacs of the cave orb-weaver, a spider with a strange biography — her spiderlings are born loving light and climb toward it and scatter on silk, while the adults turn away and walk back down into the black to breed. At night, in the mouths of the springs, the water carries out a few of its white amphipods: blind, translucent, unhurried, the descendants of the ones who lived here when the river ran under a mountain. They were not displaced when the roof fell. They simply stopped being the only ones.

One seep on the east wall runs milky and smells faintly of struck matches, and its threads are dressed in white hair — bacteria that eat the stone's chemistry and have never seen the sun. The cave had a metabolism. It still does.

The one furnished room of the old house is the surviving span, and it has tenants by season. In summer the space under the bridge belongs to crag martins, mud-cupped against the old ceiling — cliff birds that did not exist here until the collapse made cliffs, and arrived to find one cliff with a roof still on it. In winter the span is a dormitory. Bats hang there by the dozen, wings wrapped, in the last piece of the valley that is still a cave; they are the only citizens who still live in the Stair as it was. Herald moths sleep beside them, fretted like dead leaves, and peacock butterflies, wings shut, dusted with cave-calm — the only creatures who will see both the valley's winter and its spring. At dusk in the warm months the bats come out and trawl the ponds, low over the water, feet down, gathering insects off the surface like something being swept up. Every one of them returns before dawn to the dark under the bridge. The old address.

Above, in the light, the founders are at work and have never once paused. Call them the moss people: a few species of lime-loving moss — one of them named for tufa itself — and the algae and cyanobacteria mixed through their felt, laying down crust over crust at the pace of fingernail growth. As populations go they are the most patient in the gorge and the most consequential. They are also, accidentally, the valley's archive. Where the lime is richest, the tufa has crept out over drowned stick-dams and begun to set them in stone, twig by twig, so that the beavers of today are building on the petrified works of beavers dead a thousand years, and do not know it, and the moss does not know it is keeping records. Neither builder has ever seen the other's face. Each reads the other's work and continues it.

Then the engineers. No one has counted the clans; the scent mounds know. A dozen territories, perhaps, stacked down the gorge's middle length, each one read and renewed nightly in castoreum — a musky, almost sweet writing that says who holds this reach, who is mated, who is well. The oldest beaver in the Stair is a female of about fifteen winters who holds the pond above the bridge. Her teeth are deep orange with iron, her lips close behind them so she can gnaw underwater, her hands are small and black and nearly clever. She has outlived at least two mates, and some of the dams downstream are kept by her grandchildren — which the mounds record, and which she reads on her evening rounds without comment, the way anyone reads the local news. Her kits stay in the lodge a month before the water takes them. Her two-year-olds leave in spring, walking up out of the crack and across the plateau — the valley's only emigrants, the only ones who leave by the sky. Everyone else arrives by water and stays.

The willows are citizens too, in their way: stools older than any beaver, gnawed to the nub and resprouting, gnawed and resprouting, tended by teeth for centuries into a coppice no one planted. The covenant is total and unspoken — the beaver takes the branch, the willow takes the light the branch's fall lets in. In the ponds themselves the previous forest stands drowned and silver, and the dead trunks are not idle: the black woodpecker hammers doors in them, and the doors, once he leaves, are taken by goldeneye ducks — the ducklings leap from the hole on their first morning of life, trusting water they have never touched — and later by a small owl, and later by hornets, and the snag's slow fall feeds the pondweed that feeds the moose. Nothing is wasted because nothing is finished.

At thumbnail scale the masonry never stops: caddisfly larvae crawl the gravel in cases of sand grain and shell shard, and among them go the caseless ones, fanged and free — the wolves of the gravel-bed. Cracking their little houses open is the valley's least-known citizen, the water shrew, dressed in soot and silver, fringed hairs on its paddling feet, venom in its bite that it uses on animals twice its size which it cannot see. It hunts by touch along the pond bottoms, which is to say it lives where everything else only visits. When the ice comes and the city contracts, the shrew enters its own season, working the spring runs under the snow, warm in the only moving water, unsuspected by the winter above it.

The fish were here before the light was. Their ancestors swam under the mountain, in the dark river, and when the roof fell they did not flee; they simply became fish with a sky. Every pond has one trout that matters — a brown trout the size of a forearm under the undercut bank, the color of the stones it refuses to leave, a pensioner-tyrant that takes the first of everything the current brings. Under the stones lie sculpins, all head and patience. And in the silt of the slow backwaters, buried to their mouths, live the lamprey children: blind, legless, years-long filterers of the mud, citizens no visitor has ever seen, waiting out a childhood underground before one brief silver adulthood.

Of the birds, the dipper is the one the gorge seems to have invented for itself. A bird shaped like a wren and built like a plumb bob; it bobs on the dam crest, then flies low and fast down the stair of ponds, then walks into the water and along the bottom, gripping the stones, picking the masons out of their houses. Its address is a flight of stairs. It sings in the coldest months, a thin silver threading that the walls swallow almost whole, and when it blinks it blinks white — a feathered flash, a signal lamp, the brightest small event of a dim afternoon. Its nest is a ball of moss tucked behind one of the hundred threads that spill off the tufa lips, so the chicks' first sound is falling water and their first view is through it.

The larger traffic keeps its own customs. The heron is the licensed stillness; it stands on a dam crest like a fence post that has outlasted its fence, and when it finally heaves into the air its wings drag audibly — one of the day's sanctioned loud events. The otter is the only citizen who reads the whole Stair end to end in a single night, porpoising through every territory, and over the beavers' scent-law it writes its own, in spraint left on the flat midstream stones — a musk that reads, to those who can read it, like sweetness at the edge of fish. In the green, gone-soft dome of a lodge the beavers abandoned, a mink keeps one room dry; it builds nothing and repairs nothing, the valley's one pure tenant. Water voles hold the bank towns, blunt and chocolate-brown, sitting up to eat sedge held in both hands, dropping into the ponds with the roundest sound in the gorge. The moose acknowledges no dam: he steps over jurisdictions the way weather steps over fences, and when he stands chest-deep hauling up pondweed, the whole pond gives way slowly — wakes, then stillness — and the beavers, who will feud over a scent mound, watch from the far side in silence. The lynx is a post that occasionally becomes a paw. The wolves are among the dam-builders' unwitting beneficiaries, crossing dry-shod on crest after crest; they have learned the city's sounds, and the tail-slap that empties every pond of its public life is no alarm to them — it is merely the town announcing that they have arrived.

Summer, brief and slant-lit, brings the ephemerals. In the two hours when the sun stands in the gorge, demoiselles work the slack water, wings like spoiled ink. Whirligig beetles scribble on the still corners, their eyes built in two stories — one for the sky, one for the pond — writing a script that erases itself as fast as it is written. In the stillest margins the surface itself walks, a gray nap of springtails. And in one seep-warmed pocket pool above the third barrage lives the valley's entire amphibian nation: eleven frogs. On three evenings in June their singing is the loudest thing that happens all year, and the whole gorge, which keeps its quiet like a rule, lets them have it.

Winter reverses the geography. The ponds skin over; the beavers close their underwater doors and live by the cache, branches sunk against exactly this; the trout hang like held breath; the dippers commute through holes in the ice, vanishing into black water and emerging frosted and singing. And then, because the Stair keeps the only open water for a day's walk in any direction, the plateau comes down to it. The snow on the rim is written over with arrivals — hare, fox, the raven's two-footed waddle, wolves, once a winter the great unhurried script of an elk — everything coming down the crack to drink, to hunt the drinkers, to wait. All summer the gorge receives; all winter it hosts. The ravens, who nest on the raw white scar of last year's rockfall because they like their real estate new, are the only voices allowed to be loud against the walls, and even they seem to save it for the open sky.

At the lower end, where the ponds fray into fen and the fen into the blue slot, the water goes under and takes the census with it: lime, silt, seeds, shed caddis cases, a stray white amphipod swept home. The spring throats around that doorway never quite freeze, and in them, at the edge of the dark, the first citizens wait in the water that is going back to their world.

Because that is the thing the new arrivals cannot know, building their terraces and lodges and nests and lives: the city is sediment. Every citizen is laying down the valley's future floor — in lime or sticks or silt or bone — and a floor thick enough, in a crack like this, is a roof. Given time, the last pond will silt to meadow, the meadow will slip toward the slot, and the crack will be a cave again, river under mountain, dark and dripping and whole. No one here intends it. But the ones who will be fully at home in that finished building are already home. They are in the doorways, blind and white and patient. They never left. They are simply the only ones who know how the story continues: the water, the slow stone, the ceiling regrown over everything — dam by dam by dam.
