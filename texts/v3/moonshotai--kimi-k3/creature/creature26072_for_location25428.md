The water comes first, because the water is almost everyone.

Ask the town what lives here, and if the person you ask is in a truthful mood they will say: the water. Everything else — the two thousand people, the ten thousand fowl, the kites and bees and lizards, the dark tangle of the gardens — is shapes the water wears, a generation at a time. They mean it as fact and as faith. The working water has passed through everyone who has ever lived on the crown, breathed out and sweated out and washed off and caught again in the cold deep stone, and there is no cup in the town that does not hold some of everyone. At a birth the midwife touches one drop of cistern water to the new tongue — the return, they call it. At a death the body is washed and the wash-water carried out to the family row. In between, a person carries a cup: given at seven, line-marked, outliving its owner, passed on with the name, skipping a generation — so that you drink all your life from the cup of the dead, and someone not yet born will one day drink from yours. At a cup-joining the two pour their cups into one bowl and drink by turns, and what is said is: *we are all the same water.* It is not a metaphor. It is the town.

The people themselves are lean and deep-chested and dark-eyed, their eyes gone wide over the generations for the bronze low light; a child here can thread a loom by one candle. They number two thousand, give or take a birth — the Line-Keepers could give it exactly, but exactness is thought bad manners in a matter so fluid. Children born within the same leaning of the light are a *pour*, raised together and cupped together. The day is a turning of the sun's slow circle, told by the shadow-ring at the Stair's foot; the night is lowsun, the hour when the circling sun nods down to the haze and the bronze deepens to ember. And every person here speaks two tongues: the low speech pitched beneath the chord, a voice that runs under the Voice and is, to those who live in it, perfectly clear — and handtalk, used in the gardens when the swells roll through, on the Stair where the Voice swallows speech whole, on the ledges, by the old, and by children keeping secrets in plain sight. Everyone is bilingual from the cradle. A baby signs before it speaks.

A life here is an arc of hearing. A child hears everything: the breastbone note, the teeth-note, and the thin high thread at the chord's edge that adults lose without ever noticing. Middle age owns the middle — speech, fowl-croon, the knock and hum of the rotors. The old lose the thread first, then the teeth-note, then more, and drift down into handtalk and vibration — going below the Voice, the town calls it, and speaks of it as a promotion, not a loss. The deaf elders are the Keepers' finest instruments: in the listening-cells they set palms and bare soles to the stone and say whether the chord is true, and the greatest plug-tuner in memory — the guild still speaks her name first at every setting — had not heard the Voice with her ears for twenty years. She heard it with the rest of her. Every child learns the story of the hour the wind faltered, generations back; the date is cut in the Measure; and every child, sooner or later, asks some soft-deaf elder whether the quiet is terrible. The answer is always the same, signed slowly, smiling: *It is not empty. It is roomy. We live there now.*

The Keepers of the Voice are the oldest guild, and they are chosen for their ears. An apprentice must find the chord's third note and hold it in the throat against a swell before being trusted with a rope. The plugs they carve are gypsum-throated, tuned by shaving, and a setting takes days; the rope-teams haul on the swell, working with the mountain's breath because there is no other rhythm to work with. Every setting is recorded — throat-widths, dates, the note that resulted — a kind of music written backward, so that a Keeper two lifetimes from now can learn what a century sounds like.

The water-keepers are chosen for their tongues. A keeper tastes each shaft's condensate daily, and each cistern has a voice on the palate — sweet-stone, dust-bitter, the flat taste that means a seam is dying — and an apprentice spends a year learning the water by mouth before being trusted with a lid. In the deep shafts they grow the shaft-caps, pale fans of fungus on scalebark chips, sliced and chewy, the keepers' quiet perk; and deeper still, where the air goes cold, live the shaft-crickets, singing a thin high tireless thread beneath the great chord — the small Voice, audible only to young ears and only down there. Every child is brought down once to hear it: the nearest thing to silence the world affords, the mountain's note gone to a hum in the bone, and over it, tiny and patient, the small Voice going about its business. And every cistern keeps its eft: pale, lidless, unhurried, long as a forearm, descended — the keepers say — from things that come up out of the deep stone with the Old Water's seep, for where else would they come from. The efts drink first; they are the only creatures in the world who drink before people. An eft's thriving means the water is sound. An eft's death means a cistern-mourning — the water drawn off, the chamber scrubbed and re-cut, a new eft brought up from the nursery dark. The Measure's eft is the oldest animal in the world, older than any person, older than most of the walls, keepers beyond counting outlived. It drifts in the dark beside the marked column and the falling line, and *patient as the deep eft* is the highest praise the town bestows.

The Blackhands tend the soil, and their hands are black to the wrist, and it is the proudest mark in town. The soil is a meter deep and older than any wall, and they know it the way the keepers know water — by hand, by smell, by beetle. Three kept kinds of beetle work the compost vaults, introduced to each new batch with a spoken line, and the vaults' slow heat warms the wash-cells. The dead go down into the deep pits and come up, years later, as garden soil; it takes years to become soil, and the town is in no hurry. The Blackhands keep the maps of who lies where, so that a gardener may know which row holds her grandmother, and plant accordingly. They do not say *gone* of the dead. They say *planted*, and they mean it as a promise.

The Line-Keepers are the memory that walks. For a pairing they recite both lines nine generations back, sung under the Voice in the cistern-hall while the families listen for the meeting-points; it takes a day and nobody minds, because it is beautiful. They keep the names — some four hundred names for two thousand people, each resting a generation and returning with its cup — and the knot-tallies, and the soot-ink notes on sherds, and the archive carved down the stairwell into the dark, where the oldest marks are no longer readable and are recited anyway. When something must be decided, the town meets in the cistern-hall, under the water; and when a question can bear deferral, it is put to the Measure — chalked beside a line not yet reached, left to the grandchildren. The oldest chalked question has been deferred eleven generations: whether to send a walking-party to the near brother. Each generation it is read aloud, and each generation it is chalked forward again — not refused, never refused — deferred. It is how the town keeps a hope: in the future tense.

The smith keeps the sky-iron and the ironbook, in which every shard of fall is accounted — its weight, its finder, its weddings. Blades worn to slivers are married to new fall at the forge, witnessed, and so there are knives in the town that have been knives continuously since before the guildhall. A knife is a citizen. It is inherited, sharpened down, married, and goes on.

The salt-crews are the town's far arm, and they have their own laws. Two days down the Stair with harness and sledge; and on the plain, nothing taken from the brother-graves — the dead mountains are not quarried, and a crew passing a ring-dome touches the stone once and moves on. They spot the meteorites dark and obvious on the white salt; the finder keeps a shaving and the shard goes to the smith. In the midge years the seeps swell and the sour ponds bloom, and the crews camp out on the crust with nets of woven ropeleaf and come home with sledges of pressed black cake, the kites streaming out over the flats and home fat behind them — the Fat Days, the town's one irregular festival, the sea briefly remembering itself. In those ponds wake the shield-shrimp, thumb-long and pale-armored, hatching from cysts that have slept in the salt since before the town, each pond-year their sleep longer and the water later. The crews scoop them for the fowl, and keep a vial of cysts in the archive, labeled in an old hand: *the last sea, sleeping.* And further out, generations back, a crew found the Walkers — a line of bones far out on the salt, wind-polished to ivory, pointing, as the crews read it, home. The bones are in the archive too. At cup-day the children are shown both: the ones who wait, and the ones who tried.

And there is the Watch. Every lowsun one watcher climbs to the lamp-platform at the crown's rim, where the great signal-lamp — the Answer — is kept trimmed and ready and lit for one hour, every lowsun, whether or not anything has ever come, so that if any eye is out there the dark is not total. The log runs eleven generations and most entries are one word. Once, three generations back, an entry says *seen*, struck through, and under it, *nearly*. The town does not laugh about the nearly. The town keeps it. The watchers are the young, for the eyes, and the old, for the patience; and it is not waiting — the town is not waiting for anything — it is speaking. The lamp says, nightly, into the bronze dark: *here. Still here.*

The ledge-fowl are the town's second voice and first wealth: plump, slate-backed, rust-throated, small-headed, crooning their three-note descending hum that sits inside the chord's middle note. At the peak of every great swell, all ten thousand of them croon together — the Reply, the town's beloved commonplace, by which small tasks are timed: *by the third Reply.* A family's eaves and shelves host its flocks by ledge-right; a rich house is loud with fowl; eggs are gathered at first light and lowsun; shell goes to lime, quills to pens, down to bedding, fat to lamps, and an old hen ends as broth with no apology, having been eggs for years. One-Eye, the ancient cock of the west eaves, has outlived three fowlers and answers to no sign, though the fowlers have handtalk-signs for individual birds; he is less a kept bird than a neighbor with opinions. Every child is given a first hen, and learns the ledge-harness before the ledge.

Over the windward face the plain-kites wheel in their permanent gyre, broad and black-backed and bronze-eyed, patient beyond anything else with a heartbeat. The town casts its leavings from the windward rim into the rising air, and the kites take them on the wing; nothing edible touches the plain. The highest flutes above the town are the kite-crown, theirs by custom, the one ledge with no fowl-rights; the young kites breed there, and the old ones give up the ground entirely and live on the column, sleeping half a brain at a time, hardly landing for years. The Ragged One has been known by name for forty years; the children know her torn silhouette the way they know a grandmother's walk. A kite feather found on the salt is carried up like a relic and bound into a child's hair at cup-day, and the blessing spoken over travelers going down the Stair is: *land rarely.*

The smaller folk fill every seam. The bee-wards go bare-handed and slow among hives set in wall-hollows and flute-cracks, and the hives hum a hair off the mountain's middle note, and the bee-wards tune their ears by it. Wax is light: the small flames in the windows are tallow and beeswax, and a household's candle-store is its visible comfort. Honey is measured by the spoon, and once in a long while comes a honey-year, when a hive must be taken, and then the long mead is brewed and cellared for the cup-days of a whole pour. Door-lizards keep the cells, each with its dish of water by the wall; a house with a fat lizard counts itself lucky. Rock-rats keep the children busy — snares, and ledge-lamb on feast skewers, and glove-leather for the salt-crews — and a white rat, rare, is given to a new household and cherished absurdly. The pipe-adders hunt the rats through the honeycombs and are left strictly alone; a snake drinking condensate on your threshold means your walls are sound. Dusk-moths broad as a palm come to every candle, eye-spotted and soft, and in their plump years their young are gathered off the ropeleaf and roasted — the poor year's meat, eaten with ceremony anyway. There are no butterflies. The word survives, meaning a creature that needed daylight.

The scalebarks are citizens. Each standing tree has a keeper and a biography — planted under whom, scarred in which setting-fall, promised plank by plank to roofs not yet built — and its successor grows in a garden row against its death-day. A felling takes a season and is half funeral, half fair: the biography spoken whole, the wood allotted by need and lot, the last leaf pressed into the archive. The eldest scalebark is older than the guildhall and broad as a doorway, and children are brought to set a palm against its black scales; its keeper holds the most honored and least busy office in the world. Ropeleaf is the town's tendons — fiber, caulk, potherb, bee-blossom. Gourds are the second pottery: flasks for the Stair, floats, drums. And in every kitchen, in the warm dark by the hearth-stone, lives the mother-jar — the leaven, fed daily, divided at cup-joinings, some of them older than the guildhall, the small immortals. Nobody remembers the first mother. She is everyone now.

There are no dogs — nothing to herd, nowhere to run — no cats, no goats. The town's working beasts are wind and beetle. The largest animal in the world is the plain-kite, and most of that is wing; the heaviest is a fat ledge-cock or a new child, about the same, and equally doted on. The oldest animal is the Measure's eft. The oldest life is the soil, or the eldest scalebark, or the mother-jars — the town argues it at feasts, and some wag generations back put the question to the Measure as a joke, where it is chalked still, deferred from line to line with increasing ceremony. The smallest inhabitants are asleep: out on the plain the cysts of the shield-shrimp wait in the salt in their uncounted millions, for water that comes later and later, and does not yet fail to come.

At lowsun the town does not sleep all at once — nothing here ever entirely stops — but it settles, layer by layer, and you can take the census of everything alive. The fowl to their ledges, crooning. The kites asleep on the wing, the gyre wheeling by feel above the flutes. The bees home, the hives' hum sinking into the chord. The lizards tucked into warm seams; the adders hunting; the rats, cautiously, about their errands; the dusk-moths soft against the screens. The keeper on her last round, palm on a cistern-lid, listening to the drip; the Measure's eft drifting in the dark beside the falling line; far below, the crickets taking up the small Voice. The mother-jars bubbling in a thousand kitchens. The beetles at work in the warm vaults, turning the dead toward soil. A Blackhand asleep black to the wrists; a Line-Keeper murmuring tomorrow's recital in her sleep, her fingers still counting lines; the deaf elder with her palm flat to the wall, dreaming the chord through bone; the child with a kite-feather in her hair, asleep mid-sign. And on the rim the watcher, lighting the Answer, sending the one word out over the dark salt toward the brothers: *here.*

The water goes around, wearing all of them. The chord holds. The Answer burns its hour out over the plain. And when the next slow swell comes up from the flutes and rolls across the crown, ten thousand small voices rise inside the great one — and the mountain is answered.
