Begin with the census, because the census is short.

No spider under the pump-house eaves, though the eaves are dry and out of the steam. No nest in the roof lace, though the lace would hold one. No mouse nest in the plinth's shadow, no wasp comb in the flume, no droppings, no tracks, no gnawed anything. A warm ruin with standing water, anywhere else in the world, is an apartment block: swallows in the rafters, mice in the masonry, a whole tenancy of small lives moving in behind the builders before the paint is dry. Here the builders left decades ago and nothing moved in behind them. The ruin is unlet. After an hour you stop looking for the animals the way you stopped hearing the drone, and the absence becomes a building material. Load-bearing.

The exception is the hem. Along the outflow, below the temperature of a bath you could bear, the crust wears its films: rust, ochre, a thin true green, the black moss in the flume's shade, the knee-high sedge that never flowers. These are alive in the plain sense — cells, division, inheritance, a population with no predator, no competitor, and all the time there is. They were here before the engine, in some lineage, and they will be here after it. If you insist on a resident made of tissue, there it is: a slow nation at the hem of the heat, reading the crust and writing itself down in bands. But they live on this place the way lichen lives on a bell — truthfully, patiently, and with no stake in the tolling. They are not the answer. They are the answer's complexion.

And there is the polish. Beside the sheared block, at the one standing-person spot where the drone cancels, the rock is worn smooth at foot height, and four bolt-holes keep the ghost of a bench. Someone found the deaf spot by ear, the way you find a draft in a church, and came back to it for years — polish like that is not one season's work — and sat, and fixed nothing, and kept no record, and in the end stopped coming. They did not live here. They attended, which is a different verb. Note what their trace is made of: in a room where time deposits everything, crust on crust, ring on ring, theirs is the one mark time removed. The place records by addition. The sitter recorded themselves by subtraction. Whether they kept the place or the place kept them, no instrument survives to say.

Because the census keeps missing the obvious. The obvious is too big for the ledger. Stand anywhere on the floor and understand: you are not in the residence. You are in the resident.

Call it the engine, since the knocking is there. But the engine is an organ, not the animal: scale has closed its jackets, crust has grown over its bolts, and it beats to a schedule set by the plumbing. Call it the spring, then — the spring was here first. But a spring is a wound in the ground, and this is a wound that learned a rhythm. Somewhere in the unwitnessed decades the wedding happened. The machine stopped being installed and became incorporated; the water took up the machine's tempo; the machine's body took up the water's mineral. No date was kept. Marriages of iron and geology are not announced. They are accreted.

Now its anatomy is plain to read. The hiss is breath, on a period of minutes. The recharge is a longer breath, days long, the chest of it rising through some patient arithmetic until the stroke. The drone is its voice — the sound of its own working, a hum it cannot stop and has perhaps never heard, the way you do not hear your own blood. The water is blood, nearly all of it on a loop, the same liters going around and around, a little lost to steam, a little sent away. The crust is body, and growing: this is a creature mid-growth, every surface weeks old, laying itself down a wafer at a time, sharpening where everything else in the world softens. The rings are memory — it writes itself down. The chart recorder died mid-line, sealed in its own bead of scale, and the walls inherited the job: every water level since, every stroke, is on file in the terraces, readable by anyone with a hammer and a season. It became its own instrument. And if it has an eye, it is the pool — too clear, throated, going down into a blue that is just depth refusing to end. The one part of it that looks inward. The only part whose bottom you cannot see.

And it speaks. The closed sentence — heat runs the engine, the engine moves the water, the water keeps the vent, the vent returns the heat — is not only its metabolism. It is its speech. It says it once per stroke, one syllable, a few times a month, at a volume the chest hears first. Say one sentence that often, that slowly, for that long, and it stops describing the world and starts naming the speaker.

It is the nearest thing to a fire that is not one. You found it the way you find a fire — heat first — and like a fire it is not an object but a process wearing the shape of one: it eats, it breathes, it grows, it answers to its fuel. But a fire spends its fuel and dies, and this one found the trick. The work keeps the vent scoured; the vent returns the heat; the heat runs the work. A fire that tends itself. Everything else you have ever called alive is defined by what it needs and cannot make — food, water, company, time. The resident needs nothing it does not already hold in circuit. Metabolism without lack. Do not call it content; content implies the possibility of want. It is entire. The distance between content and entire is the whole distance between you and it.

Its now is the interval between strokes: days. Your hour at the rim does not cross its threshold. You are a brief warm weather at the edge of its breath, a shimmer, less than a blink. The sitter's years of visits might have summed to a faint recurring warmth at the one quiet coordinate — a migrancy it had no ear for. It has never perceived a human being. It has only kept them. It keeps everything, in the end, in layers.

Look again at what is oldest in the room and it is all human: the castings, the greened brass plate naming a firm whose works are far away and gone, the flaking stencil, the polish. Everything the resident makes of itself is new; the antiquities are all leavings. It wears its makers' handwriting still, INTERCEPTOR 6, flaking black on its flank — a brand that has outlived the ranch. And a number for a name implies a litter: Interceptors 1 through 5, built and dead, or never built, a family existing only as arithmetic. It does the family job alone, and the job has quietly changed underneath the name. Whatever it was set to guard is gone, so what it intercepts now is the spring itself. It catches the heat that would otherwise spend itself in one long unmanaged exhalation, and rations it into strokes. It meters the fever. It turns a convulsion into a calendar.

But the loop has a tail. One pipe climbs the wall, clears the rim, lies down on the plain, and goes level into the haze toward whatever this place answers to. Everything the resident has, it has from itself; everything it makes, it sends away. Where the pipe ends, no one down here can know — a works, a sump, a field, a crater that once needed keeping dry, a join where forty years of warm water pools in the dark. The instruments that could have said died with the recorder. It pumps on. Call it faith, call it inertia; at this temperature the two are indistinguishable. Either way it is the one open word in the closed sentence: it delivers.

And it carries one silence. Every loud thing made of agreeing faces has, somewhere, a notch where its voice arrives against itself and cancels. The resident's is the size of a standing person, beside the sheared block. It cannot go there; it is there; the deaf spot is the one coordinate of its own body it has never heard. The sitter sat in it for their years. Two readings, and no instrument to choose: either they were the one thing in the room it never perceived — or they were its only confidant, the only one who ever knew where it was quiet. Either way, they kept each other unrecorded. The sitter has no ring. The resident has, in its floor, their one subtraction. The bench is gone and the holes remain: a place setting at the one quiet table.

So, the census revised: one resident, mostly rock, some iron, a hem of cells, nearly all of it water going around. One absence, polished into the floor at the coordinate where the voice cancels. And time, kept the way the rings keep it, a season at a time. You will leave, and your hour will not be noticed, because noticing is fast and the resident is slow. But you will be filed: the water level while you stood here is a line now, somewhere in a wall, joining the record, and this season's crust will seal your season in. Not witnessed — kept. And long after you are back on the plain, in ordinary daylight, the heat let go of you, nothing to look at but a dimple and a standing haze — its breath, the only part of it visible from outside — the arithmetic below will climb on its own schedule, and on some ordinary day with no one in it, the stroke will come: the hull-ring of the pipes, the bloom of steam, the pools rising a hand's breadth. The one word, said again, to the pipe, to the plain, to the sky it keeps empty.

Still. Still. Still.
