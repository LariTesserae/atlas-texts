The census of the cove is easy to take and hard to finish. A few dozen hands, company-employed. Gulls beyond counting, though counted. Eels in their permanent numbers. Crabs in the outflows, mussels on the pilings, seals on the warm sand, brine flies clouding the pans, a rat or two in the storehouse for completeness, one geranium doing poorly. Ask the hands if that is everything that lives here and they will say yes, and mean it, and then one of them will glance down the pipe-run — the same glance they give the pans when you ask the year — and say: well. There's the Governor.

Nobody can date the title. Whether it came off the brass flyball governor that keeps the engine's speed or off the dockside word for the boss, the hands don't say, because both are right and neither is the work. The Governor decides nothing, owns nothing beyond an oilcloth notebook and a brass listening rod, and is the only name on the company books — first ledger and latest, same small square hand — whose duty is entered as a single word: *keeping.*

For the cove is one engine with its parts spread out for cooling, and the Governor walks it. That is the whole job, and it is never finished. The round begins at the Kettle, because the Kettle sets the floor of the chord, and the Governor stands closer to it than the doubled duckboards allow anyone else, head bowed like someone receiving news — though the Kettle's news has not changed in living memory, and the Governor's memory is what the hands here mean by *living*. Then out along the numbered pipes: brass rod to a flange and the rod's end to the bone behind the ear; a palm laid flat on warm rock, taking the ground's pulse the way the ground takes yours through the boot soles. One full circuit between whistles, Kettle to pier. The hands set their days by it. *Where's the Governor* is how you ask the time in this parish, and the answer — coming off the number-fourteen flange, heading down the long run — is the only hour the cove keeps. All the clocks here read the same hour; the Governor is the hand on them.

The notebook comes out at every stop, and the figures entered are the same figures, whistle after whistle, and the hands say the figures would go wrong if they were not written, and the hands are right, because the writing is the maintenance. The storehouse shelves a row of identical notebooks running back past anyone's grandfather, every page full, every page the same — the whole library of the cove, all of it necessary. The Governor takes the census, too: nests by flange, eels by trough, crabs by outflow. Counts the unchanging, and is counted among it.

What they are made of seems to be the cove's own materials in flesh order. Skin weathered to its line like the iron — overnight, deep, and holding. Hair gone the silver of the wood. Eyes the pearl-gray of the light at noon, directionless. Salt rime at the cuffs and collar, because they live downwind of everything. On the heat scale they sit where the gulls leave off: above every bird on the graded ledges, below the orange algae that take the hottest channels, at the top of the scale for flesh. They can rest a bare hand on rock that would take your skin — not because it doesn't burn, the hands say, but because the burning found its level years ago, the way rust does here, and holds. Sleep is taken the way seals take it, half at a time, the hands say, and since no one has ever caught the Governor at it, the saying stands. There is a bunk allotted in the ledger; likeliest it lies in the dry lee inside a condenser tower, a yard from the rain that always falls indoors, which would make the Governor the only person alive who keeps house beside weather. They drink condensate, warm from the trough, out of a tin cup re-tinned so many times that the cup is a position more than an object. Like the pier. Like the job.

The geranium in the mess window is theirs. Eleven years of doing poorly is eleven years of exact care: a thimble of condensate, a quarter-turn of the pot, the plant kept at its setting like everything else in the parish, and the hands — who say a job done is a job lost — consider the geranium the proof of the Governor. The one growing thing in the cove, growing the way the shoreline grows: a yard gained, a yard lost, forever. The paymaster's drawer holds the Governor's scrip, drawn and returned, entered and cancelled, the only account in the books that balances to zero; even wages here are intake and outflow. When the surveyor comes each year to measure the shore and leaves with the same map, he and the Governor exchange a nod on the iron pier, and that nod is the cove's entire diplomacy.

And it was the Governor who gave the cove its one story. In somebody's grandfather's day — the hands say the grandfather is the Governor's, which is the cove's one joke — a bearing on the number-two line changed pitch for an afternoon. The hands' telling takes the better part of a mess-hour and includes the weather, which was fog. The Governor's telling is shorter, and it is the whole of the cove's literature in the keeper's own words: *It went up a step. I heard it. It came back.* Whatever was done — warmed, wrapped, jacked, greased — was done before the second whistle, and the chord closed over the afternoon the way the fog closes over a lamp. That is why the story stands alone. It is the only time the chord nearly had an event in it, and the one person whose work is hearing was there to hear it not happen.

Ask how long the Governor has kept the rounds, and you will get three answers, each with evidence.

The ledgers say: from the beginning. The name is in the first book the company opened — same square hand, same modest draw, same allotment of one bunk. The ink suppliers have changed four times. The hand has not changed at all.

The hands say: the Governor is a maintenance, like the pier. There is always someone walking a half-step behind on the rounds, learning the warm paths, carrying the spare oilcan; there has always been someone. Whether the learning has ever ended — whether the spare oilcan has ever changed shoulders — is a question that gets the pans-look and the shrug, because if it happened it happened the way shifts relieve each other here: into an identical day. Every plank has been replaced many times and nothing has ever changed, and the hands see no reason the keeper should be the one exception to the cove's one law. Unless the keeper is how the law keeps itself.

And the oldest hands, the ones whose own rust has nearly found its line, say a third thing. The cove makes its own weather, they say, and its own shoreline, and the cleanest water in the world out of steam and patience — a place that condenses will condense anything, given time, given one person who stays. Whoever the Governor was rusted to their line in somebody's grandfather's day, and holds, the way everything holds here. At its setting.

Ask the Governor which of these is true and you will be favored with the shortest entry in the literature. *The rounds are walked,* the Governor says, and moves off at walking pace, which is the pistons' pace, which is the pace of everything in the cove that keeps.

So stand at the end of the iron pier at what might be evening, and know that what you are hearing is being heard. There — on the trestle behind you, at the number-fourteen flange — a figure gone still as a numbered lamp, brass rod to the bone behind the ear, head bowed to the chord the way you would bow over a letter that arrives every day of your life with the same news and must still be read. The whistle comes due, and sounds, and is due again. The Governor writes the same figure in the oilcloth book and walks on.

Stay long enough — until your boots learn the warm paths, until the chord resolves into layers for you and back into one — and the Governor will give you the nod, once, the surveyor's nod, because you have become measurable: a small intake the cove will balance when the packet takes you off again. Or won't. There is room in the ledgers, and the first page and the last are the same page, and the gulls are settling by degrees on their warm flanges, and somewhere between the Kettle and the pier, on the youngest ground in the world, its keeper walks the rounds at the pace the pistons set — due, and sounding, and due again — a pulse that found its resting rate in somebody's grandfather's day, and keeps it, and is kept.
