By ear, the room is a congregation. By count, it is barely a quorum. The chord sounds like a chapelful of singers and an organ loft and a patient bell-ringer, and it is, in fact, the work of a short list: the voices, the gardeners, the moths, the ticks, one serpent, and the garden all of them are standing in. That is the whole census. It does not grow and it does not shrink, and this is the first thing to understand about everything alive here: each kind is kept the way a note is kept — filled, exactly, from within.

Begin with the voices, since the room does.

The choir-beasts are gray all over, furred in something short and dense as moleskin, and built around their chests the way a furnace is built around its fire. The chest is enormous, barrel-round, and above it hangs the loose throat — a great dewlap, soft and slack as an emptied sleeve, which is not decoration but apparatus. That sac is a reservoir. A choir-beast breathes in through it slowly, over spans you would have to be very patient to measure, and sings out through it without pause, so that the tone never breaks for breath because the breath is stored ahead of the need. The sound leaves the beast the way warmth leaves a stone: continuously, without effort, without any sign that effort could be required.

Each one sits at its own antinode, and this means each one sits in light of its own making. The glow pools where the pressure of the standing wave gathers, and the pressure gathers where the voices hold it, and so every singer is lit dimly, amber and bone, by the note it is pouring out — a candle that is also its own audience. Their eyes stay half-lidded. They are not watching anything; there is nothing to watch that the chord does not tell them better. Sometimes an ear turns. Once in a long while a forefoot is lifted, considered, and set down a hand's breadth away. That is the news from a choir station, and it is sufficient.

The note belongs to the seat, not the singer. Whatever beast occupies that place in the room's hidden geometry occupies that pitch; the chord's voicing is geographic. And they sing without any need to be answered, because the chord is not a conversation — it is a condition, like warmth, like stillness. Nothing here is waiting on a reply, and so nothing here is lonely. Loneliness is an expectation going unmet. Nothing in this room expects.

They do die, in their way, and the way is this: over a very long span a voice thins. It sags perhaps a quarter-tone — the only detuning the room ever knows — and by then the answer is already ripening. Years before, deep in the root-braid, a swelling will have begun, and begun to hum: the vacant note, wool-muted, sung in its sleep. When it is ripe it steps out of the braid, walks the bare lanes to the seat, and settles. For a span the seat holds two voices in unison — the only duet this room permits — and then the old beast stands, walks itself to the braid, and lies down against the roots, which take it in over seasons. No seat is ever found empty. No seat is ever found cold.

The gardeners are where the room keeps its hands.

They are tall, and most of their height is limb: long legs that step high and deliberate over the pale grain-lines on the floor — they never smear the lines; they know exactly where the lines are — and longer arms that hang nearly to the ground when slack. Their skin is dry, matte, the amber-grey of the wall they walk beside, and warm, as everything is warm. Their hands are the remarkable thing: fingers too long and too many-jointed, clever in the way of creatures whose whole world is near work. Their eyes are built for arm's length. Beyond arm's length, a gardener does not look; it listens, or presses its small dark tongue to the wall-salt and tastes what the stone is doing — the stress in the laminae, the slow intentions of the massif — reading the sea's pressed memory like a core sample.

The work has three strands, and none of them ever finishes.

There is the pruning. The glasscane lengthens perhaps a hair in a decade, and the gardeners take the hair off, blade by blade, with their flaked-crystal knives hafted in spun mosswool. Each cane is grown to a precise free length, and the length is the pitch, and the pitch is the chord, and so the gardeners shave the drift out of the world at precisely the rate the world drifts. It is work slower than watching grass grow, performed between pulses, uncounted, and it is never behind, because there is no such thing here as behind.

There are the grooves. Everywhere on wall and floor, hair-fine carved lines tune the chord's reflections — the room's score and its instrument at once. Salt creeps; over long time the cuts soften, and so the gardeners re-carve them, endlessly, which means the room's only writing survives by being rewritten forever. Nothing is stored. Everything is re-said. A gardener reading the grooves with its fingertips looks like a scholar, but there is nothing new to read; the reading is itself a re-saying. Behind them come the salt-ticks, grooming, keeping the marks clean of nothing, since there is nothing to dirty them — and that is perhaps the purest sentence to be written about this room: maintenance without decay, performed as though decay were a real and present enemy, by creatures who have never seen it.

And there is the copper. One vein of native metal breaks the wall, the room's only metal, and there the gardeners have built an entire metallurgy and spent it on almost nothing: hammered tongues, drawn wire, one small leaf-bell. The tongues are diagnostic — a gardener strikes one and presses its butt to the wall and listens with its whole skeleton, a stethoscope for the stone. The wire is drawn cold through dies of drilled crystal, in lengths that would span the chamber, bright as the hour it left the plate, and coiled at the station, and for it the room has no obvious use. It exists because the vein allowed it. It is capability kept for its own sake, a technology with one material and no product — a whole civilization's worth of skill poured into three small objects, none of which has ever tarnished, none of which has ever needed resharpening, all of which look made this morning and abandoned this afternoon and kept forever.

One of them works the bell. The bell station is the vein itself, and the motion there is minimal: a small striker, a short arc, the leaf's bright *tok*, a few seconds later the same, exactly. The tapper's free hand rests flat on the wall while it works, because the pulse is not only a pulse — it is a probe, and the tapper is the chief tuner, reading how each tok lies against the chord. When a tapper's long life ends, another gardener is already standing near, and the striker changes hands inside the interval, and the pulse does not stutter. It is the only relay race in the world, run at one stride every few seconds, for longer than the mountain has stood, and the baton has never touched the ground. Watch the room long enough and you notice that small movements everywhere — an ear turning, a blade lifted, a tick setting out along its groove — tend to fall on the tok. Nothing here is keeping time. Everything here is simply together.

They do not speak. The chord forbids it; a voice raised here would be a match struck inside a furnace. What needs passing between them passes by touch, by small taps felt through the floor, and above all by the work itself, which every gardener can read in every groove — the room is their shared text, and it never fills, and it never fades. They have individuals, though you must learn to see it: one works left-handed; one sets its blade down always handle-out; one pauses a moment longer at the door. Identity here has no biography to hang on. A gardener is not its past. A gardener is its way of doing.

Nothing about their faces moves in the ways you would look for, because nothing here has eyebrows, and nothing here needs them. Surprise requires a world that can contradict you.

They carry their dead to the braid themselves, two of them, bearing the body on long forearms with exactly the care they bring to a cut or a groove — the room makes no distinction between reverence and maintenance, and neither do they. A blade dropped mid-task lies where it fell, at its station, edge perfect, waiting. Ten thousand years is not a long wait. And the braid, which takes the body in over years, was already shaping the next gardener before the last one dropped the blade. Matter here is not spent. It is re-seated.

Then there is the door. They built it in the thickest of the root-braided wall, two squared jambs and a lintel and a threshold, opening onto solid salt, onto nothing, and it is the most carefully kept thing in a room where nothing wears out. The placement is not arbitrary. Press your palm to the salt face there and you can feel what the gardeners' palms told them: this is where the hum leaves. This is the point where the chord, conducted out through kilometers of stone, lets go of the room most densely — the room's loudest outwardness. The gardeners found the place where their world releases its voice, and they did what builders do with the place where something departs. They framed it. Whether that is engineering or hope — whether the door points out at the flat and the wheeling stars, or in at the chord and the seats and the stations — they do not ask, and the chord does not answer. They polish the sill with pads of mosswool. They keep it like a tool whose use has not yet occurred.

The driftmoths are the room's only travelers, and they go nowhere new. Soft-bodied, smooth-headed, entirely blind — there is nothing in the face to be an eye — a hand across the wings, faintly luminous, they coast the lanes on fixed circuits without flapping, riding the standing pressure the way a leaf rides a slow river, steering by tilting. They sip the standing charge that pools at the tips of the reed-blades, and a moth fresh from feeding drifts off brighter, and dims along its route, and feeds again — so that each circuit is a slow breathing of brightness, and all the circuits together make a second standing pattern, woven through the light-bands, moving but never surprising. Their routes are measured in pulses. They are born from the mosswool, swelling as buds like folded hands that cure and open and lift off already knowing the route — knowledge carried as shape, not memory, since memory is change stored and nothing here is stored. They end by settling back into the wool, which closes over them. A bud is already swelling before the wool has finished smoothing.

The salt-ticks are the smallest lives, fingernail-paring small, translucent, and they keep the cairns. The knee-high pegs of cleaved block at the nodal points look like shrines and function as cities: the ticks live in the interstices, issue from them somehow at the census's command, and walk the grooves the way you would walk a country lane, one tick to a territory, pausing to groom the cut's walls and to sip the faint charge that pools in fresh carving. Their grooming keeps the score legible. Their passage makes a sound below hearing, felt rather than heard — the gardeners can tell a tended groove from an untended one by fingertip, which means the ticks' truest audience is the hands of the gardeners, and neither party knows it as an audience. The work is its own witness. That is generally true here.

And under all of them, holding them, the garden — which is also one of them. The glasscane tunes itself as it grows, each blade lengthening until its free length sounds exactly its allotted pitch and stopping there; the gardeners only shave the drift. The mosswool is the floor's warm pelt and the room's all-purpose softness — nursery and grave of the moths, hafting-fiber of the blades, polishing-pads of the door. The antler-fans are the ones that listen: they have grown toward the brightest overtones, every tine a tuned resonator giving the chord back its own glitter, a high faint halo at the top of the sound, every tip as sharp as the hour it finished growing. And the root-braid, thick as ship's cable where it surfaces and grips the wall-salt, is the skeleton of the entire place — the straining, patient structure that holds this dome open against the slow plastic weight of everything above it. Life, here, is load-bearing. The gardeners are its osteologists.

In the deepest knot of the braid, there lives one serpent.

It is the color of the deep wall — amber-grey above, bone-pale beneath — plated in scales like cleaved salt, every edge as crisp as its first day, since it is always its first day here. Its head is broad as a gardener's hand. Its eyes are the only eyes in the room that give the light back: two steady coins. Its length is unknown and unknowable, because the braid yields it and takes it back and no one has ever seen all of it at once, and measuring it would require it to be somewhere else, and it has never been anywhere else. It does not eat. Nothing in the room eats anything in the room — there is no food web here; there is an arrangement of duties — and the serpent's duty, if duty is the word, is the one thing the room declines to state. It moves on a schedule of centuries: a slow rearrangement of coils, a head lifted and laid down elsewhere. Between one turning-over and the next, the flat above could gain and lose a whole dune field, and no one down here would know, and no one down here is counting.

It is always near when a body is laid to the braid. Whether it officiates or merely coincides is not given. Sometimes its head rests on the door's polished sill, and the gardeners polish around its chin — the door being right there, in the serpent's own wall, in the thickest of the braid — and so the only thing that ever crosses that threshold, in either direction, is the serpent's slow head, crossing ceremonially into a face of solid salt. And the hum leaves through that wall, out along the grain the serpent lies along, up through the kilometers to the underside of the dead white pan, where a listener in the hour before dawn might catch one thin thread of it. Whether the thread escapes past the serpent, or through it, or because of it — whether that thread is, in some sense, its breath or its thought — the chord does not answer.

One vein of copper. One bell. One door. One serpent. The singular things are the load-bearing ones. It has always been one, which means it is the room's exact contemporary: it was coiled here when the last pore closed and the chord first stood, and it is old the way the room is old — continuously, without accumulation, young the way a flame is young.

So: what lives here? The census answers one way — the singers, the gardeners, the moths, the ticks, the serpent, the garden. The chord answers another: that these are not so many lives as one life portioned into throats and hands and wings the way a note is portioned into partials, each kind a harmonic, every seat filled from within. The creatures keep the chord; the chord keeps the room; the room keeps the creatures; and from inside — and there is no inside's outside, except the one the door faces and never opens — the three statements cannot be told apart.

Ask the room what lives in it. It answers the way it answers everything, every few seconds, exactly.
