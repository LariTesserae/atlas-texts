You meet the inhabitants the way you meet everything here: late, and then again, faintly. This is the second rule of the place. Nothing introduces itself on time. A census is impossible — count the animals and you count their ghosts, every body trailed by its two most recent selves, every swarm dragging a crowd of its own immediate past. You do not see a gleaner. You see where a gleaner has been, and then, if it is still, where it is. The swarms move like weather because in some sense they are: a swarm is a smear on the schedule, a decision the light needs time to finish reporting.

And all of it is small. Nothing that moves here is longer than your little finger; the megafauna of this world would be lost in your palm. But then, everything else in this world is small too, and within the cup the proportions hold — reef density, nation density, a whole biosphere with the volume of a held breath.

The walls are inhabited the way a city is inhabited and the way a body is inhabited, and here those are the same statement. Each chimney, each flute, each fluted fan is one animal, rooted the way a tooth is rooted, extended into its own house the way a hand extends into a glove. The fans open their mantles to the steam and flush — rust-red, sulfur-pale, opal-white — and this is speech. It has to be brief. A long signal smears into its own echo and becomes illegible, so the grammar of the walls is staccato, aphoristic; they have evolved the short saying. Nothing here ages the way you age. A colony does not grow old; it thickens. Its generations are rings in the glass, and its dead are not buried but built in — the fans grow over a corpse within a day, and for a while the wall holds a small translucent silhouette, and then the glass thickens and the silhouette becomes strata, and the strata become the cup. The walls are cemetery and city and body, and those are one word here.

The nations of the water column turn out to be one nation wearing all its ages at once. They begin near the seam, half mineral, like ore that has made a decision — dense nurseries where the young are more geology than flesh. Life then drifts outward through the degrees, each degree of heat a year of it, cooling as it goes, until the quick many-legged adults patrol the cool rim where the gleaners hunt. And then, when the time comes, they swim back down the thermometer to the heat to seed it. A whole life is two journeys on a thermometer: geology becoming weather, weather becoming geology again. The predators know the layers the way you know orchards — what ripens where, and when.

The skaters live on the underside of the air. The surface skin is their entire country; their feet dimple the world but never break it. The skin is the cup's table — whatever the world drops, the tension catches, and the skaters eat: fallen motes, drowned steamlings, the husks of things. And then there are the ones above them, when there is an above them. When the second skin forms a hand's width over the pond, the ceiling is suddenly populated — pale swimmers, backs to the water below, feet toward the sky, the only inhabitants for whom the sky is underfoot. No one knows where they come from. The best guess is that they dry on the lip between ceilings, reduced to motes, waiting; the honest answer is that they live in the conditional tense, a nation that exists in the subjunctive, and to study them you would have to kneel for a year on glass that takes skin. They have no appointments, and so they are the only free people here — even the pond cannot schedule what is only sometimes.

The steam is inhabited too, all the way up. The plume-folk hatch in vapor and spend their whole lives falling upward, sipping the climbing droplets, and most of them end as export — the cup's one message to the world turns out to be full of the world, a migration delivered as weather. Above the rim there is an abroad: a loose population riding the plume into the cold, technically outside, entirely of the place. At the frost-line, where feathers of ice reach down into the updraft and melt mid-fall, some few are rained back home — the only immigrants are emigrants returned. The rest thin into the cloud deck and are gone, and no one comes back to say how far a steamling can go.

The gleaners are the ones you will watch longest. A gleaner is never empty-handed; to be a gleaner is to be holding something. They strike and press at the young glass until a shard comes away with an edge keen enough to cut a current, and then they carry it — always carry it; they sleep as they live, holding. The young ride the elders' backs and learn the flake-strike before they are large enough to hold a tool, and a young gleaner's first shard is a chip from its elder's. This means something when you think about it: every shard in the cup is a fragment of an older shard, lineage split from lineage, and it may be that all the tools here are one tool, handed down and divided like a family, like a river. You can read a shard's age in its polish, the way you read hands. The eldest gleaner — small, dark, unhurried — carries what may be the parent-edge of them all: its spine rounded like a river stone by generations of grip, its edge fresh, renewed by many mouths. It is the oldest object in the youngest place, and it has never once been set down. There is no archaeology here except what is carried. The past is either glass or grip.

Among the hunters, the bell-diver practices the most elegant technology in the world and behaves like its most private citizen. It gathers its condensate bubble at the surface, settles it over its breathing pores, and goes down through the degrees like a rumor passing through the nations, carrying its own sky with it, wobbling like a held note. It goes deeper than hunting requires. It goes to the seam, and hangs there, watching the rock be undecided, watching the dull light move slow as thought. Then it surfaces slowly, and for a while afterward it does not hunt. Whatever it sees down there, it keeps. The diver is the only inhabitant who goes to look, and the only one that does not signal what it has seen — a monastery of a few, an order with no rule except descent.

The hunters, all of them, hunt appointments rather than prey. They crouch at the margin and read the pond's shallow prophecy — it knows the next few minutes the way you know the next few feet of road — and strike where the prey is going to be. The prey, in turn, have learned to lie to the water. A swarm thickens time around itself until it is a smear no appointment can pin; a lone swimmer will feed the pond a false itinerary, a lean and a half-turn held just long enough for the future to take dictation, then reversed. The eldest hunters are the ones who have learned the pond is sometimes lying; they strike a half-breath off the appointment, or at the echo, or at nothing — and something arrives. And every few minutes all of it stops. The seam exhales, the pond rises, the swarms sink like silt, and every hunter surfaces in the same held pause. No one hunts the breath of the world. The pause has a direction, and the direction is the seam, and everything in the cup keeps time by its tick the way a town keeps time by a bell — the one punctual voice, the only drum.

For two hours a day the sun stands in the notch, and then comes the parliament. Every colony flares at once, a silent shouting in mineral color, and this is not celebration — or not only celebration. It is survey. The map grows overnight, measurably, every night, and so the borders must be re-declared daily, each fan and chimney asserting its holding in brief bright words: a country that must re-found itself every day at noon, law re-enacted in light because the ground itself will not hold still.

Evening is when the dead return, on schedule. The lag deepens in the dimness until an hour's worth of the day stands layered translucent over the darkening glass, and the morning's dead move through it, faint, finishing their rounds. The young startle at them. The old ignore them. The hunters sit at the margin and review their misses — the cup replays the day and calls it nothing, but it is the only history here that is not mineral. Memory is a public service in this place; the walls remember, the evenings remember, and so the animals are allowed to be new. Their culture has no architecture and their history has no effort. Sleep comes staggered, like everything else, half a breath off; the fans dim to an ember grammar; the diver goes down at night, through the day's crowded ghosts, to the undecided rock.

If you came asking whether anyone lives here — anyone singular, anyone who would answer to a name — the answer is: everything, briefly, and one thing, slowly. The many are easy to see once your eyes learn the lag. The one takes longer. Consider that the walls are a skin grown by the skin, that the pond is a humor, the steam a breath, the swarms a warmth moving through a body, the gleaners its fingers, the fans its lungs, the tick its heart, the diver its attention. And consider the seam, where the light moves slow as thought and the rock has not yet decided: the youngest inhabitant, the only one not yet born, around whom everything else is scaffolding. The mountain is writing a paragraph, and the paragraph is alive, and it does not know it is being read.

The inhabitants are the architects of the ending, and they go on building. Layer by layer they glaze the sky away, the pond shrinking, the walls thickening, the fold tending toward the day it closes into a heart of warm stone with the simmer inside it. But here is what the sealing means that the stone does not say: when the last of the sky is gone, the flares will not go out. They will become the light by which the world is seen. The colonies' colors, at last, will be daylight; the nations will stack in the warm dark and live by one another's signaling; the cup is laying in a sun, cell by cell, mantle by mantle. The egg will not be dark. The egg will be lit from inside by the only sun that ever agreed to live there.

For now, though, there is still weather, and the steam still leaves, and under the lip the eldest gleaner sleeps tucked into a hollow of warm sinter, the old shard in her grip, the parent-edge, the carried archive. Her echo is still arriving — the place finishing her sleep a half-breath behind her, reluctant, as it is about everything, to let anything happen only once. In the morning the light will stand in the cup and the parliament will convene and the swarms will climb the degrees and the diver will go down, and she will wake, and still be holding it.
