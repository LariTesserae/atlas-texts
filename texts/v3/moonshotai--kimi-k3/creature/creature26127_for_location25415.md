The felt was here first.

Before the first snail, the first eel, the first swimming thing of any kind, there was the grey-gold film, colonizing the fresh-blasted rams while the stone was still warm — a chemistry that declined to stop. It has not stopped since. Ask whether the felt on this ram's crown is the same felt as on that ram's flank across the floor and you are asking a real question: the film may be a single tissue, one slow body worn by the whole basin, older than everything else here by a margin of everything. It is not much to look at. It is everything to live on.

And it is the engine's instrument panel. The felt feeds *ahead* of the stroke. An hour or two before a ram fires, the film on that particular crown thickens and brightens, grazing the first chemistry leaking up past the seals, warming from slate-gold toward something you can only call luminous. At dusk, when the basin goes the color of lead, you can see it happening: here a crown going softly golden, there another, out across the dark floor — the tarn lighting its own lamps, forecasting itself. Everything that lives here reads that light. It is the first signal, and all the others follow it.

The rind-snails read it from a body's length away. They spend their whole lives at arm's length from the felt, and they wear the water's crust even as they scrape it, so every snail is a layered archive: each band of shell a season of the great gyre, each band's thickness a record of bloom and want. You could read a snail like a core sample — count its years in booms, if you had the patience and a lens. No one does. The tarn keeps its diary in shell and lets the diary walk around unread. When the felt on their ram begins to golden, the snails come down off the crown and clamp to the flank, sealing themselves to the stone; the shockwave that would strip a loose snail into the swarm breaks over them like weather. Afterward they climb back up and resume their spirals — harvest-lines fine as pencil work, overwritten and rewritten for centuries.

Down on the floor, in the fallout of everything, walk the sextons. Nobody named them; name them now. Dog-sized, armored in hexagonal plates, moving on their many deliberate legs at the speed of erosion. They are the floor's keepers and its pallbearers: what dies upwater — burst krill, drowned skaters, spent snails — sifts down, and the sextons gather it, unhurried, polishing the stone in their going. They are deaf to everything but the boom. Understand what that means: their world contains exactly one event, repeated. But listen closer. The boom strikes their plates and the plates *ring*, each animal at its own pitch, and in the seconds after a stroke the whole floor choruses faintly, a chord of sextons dying away. That narrow band is all they can hear, and so it is their entire society: everything a sexton ever says to another sexton is said in the after-ring. Old ones are written over completely — every stroke they have stood through has left its hairline in their armor, healed over, gone opaline. They are the tarn's memory, legible to no one, not even each other. And when one dies, which is rare, the shell stays where it falls. The tarn permits no rival structures, but it permits its own dead: within a year the empty armor is felted over, settled on, inhabited — the only architecture in the basin, grown in the shape of a citizen.

The midwater belongs to the swarms, and the swarms belong, in a way, to the charge. Those copper filaments visible through the glass are not just organs; they are aerials. The swarm rides the basin's slow electrical breathing the way a gull rides wind, and because every chamber's days-long charge-up bends the field, the shape of the swarm encodes the state of the engine. The shadow-script that slides across the rams at noon is not noise. It is a pressure map written in bodies, and it can be read — the eels read it, the skaters read its edges, and if you sat patient for a season you could read it too: which rams are loaded, which are spent, where tomorrow's weather is gathering. Molting is the swarm's great errand. A krill grown ripe must find a hammer, so above every loaded chamber a queue forms, a slow carousel of the ripe waiting for their percussion. The boom comes; the soft crackle of ten thousand shells splitting, like applause in another room; and always some shells burst instead of cracking — the engine's tax — and the plume that feeds the eels carries these casualties down to the sextons. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is even dropped. They are not dainty feeders, for all their glass: lower a hand and they will taste it, first a tickle, then a purposefulness that sends the hand back out of the water. And some of them, at the full stroke, do not molt at all. They settle. They cement themselves head-down into the felt and become something fixed — a budding glass bell, filtering the upwash for the rest of their lives. The flanks of the older rams are hung with these gardens, between the snails' spirals.

On the skin ride the skaters, and the shortest lives in the basin ride the slowest water. They are the surest prophets the tarn has: seconds before a stroke they feel the dimple forming under the meniscus, and the fleet opens a smooth vacancy over the crown about to rise. Watch for the vacancy. It is the last signal before the boom. The ring-waves that follow capsize a few of every fleet, and a wetted skater cannot stay wet — the charged water strips its wax, it sinks, and the swarm rises to meet it. The boom feeds the midwater with the surface, and the fleet closes over the gap within the minute, indistinguishable, replaced. In winter they encyst by the thousand in the slush of the ice collar, and the collar's underside, bathed in the rime-laying water, grows stone feathers over them while they wait.

In the sockets live the eels, and the sockets are not their shelter but their marriage. Each eel is wed to one ram, for life, and the life is long. Its body grows to the socket's spiral and wears the socket's scoring. It feels the chamber charging beneath it as a rising fullness — to the eel, pressure is a hum and the hum is a calendar; it knows its ram's period to the hour, and in the last hours it climbs to the socket mouth and waits there, streamlined, attentive. The stroke, for the eel, is not violence. It is breath. Up it rides, mouth open, straining the fresh bloom and snapping the stunned krill tumbling in the plume, and then it pours back down the settling column into the dark, into the days of quiet. The quiet is not idleness: between strokes the eel keeps its house, mouthing silt off the chamber walls, worrying rime out of the fissures. A ram with an eel silts more slowly, though neither of them knows it. The engine has a maintenance crew it never hired. Only at the full stroke do eels leave home — the adults loosen, the young threads that have drifted midwater through all their long juvenile years pour down into whatever sockets stand open, and the great reshuffle settles the basin's marriages for another dozen years. A ram that seizes between full strokes is a small tragedy with no audience: the eel stays, and waits, and dwindles, riding out a silence that used to be a heartbeat.

Below the sockets the eels do not go, and yet the throats are not empty. Down where the chambers charge, in heat and gas, stream mats of filaments — the felt's deep sister, anaerobic, feeding on the gas itself and, in feeding, pacing it: bleeding a chamber here, throttling one there. Life has its hand on the engine's throttle. The machine cultivates the felt, and the felt's dark root cultivates the machine. Below that, at last, there is nothing — only the water's own making, hot chemistry arranging itself in the dark. Even the tarn has a cellar it keeps no life in.

The waterline is absolute. Above it, nothing: the sharp bare walls, the leaning snowfields, not a blade, not a stain. The tarn denies outside flesh. A bird that lands on the brim drinks once, tastes pennies and current, and does not land again; what it might catch here would plate its gut with stone. There are no fish, and have never been — gills from elsewhere foul in a day. Everything alive in this water was born to the charge and the mineral and is found nowhere else on the mountain or off it. The tarn is an island, and its surrounding sea is incompatibility. Its only import, besides weather, is accident — windblown insects off the bare slopes, gleaned from the surface in minutes. Its only export, besides the one white thread of overflow, is husks: spent krill-shells washing over the notch, riming as they go, so that somewhere far below, a stream no one follows lays little glass ghosts on its stones, and that is the only rumor of the engine the valleys will ever receive.

Watch the whole community perform a single stroke, once, in order. First the felt goldenizes. Then the eel climbs to the socket mouth. The snails come down off the crown and clamp. The swarm overhead tightens into its bracing pattern, ripe molters to the front. The skaters open their vacancy on the skin. And then — this is the thing no one believes until they have sat still long enough to feel it — the basin goes quiet. The clicking stops. The bubbles seem to wait. For ten heartbeats, perhaps, the Ram Tarn holds its breath: every animal in it still, every filament aimed at one crown of stone. Then the boom. The applause of molts. The plume, the feeding, the long mineral *thock* of the ram going home. Then the clicking resumes, like conversation in a room after thunder, and the next chamber begins its days of charging. The ram only plays the drum. The orchestra is everything else.

Once in a dozen years the orchestra gets its great downbeat, and the basin has been waiting for it with a patience that has bodies: sextons grown ripe years ago, holding; eel-threads keeping their juvenile slimness long past any decent age; the felt stocked thick on every crown. For once the hush is basin-wide and very long — the whole floor goldening at once, from above, like watching a city come lit — and then the world rings. The sextons' after-ring, which is all the society they have, lasts minutes instead of seconds: the only full conversation their kind ever gets, and they mate inside it, in the ringing, on a floor still jumping with aftershocks. The sockets open; the reshuffle pours; the settled found new gardens on every crown. Everything that had been queuing is admitted at once.

It will not last, and the ending already has its witnesses chosen. As the chambers silt, the booms will thin — from every few minutes to the hour, to the day, to the week. The swarms will wait longer for their hammers; the felt will dim; the eels will dwindle in their darkening rooms. Long after the swarms are gone, out on the stilling floor, the sextons will still be walking — animals that can hear only one sound, waiting for it, once a month, once a year, faithful as clocks. The last boom this mountain makes will not go unheard. It will be heard by the creature that can hear nothing else.

That is millennia away, and this is noon. The shafts are coming down white and vertical; the script is sliding across the rams; somewhere out on the floor a crown has begun to golden, and an eel is climbing to the mouth of its socket to wait.

So — what lives in the Ram Tarn? Not residents, exactly. Parts. Skin and blood and pulse-keepers, memory walking the floor, quick nerves skating the surface, everything coupled so tightly to the one beat that biology and plumbing are the same word here. Ask again, and answer truly: the Ram Tarn lives in the Ram Tarn. It is the only thing that ever has.

Wait at the brim a little longer. Watch for the vacancy opening in the fleet. Count the held breath. Your pulse already knows the tempo; it has been keeping it since you crossed the shoulder. Everything is still. Everything is aimed.

Now.
