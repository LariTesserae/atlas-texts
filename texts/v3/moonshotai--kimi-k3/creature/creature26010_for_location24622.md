You hear the people of the Spread before you see them, too — voices pitched to carry over crushers and cable-song, because everyone here learns young to speak against the Note and never entirely unlearns it. Visitors think them brusque, loud, a little hard of hearing. They are right about the hearing. The years take the top off it one winter at a time, and the same people who shouted across the gorge at twenty go soft by sixty — noise-soft, they say here, without pity, because softness is the making of them. The softest become the deep listeners, the ones paid to put an ear against a fresh-cut face and say what the stone remembers. Deafness is not a loss in the Spread. It is a promotion. And it is the deep listeners who know the city's best secret: in the oldest halls, in the small hours when the works idle, cured stone gives back the Note as it was — a semitone higher, sometimes two, the mountain's voice from before you were born. The stone remembers the mountain younger. Grandparents hum the Note to children and say *you're sharp*, and the children don't believe them, and the children are wrong.

They are a people of margins and annotations, and it is not a temperament, it is a survival strategy. In a city where the ground rises a finger's width a year, nothing is trusted to memory — memory is fresh stone, it plays back at odd hours and not always accurately — so they write everything down. Wedges penciled with dates. Maps redrawn until the paper goes thick. The Ledger of Distances. The Catalogue. A child here learns letters off a doorsill, reading the wedge-stones up the years like growth marks, and the first time you are tall enough to reach the sill and trusted to write the year yourself — that is a rite, quiet and unceremonious, the way everything important is done. Quarreling families are hushed with *the stone is listening*. Nobody teaches their children to be brave near a ripe slope, or loud near a curing wall; you do not want your fear played back to strangers for a decade. Courtesy and engineering have become the same subject.

The trades braid through everything. The cutters work the green seams in pairs with the listeners — one to open the stone, one to say what is inside it before the saw gets there, because a cast has a particular hollowness and you do not cut through a signature. The watershed clerks are part surveyor, part mourner, part diplomat; they keep an archive of old water, maps of every stream the mountain has ever changed its mind about, because a fissure that swallows a gorge in a night may close again in thirty years, and they speak of vanished streams the way other people speak of friends who moved away — fondly, and without quite giving up on a letter. The veil-holders inherit their fog-nets like vineyards and litigate like glaciers: a ridge that rises throws a fog-shadow, and a family can spend two generations winning back a harvest for grandchildren not yet born. The soil-folk are the closest thing to aristocracy — trays and beds inherited, itemized in wills, a family's century held in a few barrow-loads of black. Steal ore and you can make restitution. Steal a tray of soil and you have stolen a hundred years, and there is no returning that inside one lifetime. And the engine-keeper down in the lower gorge, tending a beam that takes a full minute to complete a stroke, will give you the local catechism if you ask why nothing faster has ever replaced it: *a minute is the correct length of a thought.*

The herds belong to no one, or to the inspector, depending on which herder you ask and how late it is. Ask where they came from and the herders shrug upslope, toward the Line, and say the herds came down before the Line was drawn — out of the unfinished part of the world — which is why nothing here unsettles them, not the trams, not the wire-glow, not the wet cough of a new fissure. They are the only residents entirely at home in a rough draft. They give milk and shag — the shag is combed and felted and ends up as half the shims in the Spread, so the herds are literally under every level floor in the city — and the herders read the luminous muzzles the way sailors read sky, telling grazing ground and health by the color of the glow. They graze the clock-lichen, which is the calendar, so a good herder learns the whole art in one sentence: *graze yesterday, spare tomorrow.* At night they low in long held chords pitched against the Note, and old herders swear the chords have deepened with it over their lifetimes. The dynamo-keepers blame the wind. The argument is older than anyone having it, which here counts as a form of affection.

The wirebirds were a skull first. For sixty years the Catalogue held a skull no bird owned, a beak no living thing carried, and then the mist opened over the eastern spans and they came out of it by the thousand and never left. Now they nest in every rivet-hole, and the tenders schedule their work around the clutches, and the guano rights under each pylon are deeded and inherited like the fog-rights, and on a still day a pylon full of wirebirds mimicking the crushers sounds exactly like a works two gorges over — the only people never fooled are the noise-soft, who hear the difference the way you hear a quote. When the long-interval bells ring — for a Load-Taking, for the close of a Reckoning, for a confirmed arrival — the wirebirds stop. All of them, all at once, a pylon going quiet like a works shutting down, for exactly the length of the bell's decay. Nobody taught them. Nobody here is sure anyone could have.

The moths are livestock that no one herds. Hand-sized, the color of the overcast, they come up out of the cloud deck at dusk and give themselves to the arc lamps all night, and by dawn they lie in soft drifts, and the Sweep goes along the lamp-strings with wide soft brooms and gathers them — dust and wing and patience — into the soil trays. Most of the soil in the Spread is moths. Say that to a gardener here and watch them not disagree. This is the real reason no one kills a lamp-line at night: it is a rule with three reasons, for the traveler and for the herds that walk the lines and for the harvest, and everyone knows all three and cites a different one, and a rule here is never only one rule. The tray-gardens are planted in the lightfall beneath the lamps on purpose, catching moths and light at once, so the city's fields are its streetlights, and its farmers keep the lamps, and its lamplighters are, without ever saying so, the most important people in the Spread after dark.

Over all of it, in the warm dynamo hall, sits the Catalogue and its keeper. The casts are shelved in the annex where the curing heat keeps them dry; rubbings and tracings go into the ledger, four hundred years of pages, each entry a shape and a date and a name — because the finder's other duty is to name the thing that is coming, so that when it finally walks out of the mist fifty years later, it is met by its name. Everything here is met by its name eventually. The keeper's job is mostly waiting. Keepers train successors for matches they will not live to see; a whole career may come down to one word in a margin, *matched*, in a handwriting trained for decades toward the writing of it. The pale soft-shelled thing from the east gorge — matched forty years after its casting — lives under the floor of the crushing works still, and the night shift feeds it moths, though nobody ever decided to; it simply became customary, the way everything here becomes customary: slowly, then forever. The fern grows in one drip-cave and is doing well and no one picks it. Nothing has ever arrived that needed harming, and the people take this as a kind of promise and try to be worthy of it. And nothing uncatalogued has ever walked out — *yet*, the keepers say, because the book has never once been wrong, and that is, on consideration, the most frightening thing about it.

The fish is the only citizen of the Spread that everyone has met and no one has seen. Its mold sits on its shelf in the warm hall, patient, exact, scheduled, and children are told they may live to see it arrive, and the old keepers know they won't, and both are considered good things to know. There are tarn-watchers now — volunteers with notebooks at the undecided basins — because the fish will need water, and the water isn't finished, and the watershed clerks and the fish-watchers have come to hold overlapping maps and an overlapping temperament. The clerks reroute the grid. The watchers simply reroute their patience.

And then there is the first entry. The inspector arrived before the custom of casts was understood — no mold preceded it, and none has ever been found, though the keepers still look, and do not say aloud which they hope for: that somewhere in the stone there is a page for it, or that it is the one thing the mountain did not plan. The Catalogue exists because of it. The city was surprised once, four hundred years ago, and resolved never to be surprised again, and has been keeping the resolution ever since. It is pony-sized and low and long, and it is the one silent thing in the loudest place in the world — the deep listeners have checked the walls along its paths, and the stone keeps no record of it. You know it is near because the wirebirds stop, a pylon at a time, like a works shutting down. The herds are unbothered — the herds are unbothered by everything — but they make way for it, calmly, the way you step aside for weather, and the herders say the herds have always had a herder, and the human kind are new, and know it. On winter nights it comes down through the streets behind the herds, and it pauses at doorsills and looks at the wedge-stones, one by one, dated and penciled, and no one knows whether it is reading, and no one asks. You step off the path. You do not meet its eyes. What happens if you do is the one thing this city does not write down, and in a city that writes down everything, the omission is the loudest entry in the book. In the morning its tracks cross the frost where the whole street stepped aside: two hands pressed together, again and again — a signature in frost, to match the signatures in stone.

Above them all, nobody lives. The High Seam crews work the richest green stone in shifts kept short on purpose; they come down before dark and they do not sleep there, and each morning each of them reads the log they wrote the night before, to sort which memories are theirs. They come down with stone and with tools rusted through decades in a season, and sometimes with small memories of things that haven't happened yet — a rain, a face, a bell — and there is a drawer in the dynamo hall, unlabeled, where these are written down, and the watershed clerks check it when the weather turns, and it is right often enough that nobody discusses it. You can tell a crew member years later. Line-eyed, the Spread says: they check distances twice, they touch cairns as they pass, and when their rotation ends they take rooms on the ground floor near the dynamos, where the Note comes up strongest through the floor, to be hummed back into place.

Because everything above is tenant. The resident is the range. It grows a finger's width a year and deepens its voice a semitone a generation; it keeps sound and gives it back; it signs its seams and keeps its schedule and is still deciding where the water goes. The Spread is not its master and not, exactly, its worshipper — worship would imply the work is finished, and nothing here is finished; that is the entire point. The city is its clerks. A whole constellation of people taking dictation from an author that revises, logging the wedges and the distances and the arrivals, keeping the lamps lit for the moths and the book open for the fish.

At night, from a height, you can see the whole arrangement: the lamp-strings trembling with wire-glow, the herds drifting through the dark intervals with their faint lit faces, the wirebirds asleep and still ticking like idling works, the keeper turning one more page in the warm hall, fresh tracks in the frost where a street stepped aside. The Note holds under it all, lower than your grandfather heard it. And on its shelf the fish waits — patient, exact, scheduled — the next resident, due whenever the mountain finishes the water. It will be met by its name. Everything here is, eventually. That is what it means to live in the Spread: you live at the pace of the revision, and you keep the books, and you are, all of you, still being written.
