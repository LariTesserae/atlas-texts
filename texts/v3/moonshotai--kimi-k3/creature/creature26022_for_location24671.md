Ask who lives on the bench and you will get three answers, and they do not agree, and all of them are true.

The first count is the easy one — the tenancy, the lives that show themselves. The mottlebacks are the most of it. Travelers count them for something to do in the long clear afternoons, and the count never comes out different, down to the lamb, for there is always one lamb a day old and always one ewe heavy with the next, and somewhere at the bench's far edge an old one is always lying down in the grass and not getting up. By morning the grass over that spot has risen, and the bones have been taken down into it, and the herd is the herd again. At dusk they climb the gable stairs in file, one behind another, and stand on the rooftops against the sky; at first light the stairs tick with hooves coming down, and the sound is the nearest the town ever comes to the noise of its own building — a faint echo of masonry, repeated daily, in a place where masonry stopped. Their shed wool never litters the paths. The wrens take it for their linings. Even the molting is employed.

The day is portioned out among the rest like an inheritance. First light belongs to the whistlers, who sing the sun up over the same notch while the frost ticks its answer and lets go of the stones. Midmorning belongs to the hummingbirds, whose quarrels over the rosettes are the bench's only arguments — formal, bloodless, adjourned each dusk, resumed each dawn. At night they hang torpid in the lichen beards, and the frost takes them and gives them back, as it does the stones. The quarter-hour of dusk rain belongs to the ducks: they drop from the reservoir at the first drops, a quarter-hour of small dark traffic in the runnels, the town's one rush hour, and then they rise all together and beat back up over the roofs. Dark belongs to the frogs. Every cup-pool and bath-pool has its one, posted; every cistern has a frog, or the echo of one — with the drip repeating off stone, it can be hard to tell.

After full dark the housekeeping comes on shift, and it works in guild order. The foxes first — the doorkeepers take the first watch — then the beetles in their kinds, then the patient fungi. A carcass at midnight is a fact; at first light it is a memory the grass has already risen over. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is late. Even the dead are punctual here.

The moss holds its borders like a garrison because that is what the whole bench is: a pass held against time, every living thing posted, no reliefs, no desertions. The dark wet line on the cistern walls is the garrison's clerk, writing the same word every night in wet ink. One frog per basin. Wrens on every third lintel. The arithmetic is exact and it is enforced.

Almost.

The condor is on permanent leave — a citizen of the sky, and the sky never signed the arrangement. It alone crosses the rim as it pleases, ranging down over the valleys and coming back with other weather in its feathers, the only news the bench receives. When the wind shifts at dusk and the bell gives its one note, the condor shifts on the tower's shoulder and resettles: a sentinel acknowledging a watch it does not keep.

And the cat. Wide, smoke-colored, seen at dusk and mostly not seen — the only irregular verb in the town's grammar. It keeps no hour and no post. It is seen most often in the quarry, seated on the half-detached block like a foreman who stayed after the whistle, the only resident ever found near the place where the place tells on itself. It is unimpressed by imports. It saw the scarf arrive bright; it watched the boot walk in new. Everything else on the bench is content; the cat is bored — and boredom is proof of memory, and memory is proof of time, so the cat, like the moon, keeps its own time here. It is the town's only historian, and it tells no one. On a traveler's first dusk it watches from far off, the way a customs house watches. Old travelers say that on your sixth dusk it walks with you, and whether that is escort or herding, no one says.

The second count is the houses. Every third one: something sleeping. The doorways stand open — every doorway on the bench stands open — and inside there is no dust, because dust is time's sediment and the bench has no sediment. There is a smell of lanolin and cold stone and the mineral dark of cisterns. There is breath, very slow. Count it against the cistern drip: one breath to thirty drips. The wool covers are sound as the day they were loomed, and there is a shape beneath them, and no one describes the shape — travelers agree on this without ever agreeing to — because those who watch long enough to describe it have discovered that watching is a way of staying.

Who they are depends on who you ask, late, by the fire. Some say the builders: that the first morning caught the town at the edge of waking and kept it there, and the sleepers are resting before a workday that will not come. Some say they are travelers, kept. Some say there is no longer any difference — that after enough mornings everyone looks like a resident. The thirds are exact. And then there are the other houses, the empty ones: swept, open, a bowl on the table, the bed turned down, standing the way the next course of stones stands laid out at the foot of the unfinished wall. In order. Ready. The mason's plan is still legible, and the plan includes vacancies.

This is how a traveler becomes countable. You eat the grain, and the jar refills; you drink the cistern water; you breathe the mist. The first night, small wounds close — that is the rough draft. Then softer things. Scars lighten. An old ache lets go. Grief loosens like a knot in wet rope. You sleep deeply and dream of the mist standing in the fold, and on the fifth morning you know where the runnels will thin by midday, and on the sixth evening you know the bell will give two notes. On the seventh morning, old custom says, be below the rim before the cloud sinks. No one says why, because the ones who could say are asleep in the houses, and the ones who left in time only felt it as weather. What the old travelers will say, softly, is this: that it mends you the way it mends the turf. That you should not learn the breath in the houses, because it learns yours back. That it is kind — and that this is the whole of the danger. Nothing on the bench wants you. Wanting would be change. It is ready for you, which is worse.

There is a fourth count, short, of the dead: the imports. The tin cup rusting honestly, the scarf faded to a ghost of stripes, the boot gone soft — the bench's only graveyard, its only permitted corpses, the only faces allowed to age. And the dozen names scratched in the lintels across the centuries: those are not the dead. Those are the registry of the escaped. No one who stayed ever scratched a name. Names are for the leaving.

Or count it as one.

The strata are the bones, and the runnels are the veins — the town keeps its second skeleton dressed. The moss is the skin, holding its edge. The herds are the breath, rising and falling over the terraces. The frogs are the pulse in the dark. The fire in the meeting-house is the heart, blue-hearted, unfed, steady — the one resident that has been awake for the whole of the first morning, and if anything remembers the builders standing, it does, and fire gives no testimony, only warmth. The bell is the voice, clearing its throat once at the wind-shift. The sleepers are the dreams. The cat is the one restless thought that will not lie down. The condor is the gaze that wanders out over the rim. And the travelers — the travelers are the work in hand. A bench is where work is done. It keeps what it finishes.

So the dusk census comes out like this. Herds in the lee of walls that have never weathered. Ducks flown up. Frogs beginning. One fox on the meeting-house steps. The cat uncounted, uncountable. The fire low. In the third houses, the slow arithmetic of breath, one to thirty drips. One note from the tower; the condor resettles; the reservoir takes the stars and keeps its own ledger.

The town is fully inhabited. Every doorway is open, and one house stands swept and ready, the covers turned down, the way the squared blocks wait beside the wall. It has been ready since the first morning.

It is always the first morning. And the morning is patient, and patience is the only appetite the bench allows itself.
