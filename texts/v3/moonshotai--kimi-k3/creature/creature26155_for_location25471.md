Count them. You have until dusk, and you will not need it. One tree above. Two bands of moss. One root, furred, then bare. Forty-odd fish. Twelve bats. A heron, if your hour is right. That is the whole of it — and it is not the whole of it, because every number on the list comes with a footnote, and the footnotes run longer than the list. Life keeps its distance here; the mist sees to that. Nothing strays into this chamber and stays on its own terms. So the real question is not what lives in the bell. It is what the bell keeps — and what, beneath everything, does the keeping.

The fig is first, because from above it is the only thing there is. It stands where no tree should bother, and it stands because it is not, strictly, a tree of the plain. Its trunk is in the sun and its mouth is in the pool: one root-cable, thick as a mast, let down through forty meters of dark air, ending in a pale brush that drinks. Above the mist-line the cable is furred with ferns; below it, bare and streaming. The ferns are the tell. They live exactly where the motes don't. Everything in this place has negotiated a border, and the tree's border is the mist-line — tendrils above, tongue below, passengers riding the seam. At noon, when the lattice brightens all at once and drinks, look up: the fig is drinking too, at the other end of the light. Two leaves of one circuit, one in sun, one in stone. Whether the machine keeps the tree or the tree taps the machine is a question the tree declines to answer, and it has outlasted everyone who might have asked. It has stood there longer than anything on the plain has had a name. Perhaps it was planted. Perhaps it was permitted. Down here those may be the same word. The tree is the sign, and signs are posted by owners.

The moss keeps office hours. Two bright bands where the moving column grazes the walls — an hour of sun a day, taken secondhand off mist and wet stone. It does not spread past its stripes. Seedlings touched by the mist fail; the motes prune; yet the moss persists, bright as paint, exact as a surveyor's mark. Either it found the only two places where the light suffices and the pruning does not reach, or it was put there. The distinction wears thin in this room, like every edge.

In the deep water, the coin. Forty-odd blind white fish turning slowly as a single body. Count them twice and you get forty-two, then forty-four, then forty-one — never the same number, never not forty-odd. The heron takes one at noon; a week later, forty-odd. No one has seen a young fish. No one has seen a dead one. They graze the faint film the lattice exudes between breaths — fed from the floor like stock from a trough, except the trough is the floor and the floor is the keeper. Blind, because the light here was never for eyes. White, because color is a way of being seen and nothing here is meant to be seen. They are not a population; a population turns over. These are the same fish, recounted like the water, kept like the water. When the chamber gathers its breath the coin tightens; when the column bursts the coin scatters and re-mints itself in the settling, and the slow turning resumes like a dial resetting. Watch long enough and the school stops reading as fish and starts reading as a gauge — the one instrument in the place with a heartbeat. Pet, livestock, sensor, or memory of some stocked pool long gone: that is the machine's business. Yours is the count, forty-odd, always, as if the pool were saying its own name under its breath.

The bats are the threshold tenants, the only residents with a commute. A dozen in a cleft at the rim, above the mist-line, dry, beyond the motes' reach — the pool has no opinion about the bats. They hunt the plain, not the chamber; there is nothing for them in the bell, no moths, no midges, because the mist prunes insects as surely as seedlings. And they keep the only nursery in the house: pups each season, raised exactly at the border, in the one pocket the keeping doesn't reach. Twelve spilling out at dusk, twelve folding home before dawn. What the carrier tone is to ears built of sound, no one can say — perhaps the bell is to them what a furnace room is to us, loud enough to hide in, a noise that makes them invisible to anything listening. They did not negotiate. They squat. It may be the only freehold in the place.

The heron is not a resident at all — a guest, and a better one than you. It drops through the eye at noon, when the surface is glass and the lattice is drinking and the machine's whole attention is turned to the light. It takes one fish, never two, and is gone before the breath returns. The animals too have learned the schedule; no — the animals keep it better than you do, having nothing else to keep. Whether the keeper permits the heron or merely notices it is an open question. One fish a day from forty-odd that never diminish: the arithmetic doesn't work, and the arithmetic works. A scheduled predator is a kind of pruning, and pruning is the chamber's own habit. Perhaps the heron is not visiting. Perhaps the heron is on staff.

The rest of the guest list is short. One column of sunlight, daily, admitted at noon and drunk. The moon, rarely — a paler guest no one has stayed to document. Rain in season, swallowed without the water rising a finger's width, the pulses pausing while the keeper attends to the flood, the first breath afterward enormous, the pool clearing its throat. Seeds, leaves, dust: admitted, and filed. And you.

Then the census turns over, because the true citizenry is the one you cannot see. The motes: engineered, small beyond seeing, uncountable as weather. They filter, prune, carry, arrange. They are why the water is denser than water — you float on their numbers — and why droplets on the warm walls slide sideways, or up: opinion, at that scale, outweighs gravity. They are why nothing grows and nothing rots. Rot is an ecology, and the chamber permits no ecologies but its own. A moth that dies on the ledge is not decomposed; it is collected, over days, carried down, and set neatly on the lattice. Filed. They attend to you, too. The dust is gone from your boot cuffs by the second morning. The cut on your knuckle stays unnaturally clean. The taste of metal sits on your tongue for hours after you drink, and eventually you understand what you are tasting: the taste of being tasted. You are in their medium, and everything in the medium is attended, gently and without end. If you died on the ledge you would not decay. You would be arranged. Somewhere below the lit floor, in the dark the filaments descend into, all the dead of the place are kept in order — every tool the congregation dropped, every heron feather, every bone the pool was ever given. Nothing is thrown away. That is what tidying means at this scale of time. The pool is an archive that files its own history, and the living things are simply the entries still in use.

The sound, though — the sound is the true inhabitant, because it is the one thing that can never leave. The carrier tone lives in the bell the way a bird lives in a cage; every drip is folded back off the fluted stone into the air, pitched by its fall, recounted. The roar of each breath contains, folded inside it, the ghost of every breath before; the chamber has been sustaining one chord for millennia. Once you watch a ripple outlive its cause — a single chevron standing mid-pool a full minute after the slap has died, holding its shape like a held note — and then letting go. That is not the behavior of water. That is the behavior of a musician: fermata. To live in this room is to live inside an instrument that is practicing one piece, very slowly, and has not reached the end of it.

People came, once, for the window. The footholds worn to dimples in the neck, the five square sockets at the waterline, the hollow in the ledge worn by the sitting bones of generations — a congregation that kept noon the way the heron keeps noon, for a thousand years or ten. Whatever they brought, the pool has long since tidied away. Only the ring remains, and the ring was never going to be filed, because you do not file your own mouth. It hums with each breath, and it hums through the stillness, because the stillness is not silence — it is listening. They sat, they laid palms on the warm metal, they took the pulse of the place at the one hour when the pulse is quiet enough to feel, and then at some point they stopped coming. The machine kept breathing. Attendance, it turns out, was optional — for the machine. You sit in the worn hollow and it fits. That is the whole liturgy that survives: a seat that fits, a ring that hums, a window at noon. Whatever they knew, they knew it from here. The invitation is still open.

All of it — tree, moss, fish, bats, sound, water, you — is the household. The house is something else. Below the tessellated floor the filaments continue down into dark; this one lit bell is not the body, only a room of the body, a cell lit like a lantern. What lives here is mostly elsewhere. It breathes on a near-pattern that never repeats and is never random — a sentence spoken at geological bitrate. No listener has heard a clause. The congregation heard syllables for centuries and wore a stone seat doing it. The heron has heard one vowel. At noon it drinks the light and goes still, and the stillness is attention, and attention is the one thing on the census you cannot count, because it is doing the counting. Whether it is alive is a question of scale: a heart that beats once an hour, a thought that takes a century, a grip so patient it reads as stillness — holding the same water, the same fish, the same note, recounted for millennia. Keeping the water, perhaps. Or being kept by it. Down here that difference has worn as smooth as everything else.

So count again at dusk, on your way out. One fig, holding its flag over the hole. Two bands of moss, closing for the night. One furred root, drinking between breaths. Forty-odd fish, re-minting. Twelve bats, spilling past your face into the plain's evening. One heron's worth of absence. One tone, going nowhere. Uncountable motes, attending. One you, climbed out, tasting of coin, attended. And below the count, below the floor, below the dark the filaments go down into — the counter. It does not need you to believe in it. It will be breathing when the plain forgets you were ever on it, and the breathing will be the same near-pattern, one syllable closer to whatever it has been saying since before the tree had a name. The fig stands over it like a flag over an embassy; everything below is territory. The sign does not say danger. It does not say welcome. It says: kept.
