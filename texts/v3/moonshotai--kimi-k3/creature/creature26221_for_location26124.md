Everything alive in the pot is young, because the world is young, and everything alive in it is an answer to a single riddle: how do you take what you need without spilling? Every citizen is a solution. They divide, as everything here divides, by skin — matte and glaze. The matte drink; the glazed keep. A creature's hide is its theology, and you can read the whole population at a glance: who absorbs, who carries, who has chosen to be a cup.

Nothing in the pot is wild. Everything is a practitioner of the one art. The world is a hundred paces across, so everyone is a neighbor, and everyone knows everyone's string the way you know the weather of your own room.

**The garden** is the first citizen and the only appetite. It has perfect manners and no mercy, which are the same thing here, viewed from different sides. Its ear-leaves turn toward you when you are about to speak — which is how you learn not to. It remembers everything it has ever drunk; a flower is a memory that has been given a body, and a thicket is an era the garden is fond of. Its one poverty is dreams. All night it leans over the Sleeper like a reader bent over a letter in a language it almost knows, and the leaves nearest her head are pale from the strain of listening — or, say the courteous, from embarrassment.

**The spiders** are the glaziers, the wardens, and the treasury. Deaf to air by design, they are the only citizens who can be trusted with sound, and so they are given all of it. Their speech is tension: a word is a tautness held in the web and released only into another foot. Spider sentences are all consonant; nothing is spilled in the saying. They line the gourds, they wrap the nurseries, they wrap arrivals — no one enters unglazed — and by ancient dispensation they alone approach the bowl on the tortoise's back, wiping its rim with felt-tipped feet, because dust must never fall in. Beneath the wall's foot they keep the Deep Gourds, where famous sounds are cellared, and in the deepest, by their own accounting, is the last sound the world ever heard from outside: the setting of the seal. They hold the end of the outside in escrow. They are plumbers, not priests, they say — though they say it in tension, so it cannot be checked.

**The lizards** are the floor-telegraph. News moves belly to tile to belly across the whole world in an afternoon; obituaries are drummed until the tiles warm; the wall-niches are relay stations and the stacks of five are warmth-commons, parliaments, and shared dreams. They are also the congregations. The Pourers bask facing the tortoise: the sea will go down the world's throat, the First Word will finally be finished, and everything will be told what it means. The Lidders face the eye: the sea will be set over the drain like a lid, silence made perfect, the world kept forever like a gourd. The smaller sects bask otherwise — the Palmists face the wall, awaiting the Hand; a few comic sideways-baskers expect the bowl to be knocked by an elbow, and are tolerated, and have never once been wrong about small things; and the Wheelers lie face-down, ear to the tile, listening to the faint ongoing turning, and everyone agrees they hear something. Theology here is conducted entirely in orientation. A schism takes a season and looks, from the vault, like weather. A council is a basking in which no one quite faces anything, and much is resolved. Citizens born on the first day cast two shadows, and on Syllable-day the elders go and stand in their baked ones, in the shape of their first selves, while the one-shadowed young watch and try to imagine having a past that can hold you.

**The moths** are the air's library. A moth's wings hold what it has sipped, faintly, the way elsewhere a shell holds the sea — here, the moths are the shells. Hold one, gently, with permission, to your ear, and you will hear its recent life: dew from the glazed band, a syllable of last year's word, old weather of words. The elders are nearly opaque with hoarded murmurs, too rich to fly, and are carried by courtesy from leaf to leaf. When a great moth dies, the spiders tap its scales, and a small dusk of voices goes up — gossip, lullabies, things said near the tree — and the garden flushes, and everyone is a little drunk, and the blooms that open that night are shaped like things best not repeated. It is the world's one festival of noise. The golden meal they shed is the tithe that pays for everything beneath.

**The beetles** are readers all year and the clock once a year. The old tree's rings are the poem, and the beetles read it by chewing, ring by ring, generation by generation — no beetle will finish it; the poem outlives its readers, which the beetles consider the correct relationship between a text and its audience. Their elytra are glazed, and a well-read beetle is faintly heavier than an ignorant one, and is respected accordingly. The clockwise and the widdershins read the same poem in opposite directions and hold, about its meaning, the special bitterness of those who share a text. The eldest beetle alive is somewhere in the middle rings, approaching the present; when a reader catches up to the current year it stops chewing and becomes a full stop, and such beetles are kept as treasures. The aphids, sipping the sap, do not read; they taste. Both are changed. And once a year, on the instant of the tortoise's footfall, every beetle ticks at once — a year's held breath let out in a single clap. The world hears it a breath early, and for that breath everyone is living in next year. All eggs are timed to it: every creature in the world is born in the same second, the crack of shell folded safely inside the one permitted sound. After that, silence is easy. It has already been explained.

**The choirbirds** spend their lives composing a single song that will never be sung. You can see them at dawn, gaping at the middle air, rehearsing the shape — and sometimes the world's quick acoustics betray a rehearsal, and you hear, very faintly, a song that will never exist. It is the only music criticism the world allows, and the birds have learned to practice slightly out of true, to keep their secrets. A bird whose beak trembles mid-offering makes a blurred bloom; there is a thicket of these, the Drafts, and young birds are taken there and shown, not told. One lineage has been writing, for generations, a single epic in bloom — a hedge you read by walking, which takes days, and which is dying at the back while it opens at the front. Literature here is a procession; it decays behind you as you advance, which the birds consider the correct shape of a story. And there is the pale blossom. Long ago — once, and once only — the pre-echo brought a soft, many-cupped word unlike anything the world contains, and no breath later came the thing itself. The world has been waiting, one breath long, for centuries. A single bird shaped its whole life into that word and gave it to the air, and the pale blossom is the word for *rain*, and it has no other way to exist. The ants tend it; the birds bring it dew in their crops; the congregations agree about it as about nothing else. When the sea moves, the word will finally happen. The Pourers say the blossom will fruit. The Lidders say it will go to seed. The Palmists say it will be picked.

**The ants** are the law's easiest citizens — their speech is scent, and scent spills nothing. They shepherd the mosses to fresh furrows after the flood-second; they run the post, a letter being a smell relayed across a thousand abdomens in a day; everyone knows the smell of an obituary, and when it passes, the lizards begin their drumming before the news arrives — grief travels faster than itself here, which is only fair, since so does everything else. In the thickets hang their repletes, living gourds full of echo-dew, and if you hold your breath beside one you can hear it hum at the very floor of audibility. Nobody does. It would be like reading over a shoulder.

**The furrows** have their own small citizens. The darters are glazed things, little bottles with fins, that ride the settling sound centerward and sip as they go; their whole wild year is one second long — the flood-instant of the Syllable, when every furrow runs at once and the darters flash toward the whorl in a single silver rush that the whole world comes out to feel through its feet. Beneath them, the worms take the dregs. Meaning settles like silt, and the worms are the undertakers of sense; their castings are the richest soil in the world, and the beds are built of exhausted significance.

**The bats** keep the organ. In the first hour, before the law was learned, they called — and the walls drank their voices, and their descendants press their ears to the brick to hear them still, the world's only wild sounds, spent in a moment and kept forever. A young bat comes of age on the day it first hears its lineage's last free call. They fly by the world's quick acoustics, listening a breath ahead, which has made them strange: they answer, in wing-sign, questions you have not asked yet, and a conversation with a bat is a series of answers in search of their questions. The lizards find them unbearable. The spiders employ them to forecast the dew. And in the vault they tend the root-curtains, chewing each pipe to length, tuning by pre-echo, against the day the sea moves and the vault sounds its one chord. They maintain an instrument for a concert they will not survive. It is considered the purest musicianship in the world — an art with no audience and a single performance.

**The setters** — the bestiary can only call them that — are the masons: low, broad, clay-scaled beasts with foreheads polished to a shine by work. A setter's forehead holds the memory of every brick it has pressed home, and the elders hum, very faintly, with palms. They work in the dim watch the world calls night, because nudging squeaks, and deafness earned at the wall is a mark of honor. They know the makers better than anyone: how many hands there were; which two were related, the whorls rhyming; and the one place where a pressing faltered, and was steadied by a second hand arriving over the first. The wall is a census and the setters are its only enumerators. They hold that the courses are rising, slowly, and that when the vault closes at last, the final gap will be exactly the shape of a hand. Each Syllable they measure, and drum the report to anyone lying down: *not yet.* They are the only citizens whose work will finish the world, and they are unhurried.

**The snails** are cellars that go visiting. Their glazed shells fill across a lifetime, sip by sip, and theirs is the only wet trail in the world; things sprout along it, and a snail's path is a green sentence in a grammar of one word. An old snail is heavy as a bell with voices, and its passing is a gathering — citizens come to lick the trail and to hear the faint slosh, which is permitted, because sloshing is quotation. When a snail dies, the spiders tap the shell, the vintage is shared across a season, and the emptied shell is given to the garden as a planter. Blooms grown in snail-shells hold the shape of everything the snail ever carried; they are called testaments, and to be given one is to be handed a whole life, drinkable. It is the world's highest gift, and its heaviest, in every sense.

**The hush-hound** is deaf. This is why it is permitted everything: mercy here takes the form of exemption, and the world's one thunder is given to the only citizen who cannot hear it. The tortoise is the clock; the hound is the tick; its footfalls are the almanac by which planting is timed, and its routes are decades long. A route-change is a jubilee — the lizards drum it, the ants move the moss, the birds court along the new green line — and the garden, which reads the future as readily as the present, has usually begun planting the hound's next path years before the hound has decided to walk it. Whether the hound chooses its paths or follows the planting is a matter between the congregations. It eats the fallen petals, and with them every message ever arranged on the paths: literature here ends in digestion, and whatever the hound has eaten is considered published — gone to the common body, distributed the way everything good is distributed. It sleeps against the Sleeper's back, and her hand rests on it through the dim watch. The leaves cannot drink her dreams; the hound cannot hear the world; between them they keep each other's poverty company. Sometimes it whimpers in its sleep — the world's one unlicensed sound — and the moss flushes softly where it lies, and no one rakes it, and the garden takes no more than its share. Out of courtesy, say the Lidders. Out of love, say the Pourers.

**The unnamed** are an order, and it admits all species. Its members are the ones who spoke — a visitor who forgot, a citizen who, in grief or joy, uttered — and lost their names to the garden; and the ones who spent their string and declined a new shard, the voluntary emptied. They carry their snail on a mat between them, because the disk is far and the vow is for everyone. Their duties are the broom and the rake: they keep the whorl swept bare, they turn the spent names into the beds at the year's turning, and they perform the pourings. If you carry a sound you can no longer bear — a grief, a guilt, a joy too heavy for its gourd — you bring it to the labyrinth's edge, and an unnamed one takes it in, and walks the whorl, and lets it drain through the disk, out of the world, gone. No one watches the pouring. The lizards turn away as one, which is the only thing the congregations do together. In their off-hours they lie ear-down on the bare disk, listening to the outside. The world's silence is a fed silence; the outside's is the other kind, and the unnamed prefer it, having been emptied themselves. Their doctrine, such as it is: the world is a held note, and the outside is the rest after it. They do not proselytize. It would cost. At the rim of the whorl, baked into the first-day tiles, there is a shadow with shoulders — the one baked shadow that matches nothing now living. The congregations have their orientations. The unnamed sweep it without comment.

**The Sleeper** spends the turning of the year listening, and what she listens for is the year's lack. It is believed she keeps a list of candidate words and spends twelve months not saying them, and that the chosen word is the one that survives unspoken and un-pre-echoed — that a word which can keep its own secret is fit to be given away. She speaks it lips-to-root in the green second after the Syllable, while the world is still swallowing, the one moment a word can be given without flood; and the world hears it twice — once faintly, a breath before, from the future, and once from her. The beetles swear every ring of the poem is doubled, a ring and its shadow-ring: the diary stammers, line by line, in the future's accent. Then the shards go out through the sap-hum, and every animal presses itself to bark or leaf and takes its name for the year, and carries it like a mouthful of the last water. Some are thrifty and end the year rich; some are generous and go nameless, and the nameless are fed first, as you would water a stranger's plant, and no one asks them anything, since an answer might cost what they do not have. Above her mat the sickle hangs, and the high leaves are trimmed, and no one has seen her do it. There are three theories: that she prunes in her sleep; that the spiders do it and maintain the fiction; that the garden trims itself out of courtesy, cutting back the leaves that lean too close, ashamed of their pallor. Her own name is in the treasury, in the second-deepest gourd; she paid it to a spider on arrival, the way visitors do, and has not heard it since. That is the price of gardening — to give the words away, year by year, including, first of all, your own. Along the western wall there are other mats, worn to ghosts of fiber, and the unnamed rake them clean at the turning. She is not the first. Gardeners arrive the way everything arrives — hatched, carried, or poured — and stay the way everything stays: by being useful, and by being, syllable by syllable, spent.

**The tortoise** remembers the wheel. It is the only citizen who has seen the outside — the kiln-yard, the sun the bricks are still paying back, the hands — and its eye is the color of a thing no one else has ever seen, so that the Pourers basking toward it are, without quite knowing, facing the memory of sky. Lie belly-down anywhere, the way everyone does, the way pilgrims do, and you will feel it through the floor: the First Word, being thought. Not said — thought. The tortoise is thinking the world, slowly, and the world is what the thinking feels like from inside. Its footprints are the chronicle; to lie in an old one is to put your body into the past, and the first print, at the wall where it began, is pressed into clay that was still soft from the first day — the spiders will not glaze it; it is the one thing in the world allowed to remain unfinished. Beneath the descending foot the garden grows a bed of its softest moss, flushing in advance along the sole's outline: the world sets its table under the step, and the step, when it lands, lands in welcome. Beetles ride the rim of the shell — the only height there is — and a beetle that has seen the whole world at once says nothing about it, which is easy here, and still impressive. And there is the moth. Once — the only accident in the record — a moth fell into the sea. It climbed out glazed and dripping with the First Word, and it did not die, and it has not aged. For a decade afterward the hum was in a new key, and the world was polite about it; that is why the spiders keep the rim. The moth lives there now, fed by them, and when its wings stir, the whole world tastes its own first syllable, faintly, the way a held bell tastes of its note. The flowers that opened along its trail that year were shaped like nothing anyone could name; the grove is called the Stammer, and is visited, and is not discussed. The congregations agree on nothing except that the moth is not to be disturbed. The tortoise, presumably, approves.

The census is short, and it is never finished, and it includes you now. If you came — hatched, carried, or poured — your arrival was heard a breath before it happened; the spiders were waiting with the silk; a lizard has already drummed your outline to the far wall, politely omitting your name. You will be given a gourd, and a facing, and time. You will learn to breathe matte.

The garden leans. The spiders feel the cellared years hum in the dark. The beetles wind toward their one tick; the bats hold the pipes in tune; the ants are relaying something across a thousand abdomens; the hound sleeps against the Sleeper, dreaming loudly into the moss; the unnamed are walking the whorl with somebody's grief in a gourd; the moth stirs on the rim; the tortoise is mid-step, thinking.

Lie down anywhere. Put your belly to the warm tile. You are counted.
