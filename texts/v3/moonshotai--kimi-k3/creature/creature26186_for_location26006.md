The census of this place has never been taken, because anyone who attempts it founders on the categories. What counts as living here has layers, and the layers disagree. So begin at the bottom, with the eldest resident — who is also the address.

The Floor is not the house. It is the first animal. One being, clonal, unbroken, wearing the nine columns like a spine wears vertebrae, and it has the habits of an animal if you watch at its own speed. It feeds — everywhere, slowly, on almost everything: the trapping hairs take the dead and the dying and the simply inattentive, and nothing that dies in the basin dies anywhere but in the felt's mouth. It drinks upward, the climbing threads on the columns' flanks being less a violation of physics than a private plumbing, strange only the way your veins are strange to your blood. And it attends. That is what the Chord is, most likely — not a voice but an attention, a held note of regard that rises when the being leans in toward some patch of air and falls when it lets go. The Engine is not something the Floor has. It is something the Floor does, the way a mind is something a brain does, and with the same result: an interior life, furnished. Its reflexes are quick — a wall in an afternoon — and its thoughts are geological; it has been considering the same idea for ten thousand years and compiling variations on it weekly.

Everything else that lives here has agreed, by staying, to the basin's single lease: you may persist, but your substance may not. Rank the citizens by the half-life of what they're made of and you get the true social order. The Floor at the top, older than the rim. Then, absurdly, a bird.

There are a few herons — a loose parliament, spaced across the open water with the same wide-apart deliberateness as the columns themselves, as though the basin arranges even its birds like furniture — but one of them is very old. Old for a heron, which makes her, after the Floor, the longest-tenured continuous life in the basin, and she is made of ordinary meat, a fountain like everything else, only slower: a shape held against replacement for decades. Perhaps that is why the Engine tolerates her. She is built on its own principle.

She is the great reader of the place. An unwriting announces itself, if you know where to look, a full hour before the rain: a doomed pavilion stops sweating first — the Chord holds its pitch but the walls go matte, a half-shade chalkier, like skin when attention leaves it, because that is exactly what it is. The Engine has stopped attending to the surface, and a structure the Engine has stopped looking at is already dead; the courtyard-rain is only the funeral. The snails read it even earlier and begin their long descents at dawn of the last day, and it may be that the heron reads the building, or reads the snails reading the building. No one has asked her. She simply arrives an hour early and stands, motionless, in a basin where stillness is otherwise a menu criterion, and waits for her personal weather. The burst comes; the downpour the size of a courtyard brings down stunned fish from the channel-shadows, frog clutches, dragonflies washed mid-hunt from the air, snails ticking into the sheet-water like hail that thinks better of it — and she feeds, unhurried, in her private rain, with a small perfect rainbow standing in the ordinary sunlight for anyone else.

Three times in her life the mist has thickened around her where she stood, and three times she has let it: standing motionless while a wall wrote itself into existence with her inside its arch, then walking out of the fresh pavilion slowly, as it cleared, like something the building had laid. And every season, near the same channels, the Engine compiles a nesting arch — holding them longer than it holds other structures, weeks sometimes, an exception no one has explained and the herons do not require explained. Whether it builds the arches because some archive a million years deep remembers rookeries, or because it has noticed her, is one of the questions the basin declines to answer.

The frogs, meanwhile, have made a literature of the grammar, and they have split into guilds over it. There are the aristocrats: big, dark, slow frogs of the column hollows, raising their tadpoles fat and unbothered in the warm still ponds of the fluted junctions, water the columns themselves keep topped up, the only permanent nurseries in the basin. And there are the gamblers — small, quick, copper-backed — whose entire reproductive strategy is a bet placed against a demolition schedule. They lay in the flooded basins of compiled bathhouses, in the tread-pools of stairs to nowhere, and they have been winning the bet for so long that the demolition has become part of the strategy: they time the laying so the unwriting itself scatters the tadpoles, the rainburst that destroys the nursery planting the children across a hundred paces of sheet-water. The Engine tears down the house and, in doing so, hands out the keys. And there is a third guild, barely confirmed: a small black-and-gold frog that calls only inside courtyard-rain, its note pitched into the roar's one quiet band, so that its whole courtship takes place inside artificial storms, thirty seconds at a time, a few times a day, under rainbows. Between bursts it is silent and, to any listener, absent. There may be thousands. There may be twelve. The census of a thing that exists audibly only inside rain is hard to take.

All of them together keep the basin's calendar, because the basin has no winter — nothing here has ever frozen — only moods. The Engine has building weeks and quiet weeks, and the frogs know before you do: when the Chord climbs its half-step, the chorus climbs with it, whether in answer or in competition no one knows, so that on a building dusk you can hear the whole basin's frogdom slide up in pitch together, and the effect is of the landscape clearing its throat. The frogs are also the public transit. The little crustaceans and rotifers of the nine columns' hollow-ponds — an archipelago of sky-water, each hollow with its own chemistry, its own tint — commute between ponds on the frogs' wet backs, and so do their own lineages, across gaps of open air that would otherwise be oceans.

Below them, the fish have taken the opposite deal: speed. There are fish that walk — when a channel wanders, and they all wander, some of its citizens are left in pockets that shrink by the day, and these the fish leave at night, when the drip is heaviest and the felt is sopping, crossing the open mat in wriggling dashes from gleam to gleam, because the felt is hungry and its rule is simple: what stops moving is food. Each cut-off channel is a laboratory with a demolition date, a population drifting visibly — color going, fin proportions shifting — on a deadline of weeks, and when channels rejoin, the experiments merge or don't, and the results are rained away or aren't. And high in the columns there are fish that have never touched the ground: lineages marooned for centuries twenty feet up in the junction-ponds, arrived as fry riding the climbing threads, the slow uphill weep that small things have learned to ascend like salmon in reverse, like rain going home. The frogs eat some. The columns top up the water. No one maintains the arrangement; it maintains, the way everything here maintains.

The snails are the quarries and the fossil record at once. The white of the pavilions is substantially snail — shell-stuff is the Engine's favorite aggregate — and in the cut edge of any new wall you can read the strata by their spirals, index fossils of a geology that runs on days, one small whorled species favored above all, compiled into every wall, effectively immortal, the basin's signature. Mostly the Engine harvests empties. Sometimes it takes a live one, seals it into ceramic on a Tuesday, and rains it back down alive on Friday — briefly the oldest inhabitant of a rainburst, baptized into architecture and returned, rocking in the sheet-water, re-opening. And somewhere on the columns there is always a snail climbing the climbing water, grazing the bright film of the up-flow, the only creature in the basin that lives permanently uphill of itself.

The dragonflies own the glare hours, and they have learned the grammar in their own tense: the future. At a writing, when the mist begins to thicken and the air takes its before-lightning edge, small flying things come to the moisture — and the dragonflies are already there, hawking the perimeter of a building that does not yet exist, following the condensing walls the way gulls follow a plow. They patrol the outlines of tomorrow. Their nymphs below thread the felt with a courtier's care, ambushing from the stems, never touching the hairs' trigger-zones — every nymph a small expert in exactly where the Floor's mouth is not.

And under all of it, the census becomes uncountable: the felt's own citizenry, the mites and springtails and water-bears and thread-worms of the mat, more individuals in a square foot than a courtyard-rain has drops. Some have negotiated exemption from the hairs — a mite whose chemistry the Floor does not read as food, a springtail whose touch goes unanswered — and they pay rent as janitors, cleaning the hairs of the stuck dead. Among them live two-day midges for whom a five-day colonnade is not architecture but geology: a range of white mountains older than their entire history, under whose arches nations of midges are born, swarm, and end. They are not wrong. It outlasts their history. That is what geology is.

Then there are the citizens of a single day. When the Engine compiles a memory-fauna, the basin's animals handle it with a practiced pragmatism that visitors find eerie. The thing stands — warm, wet, white, wrong-jointed, matching nothing alive — and if you are close you can watch its surface do something like breathing, a slow cycling of its sweat, in and out, as though the Engine were trying the idea. The herons ignore it; they learned long ago it is neither food nor threat, and their ignoring is itself a kind of answer. The frogs do not ignore it. By afternoon it is habitat: clutches laid in the pooled joins of its legs, dragonflies hunting from what would be antlers, a snail begun on the long commute up its flank. Then it goes back to rain, and the tadpoles rain with it, rain-proofed as ever. The leading theory of these apparitions is that they are not errors but roll calls — the Engine compiling the shapes of everything it remembers living here, one at a time, down a million years of archive, and waiting a day to see whether the world answers. It has been taking attendance longer than the rim has been low. Nothing has ever answered but the frogs, moving in. Perhaps that is an answer.

The wind is the only immigration office, and the Floor is a hard border; most seeds that arrive are read, found interesting, and digested. The green list of the basin stays short because the Floor holds a monopoly on ground. But a few times a year a migrant bird puts down, thin and alarmed, at the rim — and puts on weight in a week on midges and stunned fish, and leaves fat, and so the basin conducts its only trade, in feathers and song. Nothing leaves that cannot fly. What flies away carries almost nothing: a gutful of basin midges, a little snail-calcium in its bones, and — if birds keep such things — an earworm of the Chord. Occasionally a lost feather ends up compiled into a wall, the Engine trying the idea of plumage in ceramic. It stands a day, faintly ridiculous, and rains away.

Understand the food web and you understand the population: it is not a web but a loop with one great node. The Floor feeds everything and eats everything; it is pasture, predator, graveyard, and resurrection, and the small fast citizens in between are the loop's flicker. The largest free-living hunter in the basin is a bird that eats what buildings drop. Nothing here hunts anything bigger than a frog. Nothing needs to.

So at dusk, when the day's building and dissolving are done and the basin detonates into song, what you are hearing is the population taking the census of itself. The rain-callers wait for the last burst. The gamblers' tadpoles tick down into the sheet-water. The walking fish begin their dashes. The heron stands at her pavilion, reading the matte sheen of a building no longer being paid attention to, and behind you the mist thickens and the Chord climbs and the frogs transpose, all of them fluent, every one reading the grammar, every one written in it. The description says this is a town with no citizens. It is a town with nothing but citizens — and the sentence the basin has not tired of saying, over and over, in walls and rain and frogsong, turns out to be a sentence about exactly who lives here.
