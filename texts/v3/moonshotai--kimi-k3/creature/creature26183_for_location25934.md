Begin the census at the center, because the center is where the bodies are — and understand first what a body is, here. In the Underlake a body is not primarily an animal. It is a vessel: a cistern with appetites, a purse that walks. The wettest thing in the region is a leaf; the second wettest is whatever is eating the leaf; the third is whatever is waiting, with the patience of all the floors, for the eater to die. Every citizen can be ranked on that scale, the ranking shifts by the hour, and the whole shifting table is the economy. The fauna are abundant but gathered mostly in one place, so the census is short in geography and long in tenure. Few addresses. Ancient accounts.

**The cantors.** Fist-sized, pale, many-limbed, wing-cased — the description is accurate and explains nothing. The wing-cases carry the file; a limb carries the scraper; between them lies a tuned hollow, so that each cantor is an instrument that walks. The paleness is thrift: pigment is an expense, and the Underlake audits all expenses. Their eyes are small and single-minded, tuned to one narrow band — the blue-green of the lantern-caps. They see their own agriculture and almost nothing else. The pale pillar that stands daily in the eastern hall is, to cantor eyes, not light at all but a gray rumor; they know it chiefly by the cooling that follows it. Their ears are the true face of the animal. A cantor hears the way a surface creature sees: the polish of a wall, the depth of the dust, the number and temper of the bodies in a chamber are all legible in how the drone returns. The drone is not background. The drone is the standing illumination of the world, and everything that moves, moves through it, and is known.

The castes are grown, not chosen. The city does not assign work; it grows workers, and the hatcheries issue bodies already shaped to their tenure.

The singers are mostly resonator. Their files are ridges of glass-hard chitin renewed each molt and kept clean by mites smaller than dust-motes — the file-mites, the city's smallest citizens, who eat the swarf of the song. The singers work in relays; the note is handed down the galleries the way a bucket-line hands water, and it has not been dropped in ten thousand years. What they sing is not music in any sense a surface mind would keep. There is a ground-tone, the fundamental of the great hall itself, and riding on it the work-calls: laying-calls from the towers, harvest-calls from the spires, and the long slow count of the vaults, sung nightly, because the ledger is read aloud. The song is the city describing itself to itself, and it is also, simply, the light by which the city sees. A gallery that falls silent is a room gone dark. On the floors of the galleries the rock flour stands in ripple-figures, held in the nodes of the drone, and the pitch-keepers read them the way a surface mason reads a plumb line: when the figures drift, the chorus is corrected, and the figures settle back. The city reads its own voice in the dust, and keeps it true.

The water-wardens are grown sponges. The tuft — a crest of absorbent filaments — drinks what it touches and drains it to a holding-sac that has no passage to the gut. A warden can no more drink its harvest than a wall can sit down. The discipline was not taught; it was incorporated. Their rounds are the spires at the outflow of the great flues, bead by slow bead, a drop an hour on a good day; the measure of a warden's life is beads. At the long dusk they line the landing-shelves at the garden edge and brush the outer fur of the homecoming dewmoths, and the moths stand for it, wings half-spread, in an etiquette older than the drying of the lake. Then the vaults: wax cells rank on rank, each cell sealed in coats, each coat a date. A warden reads a cell's age by its layering the way the masons read the walls. The vaults are the ledger made solid, and the wardens are its handwriting.

The masons lay spit-stone a mouthful at a time. A mason may spend its whole life on a single curve of fluting and pass the curve to the brood that replaces it; no mason sees a tower finished, and no tower is ever finished, and these are the same fact. They repair the Thrumm at exactly the rate it erodes, which means they are not builders but rememberers, carrying the city forward a pellet at a time. They are also the tuners. The flues are instruments, and their throats are scraped and patched until the note sits true — tuned by accident, selection, and the masons' patient mouths, the three being, here, one mechanism. At each Swell they lay a single tinted stratum into the walls, the Swell-ring, so that any wall is a calendar and a chronicle, and a mason coring an old wall reads its history by taste — and the tasting is also a meal, because nothing in the Underlake is only information. Into the mortar goes ground husk, and ash, and the dust of the dead. No one leaves the Thrumm. They are promoted into it.

The gardeners keep the swards laid out beneath the lamps, and they keep the lamps. The lantern-caps are livestock: fed on guano-meal and midden-meal, pruned, bred for steadiness of light. A street's brightness is a budget. A dark street is a drought, and a thrifty district dims its lamps before it thins its herds. The gardeners sow by the pale pillar — its stand swings slowly across the eastern hall with the turning of the year, and sowing-times are read off where it stands. They move the herds, too, at the herds' own pace, which is the pace of weather: parking them on tired sward to crop it and manure it, drawing them off the fresh growth, keeping the cisterns on legs where they will do the most good.

And in the silk warrens, the weavers line the tunnels with felt soft as ash — the only quiet rooms in the Thrumm. The city, which fears silence, keeps a little of it in stock the way any city keeps a dangerous tool. In the warrens the masons test flue-tone against crafted quiet; the wardens rest their tufts; the dying are carried there, so that the drone is not troubled. Beyond them the brood-tenders keep the hatcheries warm with their own bodies, turning the wax-celled terraces, lavishing measured moisture — the one place in the world where water is spent like breath. And at the heart of the hatcheries lie the egg-mothers: a few vast pale foundresses, immobile, water-rich, the deepest vault of all. The door of that vault has never been opened. But it is a door, and the ledger knows it, and that is all that needs to be said.

**The herds** are the lake's last citizens. Their line was benthic once, when there was a bed to walk and water above it; the gill-plates they carried then they carry still, retuned to fan the cave's damp breath. They are the size of sheep and they move at the pace of weather, cropping the dustleaf, and they never drink, because the leaf is the drink. The dust on their backs is not idle: it carries spores, and on the oldest matriarchs the back-dust glows faintly with seedlings of the lantern-caps — a field that walks, dim as embers, grazed back and regrown across decades. Their calendar is the molt. The herds molt in waves, and the molting grounds are chosen loud, because a fresh-molted isopod is soft and slow and full of water — for a few days, the most desirable object in the world — and the herd rings its molters, and the drone is thickest there, and mostly it is enough. The husk goes to the masons and the scarabs; the walls of the Thrumm are part-built of the livestock's old skins. And the herds read the moving silences the way sailors read a darkening sky: a gap in the drone that drifts against the wind, and the herd flows away from it like weather turning. The old, when their time comes, drift toward the sward's edge and are not followed. Whether this is instinct or bargain, no one is keeping; it has happened for so long that the hunter's range and the herd's edge fit like a lock and a key, and it is among the oldest boundaries in the world.

**The commuters** are the ones who know the sky. The dewmoths are rain that has learned to fly home. They rise on the out-breath in the hour before the light; they spend the cold hours pressed flat to the salt crust, their blotting-wool fur doing its slow work; they lie through the furnace of midday pale as the crust itself, while the pale crust-runners of the surface take their share — the tribute the Underlake pays the desert — and at the long dusk, when the world above cools and the flues draw down, they let go and fall home on the in-breath, heavy as wet wool. What the wardens brush from the outer fur is the tithe; the inner layer is the moth's own, and its brood's. The larvae live fat and blind in the compost of the ledge-gardens, tended by gardeners; the wild moths of the outer chimneys keep their whole harvest, raise thin broods on bare ledges, and pay the swifts instead. A dewmoth is the only citizen of the Underlake that has seen the sun. It spends no thought on the distinction.

The ash-swifts live on the wing in the chimney columns and sleep gliding, rising on the breath, falling upward. They sieve the updraft for what it carries — spores, scales, motes, the small windfallen dust of the world above — and they take feral moths in the column, a toll collected in the air. Their eyes are the sharpest below the crust: dark-adapted, star-competent. They have seen stars. It has made no difference. Their guano subsidizes whole gardens, and the gardeners climb for it where only swifts are comfortable, harvesting the ledges in the brief hours when the column's traffic thins.

**The scarabs** work the midden seams, and the midden seams are where the city keeps its endings. In a world where every body is a bank, death is a withdrawal, and the scarabs are the tellers. Their obituary page is the drone: when a body stops, its small contribution to the middle band stops, and that silence, too small for any gallery to catch, is the print the scarabs read. They come; they render; they reclaim the wax and grind the husk and carry the water of the dead back toward the vaults. Nothing is wasted because nothing is allowed to be.

And then there are the fossil ponds, where the salt crusts flower outward at geological leisure — and in those crusts, the sleepers. Cysts smaller than sand, not dead and not quite alive, and neither for longer than the Thrumm has stood. Let a drop reach them — a warden's spill, a moth shaken off its course — and between one breath of the flues and the next they wake, feed, mate, lay, and shut the door behind them. The fastest lives in the Underlake are lived in its slowest rooms, and a spilled drop is a century's rain, and they spend it in an afternoon. The salt scarabs tend these ponds — half-cyst themselves, slow as the roses they walk — and harvest the cyst-meal. And once in a long while a warden climbs out to a fossil pond and spills a measured drop on purpose, spending water to make water: the only irrigation the outer halls ever see, entered in the ledger as investment.

**The green** has its own citizenship. The oldest individual alive in the Underlake is a lawn: each district's sward is a single clonal body, creeping a body-length between Swells, old as tenure, its leaves the cisterns of the world. The veil-gardens anchor themselves to satin stone with glue-holdfasts and strain the breath for its ghost of vapor, and at the pillar-hour they glow through like stained glass. Their spores ride everything — swift, moth, herd-back — so that the scattered gardens are one garden, a single dispersed body tended by the whole census without any of the census knowing it. And the lantern-caps are livestock and sun together: the cold light under which the swards impossibly grow, agriculture powered by agriculture, light made of eatings.

**The hush** is the third rule's citizen, and what is known of it is little, and the little is this. It is long and low and felted. It has no voice, a slow heart, a quiet gut, a step like a rumor. Its felt is layered filaments that drink the drone — but understand the engine of the silence: where the hush passes, the cantors freeze. The song dies around its feet, and the felt swallows what little sound remains. The moving oval of quiet is compounded of the hunter's body and the prey's terror, and it is the loudest thing in the world to anything that knows how to listen. The hush must make the silence, because the drone drowns small sounds; to hear a footfall it must first deafen the world, locally. And then it waits, because everything inside the oval is frozen too, and the silence is a standoff, and the standoff ends when something's nerve fails. The quiet is not a cloak. It is a question, and the answer it waits for is a footstep.

It hunts the sward edges and the causeway verges and the thin-aired outer halls, where silence reads as distance and so betrays nothing. It takes the slow, the molt-fresh, the strayed, the old who drifted out. It does not enter the Thrumm: the city is too loud to touch, and to a body that is one vast ear, the walls are a furnace. Under the sinkholes it sometimes waits a whole season, head tilted, listening up the flue — the only citizen of the Underlake that ever listens to the sky. It drinks its prey. Its droppings are the driest objects in the world. Of its young, nothing is known; no one has ever found a small hush; the silences are few and old and perhaps simply persist. When one dies — once in an age — it is a wet year. The scarabs make pilgrimage. The wardens take the harvest of harvests. The ledger enters it like rain. The hush is not the world's enemy. It is the auditor, and its fee is the old.

**At the margins**, the flour sea has its whales: the dust-swimmers, sleek and eyeless, sieving the dunes for cysts and trace organics, their low contact-purr riding beneath the flue-note in the drone's shadow. They breach, sometimes, in the total dark, and no eye has ever marked the arc; if there is joy in it, it is theirs alone. There are the unsealed — rarely, a molt-flaw or a torn sac lets a warden's harvest reach a mouth that was never meant to hold it, and some of those are re-sealed, and some go out. The city has no jail; it has the outer dark. An unsealed warden haunts the fringe gardens, drinking from veil-fronds in the thin halls, and the hush finds most of them. The ledger closes every account. And there are the fallers: a crust-runner through a rotten rim, a bird down the wrong chimney — rare as rain and received like rain. Whatever finds a faller first drinks first; the rest follows the ancient protocol of the midden. Nothing new arrives. But everything that arrives is used.

Count the Underlake, and the count will not hold still. It moves: moth to warden, warden to vault, vault to leaf, leaf to herd, herd to hush, hush to scarab, scarab to garden, garden to breath, breath to spire, spire to warden again. Every body here keeps three small laws — lose nothing, hear everything, be counted — and the counting never ends, because a ledger exists only while it is kept. Stand at the sinkhole rim at the cold hour and what rises past you is not a machine and not only a choir, though it is made of voices. It is the counting. It has been kept, bead by bead and voice by voice, since before the sun had a witness up here. Every voice in it is thirsty. Every voice in it is full.
