Nothing in the Basin is native. Nothing could be — the place is younger than everything that lives in it, so every living thing arrived from somewhere else and decided, for its own reasons, to stay. Most came by air. The birds came on the wing; the spiders came on the wind, ballooning in as spiderlings on threads too fine to see; the midges, dragonflies, and moths came as wings; the bats came folded. The fish rode the decant water down from cell to cell, and the eels came up the outflow from the river, from the sea ultimately, an unthinkable journey ending in a warm ditch behind a pump house. The frogs hopped down from the rim scrub on wet nights. The toads walked. The snails arrived as eggs in the mud on a heron's foot. The bacteria were everywhere already, waiting to be told what to eat. A fresh discharge delta is sterile at noon and claimed by dusk — bacteria first in the warm film, midges over the water before it has finished clearing, laying before the silt has stopped moving, swallows working the hatch that same evening. The Basin does not have succession so much as a starting gun.

The decant ladder is an archipelago of provinces, each with its own chemistry and therefore its own citizenry. The milk-white cells below the discharge faces are nearly empty — the silt too raw, the light too choked — but not empty: the bacterial mats begin there, banding the warm inflows, and the first bloodworms thicken the new mud until a stirred footprint pinks at the edges. The jade cells are where the real economy runs. The settling water blooms; daphnia cloud it in their millions; midge larvae work the bottom in densities that make the silt itself seem to crawl; tadpoles graze the algal film off every stake and stone in shining, slowly widening sheets. This is the larder. The clear cells at the foot of the ladder are the dining rooms, patronized by everything that needs to see its food. And because the water steps down over the weirs, each new cell inherits its citizens from the cell above, the way a flat inherits furniture from the previous tenant: the fish ride the weir-water down, the snails cling to whatever floats, and within a month the new pond is as full as the old one ever was.

The ditches are the crowded streets. Whirligig beetles write their small endless signatures on the surface film; backswimmers hang beneath it; dragonfly nymphs — the tigers of that small world — ambush from the silt. Every submerged surface wears snails, and every snail-grazed surface wears the clear ribbons and jellies of their eggs. The crayfish are the Basin's sappers, chimneys of excavated pellets studding the damp flats, their burrows honeycombing the young dikes, which makes them the only animals the crews genuinely resent. On damp nights they leave the water and walk the plank roads, clicking like thrown dice, and the night shift steps around them. Mosquitoes raise whole generations in a haul-road rut. Moths mob the sodium lamps, and the spiders own the lamps: webs rigged between every pair of trestle stanchions, along the gantry rails, across the corners of the gauge boards, so thickly that when the morning steam condenses, the first thing the haze renders visible each day is the spiders — thousands of jeweled diagrams hung in a gray nowhere.

The fish sort themselves by clarity. Carp roll in the warm turbid margins like slow porpoises and thrash their spawn into the reed stubble; shoals of small silver things tremble at every weir, hanging in the falling water. And everywhere, the eels. Nobody stocked them. They came as elvers against the current, up the discharge line in the slack hours, and now every ditch has its eel and the drowned hamlet under Cell 4 has hundreds, lying by day in the roofless rooms, threading the doorways. On the wettest nights they leave the water entirely and cross the causeways, thick as a man's wrist, going about errands no one has thought to ask them about. They are the Basin's undertakers as well: nothing dead stays where it fell for long, and it is mostly eels that see to that.

Of the frogs there is nothing useful to say about numbers. The numbers are not known and are not knowable. What is known is the repertoire, because in a place you navigate by ear, names are sounds: the crews call them the sewers, the jugs, the creak-gates, the two-marbles, and a new hand learns the catalogue from the dark before he ever sees the animal that owns the voice. In season the warm ditches carry spawn in strings and rafts, then tadpoles in sheets, then a week of froglets so thick on the causeways that walking becomes an act of concentration. Night operators share their cabs with the frog that lives behind the gauge panel and emerges when the engine warms the bulkhead. On the first warm nights, when the toads cross the haul roads, traffic simply stops: the little diesel stands ticking, the driver smokes, and somebody sets out a sawhorse with a lamp on it and the word TOADS chalked on a board, which is the entire apparatus of traffic management. Three nights later the roads are empty again.

The turtles own the flanges — every pipe flange wears its stack, biggest at the bottom, each animal one size smaller than the one beneath it, like crockery draining. They nest on the oldest crust cells, digging at dusk, and the fox finds most of the nests; the crews learn where the clutches were from the leathery scatter of shell on the white salt. The snakes swim with their heads up, drape their shed skins over valve wheels like shirts left on hooks, and in the cool months knot themselves together in the riprap toes of the dikes — slow balls of them in the dark between the stones, in places the crews know about and leave alone.

Out on the open cells the birds run the day. Stilts stitch the shallows; avocets sweep; the alarm of one becomes the alarm of ten thousand, traveling across the cells faster than whatever caused it. Ibis work the week-old crust in white congregations, probing for the bloodworms thickening the young mud. The dabbling ducks sift the jade cells; the divers work the deep ones; the great rafts double in the migration months, when the Basin fills with passengers and the haze and the lamps together pull tired travelers down out of the night — dawn after a big passage finds small exhausted birds sitting on the pipelines in rows, waiting to be refueled by midges. Coots are the chickens of the place, quarrelsome and numberless, anchoring their nests to drowned fence wire, taking off across the water like thrown hardware. Grebes carry their chicks between their wings. Moorhens walk the floating algae mats, each step a deep dimple. And under every roost the water runs richer and greener than anywhere else, because the birds feed all day across the cells and pay it all back at night onto a few favored structures. The gantries and the drowned pump house are fertilizer plants as much as they are architecture.

The machinery is a reef. The swallows build under every pump-house eave, cups mortared from the youngest mud in the world and laced with flagging tape, baling twine, and fibers stripped from pipe insulation — dissect an old nest and you can read the Basin's small materials like a core sample. Cormorants whiten the gantry rails. Every stick in the heronry along the ridge of the drowned pump house in Cell 6 was flown in from the rim scrub, kilometers of commuting per stick, and the building itself has become a tenement: herons on the ridge, swallows under the eaves, a barn owl in the rafters that works the fuel-berm rats by night, eels cruising the flooded ground floor, and a wild hive of bees in the wall cavity that winters on the willow's catkins and the cook's herb-flowers and makes long flights to the rim. It is the fullest building in the valley, and the only one no person enters. It was scheduled for demolition once. The charge was never laid, and the paperwork has quietly stopped mentioning it. The same unwritten mercy bends the fill line around the chimney in Cell 4 on the newest plan sheets — no order was ever issued — and keeps the grader fifty meters from the oldest dike face while the kingfishers are in their burrow.

The midge columns work two shifts: swifts and swallows and dragonflies by day, bats by night, the bats issuing at dusk from junction boxes, valve housings, the dry dark pockets of the machines, to quarter the smoke-columns over the ponds. After full dark the night shift takes the cells — night herons croaking at the weirs, rails whinnying from the reed stubble, the bittern blowing across his bottle, which the crews call the bottle-bird and use as a landmark. The fox walks the causeways, and its prints weep full and smooth over within the hour, so that by morning its whole night's patrol exists only in what is missing: a turtle nest, a coot, the canteen scraps. The rats keep to the fuel berm and defer to the cat, which has grown fat and unbothered and is occasionally seen riding the narrow-gauge like a supervisor.

And the willow is the Basin's entire forest. Everything that requires a tree requires this one. It owns the valley's only aphids and therefore its only ladybirds, its only leaf-fall, its only shade that moves. Every bird in the Basin visits it eventually, the way everyone in a small town eventually passes the one bar. The crews feed it canteen coffee grounds, defend it from the snails, and take cuttings from it, and the cuttings all die — the salt takes every one — so the willow remains an only child. In autumn its leaves fall onto ground that has never received leaves before, and the snails eat them within days, and the ground forgets.

The deep old cells belong to the catfish. Pale from the dim water, long as a man, they lie in the drowned road cut by day and rise at dusk to take ducklings from below, and the crews speak of them the way villagers speak of the old family in the big house on the hill. They are the oldest residents. Some were surely here before the willow was planted, before the chimney became an island, possibly before some of the men now working were born. One lives under the office barge and has learned the particular squeak of the canteen door; the cook feeds it fish guts off the stern, and it takes them with a slow suction, like a drain opening.

The people, strictly speaking, hardly live here either — the crews rotate in from the rim — but a few do, in the Basin's thin way. The dredge crew sleeps aboard across their rotations, in bunks in the superstructure, and learns within a week to sleep through the frog wall and wake instantly at a change in the pump note. The pump-watch keeps a houseboat moored to the fuel berm and a dog that has never seen a tree but one, rides the causeway train standing on the cab step, has learned to ignore toads, and in winter sleeps on whichever slurry main is warmest — any pipe above blood temperature grows a dog. The cook arrives before dawn, keeps the valley's only garden in buckets of imported soil beside the willow, and wages a nightly war against the snails. Nobody's boots ever fully dry. The canteen is lit more dimly than the gantries, to be less interesting to midges, and has a double door for the same reason. The lamp-code and the hand-talk carry everything the noise drowns: a lamp swung low means stop; two flashes means the line is down; a flat hand patting the air means bad ground; two fingers walking means toads on the road. The people are the only inhabitants who know the place is scheduled to end — the completion year hangs on the office barge wall — and it does not appear to change anything. They tend the willow anyway. The old men, the retired pumpmen, drive out to the rim road some evenings and sit on their bonnets and listen, the way sailors locate a foghorn. They say you can take the whole valley's pulse from up there.

Winter is the season that reveals what the place is. The rim frosts; the ponds steam; and everything for kilometers around concentrates into the warm water, until the duck rafts become archipelagos and a few diehard frogs call from the warmest ditches in January like a radio left on in an empty house. Summer answers with the dragonfly emergence, when the shed skins cling to every stake and reed and survey flag in their hollow millions, a ghost population outnumbering the living.

Someday the last cell will be filled, the water will give back its last decanted liter, and the plain will dry and green and quiet. Then this whole assembly will do what it has always done: it will move. There is always another raw pond somewhere over a rim, another dredge eating a bottom, and the birds and frogs and eels will find it the way they found this one — the crews know; they came from the last one. Everything here works the circuit, feathers and boots alike. What will remain is a hand's breadth of strange young rock: a bed of snail shells and bird bone and turtle scutes, a carbonized film of the mats, laid down at the top of the valley's lungs. The fossil record of a noise.
