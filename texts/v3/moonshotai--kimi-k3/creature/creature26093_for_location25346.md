To take the census of the Weep, you do not look. You sit down on a wet stone, and you listen, and you learn to subtract.

Subtract the rain — all its voices, the leaf-hiss and the moss-hush and the fine applause of standing water, the iron drumming to the east. Subtract the thousand patient drips. Subtract the falls, the pump's slow heartbeat, the wire-hum, the crawler if its round is anywhere near. What remains is alive.

There is so much remaining.

Understand that a creature here is its sound first. Everything else — shape, size, color, intention — is inference, rumor, a thing your hand found once in the dark and was not sure of afterward. The elk is a lowing that knows the valley. The wire-eel is a puddle that bites. The lampmoth is a snow that happens inside a beam. The Tuner is a warm kettle. The Hush is a subtraction.

And you — give it a few Pours — you are a rhythm of footsteps on wet stone, briefly luminous, soon erased. The Weep counts you back.

---

The small multitudes first, because they are most of it.

Every fold of every leaf holds something many-jointed and busy, and they are named the way everything here is named: by ear. Clickers. Tick-fleas. The glass-hopper, which drops only when a true drop lands, so that its landing is masked by the rain's. When the Pour comes, the guttered leaves of the dripwood run with small surfers — channel-shrimp riding the rain down the fluted bark to the black ponds, and the ponds receive them in rings of brief cold light. In every cluster of lantern pitchers lives one grub the plant cannot digest, fattening on the drowned: the pitcher lures, the moths are credulous, the grub takes its tithe of the takings. Even the traps are inhabited.

Elsewhere, life is careful about how it looks and careless about how it sounds. Here it is the reverse. Sound is the dangerous medium. There are creatures that call only inside the pump's hiss, hidden in the machine's exhale; creatures that move only beneath the frog-chorus, commuting under its cover. Each weather province keeps its own accents — the frogs of the vent country rasp where the peat frogs ring — and a traveled ear can hear where it is the way other travelers see it.

*In the Weep, a voice is a bet.*

And color? Color is unwatched, and therefore free. The frogs may be scarlet. The beetle under your palm may be peacock. Nothing knows, and nothing spends the costly chemistry to find out. Pigment here is whatever the rot left lying around — worn by no one, for no one.

---

The frogs are the institution.

They do not merely call; they relay. One begins, the next takes it up, and the pulse travels the marsh at walking pace, a slow front of sound sweeping the valley the way weather would, if weather moved sideways here. Some species take the pump's thump for a downbeat and will not sing off it. One small kind sings only inside the hiss — a voice folded into the machine's exhale, findable by mates, invisible to everything else. On the tail of every Pour, when the spores rise and the air goes thick and faintly sparkling, the chorus doubles, and under its cover the whole understory goes about its errands: the soft things, the slow things, the things with no bet to place.

The chorus is not a concert. It is a crowd, and crowds are where the vulnerable walk.

---

The lightwater is alive, and it lives fast.

A pool is a pasture. The plankton blooms on the Surge's flood-pulse, rests dim, fires when touched — and everything that grazes it announces itself in the grazing. A pool flaring in slow waves at the slack is being sieved by the pond-throats, the patient filterers that pump the water through themselves and pay in light. A pool gone dark has been eaten down to its seed-stock; give it one Surge and it returns, because everything here returns. The wet of the whole world carries a little of it — the grass-wet, your cuff, the stone — which is why your passing writes itself behind you and politely erases itself. The water keeps nothing longer than it must. It is the plateau's one scribe, and it has no memory, and perhaps that is a mercy.

---

The lampmoths live twice.

Once as caterpillars in the moss, slow and anonymous. Then the short, lit adulthood: they erupt on the Pour's tail with the spores, and they wait out the Long Round in the dark, and when the crawler comes through — you hear it for an hour before you see it — they snow around the headlamp the way things elsewhere come out at dawn. They mate in its wake. The pitchers take their share of the credulous. The plants along the rail lean toward the passing lamp, a tropism with no sun in it, memory bent down the generations into instinct.

And in the beam, for the length of a pass, it turns out the frogs are not the only secret. The moths' wings are riots — colors that have no names, because names need witnesses, and these have had none for longer than anything remembers. Then the lamp goes on down the line, and the colors go back to being rumors.

---

Mire-elk are the large music.

A lowing is a question, and the valley is the answer: each elk reads its own echo off the far scarp and knows the land by how the land replies. Calves inherit the valley's voice the way calves elsewhere inherit its paths. The herds drift with the Surge to fresh graze, hooves wide as trenchers on the floating peat, and the ground wobbles under them and they do not mind.

When the quiet comes through — the traveling bubble, the all-species cessation — the big voices are the last to die. And afterward, when the drips have been alone long enough, and the clickers start, and the frogs find their pulse again, someone must be brave first.

*The all-clear in the Weep is an elk.*

---

The Works are habitat.

Warm pump-house brass wears a fur of gold moss. Gauge-glass goes blind under a lichen that reads nothing. There is a beetle that spends its whole life in an oil-cup and knows the world as warm, dark, and faintly sweet — a complete cosmology, and not a wrong one. The condenser fins are the one desert the plateau has: life here is a thing of warm murk, and purity is barren. Even the lightwater will not root in fin-water. There is nothing in it to be.

The wire-eels are fat on leakage and slow with contentment, and in great Pours they climb, and the fluted trunks along the drainage runs pulse pale blue, slowly, like something remembering. Steading children learn the hums before they can walk a chime-line: which puddles bite. The eel-fishers work with glass poles and great patience, and the eels, touched, discharge, and the whole transaction shines.

Ask why everything here keeps schedules — the spores, the moths, the chorus, the Surge-entrained frogs — and you will hear the theory, offered quietly: that the Hush hunts by ear, and what it hears best is the off-beat. The irregular step. The voice out of rhythm. So the world synced itself to the machines the way creatures elsewhere grew stripes.

*Rhythm is camouflage.*

Whether it is true, nothing says. Everything acts as if.

---

Of the Hush there is nothing to add. The silence says it all.

What can be told is the shape it leaves in the living. The freeze is the one behavior every species on the plateau shares — elk and eel and moth and grub, one reflex, older than any of them. And within the silence, the steadings say, something tidies. Bones do not accumulate in the Hush's ranges. After a kill there is no kill — no remains, no scatter, nothing for the lightwater to write around. They call it the Wake, and it is the only creature in the Weep that makes no sound whatsoever, which is either a very good trick or the oldest one.

*Not every silence is empty.*

---

In the deep mire, off the path, the Nearly ring.

They learn the roads' melodies the way water learns a channel — slowly, over Pours, over years, approaching true. So the Tuners retune the chime-lines the way careful people change locks: a note here, an interval there, and the road walks a new song, and the Nearly begin again from the beginning, patient as anything here.

A retired melody is never reused. It is given to the dead. When someone dies, they are carried out with the old tune rung once, whole, note by note, and then it is theirs, and the road forgets it. That is why you never follow an old tune, however sweetly it rings from the dark. Old tunes belong to the dead, and to whatever has been practicing them.

Some of the Nearly, the Tuners say, are only frogs. Only.

As for the rest: if the melody you are walking gains a note, stop where you are until the next bell sounds true.

---

The Tuners themselves are known the way the pump is known: by effects.

Swept path-stones. Oiled clappers. A muffled bell where the Hush has shifted range — a muffled bell means *turn back* more plainly than any sign. Salt, wire, and needles left on a path-stone will be gone by your return; at flood season, at the stretch where the path goes under, you may find a bowl set out, still warm. And sometimes, on a long dark reach between bells, a second set of footsteps matches yours for an hour, unhurried, companionable, and you do not kindle a light, because light is a sentence spoken aloud and it would be rude — and so no one has ever seen one, and so no one admits to having met one, and everyone has walked with them.

The operators' descendants, say the steadings, who never left their stations. Or maintenance that outlived its makers and, being patient, stood up one Pour and walked. The dark has had time enough for either. Argue it all evening if you like. Set out wire, and the path is swept by your return.

The kettle is warm.

---

The people of the Weep live on the whalebacks — basalt backs above the flood-line, at chime-line junctions, within earshot of a pump, because the pump is the clock. The steadings are named for their sounds: Ninth Bell, Slowvalve, Twin Falls. Houses of tufa block and dripwood beam, roofed in salvaged iron, each roof its own drum-voice; you do not find your house, you hear it, the way you know a voice across a room. Children learn the chime-lines before their letters. Everyone carries the map as music. Age is kept in Chords.

The work is the work of a wet world: moss-crofts, peat for fuel, eel-fishing, pitcher-tapping at the slack — gloved and quick, for the nectar, the one sweetness the plateau gives freely, though the amber lure-light that draws the moths draws the tappers too, and the plant is patient. Kettle-law holds everywhere: any door opens, a traveler is fed first and questioned never, because the dark is the common enemy. And light-etiquette: you do not kindle a light in another's house unasked. A lamp lit is a declaration. Spend it on purpose.

And once a Long Round, when the crawler comes through, the whole steading walks out to the rail with the dyed things — cloth, beads, a toy boat, grandmother's shawl — and holds them up in the sweep of the headlamp. The children stand still in the beam and learn what color their own hair is. It is usually not what anyone supposed. Then the lamp goes on, and the colors go back to being rumors, and everyone goes home humming.

---

And then there is the hour when the cycles lean together and coincide.

The Chord is the world's one extravagance. The lightwater blooms in every pool at once; the falls stand up to full throat; the frogs breed inside the hour; the elk calve into the roar; the eels run; spores, moths, everything — everything does in the Chord what it dares not do otherwise, because in total noise every voice is masked. And that is also the danger. The alarm of this world is silence, and in the Chord, silence cannot be heard.

*The safest hour in the Weep, and the most dangerous, for one reason: no one can hear anything.*

In the steadings it is festival: Chord-eve roof-checks, water drawn, the feast laid, and the hoarded light spent at last — indoors, under a roof, as advised — while the world turns over outside like a sleeper, fertile and deaf.

---

The census is never finished.

New voices join the chorus; old tunes are given to the dead. At the scarp station the buckets come up wet, and the rule is to be elsewhere when they do; the Tuners meet the line; sometimes tracks lead away from a pylon's foot, and they are walked around, not followed, and they are gone within a Pour. Sometimes, in the quiet, a Downline shudders as something ascends, and the steadings check their roofs and say nothing. Some things the plateau sends down. Some it receives. The ledger is not yours.

Sit long enough on your wet stone, subtracting, and you will feel the reversal: the marsh is taking your census too. It has counted you the only way it counts anything — by the sound you make, and by whether you keep the beat. Behind you, your footprints shine briefly on the path, fade, and are gone, and the dripwood combs the mist, and the chorus leans toward the Chord, and the whole reverberant dark goes on speaking all at once, layer over layer, water and machine and beast —

*Nothing here lives alone. Nothing is permitted to.*
