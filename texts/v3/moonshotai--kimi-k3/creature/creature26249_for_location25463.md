# What Lives in the Loom

Ask what lives here and the answer depends entirely on where you are willing to stop counting. Start with the countable, then.

## The Tenders, up close

A Tender is born onto a strand the way you might be born into a country — the span is the given, the thing that was there before you and will be there after, and your whole life is a way of relating to it. At birth the family ties a single knot in a new cord. That is the name. Spoken names exist, but they are conveniences for the rare work of the air; a Tender's true name is a knot-structure, grown more elaborate across a lifetime, carried at the wrist, read by hand. When two Tenders meet, the first thing exchanged is not words. It is cords. You learn a person's name the way you learn anything here: in the dark, by touch, slowly.

Childhood is mostly listening. A child learns the felt-note of the home strand before speech — the specific hum of this span under this weather — and they say a child who cannot tell the strand's hum from her mother's tap is not yet entirely born. The first tool is a blunted thorn-hook. The games are casting at targets, following dew-beetles to learn patience (a beetle walks its capillary river at one pace and cannot be hurried, and a child who can walk behind one for an hour is judged ready for grafting lessons), and globe-watching — which is forbidden, and universal. Every child has looked once into a well-globe. Every adult remembers what they saw there. Nobody says.

The work is walking. A family holds perhaps a hundred miles of strand and never sees all of it in a season; they walk it in rotation, reading the lichen, testing tension by palm, pruning misgrowth. And here is something the Tenders do not advertise: they do not prune all of it. Sometimes the Loom grows a spur, a loop, a cord reaching out across a hollow toward nothing, and by old custom some of these are left alone — left, the saying goes, *for the Loom to finish its thought.* Whether this is humility or hope is a matter of family temperament.

Meeting is ceremony. Since a shout dies in yards, two Tenders on a span see each other's lantern long before they can speak, and there is a light-etiquette: you raise a lantern-globe of moths and pulse the old pattern, *I come open-handed*, and wait for the answer. Then, on meeting, both parties stop a body's length apart and press their palms to the strand together — because every word spoken on a strand enters the fiber and travels, and nothing said on the Loom is private from the Loom. The span is always the third party to a conversation. News is knotted as it is told; a good conversationalist ties while listening, and a meeting ends with an exchange of cords, each person carrying the other's news away legible in their hands.

Hospitality has its own artifact: the **guest-cord**. A traveler knots their name into the family's cord at parting, and decades later another traveler reads that cord by touch and learns who slept here before her grandparents were born. The whole society is a text you can hold.

Marriage is grafting. Two families, often a span apart, begin growing a bridge-shoot at betrothal, and the betrothed may meet five times in a decade while the strands close the distance. Some bridges take forty years. Some outlive the betrothed, and are completed between the heirs — the dead married by proxy of joined wood, which the Tenders consider neither strange nor sad, only correct. A marriage is not an event. It is a tension that has come true.

And death: the old grow their own gardens in advance. A Tender feeling the Warmth leave her hands — she reads it in her own tap going flat, the way you might read it in a mirror — begins choosing mosses and pips, raising the starts in a burl-trough, tending what her body will carry into the well. You plant your own raft. At the seeding, the family passes the dead one's tally-cord palm to palm in the dark — the cord in which every person they ever met is tied, one knot per soul, each in the shape of that soul's name. That is the eulogy. It is read by hand. It takes hours. No one speaks.

## The kept life

Moss-oxen are cat-sized, silent, and they **stamp**. Of course they do — everything here talks through the strand. A lost ox stands still and stamps its slow note into the fiber, and the herders count their animals by palm in the mist, forty hearts' worth of vibration arriving up the span. Their wool felts itself in the endless wet and is sheared with oldgrain; their milk is thick and faintly sweet and keeps badly, like everything here, because nothing here was ever meant to be kept dry.

Dew-beetles are the clocks. A household beetle walks its capillary channel from knot to cistern in a day, and the hours are read off where the beetle has got to. Nobody feeds it. It is less a pet than a small punctual neighbor.

Message-moths are the long-distance service: bred lines that will fly a trained span and pulse a pattern against a darkened burl-wall, a sentence taking an afternoon to arrive. Moth-keepers are the nearest thing the Loom has to scribes who work in light, and a moth-keeper's loft — a hollowed burl hung with sleeping constellations — is the one place a dispersed people goes to feel crowded.

Cistern-fish live pale and unhurried in the tank-flowers; a traveler fishes a family's cistern only with a permission-knot. And the capillary rivers have their eels — long blind things that swim the water-channels for hundreds of miles. An eel arriving from upstream is news: the channel runs, the span holds, the water still remembers the way. When the eels stop coming, a family walks the strand looking for the sag. The eel is a stress-test that feeds you.

Nobody keeps a caster. But Tenders glean after them — harvesting the anchor-fans a caster abandons when it moves on, the finest silk in the world, left behind like a shed skin. You do not farm the thing. You keep to its customs and take what it drops.

## The well-dwellers

The gliders never land. They are born in the largest hanging globes, eggs drifting in the suspended water, and they hatch by engulfment: when two globes collide and merge with that soft engulfing shudder, the shock hatches what was waiting, and the young glider rides the shudder out into the air and does not touch the world again. They sleep in thermals. They mate at confluence, on the wing, over the pollen-dunes. The oldest grow vast and pale and are seen only at Flowering, rising out of the golden haze like something the mist is slowly deciding to become.

Sifters cruise the wells with their wide soft mouths, straining the globes of what lives in them — and their throats glow faint green as they feed, so that a sifter at night is a slow lantern drinking. Everything in the wells is lit from inside, a little, as if the emptiness were embarrassed to be entirely dark.

And inside the globes themselves: whole ecologies. Translucent shrimp-things, ribbon-larvae, a green snow of drifting algae — every globe an aquarium, and the old globes, aloft for centuries, have citizens who have *changed*. Here is the wrongness the Tenders will gesture at and then stop gesturing: a well-globe shows you another place, a far strand, a view from no vantage that exists. And sometimes — in the old globes — the image is made from within. The life inside drifts into arrangement, the way a school of fish might form a face. You look into the globe and see a distant knot, and the knot is being shown to you by the things that live in the water, gathered into the shape of it. You do not drink from a well-globe. Whether out of fear of the water or courtesy to its inhabitants, the customs differ.

The casters hang at the wells' edges, anchored to the undersides, holding their fans of silk for years at a time, reading the drift of prey through the air on their lines the way a Tender reads a span through the palm. Old casters are known. Families name the local ones the way you would name a mountain or a weather, and there is a truce with customs, never negotiated, settled the way spans settle: you do not cross under a fan at Flowering, when the casters are mad with plenty; you do not take a line a caster is still holding; and the casters, for their part, do not take the seeded dead. The dead drift past the fans untouched, green and turning. Who taught whom to cast is an old argument. The Tenders say they learned by watching. The heretics say the casters learned by watching them. The archivists point out, gently, that the oldest burl-rings predate the question.

## The half-seen

There is something that combs the felt.

No one has seen it clearly — descriptions disagree so completely that the Tenders take the disagreement itself as the shape of the truth — but its work is everywhere: patches of moss combed overnight into spirals, paths of flattened felt that fill in by the next mist. Families step around the fresh combings out of courtesy. It does not bother the oxen. The oxen, presumably, know something.

And below, in the Below, are the ones whose size does not stay consistent with their distance. What is known is this: when one passes beneath, the strand's felt-note flattens. The beetles stop. The oxen turn inward and stand. And the custom — the very old custom — is to press both palms flat to the strand and hold them there until the note returns. The old say this is to listen. The very old say it is *to be counted.*

There is one account of the Below, and it is kept the way a dangerous thing should be kept: in pieces. The **Sharded Account** is told in fragments, each fragment carried by one Tender, assigned at coming-of-age, never shared, re-knotted into a new carrier when a piece-holder dies. The story exists entire nowhere and complete everywhere. It has never been assembled. It must never be assembled. Whether it describes something seen once or something seen always, no living person knows — that is rather the point of the arrangement — and it is not discussed after dusk.

## The listeners and the knots that answer

Most keeper-knots echo. They keep a voice and leak it back, softened, over hours or days, and the Tenders use them as one uses a patient post.

A very few of the oldest do something else. They *rearrange*.

The **listeners** are a vocation — Tenders who sit in the dark burl-chambers reading the leaks by ear and by palm, and who maintain the distinction that matters: echo is memory, utterance is otherwise. An uttering knot builds its sentences from the voices it has kept — every word a different dead throat, a different softness — and what it says, when it says anything, is questions. The questions are always the same kind. Whether the water is still sweet. Whether the warmth holds. The names of the moths. The questions of something waking very slowly and checking, one by one, the parts of its body.

You may leave a message in such a knot. You may ask it nothing. If it answers a question you did not ask, you do not answer back — you walk to a listener, and you report it, and the listener will not knot it into any archive. Some things they refuse to add to the record, from a fear never stated quite plainly: that the archive might learn.

## The largest answer

Which brings the census to its edge, and to the heresy, which is also the oldest answer.

Consider the evidence as the Tenders hold it. The Warmth: a metabolism, continent-sized. The breathing chambers: respiration, slower than sleep. The felt-note under every palm: a pulse you are welcome to check. The give: distances that behave under attention and wander when unwatched — the way a creature behaves. The misgrowths left unpruned, reaching across hollows toward nothing anyone planted. The knots assembling questions out of the dead.

The heresy says the Loom grows the Tenders the way a strand grows leaves — that a thing being built for this long, at this scale, with this much patience, would eventually grow itself hands, and then grow itself the habit of wondering about its hands. That the oxen, the moths, the casters, the drifting gardens of the dead, the answering knots, are one thing, wearing shapes, being woven toward some finish no one has been shown.

Ask what lives in the Loom, and a Tender will not argue. She will do something else.

At dusk — it happens at dusk, everywhere, in every family, and no one will say who started it — she will press her palm flat to the strand and tap a short message with no addressee. Every hearth does it. Across the knots, across the spans, across the whole breathing wickerwork of the world, at the failing of the gloaming, the hands of the Loom tap the same small greeting into the fiber: water still sweet, warmth still holding, we are here, we are here.

The strand carries all of it, every message at once, billions of taps arriving from a day's walk in every direction and from farther than anyone has walked. If the heresy is true, this is what the world hears, each evening, from everywhere at once: the sound of its own hands, telling it that it is alive.

The Loom is silent to the ear. It has never once been quiet.
