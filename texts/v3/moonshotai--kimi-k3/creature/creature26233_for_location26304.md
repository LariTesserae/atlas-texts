Ask the marsh first. The marsh's answer is the oldest one, and it is short.

The sheep drink from every black pool in the sedge but not from the Kettle. The dogs will not swim it; a dog that wades in comes out at once and shakes for longer than the wetness explains. In the deep winter, when the ice is a single black pane with one steaming hole in it, ravens come and stand around the hole at a respectful radius, and look, and do not drink, and fly home. The shepherds, who have names for every hummock and drain in the flat country, gave the pond a kitchen name and one piece of information, handed down the way boundary marks are handed down: *it keeps a crew.*

That is the marsh's whole testimony, and it happens to be correct. The stack standing out of the ice is not the resident. It is where the resident shows.

---

In the top two meters, in the brief summer, there is ordinary life, and it knows nothing. Beetle larvae, midge swarms, the quick small business of a warm marsh pond — all of it conducted in the attic of a building it has never entered. The smear layer is the border. It has no guards and perfect enforcement. Heat crosses, and light, and once a year a breath, and once in living memory a duck, headlong, in a convulsion of bubbles, which is still, by the town's method of record-keeping, mid-passage. Nothing crosses that has a choice in the matter.

Below the layer the census begins in earnest, and it begins with the fish.

Everyone who has seen the fish calls it the Lamplighter eventually, without ever being told the name. It hangs under the junction lamp like a thing hung up to dry, white as paper, thick as your forearm, and it has rounds. The round takes most of a day, and the reason it takes most of a day is that the fish is not commuting. It is grazing.

Each burning lamp keeps a garden — that perfect circle of pale algae, cropped to the exact radius of usefulness — and inside each circle, in the lit green, live the small pale things: glassy shrimps, seed-shrimps, isopods slow as handwriting, a whole cropped flock the size of a dinner plate. Four lamps, four pastures, one herdsman. The Lamplighter moves from field to field and lets each one recover behind it. Whether the light draws the circle or the fish does is one of the things no one can say, and it does not matter, because the circles are exact either way, and the fish is fat either way, in three-degree water clear as glass, fed by a schedule.

It is not a settler. That is the thing to understand. The spring had trout before the works had bottles, and when the water rose a century ago, the spring's own trout rose with it and stayed on. A trout in that cold might live twenty years; the post is a hundred years old. Do the arithmetic and then discard it, because no one has ever found a body, and the round has never been found empty. The changeover has never been witnessed. Somewhere in the seams below the well, the town keeps a supply of its own keeper, and the post — not the fish — is the resident. The Lamplighter is the oldest family in town, here before the flood, still on the payroll.

---

The eel is the only other animal anyone calls a resident without being prompted.

It lives behind the outfall grille, at the far gate, where the town's slow current pays out into the black corners of the pit. The current delivers; the eel receives. That is its whole economy — the sweepings of the long lane, the drift of silt, whatever the town sheds, brought around to the back of the bars like a meal left for something nobody mentions. It came the way eels arrive anywhere: young, at night, in rain, across wet grass, by will. The marsh is wet and the night, presumably, was persuasive. That was decades ago. It has grown since then the way the town grows — by deposition, layer on layer of years — and it is black, not white, thick as a wrist, and older than the Lamplighter, and possibly older than the Lamplighter's line. It has outlived its own migration. The Sargasso is a rumor it declined. It moves perhaps once a season, sliding a single slow length through its dark, and that movement is the event of the season, logged by no one, felt by the grille.

The Lamplighter's round ends a body-length from the bars. Always. What that courtesy costs either of them is not recorded.

Below notice, the census thickens. The hand-sized dunes of silt in the doorways are not empty — sieve one with a lamp beam and it hops: amphipods, pale as everything, the town's mice, living on the fine fallout of the gardens. The moss felt on the shaded brick is a country with no capital. And not all of the coating on the town is stone. On the ironwork the rusticles hang like wax drips, and inside them, at a pace that makes the moss look hasty, live the iron-eaters — whole nations of mouths, digesting the girders a molecule at a time. They are the only appetite in the Kettle that is not on the payroll, and they are losing. The water lays down faster than they take up. The town is being eaten, slowly, and is winning, slowly. The surplus is the sinter skin, and the surplus is forever.

---

In the watchman's upstairs room, where the water stops at chest height and the air is a century deep, the census finds its smallest district.

The ledger has booklice. The ink is sharp but the glue is a hundred years old, and old glue is a pasture. The booklice have, presumably, a herdsman of their own, because there is a web in the kneehole of the desk. It is always intact. No one has seen it occupied, or made, or mended, though the room is small and the search would be short. If there is a spider, it is pale — everything in the Kettle is pale, except the lamps — and its family has been in that desk since before the water rose: the second-oldest family in town, sealed in on a weekday in the last year, running ever since on paper-mold and patience, in the dark, off no one's grid, in a food web you could hold in a teacup.

The tidemark stain on the wallpaper, which the water still touches exactly, is itself faintly alive — the one garden the town tends in air. The room breathes, and at the edge of the breath, things grow. That is all the tide is for, perhaps. It has not been decided otherwise.

---

Then there is the staff, and here the census starts to blur, because the town does not draw the distinction the question assumes.

The clock, for instance. No one has set it in a hundred years. It is driven by the line shaft, which is driven by the spring, which has never once been late, and it keeps the correct time because down here it is the thing being measured — the clock is the town's own pulse, read off a dial. It is correct the way a heartbeat is correct: by jurisdiction.

The bottling line is the town's memory practice. Every shift it takes the standing present, caps it, and stands it in ranks — each bottle a sample of *now*, dated by nothing, true as a relic. The ranks are the archive. The scree of glass on the floor is the archive's deep storage. Once in a while a bottle settles wrong and ticks, and that is the town clearing its throat, and the Lamplighter flinches once, and the round resumes.

The telephone is the town's one correspondence. Lift it: a hum, then a silence that has answered, then the hum resumed. Nothing is said on either side, and nothing needs to be. The message is the wire's survival, sent and received in the same instant: *still here.* And the meter is the town's signature on the world's paperwork — one number a quarter, nearly the same every time, growing by the exact increment of four amber lamps and one shift a day, filed by an office that has changed its name four times without ever changing the file.

But if the question means *who* — then the answer is the water.

The spring worked here first. For fifty years it was pumped, turned, measured, bottled; it knew the works from the inside, the way a horse knows the cart. Then it rose, and took possession of its own machinery, and has run the place ever since with a former employee's exact respect for procedure. It sweeps its street with its own current. It tides the rooms. It turns the turbines that turn the generator that spins the meter that pays for the light in its gardens — it is on both sides of every transaction, landlord and tenant and rent. What drowned the town now keeps it, and keeps it well: oiled, exercised, swept, correct. The flood was not a catastrophe. It was a promotion.

---

The membership extends past the waterline. Somewhere, four reorganizations deep, there is a clerk whose procedure is the residue of a person. The clerk has never visited; visiting is not in the procedure. The town is also lived in on paper. And someone keeps the padlock newer than the century — a shepherd with an inherited key, an office's field hand, no one who would admit it — the above-water post, held, like the Lamplighter's, by succession rather than by person.

The divers are not members. They are weather. They come down through the doubled layer a few times in a decade, breathe recycled breath so as not to roar in a place where a settling bottle is an event, touch nothing, take nothing, and carry away only the hum in the sternum. Their rule is the marsh's rule, and it is the whole etiquette of the Kettle: this is not a wreck. Do not behave as though it were a wreck. It is a workplace, and you are on someone's shift.

---

Once a year, in the deep of the freeze, the relief valve lifts and the hooter sounds — one low word, several seconds long, felt through the ice by anything with feet. The word is the town's whole name, and the name is *here.*

And in that long note the census takes itself. The Lamplighter between the second and third lamps. The eel at the far gate, tasting the year's sweepings. The pale flocks grazing the four lit circles. The web whole in the dark air above the ledger. The meter mid-turn. A bottle half-full and filling. The clock correct. Every post manned, nobody waiting, nothing missing.

Take anyone off that list and the place would be a wreck within a decade. Leave the list as it stands, and it is something the language keeps coming one word short of. The shepherds are not short of the word. They have had it all along.

It keeps a crew.
