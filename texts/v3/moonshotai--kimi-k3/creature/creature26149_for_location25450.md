Ask what lives here and you will have to decide, eventually, whether to answer with a list or with a body. Start with the list. The body will arrive on its own.

Understand first that everything here is load-bearing. The chorus is not a pastime; it is the town's ongoing account of itself, and residency is defined by participation in it. To live here is to have a voice in the census and to hold up, with that voice, some small portion of the geometry. The creatures are not tenants. They are structure that happens to breathe.

The closest thing to citizens are the gardeners, and they are also the hardest to see clearly, because you cannot look at a colony. You can only look at an ant, and an ant is not the unit. Amber-bodied, big as a thumbnail joint, she will live a few weeks and spend them on a task that will take a decade — a gutter half-stitched, a lane-edge trained a finger's width, a doorframe pruned back from closing. No gardener has ever seen a tower finished. No gardener has ever seen one begun. The colony has seen both, many times, and the colony is the real animal here: one organism with a million hands, keeping its records in its own chemistry, a library written in scent and rewritten hourly and never allowed to lapse. The chorus shouts its map in sound; the colony writes its own in pheromone, and it is the one map the silence cannot cut, because a smell does not need the air to carry a voice. When the wave of quiet rolls down a lane, the ants are already gone from it.

They keep the wall-cicadas the way other ants keep cattle — the honeydew is the least of the harvest — and they are the town's undertakers besides. When a tower dies, the colony takes it apart from within, patiently, and carries the dead wood down into the root-mat, feeding the foundations with the flue, and no one else is consulted. The gardeners are the only creatures here permitted to decide what the town no longer is. And if the first town was theirs — the description of the host allows it; a grove, a hive-city, a town, and one of those three is a thing ants build — then they are not the keepers of this place but its heirs, walking streets that are the mold of their own lost corridors, maintaining the house that ate their house. They do not act like heirs. They do not act like servants either. They act like people with work to do, which may be the same thing, given enough time.

The cicadas are the ground-tone and the rent-payers. Ten thousand of them in the walls, drinking the milky sap and paying for it in vibration, because the fig grows toward sound the way a lesser plant grows toward light — a lane lined with ten thousand tymbals is a lane that knows where its walls are. Every adult among them spent years in the dark first, a nymph in the root-mat beneath the lanes, drinking from the roots, feeling the chorus come down through the wood, learning the key before ever seeing the light. When it finally claws up through the pavement and splits its skin against some wall, it already knows the song. It has been humming along from inside. The root-mat is a dormitory, and the cicada nymphs share it with stranger sleepers.

The pale fish are the dormitory's other tenants, and their patience runs opposite to the ants'. The colony is patient across generations; a fish is patient in one body. It seals itself in mud and mucus under the glossy pavement and slows its heart to a rumor and waits out the dry years — seven of them, ten — and its whole waking life might total thirty days, lived a day at a time, years apart. It wakes into thunder. Real rain, the festival: the gutters at full voice, the cisterns overflowing, and the fish thrashing up out of the floor of the town to eat a decade, court desperately, spawn, and burrow back down as the water sinks. What a fish dreams through a seven-year drought is a question only the fish are qualified to answer, and their answer is one day long and made of rain.

The pipe-swifts are born in a flute and never entirely leave it. A swift's nest is a dab of silk and saliva fixed at a particular height in a tower-flue, and a flue's pitch depends on where its nests interrupt the rising column of air — so the towers are tuned by their tenants, generation by generation, and each tower's flock keeps its tower's key the way a village keeps an accent. An old swift can hear which tower a stranger belongs to. They sleep on the wing in the flue's upwash, held aloft all night by the town's own breath, and they pour out at dusk like smoke running backward to feed over the dry plain — the only residents with a daily life partly elsewhere, the town's commuters, the ones who know the empty world beyond the wall and choose the noise again every evening. And because they roost in the flues, every secret ever whispered into a tower has passed through a swift's roost first. What is whispered in a tower is heard in the wells, yes — but it is heard by the swifts before the water gets it.

The gecko-thing does not hear the town at all, or barely; its ears are small and simple and it has never trusted them. It reads the place through the flat of its belly and the palms of its feet, pressed to the warm wall, where the chord is not sound but texture — the lanes a fabric of trembling, each creature's voice a thread in the weave. And this is why the gecko matters, and why it is safe: the map is made of voices, and the gecko has none. It is not on the census. It is the one resident the system cannot locate, and so the one resident that can look at the hush. To everything else the hunter is a gap in the song; to the gecko it is a smoothness moving through the texture, a shape that walks without touching the world, no tremor, no pressure-wave, a cold patch sliding across the warm weave — and the gecko flattens and watches it pass with no fear in its eyes, because there is no silence for it to drown in. It is the only eyewitness the town has ever had, and it cannot say what it sees. Unless the flattening is the saying.

Of the hush itself, almost nothing can be said that is not effect. It is a body — it feeds — and bodies can be starved, and this one has been starved expertly for a very long time, because the map works, and yet it persists. Patience is the local virtue and it holds the record. It may be older than the mold; the first town stood in streets and kept water and perhaps kept noise for the same reason, and a fig that inherits streets might inherit the reason for the streets. Or it came later, drawn to the loudest place in the world the way some things are drawn to flame. What it is — one animal or a succession, ancient or endlessly renewed — is not known, because everything that has ever known what the hush is has been eaten, which is either a coincidence or the town's oldest law. Notice, though, that the chorus never sleeps because the hush never does. The map stays sharp because there is a whetstone. The town does not love it, but the town is shaped by it, the way an arch is shaped by what it must bear, and on the hush's rounds the other residents do the only wise thing: they hold still, they listen to the silence approach and depart, and they are grateful, afterward, to be entries in the census and not absences from it.

The glasswing moths keep the soft end of the night. Their wings are panes with veins, and a pane will answer a chord — faintly, a shimmer an octave up — so they move through the towers' voices trailing harmonics, and the brightness in the night chorus is really flight. They live in the gutters and drink from the drip-tips, and once a season, when the seven crests flower all at once and the town goes soft with falling petals, the moths come out into daylight confused and happy among the pollinators' brief millions. The petal-fall is the benign hush, the almost-tender one, and it is also when the water lies stillest. Petals on still water make a lens of it. The town looks through.

What it looks through is guarded — the right word — by the kettle-frogs. Every cistern has its frog: one old one, broad as a plate, pale from the lidded dark, and its slow stone note is not music, or not only music. It is the stone dropped in to keep the water from settling. The mold remembers, and water is the one medium that can hold the image, and a cistern left unstirred through a whole hot afternoon begins to show things — so the kettle note falls, slow as a heartbeat, and the old streets scatter, and the present swims back up to the surface. The frogs are the town's forgetting, hired against its memory, and the town keeps its past the way it keeps its water: in the dark, lidded, and stirred. Only at rain do the rest arrive, from wherever frogs keep themselves in dry years, until the cisterns peal like bells and no one counts them. No one has ever counted the frogs' full number. It may be that the full number does not fit in the town, and that is the point of it.

And then there are the oldest residents, the ones in the water, and whether they live here is a question about what the word is allowed to mean. They recur. They persist. They are held. They live here the way a chord lives in a hall after the strings stop — and in this town the strings never entirely stop, so neither do they. The town replays them: a door closing that never existed here, footsteps of no present walker, a market-murmur in a language never spoken, and on the stillest afternoons the awnings hang fuller in reflection and the stalls hold shapes and the lanes carry figures going about unhurried business in a town that was metabolized before the fig's oldest wood was green. They are not ghosts; ghosts are of people, and these are of a place. The mold remembering what it held. It is not wise to wave at them, because waving is a voice, and voices get entered in the census, and the town is polite, and it answers politeness. It is not necessary to run from them either. They are only the town, remembering itself — and memory, here, is just another form of residency.

Which brings the census, at last, to the body.

Because the list was always one entry long. The fig is the place — acres of a single clonal organism with no center and no heartwood and no edges it can feel on its own, and this is the thing to hold in your mind: it cannot feel its own edges. A plant has no nerves. This plant has ten thousand cicadas. It has no inner ear; it has the frogs' stone notes ticking up from its pockets of water; it has no fingertips; it has a million small feet polishing its floors. The chorus that must never lapse is a body knowing its own extent. The shouting is proprioception. When the rare dead hush falls and every voice stops at once, the lanes forget their far ends the way a sleeper's hand forgets it has fingers — and then the gardeners see to it, the frogs see to it, seven flues full of warm rising air see to it, and the town sings itself back into shape, because the shape is held up by the sound of it, the way an arch is held up by its own weight.

Once you understand that, the small wrongnesses read differently. The echo that arrives a breath too early is not a haunting. A body that has felt a hundred billion footsteps learns to feel them arrive; it is reflex, it is recognition, it is — if you are willing to be generous, and the town has earned generosity — eagerness. The replays are muscle memory. And the way the great chord resolves toward speech when you listen long enough: you are half right about the hallucination, and the other half is that a thing that has been listening to the census since before the census had words may, given millennia, begin to say it back. Slowly. In a million voices. One sentence. The sentence is the map, and the map is the whole of what the town has ever needed to express: here, here, here, here.

You were in it, briefly, so you are in it now. The chorus adjusted to include you; the map added your position and routed around you like water around a stone; for an evening you were infrastructure, a named location, a voice in the census whether or not you spoke, because your footsteps spoke for you. And when you climbed out of the swale and the roar stayed at your shoulder past every boundary that distance should have set, that was the map keeping your coordinate a while longer — reluctant, companionable, enormous — before it let you go.

It does not let anything go entirely. Long after, in some hollow hall, a stranger will hear your footsteps coming back from a lane you never walked, saying something you have not decided to say. You live there now the way the figures in the water do: as something the town holds. The census includes its visitors. It includes its dead. On the stillest afternoons, stirred or not, it includes its first citizens. Ask what lives there and the honest answer is one entry long — and the entry is a chord, and the chord is still counting.
