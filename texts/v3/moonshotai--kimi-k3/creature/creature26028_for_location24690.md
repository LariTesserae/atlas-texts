Everything that lives in this sea is, first of all, a listener.

It could not be otherwise. Light here touches nothing in transit; it arrives whole or not at all, and so vision gives you only sources and surfaces, all of them impossibly sharp — an animal a kilometer away is as clear as one at your hand, with no haze to tell you the difference, no gradient, no perspective. Sight, in this water, has no distance in it. Sound has all the distance. Sound is how the world is big. So the creatures of the sea are built around their hearing the way creatures elsewhere are built around their eyes, and the spectrum of the world's noise is portioned out among them like territory. The engines own the middle of it — the three notes and the five, the bass of the Rim underneath. Everything alive has moved to the edges.

Below the chord, in the infrasonic dark, live the ridge-grazers, and they are the sea's true timekeepers — though the first thing to understand about them is that the world gives them no time to keep. The engines run at constant load; the weather is finished; the chord is locked and does not beat. Nothing here pulses. What the locked chord does instead is write a fixed geography of loudness across the basins — standing hills and hollows of interference, permanent as the gyres — and a grazer moving through that pattern feels it swell and lessen, swell and lessen, against its skin. The world's metronome is the grazer's own stroke. Time, in a place where nothing happens, is made by the living, in the act of moving. A grazer carries its clock in its body. So does everything else here, and each body keeps a different speed.

The grazer's speed is the slowest. Its heart beats on the scale of weather. Its blood runs at four degrees and its enzymes are a chemistry of glass, and it harvests a pasture a micron thick at a pace of meters an hour, drawing its ventral rasp along the young black ridges, leaving the bright scrape-lines that re-film within weeks and are grazed again within years. From the air the ridge-flanks are palimpsests of these lines, and no two grazers scrape alike — the pattern of the rasp is a signature, legible as a hand, if there were a hand's owner left to read it. A grazer is plated in the same silicate it eats; it is continuous with the rock, magnetite-dark, and it steers by the chord, reading its position in the interference hills the way you read light. It cannot be lost. It can no more be lost than you can be lost inside your own heartbeat. Behind every ridge there is a sound-shadow — the only shade this world offers — and the grazers rest there, in the lee of the noise.

They number in the low thousands, and every one of them is known. Not named — there is no one to name them — but known, the way you know the rooms of your house in the dark. Their voices are below the engine band, single notes that may hold for six hours, phrases that take a day, conversations that take the better part of a month: a note, two hours of travel across the water, an answer arriving from beyond the curve of the world, hours later still. What they say is mostly that they are. In a sea with no landmarks, location and identity are nearly the whole of speech — *I am here, still here* — and the result is a census conducted continuously, at geological tempo, by the population itself. Every grazer tracks every voice. A calf's first note is heard across the whole world within hours, and it is an event — the rarest kind here — and over the following decades the entire basin can hear that voice deepen. Births are the calendar. In a world without seasons, the years are named for voices.

A grazer breathes perhaps once a day, and the surfacing is an occasion in two media at once. Its back breaks the skin filmed with the very pasture it harvests, and rim-gliders set down on it as on any island, and walk about, and find it cold as the rock; glint-fry rise from below to comb the plates; the breath leaves in a minutes-long exhalation and freezes as it goes and falls back on the water as a brief private snow. Off a summit island, in the hut, the needle of the brass gauge trembles. The grazer does not appear to notice the gliders, the way a mountain does not notice weather. When one dies — and no one here has seen enough of them die to say how long they live — it sinks, slowly, and the whole basin hears it land. Then the film colonizes the bones, and the bones become pasture, and the pasture becomes a place. Nothing rots in this water, so the dead are not consumed here. They are *kept*. Every grazer that has ever died is still present, white on black, in the bone fields of the deeps — the species' entire history laid out on the basalt — and the small pale gleaners who farm those fields keep the bones bright, generation on generation, and speak almost not at all: the nearest thing to silence any animal here achieves.

Above the chord, thin as static, live the glint-fry. Theirs is the high band, the hiss at the top of the world's hearing, a glitter of clicks where the engines' roar thins out — and their bodies answer their voices: glass fins, glass flesh, a small dark comma of organs, so that a school of them is not seen so much as *detected*, a boil of lenses, a heat-shimmer without heat. In water this empty, the only hiding is being nothing. They gather in the warm outflow parishes where the film grows fastest, thousands to a parish, millions in all, and between the parishes the sea is empty even of them: the parishes are archipelagos of warmth, and the fry do not cross the cold between. A school lives its whole life within sound of one engine. The standing gyres are their only roads, and every parish has its two poles — the warm giving of the outflow, the cold taking of the intake — and the fry thread their lives between them, drawn to one, fleeing the other's low bruising note, which every fry knows from its first hour. Where a maintenance crawler welds, they gather like swallows at a plow, darting at the slow gray snow of loosened film that the bright work rains down. At dawn the schools rise, and where they meet the skin the surface seems to boil. They live fast, by this world's standards: a parish turns over a hundred generations while a grazer crosses the water between two engines. The fry are the sea's second hand; the grazers are its hour hand; and the engines do not have hands at all.

Then there are the citizens of the other medium. The rim-gliders are the only eyes that matter here — the only creatures who ever see the sea whole, the drowned ranges showing through it, the dark grazer-shapes a kilometer down, the engine-lights at night across six hundred kilometers of black water. They are built from what they eat, and what they eat is built from the sea's breath, so their wide wings are partly stone; they grind them thin again on the ridge-grit as they age, and every colony has its standing polish-spots, slicked bright by centuries of wings. The air is too thin to carry their voices far, so they have made the rock carry them instead: they drum with their feet, high sharp ticks that the chord cannot drown, and the summit islands tick faintly day and night with it, a bright knitting at the top of the world's noise. Their eggs hear the chord through the shell before they hatch — every glider has known the engines since before it had ears. They float sometimes on the skin, half in each element, the only beings who hear both of the world's musics at once: the frayed small intimacy above, the immense chord below. They fly out to the Rim and ride the rising columns of spray, and look down into the fall, and see cloud. That is what everyone sees. The gliders just see it more clearly.

And the margins of the census hold stranger things still. On the one great buoy chain — nine hundred kilometers over the trench, a route no one travels — live the walkers: long, patient, many-finned, spaced out along the links like beads of their own. A walker's world has one dimension. It climbs and descends the catenaries forever; a buoy is an event; it has never touched the bottom and never will. It eats the film off the metal, link by link, a life's work, and its faint scraping transmits along the chain for kilometers, so that the chain talks to itself in both directions, slowly, in a language of small bright sounds. There is one chain, and so there is one people of the chain. Down in the deeps, the frazil snow sinks past them all and melts, and the gleaners tend the bone fields, and nothing accumulates, and everything remains.

It is fair to ask whether the engines belong on the census. They take in the sea and give it back changed; they hold their own temperature and the whole sea's; they mend their injuries; they speak without ceasing in the voice everything else must organize itself around; there are a few hundred of them and the number has never changed. By every habit this world recognizes, that is a population. The crawlers that tend them are its fixed citizenry — uncounted circuits, uncounted welds, each bead-pattern as singular as a grazer's rasp — and if the grazers are the sea's memory, the crawlers are its habits, walking the same flanges at the same pace into the indefinite. Nothing here worships the engines; they are too constant to be gods and too indifferent to be parents. They are simply the first fact. The builders are absent, but their purposes are resident, which is a way of living somewhere.

Which leaves the question of *whoever* — and the honest answer is no one, and the honest answer is also: the hut. The door stands open. Snow comes in and lies on the table and departs without melting. Gliders nest under the eaves, and the threshold is deep in bright fry-bones. The sextant keeps its color in the cold dry air; the script keeps its secret; the mark is at water level, and the water keeps the mark — the one sentence of that lost language still being spoken, spoken by the sea itself. The stair goes down into the water and on under it, and below the waterline the gleaners graze the steps bright, so that the stair stays legible a long way down the flank: a road into the sea, still walked daily, by the small cold keepers, on their slow rounds. When a grazer surfaces off the island, the needle trembles in the empty room, and the gliders shift their stone wings on the roof, and that is the whole event. The watching goes on without the watcher. Nothing needed to take it over; the sea had already agreed.

Night here is less a darkening than a subtraction: the thin light goes, the engine-lights come on, and the world becomes what it always mostly was. Sound. The fry turn in their warm parishes around the only lit points in existence. A walker scrapes along its chain. The gleaners keep the bones white. A grazer, somewhere, sounds a single low note, and the listening holds — that held attention across the hours is the nearest thing to silence anything here will ever know — and then the answer arrives, from beyond the curve of the world, and the census is confirmed: a few hundred engines, a few thousand great voices, the glass clouds, the stone-winged watchers, the keepers of bones and steps and chain, and one open door. That is everyone. The roll is called in a register too low to hear and too large to miss, it takes all night, and no one is absent. No one has ever been absent. There is only the door, standing open — which is not the same thing.
