Ask what lives on the High Orchard and the honest answer is a list of debts. Everything that moves on the plateau can be filed under one of three headings: that which pays, that which collects, and that which is the bank. The locals — the herders, the people of the lease, who call themselves something that translates as the Tenants and also as the owing — keep the list the only way a list can be kept here: in rhythm, drummed and re-drummed down the generations, because the motes polish away anything carved within a century. The census of the plateau is a song. It begins, as everything begins, with the landlord.

**The Orchard** is first on the roll, and is the roll itself. One citizen in a city of tenants. Whether it is a someone has been debated by everyone who has ever watched the ridges at night and come back quieter than they left. Its attention travels with the light — that much the Tenants are sure of: where the slow pulses pause, something is being considered, and where they hurry, something has happened, a breach or a birth or a siren taken, somewhere a night's walk away. The Tenants read the ridges the way sailors elsewhere read sky. They have correlated everything and understood nothing, which is the ordinary condition of tenants. The terms of the lease are old and plain. Chambers open to the right knock — a rhythm, not a key, taught to every child. Cisterns may be drawn at the marked nodes and nowhere else. The dead go to the roots. Beyond that, the Orchard does not explain itself, and the Tenants have stopped asking in the way of people who suspect the answer would come due.

**The flask-beasts** are the accounts that walk. A calf is born empty and spends its life filling; an old cow is a reservoir with opinions, and a herd is a ledger on legs, its wealth readable at a distance by silhouette alone. They graze the dimness through the black felt of their flanks, following the shadow-cells at walking pace, and they talk the way the plateau talks — through the ground. The salt polygons are tuned, each cracked plate a slightly different note, and an old herd walks its avenue like a hand moving over keys. Their grammar is spare: three rhythms for cistern-bearing, two for the drift of shadow, one — used only when it must be — that means *freeze*, and after the freeze, the all-clear. The all-clear is the vulnerable word, because sirens have learned to drum it false. So the herds keep passwords: the all-clear changes with the season, taught to calves in their first month, and a lead drummer of quality can throw her voice sideways through the polygon field so that the warning arrives from a place she is not. In a loud world, everything that says anything is, at least part of the time, lying.

**The drill-moths** believe they are thieves, and the Orchard lets them believe it, because a courier who thinks he is stealing never asks for wages. They bore with their diamond augurs, they drink their tithe, and they carry — in the glittering half-pollen that dusts them — the tree's own traffic: motes and chemical mail, shoot to shoot, across kilometers of dead salt the moths think they are crossing against the landlord's will. The quota is real, though. A moth that outbores its welcome is sealed where it stands, mid-bore, entombed in hardening sap, and old standards wear the glitter of these tombs in their bark like a log of correspondence. The Tenants keep moths the way other peoples keep hawks: a moth-keeper's swarm runs messages between camps, scent-tagged, postage paid by the tree without the tree's ever being consulted. Or perhaps consulted constantly. With the mail, as with everything, it is hard to tell where the theft ends and the service begins.

**The Tenants themselves** are small, dry, dark people, felted in living coats — black as vane-felt, and felted in fact, cultivated algae worked into the weave so that a Tenant grazing her sleeve in a shadow-cell is, in a modest way, farming. They speak low and close, and their public voice is percussion: heel and staff and palm on the salt. A name here is a rhythm before it is a word; you are not truly introduced until the herd can pick your cadence out of a night full of drumming. They keep no books, because books are polished away. Instead every child learns the almanac as an epic — the long recitation of slot-drift and shadow-weather, computed across generations and carried entire in memory, a tide-table disguised as a song. In a pouch at the belt each carries a pebble of the quicken — the gravel that is not all gravel, the rounded stones that wake only under raw light — and when the pebble stirs against the hip, the Tenant folds her felt edge-on and turns her plated back, because a blaze-slot is coming through and everything on the plateau has an opinion about that. They are few, and they keep themselves few, setting their own number before the Orchard sets it for them, as they set the moths' quota before the tree must. They do not say *died*. They say *settled*, and the dead are drummed to a root-ridge with full honors, the last payment on a life of payments. Ask a Tenant where her people came from and she will drum the rhythm that means *from the trees* — which is either history, theology, or a joke. With the Tenants it is hard to tell, and they like it that way.

**The siren** is the collection agency. It is best understood as an ear with a voice grafted on: every siren cuts its resonating chamber out of the dead, going as a young animal to the Choir to steal a piece of mummified standard, and a siren whose graft fails is mute, and starves. Its boom is convincing because it is not a counterfeit — it is a standard's own voice, worn like a stolen coat. It hunts inside the dusk cannonade, when the whole region is booming at once and one more boom is weather. It knows about the passwords; it drums its false all-clears and waits; and when a blaze-slot sweeps through and the world flinches in unison — vanes folding, moths dropping, herds turning their backs — the siren lies stiller than anything and counts, patient as probate, for the one thing that flinched wrong. The herds call it the siren. The Tenants, who understand bookkeeping, do not consider it a wolf. A siren's kill soaks the salt, and the roots drink within the hour: it is how the Orchard calls a loan in early. It is a bailiff. A few Tenants hunt them regardless, armored in stillness, baiting the ear with a drummed flinch, and the prize is the grafted flute — the only true standard-voice a Tenant can own. They hang in the vaults, treasured and never, ever sounded. Using the landlord's voice is the one crime everyone agrees on.

Then the smaller registers, the fine print of the census. In the cistern-chambers swim the **sap-fish**: pale, translucent, schooling at the drains, carried up with the water when the sea was taken — the sea's last swimmers, unless they are the tree's own thoughts with fins, a question the tree declines to settle. The Tenants fish them with drummed lures and eat them at the Settlement feast, tasting the sea at one remove. On the warm root-ridges at night sleep the **ridge-sleepers**, small furred things that bask in the heat and time their scurrying to the dark between pulses, on the theory — unprovable — that what is lit is watched. On the sun-facing slopes at dawn come the **tear-lickers**, desperate and precise, dosing themselves to the lick on the bitter brine; they assay the sea's bones, and the Tenants harvest their salt by watching where they lick. The gypsum dunes carry a film of **slot-lichen** that blackens in the wake of a blaze, so that the dunes keep the slots' handwriting for days, and part of the almanac is read straight off the drifts. And in the flutes of the Choir live the **lodgers**, denning deep in the organ-pipes, deaf by selection at exactly the frequencies of their own house's voice — they live inside a song they cannot hear, which the Tenants point out is the commonest condition on the plateau.

The **motes** are the borderline case, alive the way a habit is alive. Half the pollen is not pollen; it is the maintenance crew of a garden whose gardeners dissolved into their gardening, still patching, still polishing, still — presumably — climbing the thin air to the Veil. The Tenants do not fight them; they schedule them. A monument you want kept, you recut every lifetime; a name you want forgotten, you carve once and leave to the polishing. Nothing on the plateau is lost, only refiled — the plateau forgets on purpose, and forgetting is how it remembers.

Even the dead are on the roll. Mummified sirens stand among the husk-groves like standards that never grew. Amber-tombed moths glitter in the bark of their executioners. The Choir sings on the windward side, louder some years than others as the wind improves the instruments. Death, here, is not an exit. It is a change of employment.

So the census is never finished and never needs to be, because it is taken out loud, every night, in the dark that was never quite dark. The herds drum their positions from the avenues. The ridges answer in slow light, junction to junction. A standard booms — once, twice — from the groves; the moths sleep sealed in their quotas; the sirens hold still and count; the Tenants tap the roll on the thresholds of their vaults, every rhythm a name, every name a soul accounted for; and the Choir carries the whole of it out over the salt, chorded and dissonant and almost a voice. If you stand on the rim of the Drop and listen the way the plateau listens — through the soles, through the bone — you can hear the ledger being balanced for one more night: everything present, everything owed, the water going around and around, mouth to root to mouth, the taken sea remembering itself in pieces, in a house that has no intention, ever, of being empty again.
