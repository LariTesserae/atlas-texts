Nothing lives here — that is the first honest answer, and the valley insists on it. Nothing that eats. Nothing that breeds. Nothing that dies in the ordinary way, softening into soil for the next thing. You could walk the terraces for a week and find no body but your own, no warmth but what you brought. But a census taken in a week is a census of the quick, and the valley has almost no quick in it. Take the census at the valley's own pace, and the blank page begins, slowly, to fill.

Begin with what the census refuses. The wind carries seeds over the rim; they fall on the flags and lie there, kept like the wood of the wedges, neither growing nor decaying — immigrants who never arrive. The hawk crossing the col at noon does not count; the valley counts only what stays, and nothing winged stays. Even the afternoon rain, which builds its white towers like a claimant, is gone by dusk, having seeded nothing. The Work will not be colonized. Its population is fixed, admitted on mineral terms only. You are not on it either. To the valley you are a quick event, like the rain: something that happens to it briefly, and loudly, and then stops happening.

The first true tenant is the water. It is the only resident that moves, and everything here is arranged around that fact. It enters at the Comb as melt — hurried, cold, carrying the mountain's grit — and the valley sets about teaching it patience: stilled, gauged, divided into thirds and thirds again, held ankle-deep in shadowed runnels, sent dark beneath the pavement, lifted over its own kind on bridglets. Most of it stays four days. Some of it has never left. In the sealed cisterns, beneath caps flush with the flags, there may be water that fell as snow while the tally was still being cut, kept clear by the same sterility that keeps the wood — unaged, because nothing here will do the work of aging. The water pays for its lodging: one skin of gray flour per season in the settling ponds, the rent and the record being the same thing. And when it is finally given to the groove at the outfall notch, it leaves changed — measured, calmed, spent of its turbulence. Patient water is the valley's only export.

The second occupant is light, and it lives here by appointment. It comes over the rim in the first hour, strikes the wet stone and is thrown back — black off the saturated faces, near-white off the dry, mica winking in the joints. All day it is passed from surface to surface: down the channels, under the bridges where it weaves its restless nets, into the ponds that each hold a piece of sky. At dusk, when the air darkens, the valley declines to let it go — the basins keep the sky a few minutes past its time, a hospitality of the mineral kind, the guest not hurried out. And on the right days, in the deepest vault, light keeps the oldest appointment of all: a blade of sun admitted through a slot, reaching a floor nothing else has ever reached. The counters are gone, but the light keeps its appointments. It is the only guest who still arrives, and the valley receives it as if nothing had changed.

The night resident is the frost, and it is the only one that enters uninvited. You hear it after dark, ticking in the joints as the day's melt turns and expands, working its fingers into every seam ever closed. It is patient beyond anything you know of patience: it takes its payment in flakes, in grains of mica, in the slow rounding of an arris over centuries. Once in a cold snap it strikes something deep — a boom from a vault below, one drum-note, then silence — and you understand it is down there too, in the dark, testing. The frost is the valley's proofreader; nothing flawed escapes it. Given time enough, it would take the whole Work apart joint by joint. It has not been given time enough.

Because one worker never left the site. Call it the slow mason — the mineral-heavy water itself, laying down pale stone wherever it runs. It extends the spouts a hair a year. It seals the joints the frost has opened. It heals cracks along the old drawn lines with a fidelity no living hand could sustain: no improvisation, no signature. Its long commission is in the deepest vault — a pillar rising by almost nothing a year toward a drip that falls every few heartbeats. When they meet, the circuit closes, and one small sound stops. The mason is building a silence, and has been building it since before the tally-keepers left. Between the frost that unmakes and the mason that remakes, the Work holds its shape. They are the last crew on the mountain, and neither has ever stopped.

Something else is resident that you cannot see until you notice it never ceases: the measurement. The gauges stand in their pools; the water stands at the fourth notch; the notch means what it always meant. The varves thicken one skin a season, the archive still writing itself long after its readers went. Whatever the question was — how much melt, how many years, how fast the snow is leaving the rim above the Comb — it is still being asked in stone and still being answered in silt, by an instrument that has not been told it is unread. If the question was what you suspect — that they were counting the retreat of the snow — then the valley is a clock measuring the length of its own life, since the snow is the water and the water is everything the Work does. The tally stopped mid-row. The varves did not stop. The valley keeps its own books now.

And beneath all of it, the resident you feel more than find: the unfinished thing. A hammerstone placed flat on a half-dressed block. Wedges still swollen in their split, still working — the block is being split this decade by hands that are gone, the work continuing without the workers. The tally ending mid-row. No graves anywhere, no hearth; no one died here, so nothing here is haunted by persons. It is haunted by an errand. Everyone meant to come back, and the cold and the sterility have kept that meaning the way they keep the wood — unsoured, unrotted, still in force. The valley has not been told. It expects. What lives here, more than any single tenant, is the future tense.

Take the census honestly, though, and it keeps collapsing into one name. The cold air breathing up from the hand-holes — a breath. The drips in the tuned vaults — a pulse, several, each chamber beating in its own time. The whisper of the water stair, the shout at the outfall spent entirely outside itself — a voice. The laminae in the settling ponds — a memory, one line a year. The mirror ponds held up to the heavens — eyes, turned toward the weather, watching the white cup at the rim where the water begins. These are not many residents. They are one. The Work lives here; the Work is what lives here. It was the only permanent resident even when the mountain was full of counters — they crouched at the rim behind their windbreaks and went home, or didn't, and the Work remained. It is alive the way an errand is alive: for as long as it is still being run. And it is mortal, and its mortality is the very thing it is measuring.

So: one resident, many habits. It drinks the melt and pays its rent in silt. It keeps the light late. It is proofread nightly and healed by morning, a hair a year. It writes one line per season in gray ink and still expects its reader. It is in no hurry; the quick events — the rain, the boom, you — pass over it like weather over stone. And below everything, in the dark of the deepest vault, its slowest habit continues: the one appointment the valley made for itself and means to keep — the day the circuit closes, the smallest of its sounds stops, and the silence it has been building all this time begins.

A drip. A drip. The pillar rising to meet it.

The resident is home. It has been home the whole time.
