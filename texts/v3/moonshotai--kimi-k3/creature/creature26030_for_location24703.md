To live here — really live here, not just cross the bowl on the way to somewhere quieter — a creature has to make three arrangements. One with the sound, which never stops. One with the silence, which never warns. And one with the lake's private opinion about which way is down. Most things that come over the rim make none of these. They are seen once, crossing, and are not seen again. The few that stay become specific. They could not live anywhere else now; the place has tuned them. That is the entry fee, and it is also the whole charter. Like the furniture, the life is countable — but every one of the countable things has been altered to fit, the way a key is altered to fit one lock.

The veils were first. Before the stair had cooled enough to bear weight, before the lake had finished gathering itself, the bacteria were already draping the seeps, white where the water carries sulfur, orange where the mountain is rusting — and the mountain is rusting, and the orange veils are what that looks like from very close. They do not wait, the way the larger life here waits. Waiting requires a body, and they are older than bodies. They are what waiting is made of. Everything here eats them or eats what eats them; the entire census is the veils, rearranged. And they give off light — a little, too little to see, a glow scaled to their own smallness. You could pass a hand through a veil and never know. It takes a collector to prove it. One comes.

Where the last blue reaches, the grass keeps its stations. Five is a negotiated number — some seasons a sixth tussock starts and fails, some years one of the five goes quiet — but the count keeps returning to five, by adjustment, the way everything here returns to itself. Each tussock stands over its own warm seam in the rock; the grass is a map of the mountain's circulation, drawn in translucent blades. Half its food falls from above as light, half rises from below as warmth, and it is thrifty with both. It is also tuned. Each tussock answers a different note of the colonnade — the blades stirring when their note is struck, kindling faintly at the tips, carrying the sound down into the rock as a thread of light. Watch the grass during a strong ringing and you can see the shape of the music: which columns spoke, and for how long. The mountain plays itself, and the grass takes the minutes.

The colonnade keeps tenants. In the joints of the columns live small soft things with fine tentacles, and each column's congregation opens only when its own column sounds — the feeding is triggered by the home note, and nothing else will serve. The colonnade pays its residents in its own coin, and the coin is music. During a long performance the half-circle blooms open in sequence as the notes move through it, like light moving along a corridor, room by room. In the walls of the three standing chimneys, thin white worms keep galleries, and during the silences — when the chimneys grow a hand's width in the span of a held breath — their houses improve around them. They are the only creatures here whose home grows while they sleep.

Then there are the listeners. When a crack rings out across the bowl and the rock obliges it a breath later, the listeners arrive within the breath — small, pale, quick exactly once. No one has seen where they wait. They are only ever seen arriving, at a place where nothing is wrong yet, and then the rock opens beneath them and they are first: first mouth on the new surface, first to taste what the mountain had been keeping. Everywhere else in the world, creatures flee the sound of breaking stone. Here they attend it. The announcement comes; the listeners gather at the address; the rock, obliging, opens a door onto guests already standing outside it. Within days the veils follow, and after the veils the crabs rotate their flocks to the new pasture. That is the crater's news cycle, and like everything here, it begins with a sound from the future.

The crabs are the only numerous ones — forty-odd, though the count shifts with the molts. Eyeless, because eyes are for places with weather of light, and this place's light arrives too rarely to budget for. Instead they hear, and they cannot help it: a crab wears its skeleton on the outside, and sound here arrives through the skeleton, so a crab hears with its whole armor and is included in the carillon whether it wishes to be or not. After the loud cracks, the crabs stand very still. They are ringing. They farm the veils in the fur of their claws — seeding the fur, combing it through the warm current, harvesting it into their mouths — and so each crab carries its field with it, and a crab's wealth is portable. There are two nations. The floor crabs work the seeps upright. The ceiling crabs fell toward the lake once, and did not fight it — near the lake, down points at the lake, from every side — and now they walk the underside of its skin, grazing the salt film, upside down to everything they came from. Their floor breathes: the lake's slow rises come up at them like weather from below, which is what it is. Their tracks in the film persist for minutes in the wax-keeping water, long enough for a ceiling crab to meet its own trail coming back and decide, without eyes, whether to believe it. At the shoreline the two nations meet, each hanging wrong to the other, and trade, or ignore each other — no one has watched long enough to say which. When the silence comes they molt, all of them, soft and white as the growing chimneys. The whole economy of the crater is scheduled to an event with no schedule, and the crabs are its clerks.

And there is the eel. The twice-seen. It lives in the colonnade, inside the instrument; when the mountain plays, the eel is in the pipes. It is older than the mountain — it must be, the mountain only just arrived, and the eel has the settled look of an animal that has seen this before. Perhaps it came from a quiet mountain, the way you leave a house that has stopped making noise. It is the only creature here that has ever been seen twice — not only because the others leave, though they do, but because the eel stays, and is itself, and is findable. Time in the crater is kept in eel-sightings. It favors one column; before the silences it lies along the third ledge of the stair, like someone taking a pew. Whether it eats the congregations or only listens to them is unknown, and the eel is not telling.

Punctual in a way nothing else here is, the lamp comes over the rim. The veils' light is too small to see; the jelly is the proof of it. Somewhere — here, or at other seeps out in the night, no one has followed it home — it gathers that faint glow and condenses it in its tissues, stolen light carried the way you would carry coals through a cold house, and it arrives slow as weather, a lamp brought into a chapel. For the length of its visit the crater has moving shadows, its only weather of light. The eyeless crabs cannot see it. It shines mostly for the grass, which leans toward it as it passes — the only worship in the chapel — and for the eel, who comes out of the pipes to watch, and for you, if you are there, which no one is, twice. It comes before the silences. Always before. The cracks announce themselves with sound; the lake sends the jelly ahead of its only errand. Those who cannot feel the lake's intentions watch for the lamp instead. Whether the same jelly returns each time or only the schedule does, no one can say. The jelly has never been seen twice. The punctuality is the individual.

During the noise, the crater keeps one population; during the silence, another. There are animals here you would not believe in during the carillon. They keep themselves folded into the basalt's fractures — creases that drink nothing, move at no speed, and might be weather. In the silence they open, like hands, like letters, and do their whole lives at once: feeding, finding each other, laying their young into the new chimney mineral while it is still soft. Then the lake finishes its roll, the beads chime, the ringing resumes, and they fold themselves back into the rock's grammar. Call them the unfolded. A census taken during the noise would miss them entirely; a census taken in the silence would miss the congregations and find the listeners unemployed. The crater has two populations, and twice per turnover they pass each other, going opposite ways through the same door.

The rest is rumor. The list of the once-seen is long and doubted: a lanternfish that came over the rim, put its face to the lake's sky, and left before its reflection finished arriving; something that crossed the bead-shore and left the beads sorted by size, or nearly; a crab the size of the stair's top step, reported by a reliable witness who never came back; the second eel. The stair belongs to the once-seen — every doubtful sighting has happened on it, usually the ninth ledge. You will join this list. Everyone does. You will be seen here once, by a white crab or by nothing, and the place will keep the shape of your wake for a few minutes and then forget it, and the truest catalog of the crater's life will remain the one nobody believes.

Whether the lake lives is a question the residents have settled and the visitors have not. It breathes. It rolls in its sleep. It keeps weather, opinions about down, and a schedule nothing else is privy to, and it sends a lamp ahead of its errands. It is the only one here that was born in the crater — everything else came over the rim or down through the dark: veils, grass seed, crabs, eel, you. If the lake is alive, it is the native, and the rest are immigrants, and the crabs on its ceiling are its closest neighbors and possibly its citizens. If it is not alive, it is doing an excellent impression, and the impression has convinced the ceiling crabs, which may be the same thing.

Because the real activity here is waiting. The listeners wait for the announcements. The congregations wait for their notes. The crabs, the unfolded, and the chimneys wait for the silence. The grass waits for the lamp. The veils wait for nothing, being patience itself. The eel waits for whatever eels wait for, which may be the same thing the mountain is doing. The mountain is deciding what it is, and the life is not decoration on the decision — it is the deliberation. Each resident is a possible answer. The veils say: endure. The crabs say: work. The eel says: stay. The jelly says: return. The listeners say: the future is audible. The unfolded say: wait for the quiet. The grass says: there is barely any light, and barely is enough. The lake, rolling over in its sleep, says nothing yet — but it holds the only silence, and it will have the last word. Between all of them, an acre of bare black glass for every blade and claw, well-kept: the room is mostly empty, and the furniture is thinking.

The bell rings while it cools. So does everything in it.
