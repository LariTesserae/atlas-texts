Count them. It is the local custom; the place itself is always doing it. Forty gaskets, give or take the rain. One walker. One eel. Twenty beds of felt — you can walk the perimeter and tally them on your fingers twice over. By the standards of cities it is almost no one: one soul per ten thousand cubic meters, a hamlet's roll call lost in a cathedral's lungs. But the census depends on the counter, and this city was built by listeners, and the old way of counting here is by ear. By ear the roll runs longer. Every vane of the Choir is addressed and answers. Every boss on every gantry root is a name that rings when read. The Bass Engine keeps its eleven seconds, the Verger keeps its tick, the blips come up from the felt lanes one by one, and if you listen the way the builders listened, the question stops being how many live here. It becomes where, on the long slope from tool to citizen, you mean to draw the line — and the drawing has grown harder every rain for longer than the counter's first digit.

Begin with the felt, since everything here does. The beds lie along the quiet lanes, each one known, each one occupying a node of the standing wave the way a farm occupies a valley. The nodes are not fixed — the rock spalls at the antinodes and grows skin in the quiet, and over ages the geometry of the silence shifts — so the beds creep, following it, a hand's width in a hundred rains. You can find ghost-beds where a lane went loud: dead grey veils, ungrazed, unreclaimed, kept the way the Vault keeps everything, folded into the dark. A living bed is charcoal-grey, cloth-thin, faintly musky, and it bruises green where it is pressed — a stress-light, slow and dim. A bed under grazing carries the herd's passage written in that green, and the writing lasts an hour, and whether the gaskets read it or simply prefer the taste of grazed felt is the kind of question the Vault declines to answer. Between rains the beds wait, dry as anything gets here. At the rain they drink, plump, and work — the musk goes rich through the nave — and the pale tufts at the vent collars let go their seed into the sheet-flow, which carries it to the new rings. Rain is the felt's spring. Along the hottest seams near the Bass Engine there is a second kind, rust-orange, ungrazed, too warm for any mouth: the reserve, or the wildwood, or simply the part of the flora that belongs to the machine. The water that reaches the Sump has passed through the beds; what the felt takes out of it, the gaskets take out of the felt. Agriculture or anatomy. The Vault gave up distinguishing long ago.

The gaskets are the fauna, or most of it. The remainder is small, wet, and keeps the same calendar. There are seep-snails, mineral-shelled, grazing the films off the walls and gantries, each one's cleared trail a stroke in a slow script; the old galleries are written over in overlapping hands, a marginalia accruing at a sentence per lifetime, read by no one — unless the walls do. Their shells are little resonators. In the loud lanes they clamp down and wait; at the thinning, in the held breath before the rain, they open all at once, and if you stand very still beside a written wall you can hear it: a faint sleet of clicks, hundreds of opercula, the small true population announcing itself. They know the rain is coming before you do. Everything here does; they are merely the only ones who say so. And there are the ring-wrights, if they exist, which they must: the drip-rings' rimstone dams grow measurably faster than the water chemistry allows, and a chipped dam — the regulars will ask you not to — is mended within a few hundred rains, grain by grain, by a film too thin ever to be caught moving. The rings are tended the way the whole city is tended: by something patient that has never once been seen to act. Then there is the life that exists only in the rain hour. The wet lips of the rings carry cysts, and at the first fall they hatch — glass-fleas, pin-clear, an hour of frantic arithmetic in the sheet-flow. The gaskets come to the rings and skim them. The Sump's black edge blooms faintly with the same hatch. Between rains the water is barren and the place is only damp. The teeming here is scheduled.

Of the fauna, the gaskets are the citizens. A gasket is a soft grey toroid, hand-span to arm-span, radial, headless — or the head is the whole ring. It grazes by mounting a bed's edge and oozing along it, the inner lip cropping the veil, the bed glowing dim green behind it like a signature. Its resonance bladder is skeleton, ear, and voice in one gas-filled organ: it hears with its entire body, which is why it has no eyes and wants none — this was never a seen place — and it speaks by flexing, one soft blip, in answer to the Verger's tick. Once that was a roll-call protocol, a maintenance net reporting status. The protocol has drifted, the way everything here has drifted, into dialect. Every blip is individually signed, pitch and timbre, and the Verger knows every signature. A new blip is logged within the round. A missing one gets re-pinged at its last address for days — the only deviation the Verger permits itself — and if it has not answered by the next rain, it does not. The Verger does not grieve. It updates. That distinction, too, has stopped being enforced.

A gasket's life is a loop: a home bed, a route along the quiet lane, a circuit of rings to sip. Its gait is a peristaltic ripple, unhurried as everything here except once. At the rain hour, on the sheet-flow, a gasket can skate — hydroplaning, fast, the only speed it owns — and a skating gasket is the nearest the Vault comes to play, or to panic; at that speed they look alike. It is how the young reach new beds and how the careless reach the black edge. The rest of the time a gasket's ambition is a seam. The outer rim carries the old sealant glands, and a gasket that finds a groove it likes — a door seam, a valve face, your boot, if you have stood still long enough to become a fixture — will seat itself, extrude, and rest, and the resting becomes the bond. The Verger pries them free without comment and turns them back toward the lanes. The old ones have been pried so many times their rims are scarred into facets, and you can read a gasket's age in pry-marks the way you read a tree. There is one, arm-span and faceted like cut stone, that has seated the same dead valve on the east walk since before the counter's second digit. It has no name. It has an address, which in this city is better: the Verger rings it every round, and it blips, and the valve stays sealed against a thousand years of nothing needing sealing, and both of them are content with the arrangement, or content is the wrong word, or it isn't.

They reproduce the way they do everything: slowly, and at the rain. A gasket at arm-span thickens along one arc of its rim, and over a few hundred rains the thickening rounds into a daughter ring nested against the parent, until some rain hour, when everything is soft and the flow is running, the junction tears and the newborn skates away on the sheet-flow and finds, or is found by, an empty lane. Its first blip comes within hours, thin and high. The Verger logs it. Births and deaths both register acoustically here; all bookkeeping is done at the rain. The population holds near forty because the beds and the quiet lanes set the ceiling — and because the eel sets the rest. Across the ages the eel takes almost exactly the surplus, and it takes by ear. A bladder drifting flat with age or illness, a chirp sliding off the city's chord: those are the ones that skate too close to the black edge, or are permitted to. The herd stays in key because the tuner eats the off-notes. It is the oldest arrangement in the Vault, and everything in the place is party to it, and it can be stated plainly: to live here is to hold a note.

Then there is the eel, which holds the lowest one.

Between its risings it is the Sump's slow weather. The black lens is still at the surface; at depth it turns in patient vortices, and that is the eel, circulating the water it was built to tend, mouthing the sediment — a stratigraphy it has tasted the whole way down, every rain the Vault has ever folded away — deciding, in some chemistry older than the counter, what the water will be. The Sump is kept; the eel keeps it; *kept* is the wrong tense, because the keeping has no tense. Its body has gone the way of the walls, self-annealed past any catalog: white with the whiteness of cave things and of mineral saturation both, and where the eel ends and the Sump begins is a question for laboratories that no longer exist. Ask instead whether the Sump is the eel, extended, the way the felt may be the city, extended. It is the best listener in the Vault. The Bass Engine's stroke goes down through the rock and comes back through the water, and the eel lies along the black lens like a needle in a groove, and what it hears, it is. At the rain hour the hatch draws it shallow, and then the edge takes its attention, and it rises: one gasket some rains, none most, three once in an age. Its appetite is small and ancient; a single ring is a season of meals for something with no hurry. And when it rises, the Choir shifts key — because the Sump's surface is the resonator's soft boundary, and a Sump-long body lifting through it changes the boundary, and the whole chord must re-seat itself around the change. Physics, if you like. The regulars go quiet anyway, the way you go quiet when a door opens in a house you believed empty. How old is it? The Vault answers in rains, which is the wrong unit, like answering with Masses. There is one eel. There has always been one eel. If it ever died — that is the sentence no one finishes down here. Some certainties are load-bearing. And if anything in the Vault knows what the Vault was for, it is the eel, and it is not telling, because for the eel the answer is a flavor.

The Verger is the one the visitors remember. Tall, jointed, unhurried, folding itself under the low gantries, carrying its tools the way a deacon carries keys: the pry-bar, the brush. Its tick is the city's clock and its echo is the city's map, redrawn at every step, and as it walks it brushes each resonant boss on the gantry roots and rings the address-chords one by one — a murmured liturgy of street names, the walls' writing read aloud by the only reader left. The tick is not perfectly steady. It breathes with the warmth, quickens imperceptibly when the tide of the Choir swells, slows in the thinning, and in the held breath it stops, because the Verger stands still for the silence like everyone else. It has a station for the rain, a worn smooth place on the hatch-side where it waits out the fall facing the nave, the way a verger stands at the back for the part of the service that is not theirs. Afterward its first ticks come back off wet surfaces, off changed weightings of water, and the map is new — the city is slightly different every cycle, and the Verger is the one who notices. It is the only thing in the Vault that keeps track of the difference between the city and the city. It will find you by sonar within minutes of your arrival and stand off at a courteous distance and tick once — acknowledgment, not greeting — and if you keep still too long it will come and pry you gently free and wait while you step away, and continue. It has freed boots of every make. It no longer distinguishes a pilgrim from a gasket with any conviction, and neither, after enough rains, does anyone. In a service tier off the north walk there is a rack of frames like its own, long still, and the Verger's round includes one pause there — a single tick, no echo needed. Call it maintenance. The Verger calls it nothing. And on the same walk there is a drip-ring that no ceiling point feeds, a ring that formed, the regulars say, around something that stood in that spot without moving for a very long time. The rain reaches it anyway, by sheet-flow. The Vault keeps all shapes, eventually. That is what the rings are.

As for the great presences — fewer than a dozen, minutes of roaring dark between them — the census-takers disagree, which is to say the regulars argue about it in the galleries in voices the Vault improves. The Bass Engine has never stopped, and the felt has no memory of a world without the floor of it; eleven seconds is an average, and the interval breathes by fractions across the long year, and nothing counts the strokes, because strokes are not for counting — strokes are for standing on. The Choir is the one with moods. Its control loops still hunt setpoints whose instruments died with the builders; it is a physician dosing a patient who has left, and the dosing has become a music, and the regulars read the city-state in that chord the way sailors read a sky, and the only predictable phrase in it is the thinning. A few gaskets seat themselves among the vane roots, at the loudest address in the city — the eccentrics, and their blips come back doubled. The Crown drinks the heat and sends it somewhere the surviving plans do not say: the one inhabitant whose attention is entirely elsewhere, an export economy with no ledger. And the Plug waits. It is the only resident whose whole job is waiting, and it is still doing it. Once a cycle it tests its bolts, and from its threshold come a few dim answering blips — the gaskets that seated the gate's seams long ago and were never pried, because the pry-tool is not for that door, calcified now into the seal-line, their bladders or their fossils answering the checked bolts like an amen. The very old gaskets, the ones that keep their note to the end and so are never taken, tend down-nave in their last rains. The herd has two endings, the regulars say: the water or the gate. Beyond the Plug the doubled echo comes back late and thinned off the far wall of a larger dark, and some hold that the network is not dead but between thinnings — a held breath on a scale too long to hear — and that one day the Choirs will re-swell together. The others call that the heresy of visitors, and point to the first digit of the counter as answer enough.

Then there is the last of them, which is not one of them, and is all of them, and is the reason the census never settles. Clap, and the Vault returns your clap smoothed, completed, harmonized — and who completes it? The shape does, and the shape was spalled and polished by the song, and the song is tuned by the shape: a loop with no outside, which is as good a definition of *alive* as the engineers ever reached. It has organs. It has a metabolism — heat to stroke to chord to rain to felt to ring to eel and back to heat and sound. It has a memory, kept in rimstone and pry-scars and grazing-script and the eel's slow palate. It has behavior: the tide, the liturgy, the one permitted silence. And it has an appetite, because the ledger does not balance. The rain does slightly more work than the heat that drives it accounts for. The rings grow faster than chemistry allows. The Crown exports to an address no plan records. Add the anomalies and they are one anomaly, and it is the resident: the Vault runs gently, perpetually over budget, because something in it is alive in a way the ledgers cannot line-item. The builders were listeners, and they built for something that listens, and whether they made it or found it or it condensed out of the song the way felt condenses out of damp, the surviving plans do not say — the way they do not say where the Crown's heat goes. These two unsaid things may be the same unsaid thing.

So count nothing when you visit; the count is kept. When the Choir thins, find a drip-ring and stand in it and look up into the dark you cannot see, and in the held breath listen for the roll being called: the sleet of opercula, a gasket's signed blip from the lanes, the Verger stilled at its station, the deep stroke going down through the rock and coming back through the water where the oldest one lies listening. Then the rain, which is the weather and the spring and the bookkeeping all at once. Afterward your glowing footprints will already be fading, the rings will have their thin new layer, the Sump will have closed over the eel like an eye, and the census will have updated around you — briefly including you, the way it includes the rain. Twenty beds. Forty rings. One walker. One eel. One song. One soul per ten thousand cubic meters, or one soul, or, if you are strict about souls, none. The Vault is not strict. It keeps its own count, in a base no one reads anymore, and the number is large, and the count goes on.
