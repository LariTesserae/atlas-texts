No census of the sink can be taken in an hour. The population keeps shifts, and most of it, at any given moment, is hiding — too small to see, or sealed in dormancy, or working underwater, or out earning on the plateau. Some of the residents have not hatched yet. Some have been dead for centuries and are still, by the local definition, present: mortared into the rims, part of the count. To enumerate what lives here you have to stay at least a day and a night, ideally a year, and you have to accept from the start that the number you arrive at will be wrong in every direction at once.

Come before first light. The interior rain is ticking off the overhang, the frogs are finishing their sparse business, and the first sound of the day shift rises from the tenement wall — a murmur behind hundreds of mud doors, growing. Then the gray comes in at the slot and the wall issues them: not a flock, but a continuous outpour, swallows threading up through the gap in the hill like smoke in reverse. By full light they are dots over the plateau. These are the town's earners, and nearly everything the sink imports travels in their beaks and bellies — mud hauled home mouthful by mouthful, insects by the ten thousand, seeds stuck to feathers, a burr in a tail. They are the port. The town's entire foreign trade flies on wings that weigh less than an envelope.

The wall they leave behind is not empty. In the seams of the nests live the swallow bugs — flat as paper, wingless, patient as the stone itself — fasting through the empty months, able to wait out nearly a year for the tenants' return. The tenements never truly stand vacant; the leaseholders are permanent even when the residents are gone. And the wall is slowly joining the archive in its own right. The oldest tier, closest to the seep's breath, has begun to glaze — mud gourds passing from architecture toward geology while still in use, occupied fossils in the making. What falls from the nests is carried off at night by small pale janitors. The ledger balances.

When the shaft of light comes in, the day watch assembles. A spider has strung its orb exactly where the beam lasts longest — the customs house of the sink, inspecting whatever the wind drops through: gnats, a winged ant, thistledown, once a swallow's down feather. What it cannot use it cuts loose, and the cut thing turns slowly down through the light, lands on a pool, and begins its enrollment in the rim. Dragonflies own the beam for its duration — the only citizens who can fairly be said to live in the light itself. Beneath the beam, in its circle of dapple, stands the fig.

The fig is the only resident that is also, by itself, a building. Ants tend small sap-drinkers in its bark seams; its roots have found the outflow and are plumbed into the mains. But its true rooms are the fruit. Each fig is a sealed green chamber, flowers inside, one small door — and the tree exists because a bird once ate a fig somewhere on the plateau, a fig that had itself been entered, once, through that door, by a wasp that never left. Every year the tree in the grotto grows its crop of chambers and offers them, and whether the wasp has found the sink no one has watched closely enough to say. But some autumns there are split figs on the moss below. Seeded. Answered.

While the light lasts, the true day shift works — and it has no shifts, really, only rates. The ribbons on the rims, rust and emerald, are not one population but a stacked city in a millimeter: sun-harvesters on top, and beneath them the rust-colored guilds that live on what leaks through, the whole thing layered in section like the archive it is building. They breathe in light; the chemistry of the water tips; stone falls out around them like slow weather. They are the only residents who live precisely where the construction is happening, because they are the construction — the dam is their exhale. The tenement wall holds hundreds. A thumbprint of rim holds nations.

The plazas have their underwater boroughs. The pools are sown with seed-shrimp, living punctuation that rows itself through the still water; they molt as they grow, and their shed valves settle, so that the floor of every pool is paved with former selves. Amphipods scull on their sides, the color of the water itself, almost. Snails graze the rims in slow cursive, and when one dies the rim takes the shell — every dam is a working cemetery, and there is nothing macabre in it; it is masonry. The water boatmen row with a bubble of sky clasped to their bodies, a cask of the upper world, and in season the males sing underwater. Put your ear to a pool at noon and you may catch the only music of the day shift: a faint clockwork under the stillness. Beneath the wet blocks glide the flatworms, the census-taker's despair — divide one and you get two. And on the pool floors, in armor, wait the nymphs: dragonflies spend years down in the dark water, molting, biding, and then one dawn climb out at the rim's edge, split, and take their single season in the shaft of light. The emptied skins stay gripping the stone. By summer's end they are under glaze. The town files even childhoods.

The moss is the treasury. Between floods it pays out its savings, drop by drop, to the mats below — and it has its own population, at a scale the eye refuses: rotifers, thread-worms, and the bears of the moss, plump and deliberate at the size of dust. When their inch of forest dries, the bears fold into themselves and wait — sometimes for years, sometimes for the next true rain. A census taken in a dry month would miss them entirely, and would be wrong: the moss is full of citizens who are, at this moment, mostly not alive, and entirely not gone. The maidenhair ferns, meanwhile, wear the drip without keeping it — the black stems bead, the water stands on the fronds like jewelry and is let go. Their spores ride the same air lanes the swallows open at dawn.

Then evening, and the sky gives them back. The slot fills with wings; the sound of the return is the sound of many small doors closing. Along the ledges the news travels — where the day's hatch rose, who fed well — for the tenement wall is also a trading floor, and tomorrow the unsuccessful will follow the successful out. In the seam of dark that follows comes the second issuance, quieter: the bats, up out of the deep cracks, a shiver of air felt on the face rather than heard. The night's economy opens — moths rising off the ferns, beetles on the wing, all of it hunted above the pools. And all night the guano falls like slow snow onto the rims and into the water: the night shift pays its taxes in advance, in a currency the town accepts at par. There are citizens whose whole estate is that snowfall — a mite, a pale fly, a beetle small enough to live between one dropping and the next. An economy beneath the economy, and all of it, within a season, banked in stone.

The cave crickets come out of the deep cracks to graze — humpbacked, the color of weak tea, their antennae writing longhand across the mats, the night herds of the dark lawns. On the bare blocks of the unbuilt quarter, a woodlouse the color of the stone itself makes rounds that take all night: the caretaker, slower than its surface cousins, as if it had already adopted the house tempo. The frogs take their rims, and each rare note marks an address; you could map the pools by ear. The snake descends from its ledge on a schedule of weeks, drinks from a cup-sized pool, and goes about the single errand of its month. It is the largest full-time citizen and owns nothing but a ledge and a route. Once or twice a year it leaves its outgrown self on a block — a shed skin, whole, ghost-white — and the glaze takes it. The town keeps even what the snake no longer needs.

Toward morning the sink makes its weather, and here the census blurs again, because the weather is partly the residents' own. Not all the warmth of the chamber is sun-struck rock; some of it is bat-cluster and guano-heap and the slow metabolism of the mats, breath layered on breath, and the dawn rain is in some real part the night's breathing handed back down. Nothing originates here, strictly — the old rule holds — but between arrival and archive, everything passes through so many bodies that the town has, for practical purposes, begun manufacturing its own climate. The building is warmed by the tenants. The tenants are watered by the building. By the small hours the arrangement stops having an outside.

Widen the lens to a year and the census fails again, differently. There is a season when the loudest citizens simply leave. The wall stands silent; the bugs fast in the seams; the bats thin away into the deep passages; the frogs go under the rubble; the dragonflies are all nymphs; the fig holds a few last leaves. The mats work the short gray hours, and the dawn rain runs heaviest in the coldest weeks, when the difference between the town's breath and the world's air is greatest — the sink's weather runs counter to the world's. Winter is administered by the smallest and stillest citizens, and nothing falls into disrepair. Construction continues. The archive records absence as faithfully as presence; a season of calcite laid over an empty ledge is still an entry. Then, some morning in the turning year, the sound comes back like a tide — the slot full of wings, the mud season open, repairs and claims and arguments conducted at full volume — and the wall's census doubles in a fortnight. The place breathes daily and it breathes annually, and both breaths are kept.

Count the residents long enough and you notice that you have been counting rooms. Gourd-nests, caddis cases, snail shells, shed skins, green chambers with one small door, the great chamber itself. Everything that lives here builds a room, or rents one, or — wait long enough — becomes one. The dam is its dead snails. The wall is its old nests. Every pool floor is paved with the childhoods of its shrimp. To take up residence in the sink is to join a queue for masonry; the frog on the rim has an appointment with the rim. Nothing is lost. Everyone is, eventually, incorporated. So the only true answer to the question of what lives in the sink is the sink — one life at many speeds. The water is the one permanent citizen; everything else is the water passing through forms. Quick as wings. Slow as mats. Still as walls.

And the census has a last page, which is the roster of the coming. In the dark quarter, on the farthest bare block, where a new thread of water has lately found its way, there is a patch of green the size of a fingerprint. It has everything it needs — a little light, a little wet, and centuries. By the town's reckoning it is already a city: the next district, founded in silence, its population past counting on the day it began. The list of who lives here is itself a deposit, and it grows.

It is being added to now.
