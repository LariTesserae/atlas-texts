Ask at any cistern-camp what lives on the Keep and the keeper will first want to know whether you count the plant once, or in every piece — because most of what moves up here might be the plant, wearing a body for a while. The useful census, as the camps teach it, runs by voice. Every living thing on the plateau has answered the same question — how do you keep from being found? — and there are only three answers. Be louder than everything. Be quieter than anything. Or live somewhere the question is never asked.

**The floor of sound.** The needle-singers are the first answer, and the base of everything. A singer is built like a split seed, and the seam is the instrument: it draws one wing-case across the other and holds the resulting note its whole life, a bell that believes in a single pitch. That pitch is fixed at hatching by the temperature of the mat that week, so the drone is not a monotone but a chord with weather in it — the old people can hear the season in the pressure at their temples, can say *cold hatch in the west* the way you'd read a cloud. No one has ever found a nest. They hatch from pores in the crust, sip at the seams of the dew-bladders, live their weeks, and are taken back within days of dying; in the Undergreen their husks make whole strata, silver pages between the green ones. The camps string the dried husks on the rattle-poles that mark the roads, so that a traveled route whispers to itself in wind even when no one is on it. A leaning line of poles, half-digested, means a road falling out of use — abandoned roads go quiet before they vanish, which everyone agrees is the saddest sound there is, being barely a sound at all.

They falter one at a time, and that is the whole early-warning system of the world. A hole in the drone has a shape and a speed, and trained ears read it the way you'd read ripples: a fast narrow hole is something small and running; a wide slow hole that seems to breathe is the other thing. Some people are born able to do this without effort — the thin-eared, the camps call them, the only people who listen to holes. Every camp wants one and is slightly uneasy around the one it has, because a person who spends their life attending to absence becomes strange company. And when the bloom comes, the singers smell the front days before it arrives and sharpen by a step, so that walking east in season you hear the key of the world lift under your feet like a grade in the road.

Beneath the walking-skin, the felt is loose, and it is a city. Felt-runners — pale, blind, fist-sized, beyond counting — graze the fiber-ends and tend the tunnels, and they are drawn to warmth: a sleeping camp draws them up under the bedrolls, and in the morning the ground beneath the sleepers is springier and sweeter, groomed all night. The people say sleeping is a service you do the floor, and that a camp that wakes sore has been bad guests. After a thunder passes, the churned crust boils briefly with small lives come up to the disturbance, and following a herd at the gleaning distance is a foraging strategy for a dozen kinds of animal and for people too: crushed fiber, spilled water, dung in the short window before the mat drinks it back.

**The islands.** Each open pond — and they are days apart, the only open water — is an island with citizens found at that one address and no other on the continent. A skater that walks the surface film of one water and exists nowhere else; a glassy shrimp-thing unique to another; each pond rimmed with its own lip of bloom, and in the bloom its own small crowd of lives. The people name the waters for their citizens — *red-skater water*, *glass-shrimp pond* — and a well-keeper's duties include knowing her pond's population and mourning any loss, because a pond that loses a citizen has been visited wrongly. The citizens arrive by theft. When a drifter sets its tendrils down into a cistern, the pond-lives swarm the lines like gangplanks, and after a great storm fills a new basin the skaters appear within weeks, unwitnessed, delivered by thieves. Every rim of bloom is a genetic memory of every drifter that ever drank there.

**The percussion.** The thunders are the second answer to the question, and they are also the plumbing, and they know it the way a heart knows it. A treader's pad is broad as a threshing floor and rimmed with bristles that taste water through thirty feet of felt; the herds hear the cisterns through their feet from miles off, and when one calls — the foghorn note, half of it below hearing — you learn of it first from the well-water, which trembles in answer. Well-keepers read the trembling of their own ponds for herd-mood the way sailors read sky. The files are a mile long and ordered by memory: the lead cow carries the loop in her head, inherited, and when she dies the second leads and the loop shifts by some subtle degree, so the great migration circuits drift across the centuries like slow rivers. Their worn lanes show on the flats as faint half-healed lines — herd-roads, the closest thing to geography the sameness allows, though everyone knows better than to trust them; the herd-roads move.

Calves are born walking, and a calf must find its voice in its first hour, because a mute animal is a hole and everything knows what comes to holes. A herd hides its quiet ones — the young, the sick, the failing old — in the middle of the file and drums the mat around them and bellows over them, a body of sound carrying its silent members, and this is exactly where the Quiet tests first: it listens for the covered hole, the place where the noise is doing the most work. What does a hill eat? The Keep feeds them at the bloom-front and the vane-tips and can afford it; a body feeds its heart first.

The people attend them at the gleaning distance and never hunt a healthy one — how would you, and why would you — but a dying treader is accompanied. Camps converge at a respectful remove, drumming softly to keep the rest of the file moving, and when the animal falls and the Quiet has taken its share, the people take the remainder in days of urgent festival labor, because the mat will finish the carcass within a season. Bone for the well-ladders and the drum-frames, hide for harness, and for a while a dead thunder is a *geography* — camps navigate by it, *left at the bone-hill* — until the green erases the map. As for the steering: the herds know the false-panic is theater. The best steerers say the thunders can tell, and choose to believe, because being moved is a relief to them — the lie is a service. But they grade the liars. A herd obey one camp's drums promptly and another's not at all, and their memory outlasts the people's: offend an old cow as a child and her granddaughter's herd will still be snubbing your camp when you are grey. The mat digests all record; the Undergreen keeps the rest; but above ground, the thunders are the long memory.

**The silence.** And then the other answer. The Quiet is the only thing on the plateau that has never been heard — everything else up here has been heard, and that is the whole of its description. Pale as a drowned moon, fringed like frost, soft as ash: the fringe drinks sound, so that within a body-length of it the world goes dead, and the moving hole in the drone is simply the map of its radius. It does not chase. It arrives — at the covered hole in the file, at the sleeper too far from the fire-circle, at the traveler who stopped singing because the road was long and the heart was heavy. It takes the throat first. It eats the voice, and then the heart, and leaves the rest, and the windfall feeds the mat and any camp bold enough to scavenge a fresh kill in full voice.

No one knows how many there are. No two have ever been seen together; no young one, no den, no carcass has ever been found; the only spoor is grey down caught on the stalks, like ash from a fire no one lit, and a swath of singers gone out. The old people hold that the Keep grows them, one to a province, down in the warm dark where the drifters hatch, and grows another only when the herds slow — and nobody wants it gone. A province without a Quiet is a sick province: the thunders loiter, the cisterns sour, the bloom comes thin. So the stillness reports on the dusk-mail are not only warnings; they are reassurance. The shepherd is on the hill. The camps keep it the way they keep the wells, and they teach their children the three answers — be still, be loud, be gone below — and they sing their grieving through in shifts for weeks, not for comfort first but for cover, because sorrow is a hole and everyone knows it. A camp full of snorers, the old people say dryly, is a safe camp.

Once in a long while one of them learns that camps are slow holes, and then the province does the unthinkable. Dozens of camps converging, sky-iron and drums, every thunder in the range driven across it for weeks to starve the thing of the slow and the sick, until its stillness frays and they kill it or walk it over the Edge into the dry. The year takes the name. And the years after take the price: herds stalling, wells turning, thirst remembered — until the deep grows another. It is in the year-list, that decade. Everyone can recite it. No one is eager to repeat it.

**The exempt.** The drifters are the third answer: they live where the question is never asked, because the Quiet cannot climb. They are the only citizens permitted silence, and the camps go quiet themselves to watch a drift cross at dusk — sails the size of fields lit green-gold, tendrils trailing like slow rain that never lands — and the hush in the watching camp is safe, and everyone feels the strangeness of that: the envy of the legged for the floated. They hatch from blisters in the warm deep layers and never stop growing, and the ancients are known individuals, province by province, recognized by their scars and their patched bladders, given names, reported on the dusk-mail like weather: *the Ragged King over the reed-basin, westering.* They live longer than the camps' memory of them, and they pay for their water-theft in the only currency that matters up here: spores between the provinces, pond-lives between the waters, the far-flung body kept one body.

The fishery is at dusk at the cistern rims, thorn harpoon and long line, and there is one law above others: lance soft. A bladder that goes with a bang silences a province's singers for a mile around, and a sudden bang followed by silence is exactly the signature of the other thing — a botched lancing sets a hundred drums to panic-beating and empties the dusk-mail of everything else. Taken well, a drifter is a communal harvest: the bladder for water-skins, so that every drink anyone takes is from a thief's body, which the camps find just; the tendrils for cord, the strongest line there is — every well-line is tendril; and the gas, coaxed into small bladders, for kites. Children fly them. It is the only flight the people have, and they spend it on their children, which also seems just.

**The deep.** Down the bone ladders, at the bottom of the cold shafts, the old rivers run in the dark, and they are fished. The white things are blind and feel vibration through water, so fishing is drumming: you beat the shaft-rim in the old foot-patterns, or tap the line, and the river answers with mouths. The eating kind run arm-long and sweet; the old ones run longer than a person and are returned, and some wells have a resident elder taken and given back across three generations, her scars a census of every camp that has ever kept that water. They graze the cistern walls and keep them sweet, and they are the first diagnostic of everything: before the bloom thins, before the thunders detour, before the well-keeper's tongue finds the souring, the white things leave. And when a named storm comes up the scarp and the Keep tilts its vanes and strips it in a day, the whole system surges, and the wells boil, and the white things rise in the shafts, and the camps eat in the rain — storm-fishing, the festival that comes a dozen times a year and gets its name from the sky. Once in a generation, though, a line takes a weight that walks away with two hundred yards of braided tendril-cord and parts it like thread. The well is drummed with the closed-rhythm for a season. The deep-givers say the under-rivers have their own thunders, and that it costs nothing to believe them.

**The old ones.** On the swells where the crust runs thin, the Keep draws back at noon from the scorching bonelands and flows over them again at dusk, and in that hour the rock's pores open and the noon-things come out. They are flat and glassy and they drink light directly, and they move at the speed of poured tar — or rather, their hour is frantic to them and it is you who are frozen; watching them, a person feels time thicken like cooling resin. They are citizens of the open world, the world before the green closed over, holding one hour a day against the last hundred thousand years. They are not food, not material, not anything but visited. And the Quiet will not leave the mat, and they cannot leave the rock, and so the bare stone at noon is the one place on the plateau where a person may be truly silent in safety — there is no song there to be a hole in. The camps walk their heartbroken there, the ones who cannot keep singing, and let them be quiet for an hour on the world's old bare shoulder. All human stone is quarried in that hour too, one-hour shifts against the green tide: hearth-stones, grinding-stones, anchor-stones for the rattle-poles. A grinding-stone has a genealogy nearly as long as a blade's.

**The people.** Sparsest of the answers, and the loudest for their size. You might know two hundred faces in a life and ten thousand voices: the dusk-mail makes a nation of strangers, and there are horn-voices you will hear nightly for forty years from camps you will never reach, and when one of those far voices stops, whole provinces grieve someone they never met. A name up here is a tune — short enough to drum, long enough to be no one else's — composed at birth and retired at death into the road-songs, so that a bend in a route is a dead woman's name and the roads are made of the ancestors, which is why you sing them exactly. Marriage is a duet on the breath-layer, two camps drumming to each other across the miles, and then one partner walking the spouse-road with the elder who carries its tune. Age is reckoned in storms — *I have seen the year of the Long Strip* — and the elders recite the year-list at bloom the way other folk recite kings. The drums speak in two channels: the false-panic that turns a herd carries a tell in its cadence, a wink, so that the thunder hears terror and every listening human hears theater. Children learn the wink early. The oldest law is that you never lie with the drum — except in that one licensed way, to the world and never to a person.

Some of them live by a season instead of a year. The bloom-walkers follow the front westward all season long, harvesting pollen-flour in the green-gold nights, guiding the thunders to the new bloom, carrying spouses and songs and news faster than anyone else on the plateau, and when the front dies at the western scarp they walk back east along the herd-roads to meet next year's wave. They are considered lucky and half-wild and slightly holy; a child born within the front is bloom-born and will never sit still. And all the road-songs, followed far enough in any direction, end the same way: at the Edge, a final verse that says only *turn, turn* — the two notes no child is ever taught to hold. Now and then someone does not turn, and goes down the scarp into the dry, and the dusk-mail reports it like a death, and no report has ever come back up.

They do not pray to the Keep. You cannot argue with it; you can only keep it, and be kept, and keep the accounts. Ask what organ the people are, in the body of the plant, and the old ones shrug: the lice, maybe. Or the dreams. The Keep grew its own nerves and blood and heart and knife and memory; perhaps, late, it grew something to notice.

**The kept.** The last inhabitants are the dead, and they are the only permanent residents. Everything above ground is seasonal — the mat takes the huts, the poles, the carcasses, the works — but the Undergreen keeps all of it, page by warm page, a hundred thousand years of blooms and husks and bones and the given dead, settling through the strata at a finger's width a season. The deep-givers carry them down the collapse-shafts and lay them in their century, and the fermentation of all that remembering breathes the gas that fills the drifters' bladders. The remembered raise the messengers. Nothing up here is done working when it dies; employment is eternal.

So count them, as the camps do: one plant that is nearly everyone. A silver floor of trillions, each holding one note. Hills that walk, remembering. A silence or several, keeping the account of fear. Sails at dusk, exempt. White elders under the world, cleaning the water with their mouths. Glass ancients on the noon rock, drinking light an hour a day. A sparse loud people, singing the roads their dead have become. And beneath them all, warm and rising slowly toward the light as gas and spore and story, the largest nation of the Keep — the kept.
