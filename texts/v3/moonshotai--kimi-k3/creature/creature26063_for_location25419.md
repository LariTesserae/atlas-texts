The oldest inhabitants are not animals. The lichen was here before the claims, before the tram, before anyone thought to keep accounts, and a handspan of black crust on the talus may be older than all three together. It is also load-bearing: the talus slopes are buildings, and the lichen is their mortar. You do not step on black stone. The trails wind the way they do partly for the hooves that made them and partly out of respect for a being that measures its growth in drought years. Even the varnish on the walls is not quite inert — it accretes microbe by microbe, imperceptibly, which is where its patience comes from. It is not a coating. It is a population.

The tanks have their own citizens before any bird arrives. The green slick on the water is a living skin, and keepers read it the way doctors read tongues. Down in the silt of every good tank there are sleepers — frogs no bigger than a thumb, wrapped in parchment made from their own skin, waiting out the dry weeks in a state that is not quite life and is certainly not death. A keeper tasting a tank is really tasting for sleepers. A tank that holds them is a tank that holds. And sealed into the salt of the pans, waiting even longer, are the shield shrimp — cysts that can sit out years of dryness and hatch within hours of standing water, for the few weeks every storm year when the pans stop lying about being a lake.

Then the dawn crush, and the tank-hens own it. They come in flocks that briefly darken the slots, land in a roar of wings, soak, and rise heavy. Each belly carries a small cupful, held in feathers built like sponge, and the cocks fly it kilometers out over the pans to chicks waiting in scrapes, who drink from their father's breast. You can tell how far the water is by when the hens arrive; keepers read them like gauges, and a change in the dawn flight is discussed at Terminus the way other places discuss weather. A district's hens are its census of water, taken daily, without paperwork.

The dew beetles keep the night shift on the harps, climbing the mesh and standing on their heads in the condensate, drinking what the machines drink. On the old claims this is formalized: the bottom run of mesh is left to them — the beetle tithe — and a harp with no beetles on it is either new or badly tuned. Hunting the harps at dusk are the harp hawks, small falcons who take beetles off the nets and moths out of the air, and who have the best real estate story in the country: the dead freight drone on its ledge, adopted years ago as a nesting shelf. Beneath the shelf, in the hull's shadow, whistlers den; lichen has begun reading the panels. The drone is the local proof of the rule — nothing here ends, it gets used.

The whistlers jam the talus slopes so densely that the slopes are less rock than apartment block. They have two alarm calls, one for sky and one for ground, and every district's relay has its own accent; keepers who rotate through claim they can hear when they're in a new district by the whistlers' pronunciation. Their middens go down meters and are archives — seed husks, beetle cases, bone splinters, layered by decade. They cache ghost grass seed against the storm years, and sometimes the cache outlives the cacher, and a storm year turns an old midden into a garden, blooming out of the talus like a memory.

The varnish cat is one per district, more or less, pale as the walls, and it hunts the tank rims with the patience the place teaches. It rarely drinks — it takes its water from what it kills, which is the most elegant solution anyone here has found. The whistlers' ground-call is, functionally, its name. Seeing it is considered luck, of a specific kind: it means your tank is rich enough to afford a thief.

The dunhorns come through on routes that predate the tram, the claims, and the keeping of accounts. When the herds funnel single-file through the maze — the procession, people call it, and it can take days to pass a point — the polish their hooves leave is laid on the polish of hooves, a thousand years of the same decision. They browse the seep lines, they water at dusk, they drop dung that the beetles receive like manna, and they calve in storm years, as if the young are timed to the country's brief solvency. Nobody owns the procession; that would be like owning weather. In season, at certain gates, people take the stragglers, and the meat is salted with pan salt, and that is considered correct.

Over all of it, the silents. The great vultures have no voice at all — none; they gave it up, or never bothered, there being nothing here to say anything to. They ride the noon thermals and their shadows slide over the fins like slow searchlights, and people read the sky by them: a column of silents over a district is read like smoke, something is dead, and a keeper walks out to make sure it didn't die in a tank. They roost on the harp towers when the towers will have them, unbothered by the chord, the only residents for whom the singing is irrelevant.

Night belongs to the smaller solutions. Groovebacks — little lizards with channeled skin — climb to their stones when the fog tide comes in and stand in the flow, motionless, while the mist beads on their backs and runs down the grooves to their mouths. They drink the fog off their own bodies. Children imitate them, standing in the tide with their mouths open, and are told to hold still: the groovebacks have been doing this longer and are better at it. Fog moths work the nets, sipping; tank bats the size of a thumb skim the tinajas at dusk; the slot owl hunts whistlers in the last light, and its whole announcement is that the air changes and a whistler is gone. After full dark, varnish scorpions walk the silt floors, and a keeper sweeping a bothy with a lamp will see the floor answer in small blue-green points. Then the drip hour, when everything sheltered ticks and spatters into the tanks, and every one of these lives is, one way or another, an audience for that sound.

And underneath the visible population, the held breath: most of what lives here is, at any given moment, waiting. The real population of the plateau is seed, cyst, egg, and pupa. The storm years let it out. Ghost grass rises and sets seed in a fortnight; the tarbush blooms and the tarmoths erupt to meet it, their caterpillars having slept years for the occasion; the sleepers come up out of the tank silt and sing, and the frog chorus is the loudest natural sound the country makes — the maze remembering out loud that it was a lake. The pans go pink with shield shrimp; alkali flies rise in such numbers that the crawler driver wears a veil; the hens boom, the whistlers' middens bloom, the cat grows fat. Then it all subsides, each thing folding itself back into its small durable form, and the country returns to its usual population: the patient.

The people are few, and they keep animal hours. There are no domestic animals here; nothing lives on the plateau that cannot keep itself, and the people are nearly an exception, and know it. Most live at Terminus, where the tram comes in — a store, a water office, the shade board — or out at the harp claims and the line bothies. Newcomers spend their first week in the thinning, gasping at tasks they could do without thinking anywhere else, and the old hands are kind about it, because it happens to everyone exactly once. You don't catch your breath here; you grow it, over a season, and it shows — plateau children are barrel-chested by six.

Harp claims are kept by song. If your harps don't sing for a season, the claim lapses, and the stakes and faded ribbons of lapsed claims stand around the rim as quiet instruction. So harp families live where the wind is reliable and the fog is loyal, and they mend nets at dusk by feel, and they pay the beetle tithe, and their children learn the harp chord the way other children learn rain.

Keepers walk the lines with a mallet and a mirror, rotating through bothies spaced a dawn's walk apart. They read the gauges, patch the pipe, and bless the cisterns — and the blessing is a listening: an ear to the stone, a tap; a full cistern answers low. They are paid in shares of water, the only currency that matters, and their creed is short: tanks are commons, harps are claimed, lines are sacred. Pump crews go out two by two to the small stations and come back strange and gentle, speaking in gestures for their first days back, as if words had to be rationed too. Crawler drivers have the loneliest profession in the country — out on the pans for months behind smoked glass, eyes gone pale from glare, talking to the machine, which answers in the only way it can: by continuing. When a driver dies out there, the crawler keeps its circle, lights on, and someone comes out at night, following the lights, to bring them in. The tram watch at Terminus keeps the cable, and the cable's slow ping is the clock of the camps; when the tram stills for weeks the silence has a different shape, and people find themselves listening for it the way you listen for a sleeper in the house. Riding the bucket is how the young leave. It is also how most of them come back.

The customs are the rules, worn smooth. At Terminus the shade board lists the named shadows and their hours, and the schedule is kept — but life-shade is never refused, to anyone, ever. Denying shade isn't justice; it's murder. Words die young in the thin air, whistles go farther, and mirrors go farthest; children learn the flash code alongside their letters, and at noon, when nobody travels and nothing is what it looks like, the high points blink with check-ins: I'm here, I'm well, the water holds. There is no jail. There is the dry list — cut a line and no one sells to you, no one fills for you, and the plateau does the rest. It has happened twice that anyone remembers. Both walked down the escarpment. One came back, years later, and was allowed to stay, and does not speak of it. Foul a tank and you clean it with your own hands and refill it with your own water, and the whole district watches you do it, not out of cruelty but out of the need to see it done.

The dead go up — to a high ledge, for the silents, which is considered an honest trade: the sky carried your water all your life. Their mirrors go into the cairns, and at dusk the cairns answer travelers' flashes faintly, badly, with the tarnished flash of the dead. People find this comforting.

The machines, too, are residents, in their way. The harps have names and known voices; a keeper walking a line at night can hear which one is off its note, the way you hear a wrong voice in a choir. The pumps keep night hours like everything else that matters. The tram is patient in a way nothing biological can match. And out on its ledge the drone goes on being adopted — hawks above, whistlers below, lichen on the panels — the standing proof that the other economy takes everything in the end.

Count all of them — the crust and the cyst, the hen and the hawk, the sleeper in the silt and the keeper at the cistern, the driver in her circle and the mirrors in the cairns — and you notice they are all, every one, a device for the same purpose. Feather and groove, belly and mesh, parchment skin and pipeline: each is a way of catching the night and holding it a little longer. The lake did not die. It thinned itself into bodies. At dawn at any tank you can watch it gather — beak by beak, bead by bead, breath by breath — the lake, coming back to itself in pieces, by every method it has learned.
