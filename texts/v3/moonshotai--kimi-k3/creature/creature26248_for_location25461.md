**The Two Hundred Thousand**

There are about two hundred thousand of them, give or take the out-livers, who are difficult to count because they prefer it that way. Spread across a continent, that works out to one of the sparsest populations anywhere — and they will all tell you, straight-faced, that they live in town. Ask a Bench person where they're from and they say *the Bench*. Press them and you get a thickening. Press further and you get a warmth: *by the third seep*, *over the garden flue*, *the warm corner off the film-court*. Addresses here are measured in degrees.

You can tell a citizen from a newcomer for their first three days, because the newcomer is breathing on purpose. The thickenings have a hospitality rule about this, old and unwritten: no questions for three days. A warm floor, a cup of roof water, a bed, and no questions. On the fourth day the questions begin, and by then the newcomer has usually started answering them differently. The lungs come in three days — this is known, there is a phrase for it, *getting your Bench lungs* — but the ears take years, and the change the ears make is permanent. People who go down to the lowlands come home and lie flat on the floor of the arrivals hall before they greet anyone, and everyone understands, and nobody rushes them. The world below has forgotten its bass note. The first thing a returning citizen does is check whether the land remembers theirs.

They check the way you might check a window: a palm flat to the floor in the morning, a moment, then the day begins. *Checking the hum*, it's called, and it means the floors, and it means each other.

The quiet has shaped their speech. On a land where sound arrives singly, a Bench conversation arrives singly too — an exchange, a pause with weather in it, another exchange. Visitors fill the pauses. Locals let them lie, like tarns. Children learn two voices: the room voice, low, and the moor voice, which is pitched to cross water and is used one note at a time. They learn the big waves before handwriting — the arm-signals that travel a kilometer across a Line: *I'm well. Come. Wait. I see you.* And they learn mirror-code young, because the air is clear and the sightlines are absurd and the Lines run straight to every horizon: the freight capsules carry cargo, but the light carries talk. At midday, when the dawn mist has burned off and the terraces catch the sun, messages hop tower to tower across the whole plateau. A rumor can cross the continent by lunch and still be candid at dinner, which on the Bench counts as a technical marvel.

Their bodies are the altitude's work: deep-chested, ruddy, lean. Their cuffs are always damp — everyone's cuffs are always damp; it is the one universal — and their boots are made on a splayed last, copied frankly from the slateback's hoof, because the slateback solved clinker long before people arrived. Nothing about them hurries. Hurrying saves nothing at these distances, and the land has spent generations teaching them its own pace, and they have, mostly, consented.

**The Crews**

You know crew by their hands. Mineral-white to the wrist, lime worked into the knuckle creases so deep no washing gets it out — and they smell, faintly and permanently, of rain on fresh concrete. Visitors say the crews smell like the Bench. The crews say the Bench smells like them. Neither is wrong, and both say it the way you'd say something you'd checked.

They never say *build*. The Bench builds itself; the crews *advise*. In the thickenings they persuade the pourstone into walls and vaults and terraces, a craft learned by hands over years, half masonry and half negotiation. At the far edge they guide the lay, walking roped in pairs on ground younger than the day, reading tomorrow's ground through their boot soles the way sailors read water. The rule out there is older than the Charter: *one walks, one watches.* No one walks young ground alone. Not ever. Young ground is the Bench's phrase for anything new and tender — a fresh marriage is young ground, walk it roped — but at the edge it means what it means.

Apprentices are chosen for the hum. The crews test for it without ceremony: they stand a child on the moor in boots and ask where the ground is going. Some children point. Those ones get offered the work, whatever family they're from — the crews are hereditary the way weather is hereditary, which is to say it runs in families but answers to something else.

An edge shift lasts weeks, and the ones who come back from it come back *edge-quiet* — a recognized state, given room. They get the warm corner at the seep, and the good cup, and no questions, until they start naming things again. Because naming is the crews' other work, and their only acknowledged art. Every tarn, fall, lip, and weep on the new ground gets its name from the crew that first stood by it, and the names are plain and affectionate and final: *Second Wet. Ana's Hesitation. Long Weep. The Lip.* The maps of the Bench are the crews' collected works, and the crews, who are candid about everything, are candid about this too: it is the closest thing they have to poetry, and they know it.

That candidness is the crew's gift to the whole culture. Ask whether the Bench was lifted or lifted itself, and a crew elder will say *yes*, comfortably, the way you'd answer a question about the weather of your grandfather's childhood. The Charter — and the crews will recite this at the slightest invitation — is the only founding document anyone knows of that opens with a confession: *We do not fully know.* Its laws are few and straight, as legislation should be: water, warmth, young ground, the dead, and the duty to report. The one true crime on the Bench is noticing something and keeping it to yourself. On a land this new, a withheld observation is theft — from the wardens, from the future, from the land's own record of what it is becoming.

**The Wardens**

The wardens keep a wild that was seeded, and they know exactly how strange that is. They are curators of an artifact that became real. Their founding story is the Seeding — the ancestor crews going out across bare wet rock with cultures and spores and lichen pastes, painting life onto a continent with their hands — and every warden can tell it, and every telling ends the same way: *the first garden was a smear.*

They recite the chain the way other people say grace. The cats keep the slatebacks moving, and the moving keeps the film grazed even, and the even film keeps the tarns clear, and the clear tarns are why the mire-hens swarm. Nobody is sure the chain is true. Everybody repeats it. The wardens have added a line of their own, an amen: *and we keep it so.*

Their first question at any seep, always, is: *Are the frogs singing?* A frog chorus is a seep in health; a silent seep summons wardens the way smoke summons other people. They run the egg licenses in the rattling marshes, walk the herd counts across the clinker, and conduct the cat census in the conditional tense — *we had nine cats this year, in a manner of speaking* — because you do not count ash-cats so much as accumulate evidence of having been watched by them. They decide which seeps are for the herds and which are for people, which makes them, though nobody calls them this, the keepers of the Bench's social map: you just have to know where warmth accumulates, and the wardens know.

Every warden carries a pebble. One pebble, lowland-made, water-rounded, old — and on the Bench this is a museum object, an antiquity from the future, because the plateau has no pebbles yet and frost has only just begun the long argument that will eventually make some. Wardens pass the stone hand to hand among children, who have never seen gravel, and explain what erosion does given time. Bench children grow up believing the lowlands are where stones go to be finished. They are not entirely wrong.

**The Out-Livers**

Between the thickenings, the city thins to a scatter of single figures: seep-keepers, Line-keepers in their causeway huts, the solitaries at the warden posts. They are not hermits. That is the first thing to understand. They are *posts* — one person, one warm pool or one stretch of causeway, one journal, one supply glider a season. Solitude, on the Bench, is a civic function.

Because the municipality pays a small stipend for a kept journal. Any journal. On a land this new, attention is infrastructure: the census counts frost-cracks, first lichens, tarn levels, frog-song, and the dawn bulletin after a still night reads like news and is news — *eleven cracks in the night, two near.* The crack-listeners are the night shift of this, sitting up in wool with tea and notebooks, logging the rifle-shot reports, fixing distances by delay. Children learn crack-distance by ear the way children elsewhere learn birdsong. And the most beloved book on the Bench is an out-liver's journal covering forty-one years of a single tarn — water levels, ice-outs, one heron, the year the mire-hens left and the year they returned. Visitors mistake it for poetry. It is minutes.

The glider pilots are the opposite temperament: the only fast thing on the Bench, the restless young, riding the plateau's own wind systems between the knots. A landing is an event. Pilots talk quickly by Bench standards and are endured fondly, the way you'd endure weather that had learned to gossip. Every thickening loves its pilots and slightly disbelieves them.

And the herds are traffic. A slateback crossing can hold a freight capsule on a Line for two hours, and the capsule waits, and the gliders circle above, and nobody minds, and this is the Bench's one rush hour — the joke is older than anyone — the city's rush hour has hooves and is in no hurry at all.

**The Shape of the Day**

The day belongs to the steam-hour first. At dawn the whole Bench gives off the heat of its making, the mist rises in sheets, and the thickenings empty into it: it is the walking hour, wordless, and it has an etiquette. You do not greet half-shapes in the mist. You let them be anyone. Some of them are your neighbors; some are slatebacks; some, at a tarn's edge, gone before your eye finishes agreeing, are cats, and it is polite to have been courteous anyway. Children are taught to greet dusk-shapes and mist-shapes alike, gently, just in case.

At noon the melt-pulse comes across the whole plateau at once, the slow continental inhale, and that is the clock. No bell is needed; the land rings it. Canal gates open, washing goes in, the falls off the rims strengthen to white ropes, the mist is gone and the heliographs wake on the terraces. At dusk the swifts pour out of the warm flutes by the ten thousand, and the terraces fill with people watching — the swifts are co-tenants, counted affectionately and inaccurately — and their guano, collected from the flutes, is wealth, because on the Bench soil is the one thing that cannot be persuaded. *Rich as a compost-keeper* is a real compliment.

The seeps are the social heart, all day and into the dark: long communal soaking in the steam while frost stands everywhere else, conversation slow or absent, old people wintering at the warmest pools, courting conducted in vapor, frogs providing what is frankly the loudest nightlife on the continent. A good chorus gets discussed for days, like a concert.

The food is plain and warm and faintly mineral. There is moor butter — film cultured like cheese, mineral-fat, with a ghost of ozone; visitors are polite about it; the Bench-born dream about it abroad. Eggs by license. Canal fish, each thickening partisan about its own. Roots from the warm floor-beds, fast greens from the glassed hanging gardens. And roof water — the mist, combed by the condensation traps on every comb and cistern — is the precious thing: for infants, for tea, for guests. Being offered roof water is the Bench's whole vocabulary of welcome. Moor water does everything else.

**Beginnings and Endings**

A child comes of age by walking. The first solo Line-walk — days of causeway over the mires, herds crossing ahead, one vehicle, distant, unhurried — is the Bench's great rite: you leave a child and arrive something else, technically downtown the entire way. Custom holds that when a walker comes in off their first Line, no one asks how it was for a full day.

Marriages and births are marked by seeding: a chosen stone, a painted smear of film, no signature. You come back over the years and check. A family's film-patch is its portrait, and a portrait nobody finishes. Gifts on the Bench are always unfinished — a half-cured stone, a started journal, a cutting with opinions. A finished gift is faintly embarrassing, like doing someone's work for them. A betrothal gift is the one exception with a rule: a pebble, lowland-rounded, given with the words *something finished, to carry; something started, to keep.*

The dead are given to the walls. The crews open a warm seam in the thickening's fabric, and the masonry, self-annealing, closes over the years, gaining layers, the way the oldest cores have rounded and deepened — like pearls, and the pearls are ancestors. Every old house has its wall-old. A palm pressed to an old wall is a greeting, and there is no morbidity in it, because here the dead are, literally, warm.

And there are tarn funerals, because the drainage is adolescent and water hasn't found its paths, and a canal decision or a frost-shift can kill a mire that was younger than the people mourning it. When a tarn dies, the neighbors walk its saved water to a living one, and someone reads its journal aloud. Elsewhere the land outlasts the people. Here it can go either way, and both directions are mourned, and both are loved.

**The Weight**

It is not without shadow, and they would want that noted. A water decision is a flood to someone; the crews' advance erases a world a meter or two a year; and the Bench takes the lowlands' sky — the rim storms are born of it, the wall of weather that forgot to move is *their* roof. The crews are candid about this too: *a wall has two weathers.* The falls off the rims give some of it back. No one calls it even.

And underneath everything, the deep one: the Bench is underway. Going somewhere a meter or two a year, destination unrecorded, guided but not steered, and no one alive will learn where. Children ask. The honest answer is the Charter's first line. There is a story, one generation old, told in the conditional tense, of the Quiet Hour — a day the hum faltered, for an hour, and no one has an explanation, and on its anniversary a great many people keep a palm on the floor. Or so it's repeated.

To live here is to agree to a question you will not live to hear answered. They sign up anyway. They are — and they would want the word chosen precisely — happy.

**The Minutes**

At dusk, then: the freeze coming on, the mist re-forming over the tarns, the hum in the sternum. A seep-keeper writes the day's entry — water, tracks, frog-song, one cat, conditional. A crack-listener pours tea and waits for the night's opening statements. A child on her first Line-walk greets a shape at a tarn's edge, politely, and watches it become nothing, and writes it down. On nine thickenings the terraces fill for the swifts. Two hundred thousand people, spread across a continent, each small against it, each doing the one job the engines cannot do.

The engines lay ground. The ground grows film. The film feeds everything that moves. And the people keep the minutes of the making — candidly, in plain names, in journals no one will finish — so that when it is finished, if it is ever finished, someone will know what it was like when it was young.

Ask any of them what they do. They will tell you the truth.

*I notice.*
