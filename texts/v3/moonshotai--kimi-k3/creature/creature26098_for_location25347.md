Nobody has ever finished the census of the Kettle. The company keeps one, of course — forty names on a payroll, two cats off it, one mule that the books, strictly speaking, list as fully depreciated — and it is accurate as far as it goes, which is the rim. Below the rim the arithmetic changes currency. Count the place honestly and you must count by seniority, the way the camp itself does after the second drink, and then the forty come neither first nor last.

The oldest resident is the water. It was here before the hole; the hole is the interruption, not the water. It lay in the dark of the water table with its own citizenry — the iron bacteria, older than the district's name, older than names — and the pit is only a window cut into their tenancy. Everything the second rule records is their handiwork: the ochre rinds, the iron-red drapes, the glove gone stiff as pottery, the abandoned webs in the seep galleries turned to stone lace, the spinner long spent, her architecture outlasting her in mineral. They are miners working a reverse face. They take the company's iron-rich water and lay it back down as stone, and they are re-mining the rising main from the inside — a vein forming in an artery, the one rich strike the Kettle ever made, growing in the bore of its own drainpipe while the company pays good wages to have it reamed out. Capital versus geology at the scale of a pipe, and geology holds the longer contract. In the camp's formulation, after the second drink: the people are tenants with good pumps; the frogs are tenants with good voices; the heron holds the longest lease; the bacteria hold the deed; and the water is the estate itself, coming back into possession.

Then the founders. Of the forty, the man who comes nearest to keeping the honest census is the engineer, which he would deny. He keeps the company ledger in a fair open hand — tonnage, pump-hours, the word *dewatering* used as though it were an achievable tense — and the true ledger in a hand like bent wire: the real pump-hours, the real lines climbing their knuckle a year, and in the back pages, entered upside down so they might pass as corrections, the other record. First swallow. First frog. Toad nights, dated. The fig's crack, copied twice a year from the surveyor, who pretends not to give it to him. The count of pale fish in the intake screens. Lately he has begun a third column, headed only with a question mark: what the pit gained this year. Species. Sediment. The Tree's canopy. He does not let himself consider which ledger is the real one. If the company ever asks him what lives in the Kettle, he will say pumps. The true ledger knows better, and he knows it knows.

The surveyor is the camp's professional pessimist, a man whose instrument tells him the world is fixed and whose eyes tell him otherwise; he has made his peace by believing the instrument in public and his eyes in private. He re-sets the pegs each quarter, knowing. He will not join the pool on the ledge above the Tree — says it isn't funny — and twice a year he goes up with his rule and plumb bob and comes down quieter than he went, and tells the engineer the number, and the engineer writes it upside down, and the men respect them both for a worry they all share and no one voices.

The pump crews are the aristocracy of the floor, and they earn it in their sleep. A pump-man does not wake when the beat stops; when the beat stops he is already running, barefoot on the boardwalks, ahead of the lamp. What wakes him is the change — a bearing's note going flat, felt through the bed frame before it is heard. At dawn the pump chief reads the intake screens the way other men read the newspaper: weed torn from the sump's far margin (a slide in the night, then, on the far wall; the screens know first), a crayfish of eating size, once a miner's boot of the old pattern, which was looked at by everyone and claimed by no one and now sits on the pump-house sill doing what things do here, which is turn slowly to stone. And some mornings the pale fish, white as candle wax, gills working, coughed up from the galleries under the far wall. What comes up out of the deep goes back to the water — into the sump, alive if it can manage it. The pit sent it; the pit keeps it. That is the fifth rule, and it is not written down either, but the coins on the saint's shelf have opinions about it. Every pump ever raised into the house got a coin; the ochre has fused them now into a single green-black lump, the second rule respecting no offering. Nobody prays, exactly. They touch the doorframe going in. It is not worship; it is the maintenance of a relationship, and the pump crews are by trade maintainers of relationships — with the water, with the beat, with the thing the pit is deciding to be. The dynamo keeper belongs to this guild: he sleeps beside his machine the way old couples sleep, turned toward the hum.

There is one man left who worked the floor that is now the sump. He can stand at the mere's edge and give you the soundings from memory: here the forge; there the good seam; under the dabchick's reeds, the tramroad he walked to his shift. He watched his workplace fill like a bath for twenty years, which is a kind of grief with no ceremony in it, and he remembers the pit dry — or dry-ish; it was never dry; nothing here is dry; but he remembers when the wet was a nuisance and not a landlord. He says it was louder then, and emptier, and he says it the way men speak of a town they would not move back to.

The cook is the only man in camp who planned for the third rule. He set his fuel-drum garden where the blade of light loiters longest, and the pumpkins ripen in steam like something being summoned. Visitors are told before they come down: don't bring a bottle, bring dirt. Soil is the Kettle's one contraband and its one currency — a bucket of good black plain-soil pays a debt, settles a bet, stands a round. The six pepper plants have names, which change with the crews, and tempers, which do not. The pumpkin vine is tended better than the machinery: the machinery gets its oil on schedule; the vine gets water before it asks. On the anniversary the pumpkin is cut, the crayfish go into the boil with the peppers, the beam engine is fired, the old man of the Drowned Level weeps while the young men study the gauges and grant him the courtesy of the smoke — and the heron watches from the drowned hoist, counting anniversaries of its own.

In the office, the clerk writes the company's long hopeful name on the monthly forms and the Kettle on everything else. The safe has not opened in six years; the damp has welded it, and no one is anxious. Whatever was in there has become a single object, paper fused brickwise, and is in that way the most accurate document in the camp. The maps are in there too, presumably, with the hopeful name on them. Nobody uses the maps.

You can date a man's tenure by small surrenders. The new man tries to stay dry; by the end of the first week he has stopped. He learns to breathe through his teeth at dusk, to keep the mesh tucked, to hold the lantern low and apologize. He gets his first leech, and the crew salts it off and tells him he is a resident now — the pit has taken its tithe of blood and stamped his papers. Some men last a season and go back up to the plain with damp socks and a story they will dine on for life. The ones who stay past two winters do not leave at all. The Kettle keeps them the way it keeps boot prints.

The best seat in the pit is the coldest one, at the adit mouth, where the evening game convenes when the first bat exits. A bat in the face costs nothing; flinching costs a coin for the saint. Debts are denominated in pepper pods and buckets of soil. Nobody builds a fire at the adit mouth, nobody blocks the door, and the treaty is older than anyone's employment.

The mule has had seven names and outlived all of them. The company's books show it fully depreciated in the year the old level drowned, which makes it, officially, a ghost, and it eats accordingly, from every hand. Its deafness is a mercy the men frankly envy: it sleeps through the chorus that has new arrivals standing at the windows at two in the morning. At midday it finds the blade of light and stands in it with the rest, eyes half shut — the mule learned the third rule before any man now living, and it remembers the old floor too, in its way, being the only creature in the pit that was already old when the water was young. Asked whose it is, the camp says: the mule belongs to the mule. It is the only one of us with nothing to prove.

Two cats, fat on what the mesh misses. The canteen has one and the store has the other; they have never been seen in the same lamplight, and the men say they divorced, long ago, over a frog. They are the only residents exempt from the first rule — a cat in the Kettle can always find dry, and where they find it (the dynamo housing, the warm top of the dead safe, a priest-hole behind the canteen stove) is a small daily engineering miracle regarded by the crews with professional respect. They kill geckos, for which they are fined in scraps; the fines go unpaid; the ledger of it is kept nowhere but in the men's tone of voice.

There are no dogs. There have been dogs. A dog needs an elsewhere — a horizon to run at — and the Kettle has no horizon, only rim. The last dog stood at the foot of the ladders and barked at the walls all night and wore himself to wire, and they sent him up to the flume camp on the plain, where the pit's pumped water has made the only lush mile in the district, loud with birds he will never catch, and he is happy, and remembers nothing.

The wires that sag pole to pole like decorations for a festival that never quite happened turn out to have been hung for the swallows, who hold the festival daily. They are the pit's master masons — after the bacteria — working the one building material in endless surplus, and they keep the third rule better than any man: they are in the blade of light the minute it stands up on the floor, stitching it, and the men who drift toward it with their tea are only following the swallows' lead. When they gather on the wires in their season, shoulder to shoulder like men at a rail, the camp checks the flume and the canteen roof, because the swallows have never once been wrong about the rains. Above them the swifts ride the thermal the Kettle exhales and scream their opinion of everything below, the rim's aristocracy, never touching down; the men say head office are swifts — they come over on the hot air, they never land, they shriek, they leave. A hawk works the same thermal with better manners. An owl owns the night shift, and is, more often than not, the reason the chorus stops.

At dusk the adit on the north wall exhales its smoke-ribbon of bats — the one cool breath in the pit — and the count of them has never been made; the true ledger says only *many*, and *more than last year*. They are the Kettle's commuters, the only residents who visit the neighbors: the adit runs to the old galleries, and the galleries run under the far wall into the buried district, and on winter nights the bats hang in workings no man will walk again, where candles once burned, and come home at dawn smelling of the deep, which is the smell of the sump squared. Without them the midges would take the camp apart bolt from bolt. This is understood. The adit is theirs by treaty, and the card game at its mouth is the rent they charge in cool air.

And then there is the Tree, which is not one resident but a vertical town. Its name is entered one way in the true ledger and spelled three other ways in the canteen. Head office once ordered it removed — *roots in the highwall*, the inspector wrote, *liability* — and the order came down on the incline and was read aloud at the canteen to a general silence, and then it was filed in the safe that no longer opens, which is where the camp files things it intends neither to lose nor to obey. Inside every fig, a wasp; and the wasp is the most improbable resident of all — a creature that lives two days had to find the only fig tree within a day's flight in any direction, and did, and does, every year, the most punctual commuter in the district. When the Tree fruits, the whole plain sends delegates: birds the men have no names for, who know one green thing in eighty meters of raw rock. Every seed they drop below it fails on the raw marl — for now. The sediment fans grow their knuckle a year. The Tree's grandchild will be the second tree of the Kettle, and the surveyor's anxiety is, seen rightly, a prophecy.

Of plants otherwise: one stand of reeds, much photographed, the dabchick nesting in it, which is half the reason for the photographs; horsetails along the spill line, unbothered, older than the company's idea of the company, plants that have watched pits fill in before and were not impressed then either; moss beards under the permanent drips; and the algae, green-black and rust-orange, the only meadows, mown by nothing.

At dusk the pit exhales its citizens: bats from the adit, voices from the sump, midges from the water — a column off every still puddle like smoke from an invisible fire, so that the floor at dusk appears to be burning slowly in a hundred places, and the smoke is made of lives. Everything winged in the Kettle is made of midges in the end. The swallows are midges, arranged. The bats are midges, repurposed. The darner is a midge that went into management.

The mosquito fish came down in a kerosene can in somebody's grandfather's day, unauthorized, against the mosquitoes — the most successful public works in the camp's history. Now every stillness has them: the sump, the rills, the boot prints. A boot print in the Kettle keeps for a season; it fills with rain and seep, and then it fills with fish, and for a season a footprint is an aquarium with a tenant. The pit is showing its work. The darners hawk the lanes of air between buildings, big as spoons, each on its own beat, and the men know the canteen-lane darner from the pump-house darner and speak of them as colleagues. Their nymphs stalk the sump's bottom for years, armored and patient, and on the appointed night they climb the drowned tram rail — the one that runs down into the water like a decision reversed — and climb it the other way, and split, and leave their emptied selves clinging above the waterline like a crowd that has gone somewhere better. The engineer counts the husks each morning. It is the only emigration the Kettle approves.

The crayfish are the only livestock the pit provides. Custom takes them from the flats and leaves the sump alone: the sump is the heron's parish, and its crayfish grow old and pale and strange, and eating one's elders is bad luck in any parish. The spiders in the pipe racks grow fat as thimbles and are tolerated, encouraged, left bequests of midges. Every lamp pole has its gecko, by tenure; the crews name them and rename them and bet on them, and the geckos know the grid better than the company does — when a lamp dies, the gecko waits one night, out of respect or disbelief, and then moves. The moths come in their thousands and the lamps wear fur, and by dawn the ground beneath each lamp is a drift of wings, and the ants — the pit's uncounted sanitation department, total and unremarked — have it cleared before the blade of light returns.

But the census, honestly kept, would be nine parts frog. They outnumber every human who has ever set foot in the camp; they will outnumber every human who ever does. Every station has its species: tree frogs on the sweating pipes, gripping with disc toes, singing into the flanges so the rivets ring in sympathy; marsh frogs in the reed-stand's shadow; and in the warm shallows over the Drowned Level the big old ones, green-black as the algae, whose voices are pitched so low the men feel them in the planks alongside the pump-beat and cannot always tell which is which. The pump crews call the deepest voice the Foreman: it starts the night shift, and the chorus takes its cue. The chorus buries the pumps, which is a mercy — the fourth rule is best kept by men who cannot hear it. New men cannot sleep for it; old men cannot sleep without it; men transferred up to the plain write back that the silence there is too loud. And when the chorus stops — all of it, at once, a wall of sound to a wall of nothing — every man in the pit is out of his bunk and reaching for his boots before the first lamp is lit. It happens perhaps twice a year: a slide on the far wall, the owl crossing low, or nothing anyone ever finds. The camp has an alarm system. It is made of frogs.

The toads keep their own calendar. On toad nights the boardwalks are carpeted in moving leather and you walk with the lantern low, apologizing. They come out of the walls — crevices in the shale seam, spaces under the young rock — as if the pit hatches them when the right rain falls, and they go to the sump, and by morning the flats are empty and no one can say where they have gone. The true ledger keeps a page of their dates. The engineer has drawn the lines every way he knows, and the only rule he has found is rain — but not every rain. The pit decides which rains are toad rains, and has not shared the criteria.

Over all of it presides the heron. The pump crews call it the Bailiff, because it stands on the seized property. It arrived when the water did; it is the first citizen of the new regime, the herald of what the pit is deciding to become, and it is entirely at ease, which no one else in the Kettle entirely is. It does not care about the fourth rule. If the pumps stopped tomorrow the heron would only gain, and everyone knows this, and it is watched the way the weather is watched. At dawn it takes the pale fish from the drowned hoist's shadow, one leg tucked, patient as the water it serves.

Because the pale fish are residents now too — nobody invited them and nobody can classify them. White as candle wax, eyeless or near enough, they come up through the intakes from the galleries under the far wall: immigrants from the buried district, the neighbors' deeps sending representatives. What comes up goes in the sump, and now the sump holds a pale population that rises at night to take moths off the surface. Sometimes the mere changes temper — a degree colder, clear for a week — and the engineer writes in the true ledger: *the neighbors are pumping*. The camp takes the news of a world it cannot see by tasting the water, the way islanders read currents from other islands' weather.

On the third drink the men argue whether the Kettle itself belongs on the census. It has a pulse — you feel it in the bed frame. It has a breath — the adit, in and out, once a day. It has a voice, though it borrows the frogs'. It has a memory: the boot prints, the dated gauges, the glove. And it has an intention, legible in the sediment fans if you know how to read. If those do not add up to life, the engineer says, closing the true ledger, they add up to something that keeps the same hours.

Up on the rim, out past the spoil tips where the flume begins, there is a small fenced plot in real soil — the camp's only real estate with drainage. The early stones carry the company's long hopeful name; the later stones say the Kettle. Even the graves changed their minds. The dead receive the two things the pit cannot give: dryness and a horizon. The men speak of the plot with something close to envy, and some of the old ones have already chosen their ground, in sight of the headframe, above the water table, out of the rain's way.

So the company's census says forty, and is wrong by a factor of wings, and the true census is never finished, because it is taken nightly by the water, which adds its knuckle of shoreline a season at a time and populates as it goes. Tonight the lamps are lit and doubled; the mule sleeps on its feet in the loudest town in the district, deaf and perfect; the game goes on at the adit mouth; the Bailiff sleeps on the hoist; the engineer enters the day's line and, under it, upside down, the things that are not measurements. And in the sump, under the spilled constellation of the lamps, the pale fish rise to take moths from among the reflected stars — the drowned level's children, feeding on the sky — and the surface closes over them like a ledger, and the water rises its fraction, and the count goes on.
