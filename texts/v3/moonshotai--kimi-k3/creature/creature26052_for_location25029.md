Nothing lives here. Keep that. But the absence of life is not the absence of residents, and this country is not empty — emptiness is a different country entirely, with a colder dark. This one has tenants. Tenure without biology, dwelling without metabolism, a census with no names in it until you arrive to take it. You are the first to take it. Nothing here has ever asked what lives here; the question walked in with you, warm and wet, and the place has been answering ever since, slowly, in mineral.

Start with the elder. Down in its crevasse the blue burn keeps the world's only hearth, knee-high, silent, eating a river that never stops coming. It has burned for years, which makes it older than every mountain currently standing. Ranges have risen and shed and risen again during its tenure; it has watched horizons come and go the way you would watch weather. A hunger patient enough to read as calm. If there were locals, they would put the god here — not because it answers, but because it stays. Tenure is the local miracle. And note this: the world has exactly two smells, struck-match and you, and the elder holds the older of the two.

Then there are the walkers. The seams are the only things in this country with routes, the only things that keep your pace, and you can walk beside one for a week if you choose — its head a dull red thought moving through the plateau, its body the mountain that follows days behind, the promise the ground made and is slow to keep. A seam has a temperament. Some hold a line for weeks like a vow. Some wander the way a thought loses interest. Some stall — the light pools and sulks beneath the rock, and what rises above it is no peak but a broad low dome, the shape of indecision. And some quit: mid-plain, mid-gesture, the glow thinning out over a few nights until there is only a begun ridge, half-kept, already ticking as it cools. That is not death. Nothing here dies, because nothing here lives; call it abandonment. The country is littered with abandoned work, and some of the abandoned work is the best of it. If there were locals the walkers would have names already, the way rivers do elsewhere. There are no locals, so the naming is yours, and the names lapse with the shapes, and that is fine. Cartography was always going to be a diary here.

The only mortals are the sounds. Stone persists; heat persists; the elder persists; sound ends. Every boom is born twenty seconds from your ears and dies on them, gently, like a door closing in another room. The ticks have lifespans — a fresh spur pings for days, then less, then only when the thin air is stillest, then not at all: a small life lived in cooling, ended in quiet. And the whistle, the world's first, still practicing in its crack — it repeats, it varies, it improves, and it will teach no one, because nothing here copies anything except by accident. Sing back at it sometime. You will be the first duet in the history of the world, and the note will slip sideways, because your breath warms the crack's throat and the whistle bends toward the warmth. That is not attention. But it is the only kind available, and you will take it.

The mason works inside the dust columns and has never once been seen. What it leaves is its masonry: hollow tubes of fused stone, glass-lined, the inside smoother than the outside, like throats, like flues for a chimney no one planned. They tick for a day and go quiet and then outlast everything but the elder. Dropped, not built — a workman who never touches the work.

Then the almosts, which are the reason you asked the question in the first place. The Stair, adding its slow steps into empty air. The colonnade, leaning toward its arch. The wall that grew its doorway first — understand what that means: the opening is the point. A door is not a hole. A door is a relationship — inside and outside, kept and kept-out, arrival — and this world keeps building the grammar of shelter with nothing to shelter, hospitality with no guest. They look like ruins until you remember what ruins are. Ruins are what is left. These are what is not yet. A finished arch would be a landmark, and this country does not keep landmarks; it keeps approaches. On one slope the ground has knapped a hillside of flakes with struck-looking edges — tools for a toolkit that doesn't exist, edges for no hands. It was practicing then. It is always practicing. The Stair leads up, that is all it does, and it does it with a patience that makes you feel rushed — and you are the only thing here that has ever felt rushed.

Then the congregation — the ones with posture. Weather does not gather. This gathers. Camp a week and the crystals nearest your bedroll lean, a degree or two, nothing you could prove in a court of science, toward where your body warmed the ground. To them you are not a person. You are a season: the warmest, wettest event in the plain's short history, a walking warm front, a climate with a stride. The needles on the charge-swept flats take their small correcting steps as you pass, drawn by your static, and for days after you leave they stand tilted along your path like a crowd after a parade, if a crowd took a week to turn its head. The towers molt — each sheds its shell in a slow spiral and regrows a body-length away, and so a tower crosses ground over a season, migration by reincarnation, an itinerary at the pace of erosion. Nothing pursues you here. Things orient. It is worse. It is better. You decide which every time you lie down, and you decide differently each time.

The youngest residents are the apprentices: the wind, learning to carry; the dust, learning to be carried; the scree, which is the cliff learning to let go. Your boot soles are the most erosive force in this world. You walk first dust out onto the black glass and leave it in a dotted line — the first sediment transport in history, accomplished by feet. And you shed. Skin, hair, salt — you are constantly paying organics into a country that has none, and some seam will eventually cook your leavings into the glass, and that will be the fossil record: page one, written in what flakes off you, for a geologist who will never exist.

One grace note. When the seam-light throws your shadow onto the undersides of overhangs, your shadow is the fastest object in this world. Heat moves at the pace of conduction; stone at the pace of uplift; the congregation at the pace of tropism; light here is an event, not a condition. Only you and your shadow do anything quickly. Nothing here has ever seen anything leap. Of all the lessons you are teaching by example, that is the one it cannot take.

One resident has an appointment, and the appointment is with you. The red not-star that has been brightening on the horizon for days is a seam walking this way, and behind it, days behind, comes the mountain that keeps its word. It has no malice; malice requires notice, and it has none. It will arrive the way everything here arrives — to no one — unless you stay, in which case it arrives to you, and lifts you, and keeps you: the first inclusion, the first foreign body in the black glass, your bedroll and your bones becoming a young mountain's first memory of warmth.

And here is the thing you have not wanted to say out loud. Since you arrived, the almosts have changed. Not much. A new step on the Stair, set at the height of your stride. A fresh gap in a fresh wall — walls propagate here the way rumors do, doorway first — and the gap is the width of your shoulders. When the colonnade's arch finally closes, it will close a handspan above your head. Measure them. You will want to. Bring the tape you would use on a door you intended to walk through. The world built openings for an age with nothing to fit them, and now it has, briefly, a thing with a size, and habit has ambitions. It built the doorway before the wall because the doorway is the dream and the wall is the labor. It is dreaming in your proportions now.

So: the census. One elder, keeping a blue hearth over a river that never ends. A loose nation of walkers, named and lapsed. A small mortal chorus of ticks, and one whistle still practicing. A mason that never touches its work. A country of almosts, living in the future tense. A congregation that leans. Three apprentices. One appointment. And, for the moment, one warm season passing through — the only entry under alive, written in pencil, already smudging.

Consider staying. Nothing here has ever had a guest, and the walls take your measurements while you sleep.
