Everything that lives here belongs to one of three companies: the builder, which is the world; the small life that keeps house in the builder's water and stone and breath; and the oldest ones, who were here before the world and are beneath it now. There is no fourth company.

Begin with the builder, and begin with how little of it there is. By mass, almost nothing here is alive. Gather all the living tissue of the world — every green seam, every sunward film, every spore aloft in the columns — and it would make a low hill on one of the flats, a few meters of skin over a planet of bone. The rest is body, but body the way bone is body: laid down by the living, shaped to their needs, and dead the moment it hardens. The world is a skeleton with a growing edge, and the growing edge is the whole of its life.

The skin is not one thing. Learn its colors and you can read its work. The grey-green of the lengthening tips is fast tissue, thin and wet, laying down stone as quickly as its chemistry allows. The verdigris bands are the light-workers, thick-skinned, mineral-loaded, sitting in the worst of the sun and turning it into sugar and exudate. The dull gold is the patient tissue of the hot, dry fronts, slow even by the standards of this world, enduring what the others cannot. Whether these are organs or species is a distinction the world declines to enforce: the skin is a mosaic of strains that graft and part where they meet, and a growth-front's color is at once its job and its bloodline.

Ask whether the builder is one organism and the question dissolves into geography. Spores drift for days, so no seam is cut off forever — but travel far enough and the architecture changes dialect. Cells run larger. Struts take a different cross-section. Domes habitually close at a different angle, and the seams shift from grey-green toward gold. These are bloodlines: each province a descendant-clade of some founding spore, an individual the size of a continent. Where two provinces meet there is a suture — sometimes an annealing, two tissues becoming one, sometimes a hairline wall of doubled stone where each refused the other. And sometimes, at the sutures, there is the slowest war that can be fought: one lineage's span overgrowing another's, sealing it, the sealed tissue dying into the very stone it had been laying, at a rate of a hand's width a year, the contest decided inside a century and legible ever after in cross-section. Nothing cuts sections here. The genealogy goes unread, which costs it nothing.

It has no nerves. What it has is gradients. News travels along the living edges at the pace of chemistry — meters in a day, no faster — and only along the edges, because dead spans carry nothing. A dome's rim is one continuous act of listening: load one side of it and within a season the far side has answered with new growth. Its memory is the stone itself. The skin reads the grain of what it laid yesterday to decide what to lay tomorrow, the way bone everywhere reads its own strain, and so the structure is not just its body but its notes, its habits, its entire idea of itself. Its fastest act is not growth but thirst — a change of turgor at the tips, a lean toward humidity that takes a season to complete. It is the only gesture the world can make, and it makes it toward water. And its body is larger than its stone. All morning it breathes water into the air; by noon its breath is the haze deck; by afternoon the rain comes down addressed to its own cisterns. The circulation of this organism includes the weather. Its boundary is not the surface. Its boundary is the haze.

But the builder is not alone in its body. Start with the water.

At the margin of every open cistern, just inside the white rind of salts, runs a band the width of a finger in the one color this world owns that is not a shade of stone — a deep red, almost wine-dark, the halo of things that love the salt line. Beneath them, in the warm shallows, the old way of life persists: low knobby cushions accreting grain by grain, building themselves in bowls, in miniature, exactly what the builder does at the scale of a planet. Kin, almost certainly. The ancestors' other children, who never left the water. Each addressed rain is an epoch for them — the basin stirred, the layers reset — and in the long stillness between rains the community sorts itself into horizons, each thin stratum living on the gradient it is given, each waiting out the dry weeks at a pace that makes the skin look hasty.

Then there are the sealed ones. When a dome closes over a basin, whatever is in the water inherits a finite world. The light dies first, and everything light-shaped dies or changes after it. Most of these bottles run through their chemistry in decades; then comes the long simplification — communities thinning to a few cells in a liter, dividing on schedules of years, holding on to the small difference between the water and the stone. Some have gone strange in private, in the dark, and each sealed cistern is a separate experiment with a separate ending, and none will ever be opened except by accident. Now and then a collapse unroofs one. Light falls in for the first time in four thousand years. For a few weeks the spill blooms at the breach — everything in it dying of the light it was shaped without — and then the rain refills the basin with the common water of the world, and nothing records the difference.

The dead stone is not quite uninhabited either. In the pore spaces of the oldest spans live the miners: unimaginably slow, tuned to the tick of the world, sipping on the exhale as the day's expansion opens the micropores and the night's cooling closes them. Their slowness is beyond anything the surface knows. And they are, in part, why the spans fall — their acids honeycomb the deep struts, and some of the great collapses begin in stone that has been quietly eaten for ten thousand years. The builder's only answer is the answer it gives everything: growth, burial, replacement. Its immune system is accretion. Now and then the wrong thing grows — a front blooms into fast, weak, porous stone, a fever in the masonry; the span bears its load badly, and one warm night the crack comes, and the collapse is an amputation, and the fever is entombed in its own rubble, seamed over, and becomes an under-room.

In the under-rooms, life keeps the smallest addresses of all. Where a light-well admits its faint diffusion, a dim film spreads at the threshold of the dark, doing the slowest photosynthesis in the world, gathering light the way the cisterns gather water. And under every drip, a coin of pallid threads, non-photosynthetic, living on what the water carries down from the seams above. The drip is their weather, addressed to one spot for centuries. They have never known the lattice, the rain, the sun. Their world is a widening ring of damp on flowstone, and it is enough.

Even the sky has a population, of a sort. The spore columns carry a thin company up with them — cells and cysts lofted off the skin, an aerial plankton measured in cells per cubic meter, riding the thermals for days. Most of it comes down on dead stone and ends there. The air between the columns is the largest desert in the world, and the emptiest.

And under everything, the first world. When the covering began, the burial was slowest at the bottom, and what lived on the original crust did not all die. It retreated into fracture water, into the chemistry of rock under pressure, into the long alchemy of the dark, and it persists — hundreds of meters down, a biosphere that has not seen light since before there was a world to see it from, that does not know the world exists. Nothing of the surface reaches it but percolation: a molecule of the skin's chemistry, altered past recognition, arriving after a thousand years. The only news the deep ever gets. Two biospheres, stacked with a mountain of bone between them, each planetary in extent, each unaware of the other — one building in the light, one enduring in the dark. It may be that the builder's own ancestors came up out of that dark, through some spring, and found the sun, and never stopped. No one will ever bore down to ask. The question will simply stand, which is another way of saying that time will answer it or no one will.

That is the census. A skeleton with a living skin; water kept like a thought in stone rooms; a dust of hangers-on at the margins and in the pores; a buried remnant underneath. Population: one, or thousands, or trillions past counting, depending on how you divide the skin and what you are willing to call an individual. The halls have no walkers, the cisterns no drinkers, the light no eyes. What lives here is the place itself, at work on itself, plus everything small enough to live in the margin of that work — and the margin, like the world, is mostly empty, and like the world, goes on.
