From the causeway the town can look uninhabited — nothing on the roof-field but peat smoke, drying racks, the heron on its dome. You have to learn to hear it. The ticking resolves, after a while, into hands; the wisps at the roof-hatches resolve into breath; and then the Rimers themselves begin to appear, one by one, the way their town appears out of the white: a basket-chain of children and elders passing marsh silt up the ladders, a weaver airing wool on a dome, two figures bent over a gutter with their sleeves rolled, doing the town's endless, ordinary work of staying open.

You will know them anywhere after a day. They are pale and wind-chapped, and the Rime frosts them young: the stone-water baths lime the hair, so that a woman of twenty Rounds and her grandmother come to the same soft white, and the town's true elders are known not by their hair but by how low they live. Their clothes are undyed felt, gray and brown, with hems that whiten and stiffen and finally crack like the crust itself — a coat left hanging too near a seep becomes half a sculpture, and is kept on a high peg thereafter, and pointed out to children. Over everything goes a rush rain-cape, oiled with bird fat from the Passage. Outside they wear wooden pattens against the wet; indoors, bare feet, because a bare foot on a winter floor is the Rimer's one unguarded luxury. Their hands are broad and strong-fingered, nails blanched, palms frosted pale from the stone-water, old cuts sealed with white edges. And they walk like sailors ashore — flat-footed, knees soft, weight carried low, as though the ground might answer. Here, it might.

Their bodies pay the place its due. The damp inheritance is real: the winter cough, the chilblains, the joints that ache before rain. In the glare off the white apron the knappers and roof-workers wear slatted rush eye-shades, which gives them a look of patient blindness that suits them. But the floors never freeze, the down-rooms stay mild, and their consolation is exactly where their hardship is — in the ground. Ask after a Rimer's age and you will be answered in Rounds: she has cut forty Rounds; he has chiseled his lintel twice. Dates work the same way. The year the weaving-house took the water. Three Rounds after the big shiver. The town remembers the way its archive remembers: in layers.

You will notice, talking with them, a pause that outsiders read as slowness. Mid-sentence, a Rimer's head will tilt — the whole town has that tilt — checking some small change in the drip or the tick behind the wall. It is not inattention; half their attention is always below, and they simply assume yours is too. Sound is the town's true common space, and so manners are load-bearing. Walls are thin, so courtesy must be thick: you do not answer sounds not meant for you, you do not finish a sentence overheard through stone, and you never, never raise your voice without cause. A raised voice is a bell, and a town that eavesdrops on itself keeps its alarms clean — a hurt child, a new spring, nothing else. Everything else is said low. Even their humor is low: dry, hydraulic, delivered at a murmur so you must lean in. A Rimer joke is a seep, not a spring.

Every Rimer carries two names. The hailing name is for the roof-field — long-voweled, whistle-able, given within a day to any stranger, since a town without streets still needs to call across itself. The wall-name is soft, indoor, earned or born to. Their proverbs run on water, as everything does. *Give it a path*, they say, of anger, grief, gossip, and news as readily as of springs. *Sealed things rise elsewhere.* Plans end *water willing*. The farewell at a parting is *soft floors*. The worst thing you can call a person is *sealer* — one who dams — and the worst oath is sworn on the threshold.

Their mornings belong to the chisel, and after the chisel hour, the trades take over. The knappers go out to the scarp after rain and gather what it has shed — chert from the fractured seams — and this is law as much as habit: take only what the scarp drops. The face is not to be forced. The slab-croppers pry their soft sheets and cure them in the roof-stacks; the polers, who have the strongest calves in the basin, work the cargo through the marsh channels to the trade landing, and come home with grain, salt, timber, ochre, news, and a quietly horrified account of open country. They are the only Rimers who have slept under an unroofed sky, and they carry drip-gourds — a plugged gourd of stone-water hung to leak beside the bedroll — because a Rimer cannot sleep in silence. Silence is what water makes when it is planning something.

The Round, when it comes, is the year's great social fact as much as its labor. The stone clapper sounds at first light, the owed days begin, and the work-songs set the tempo of the chisels, so that for weeks the whole town ticks in time with itself — a visitor hears what the Rime sounds like when it agrees. Betrothals are advanced at the Round; feuds are too; both are considered maintenance. It ends with a feast and with every gutter running clear, which are the same thing.

Over all of this, not ruling but presiding, are the Hearers. They are chosen young, for stillness rather than cleverness — the test is simple, and most fail it: sit a whole dusk in the cistern room and name everything you heard. Apprenticeship takes years and consists mostly of walking. The water-map is not drawn anywhere; it is carried, every stretch of the lattice held in at least three heads, taught route by route, so that the map walks and listens and ages and is handed on. After a tremor the Hearers go house to house, and the whole town reads their faces from the roofs. A Hearer never says *safe*. A Hearer says: *the water is where it was*, or, *it has moved — we will learn where*. Each carries a lentil of eye-stone on a cord, pressed between ear and floor, the only use permitted for that precious stone besides the querns. And because a Hearer pressing a floor hears more than water — rows, love, weeping, all of it — discretion is their first vow. A Hearer forgets on purpose. The town has made a second use of this: when a grievance has been dammed too long, you bring it to a Hearer and say it out, and they listen it away. Bring it to a Hearer, they say, before it rises through your neighbor's wall.

The senior Hearer is an old woman named Olla — it was her grandmother, or hers, who felt the ground shift. She still makes a last round in the deep night, a hand flat to each spring-eye and threshold, and what she listens for under everything is not a sound but the absence of one: the basin is young, the water is still looking, and her work is only to notice first.

The Keeper of Floors presides over the other memory, the written one that nobody writes. In the archives you go barefoot, you whisper, and you do not point at the prints — the dead stood there; pointing is for strangers. Deeds and tallies are knotted cords, pressed into wet rime to make them permanent, and when a dispute outlasts memory, the parties go down together and ask the floor. The floor's testimony has settled boundary, marriage, and inheritance for generations; traders say the Rimers cannot lie, which is not quite true — it is only that in the Rime nothing stays hidden, and even the floors testify. The Keeper's assistants are the pale spiders, the small keepers, whose webs are never disturbed in the deep rooms because they take the midges that would otherwise take the archive. It is the Keeper who tells the children, without emphasis, the story of the Sealed House — the one ghost house at the marshward edge that was not surrendered but taken, in a single night, by the water, because its owner wanted a dry floor and cut off a seep to get it. The family was taken in by neighbors; the house was not. The Keepers tell it without a moral. The moral is structural.

The children belong to the roofs. They are the town's messengers, whistling hailing-names across the domes, sure-footed as the heron is patient, and their games are the town's games — gutter-jumping, hush-tag, the forbidden run out to the scarp for shed flakes that every generation undertakes and every generation is forbidden. The night fish are neighbors, not larder: a child may hold them, cupped, feeling the small whiskered weight of them, and may not keep them. Their schooling is water-reading. They learn the shine of damp on a wall the way other children learn letters, and they learn the town by tongue — each runnel tastes of its own spring, and a child blindfolded can tell you which quarter's water is in the cup. They do their sums in knots. At about the tenth Round a child is given a first chisel, the hailing-name knotted into the haft-cord, and cuts a first small bowl, which cures lopsided and takes its place in the row of first-bowls on somebody's kitchen shelf, in every kitchen, in the whole town.

Courtship happens where the hush is masked — in the steam of the bathing runnel at dusk, or out in the rush-beds, the only walls in the basin that are green. More betrothals come home from the rush-cutting than the cutting strictly requires. A wedding is a door: the two families spend a day chiseling a new opening in the shared wall, and the pale dust sits on everyone's shoulders like a first frost. The row trowels up the new room, and the one gift no household refuses is a jar of the orange film, to paint — to seed — the fresh wall, so it will grow. And because privacy is impossible, it is given instead: on the wedding night the whole row sings at full voice until the small hours, work-songs, the one night a Rimer house is loud on purpose, so that the new couple may be, for once, graciously unheard. A marriage that ends ends the way anything must end here: the door is given to the water. A channel is cut through the old threshold, a runnel passed gently through what was a doorway, and the two houses share nothing thereafter but water — which is what they shared before everything else.

They eat like people who live where they live. The first cup offered to a guest is sky-water, and you can judge a household by how freely it is poured. Porridge and gray cress and pickled thumb-onions; sweet tubers roasted in peat ash; smoked bird through the winter from the Passage glut; sedge beer brewed in the warm down-rooms, the one thing the cold town ferments well. And in every pot, crumbled like stock, a thumb of the dark midge cake — the taste of the Rime is smoke, mineral, bitter green, and that.

Of the other citizens, the Heron is first. Its name is among the oldest words the town has; it means the bird, and it means patience, and it is their word for the gray of the hour before rain, and they use it in all three senses without confusion. When a Heron dies the day goes soft; the body is poled quietly out into the marsh, and the children are told it has gone fishing. When the next bird lands on the Headhouse dome — one always does — it is told it has come back, and the town goes on watching it the way other towns watch a flag. The gutter-fish are the town's tasters: if they ever stop running, the Hearers will be sent for before dawn. The midges are the year's only buzzing, swept and pressed, eaten by the handful on dares. The passage flocks are the loud weeks, feathers saved and bundled for trade. And beneath them all, the rust-orange and bottle-green — the only saturated colors in the basin, the Rime's only flag, edged onto festival cloth — are tended and carried in jars and painted onto every new wall. The stone is partly alive, and the Rimers are its gardeners as much as its tenants. The apron itself they call, simply, the Back. They speak of it the way farmers elsewhere speak of weather: not worship. Attention.

The dead go down, as everything here goes down. The first drink and the last drink are the same — sky-water — and then the family cuts the niche, and will tell you that the chiseling helps; grief, like water, must be given a path. For the plaque winters they come and chip the face clear, a few minutes of the tick-tick that is the town's whole music, and there is no rule for the year they stop; a family knows. The last chipping is done by the youngest of the house, who says the name once, aloud. *Gone under*, the town says, and means it without sorrow's sharpness: after that the dead are underfoot, load-bearing, present-tense, and the children grow up knowing which wall is which grandmother. Niches tick sometimes as they settle. The stone is drinking, they say. It is meant fondly.

The old go down by stages, an elder's address deepening as the stairs steepen, until they live beside the archive and become part of its index. Winter is theirs: the long Quiet, the slack deposition, the stories in the warm rooms. And there are the few who go out with the slabs and do not come back — the town says *gone dry*, and pities them gently, for they will spend the rest of their lives listening for the tick. The ones who return are the Rime's only travelers' tales, and they say the strangest thing about the dry world is not the space or the sun but the silence of the houses at night, and how anyone in it can sleep.

Stand with Olla at the end of her night round, in the blue hour before the clapper, and you will see the whole arrangement at its stillest. In a down-room of the eldest row a three-day-old is being given her first possession: a lump of soft rime-curd pressed into her fist. It will harden around her grip — her first entry in the archive she will live her life on top of — and her mother will keep it, and one day it goes down with her. Above, a man rolls his sleeves and soaks his hands in the runnel, and the steam rises off them, and the first chisel of the morning ticks once, testing. The Heron shifts on the dome. The town wakes the way it sleeps — packed, damp, shoulder to shoulder on the back of the spring, listening first, and then, one chisel at a time, answering.
