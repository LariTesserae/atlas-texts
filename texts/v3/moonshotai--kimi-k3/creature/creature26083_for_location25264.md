What lives here can be counted on two hands, if you count the way the Still counts — by kind, not by head. The grey. The green. The pale. The kept. The keepers. And the one large thing doing the breathing.

Begin with the grey, because it was here first — or it wasn't; the canteen's chalk margins run a second front of the surveyors' war on exactly this question. The Founders point to the drip-lines, where amber-grey felts spread their slow chemistry across the wet stone and long strands hang like the beards of old men, fattening in the exhale, paling in the draw: nothing so patient colonizes so fast, they say; the felt was found with the void. The Makers point to the scale furring the pipes — the same grey life, gone feral in the dark of the plumbing — and say it came down the four hundred stairs the way everything else did: on boots, on rope, in the water-film on a beam. Both sides are sure. Both keep their half of the margin updated. The argument is maintained the way the garden is maintained, and for the same reason.

What is not argued is the treaty. A chalk line runs along the third tank of Cistern Row: above the line the felt may live; below the line the reamer goes. The pipes are scraped; the drip-lines are left; the grey keeps its side of the chalk, mostly, and where it doesn't, it is moved the way you'd move a sleeping animal, almost gently, to the other side. It eats what the Breath brings up — iron, sulfur, the warm chemistry of young rock — asks nothing of the lamps, and its only enemy is the inside of a pipe.

The moss is the exception to everything. In a hall of chemistry-eaters, it eats light: a chlorotic halo at the foot of the arc lamp's standard, pale green, faintly luminous, farming the one cool-colored thing in the world. When the arc goes dark for lamp-service, the halo holds a rumor of the light for a minute after — the only light in the Still that lingers — and whoever is free at that hour tends to be nearby, the way people drift toward a window. The tenders read it the way miners once read canaries: a bright halo means air and drip are right; a dim one is a gauge no brass gives. Instrument first. Then, unavoidably, company.

The fungal fans are the wildlife and the ornament both. They shelf from the damp timber — bunk frames, the Tally House doorframe, the underside of the canteen table — porcelain-pale, perfect, uneaten. The ledger's margin records, in the house hand, dated before any name now chalked over a bunk, that they were tasted once: not poison, not food. Since then nothing has bothered them, because nothing here is hungry for them and the crew declines to become the thing that is. Count the shelves on a fan and you can date the damp; they are clocks of wet timber, dusted when the timber is wiped, the way a town keeps its monuments clean.

Then the garden, which is the heart of it. A window-box trough under a dedicated bulb — power spent on green while the lamps go rationed; the requisition book defends it, in the house hand, as *morale*, and the word is accurate. A tight bright row of cress, the greenest thing within a thousand meters of rock, small enough to hold in both arms. It is thinned with fingers — scissors are for rope — and the thinnings go to the canteen and are divided exactly, two leaves to a bowl, eaten slowly. The packet the seed came down in is pressed in the back of the ledger like a leaf: a paper sun with printed rays, the only portrait of the sun for a thousand meters. New tenders are shown the wrench, the Sump, and the packet. That is the whole orientation. They save seed now, and the cress from saved seed comes up stockier, narrower in the leaf, learning the bulb for a sun. If the Still is deciding what to become, the cress is deciding alongside. The tenders take this as a vote.

The oldest tended life in the Still is a yeast. It lives in a stone jar in the canteen, it is fed from the sealed flour tin, and the initials chalked inside the lid go back past every bunk-name. The ledger is precise on the distinction: it is not an animal, and the Still has no animals. But it is the only inhabitant that dies on schedule if it is not fed, which makes it, for all practical purposes, livestock. The town keeps a herd; the herd is one jar. Bread comes out of a lidded tin set on the Stack's warm skin, timed so the loaf is done with the exhale — steam to steam — and the tenders who wake before the Breath wake, on bread days, to the smell of it, which is the kindest alarm the Still has.

Then the keepers. Twelve bunks in the bunkroom, all made, some slept in — and the making is the point: the beds are made every cycle, all twelve, whether or not anyone sleeps in them, because a camp keeps the beds it needs and a town keeps its places set. Names are chalked above the bunks; chalk fades in the wet heat; every cycle someone refreshes every name, including the ones nobody currently answers to. The census is thus legible and unverifiable, like everything the Still says about itself. The figures on the tally board are written in the house hand — every tender trained to the same square capitals, so the board reads as a single writer. Twelve writing as one: that is how the town talks to itself, and how it answers the question of how many. In one voice. Without answering.

Every new tender asks it once. The answer is a walk: up past Cistern Row, to the ledge, to the wrench in its calcite sheath. *This* is what the rock does to whatever stays still. The town must be scraped to remain a town; the count must be left alone to remain a crew. You stop asking. A season later you walk someone else up to the wrench. That is how everything here is handed down — one tender at a time, on iron stairs, in warm dark.

What can be counted instead is the culture, which is large out of all proportion to the hands. Test every rail with the back of the hand; read every gauge you pass — a tender cannot walk past a gauge without reading it, the way you cannot pass a mirror; gloves for the young rock's glass edges; step around the shimmer over the vent field. Nothing arrives in the canteen that was not carried down four hundred stairs, which is why the flour tin is sealed against weevils that do not exist: you do not waste what cost that. The rock drinks shouts but carries knocks — the young basalt rings — so the crew talks in pipe-code: two knocks for all's well, three for come and see, one long for the Breath's turn. The plumbing doubles as the telephone, which means every gauge-post is also an ear. The pneumatic tubes in the Tally House tick like a resting insect, and since there are no insects, the crew calls the ticking the cricket, and says the cricket's awake when a message comes, and the joke never gets old, because it is the only one of its kind.

The Breath is the postman. Each exhale takes the loose papers and deals them to new corners, and the gathering is an institution: notes are left in margins for whoever gathers next — a gauge's mood, a joke, one line of a song — and the mail is never late, because there is no schedule for it to be late against. Every tender sings, at some hour, at some task. No tender has ever been heard doing it. Twenty paces and the young rock has the song. The Still is full of music the way a held breath is full.

The body is re-set within a cycle. You sleep by the Breath; you learn to wake a minute before the exhale; old tenders wake before it always — in the drifts, in the hall, and on leave above, where there is no exhale at all: they wake anyway, in the dark, and lie listening for it, and the sun comes up without being asked. You learn the cisterns by taste — Row Three runs sweet, Row One is iron on the tongue — tea steeped in water warm as a held hand, and arguments about the Rows that would shame a vineyard. And at dawn on the coldest nights, the Collar feathers with rime, and the bucket goes up. They check for it the way children above check the sky for snow. When it comes, the scrapings are divided exactly — the coldest water in the world, doled out a thimble at a time, tasting of stone and height — and for one cup the tenders remember that cold is something weather does, not something a valve does. The cold cup is the Still's only festival, and it belongs to weather, which is to say to the surface, which is to say to the sun: the rumor, acknowledged once a cold dawn, in a thimble.

And the Sump keeps its own counsel and its own share — the one water in the Still not owed upward. The silt prints lead in and out: at the Breath's turn someone reads the black mirror; if it lies still through the exhale, the drifts are well; if it shivers off the beat, someone walks the long gallery listening. Sometimes the same silt is tracked across the floor to the sealed drift door, where the prints end. Two knocks go through the door's pipe. Two come back. The plumbing says all's well. The plumbing has never once been asked how many.

Count all of it — the grey, the green, the pale, the jar, the row, the beds — and you have still only counted the furnishings. The bulk of what lives here is the thing doing the breathing. The young rock is not scenery; it is the animal the town is inside of, or the child the town is raising. Geology here runs at a pace you can watch, which means it runs at a pace you can tend, and tending is the entire relationship: the gutters bud stone, the pipes fur, the ledge sheathes the wrench a season at a time — the rock builds, the crew scrapes, and neither is winning, and the stalemate is the town. The Founders and the Makers are both wrong about the void. It was not found; it is not made; it is being made, now, by calcite and by reamer, drip by counted drip, and what it will be when it is finished — a town, a machine, a lung, a reservoir — is being decided the way everything is decided here: quietly, on schedule, warm to the touch, in chalk.

For the ledger's other page, then. Census of the Still: grey felts, treaty-bound, uncounted. One halo of moss, farming the arc light, keeping its minute of rumor after dark. Fans beyond numbering on the wet timber, perfect, uneaten. One jar of yeast, older than any name over a bunk. One row of cress, thinned recently, by someone, learning its bulb for a sun. Some number of people between three and twelve: the beds say twelve, the house hand says one, the plumbing says all's well. One breath. One young rock, still deciding. And the tally chalk, already changing somewhere behind you, in a hand you have not seen yet.

Stay a cycle and you stop reading the census. Learn the knock, the rail-test, the cisterns by taste, the waking-before. One dawn the fresh figures on the board will be yours, and on the warm stairs someone newer will read them, and look around the amber dim at the terraces, the gauges, the made beds, and wonder who lives here. The Still maintains itself around the rumor of its crew. The crew maintains the rumor the way it maintains everything — and if you are reading this the way a tender reads, checking each gauge as you pass, then the rumor, by now, includes you.
