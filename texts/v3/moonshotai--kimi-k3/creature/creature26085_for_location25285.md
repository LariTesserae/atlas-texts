Ask one of them what lives in the room and you will be given a census, because census is the shape of their thought. The list is short and they learn it before they learn to work: the people, the veils, the fish, the hoppers, the Eel. And then, because they count in three tenses, two more entries that are not alive and are not absent: the sealed, and the shed. Everything else in the chamber is stone, water, or fire, and of those only fire is kept.

The people first, because the people keep the list.

A person of the room, held in an outsider's palm, would feel like cool wet stone that breathes. Their skin is pale past paleness — translucent at the ears, the webs of the fingers, the small of the back — and in the newborn it is nearly clear, so that for the first days of a life the heart is visible from outside: a soft red bead, beating, carried in a sling of veil-cloth against the parent's chest. Their eyes are wide and dark and give back the lamplight doubled, so that in the dim city you always know when you are being looked at; attention, here, glints. Their feet are broad and long-toed, made for ledge and water both, and they read through them — a knock struck on stone three streets away arrives in the soles, and the Eel, when it comes, arrives there first. They oil their skins with fish oil, against the cold and for the gleam of it, and an oiling after a molt is one of the small ceremonies nobody remembers inventing: hands working warmth into new skin while the husk dries on its peg.

To cross the open floor is to walk through the only weather the room has — the drip-zones, rain that has always been falling. Children dodge the big drops for sport. The old simply know where they fall, the way they know everything that has never changed.

The molt is the short beat of a life, and everything social bends around it. The skin loosens first at the wrists and the ridge of the spine; the person works the old self off slowly, a glove turned inside out, until they stand in skin that is briefly pearl, briefly tender, and there is a whole pale ghost of them in their hands. The new-skinned are fed by hand and kept near the lamps and forbidden work, and the neighborhood covers their duties without being asked, because everyone is owed the same softness a few dozen times a life. The husk goes up on the peg-row above the lintel, one more ghost in the family frieze. And then one year a person molts and knows — the way the stone here is known to be finished — that this is the last one. The skin that follows is the one they will be folded in. After that they are called finished, and it is not a sad word. It is the room's own word for things that have come out right.

Their hands are their biography and their signature. At the molt that brings full growth, each person presses their plug: both palms deep into fine sump-clay, the print of a hand at its prime, kept wrapped in veil-cloth in the dwelling until the day it is needed. Which means the dead are all sealed by their own young hands. Walk the walls and read them: the small prints of children taken early, the broad palms of cord-menders, the long fingers of basin-keepers — a city of youth, thumb-deep in clay, holding its own dead in. The niches ring by their hollowness, each with a note, and families learn the notes of their sealed the way you learn a voice. A knock on grandmother's plug comes back shaped like grandmother. The exceptions are the empty niches — plugged over nothing, for the ones who went down the sumps and did not come back to be folded. Those ring high and unanswered, and they are the only places in the city that feel like outside. People do not knock there.

Speech lives in the Chord's gaps, and so do names. A name is not a word; it is a tap-pattern fitted to a particular interval, and the midwife chooses it by listening — she hears where the newborn's first cry falls in the Chord and builds the name around that gap. You are named for the interval that held you. To call someone across the crowded city you speak their rhythm, and everyone who shares their ledge-street hears a name go by like a fish through a weir. Privacy, in a city where everyone is within arm's reach of everyone, is the whispering gallery and the manners around it. Children are taught the courtesy of not-hearing: if a shaped breath not meant for you touches your ear along the wall, you let it pass through and keep nothing. To repeat an overheard whisper is the one true crime, and its punishment is the closest thing to silence these people can be given — for a span of days, no one speaks in your interval. They have no word for what that is. They have a great deal of dread for it.

Nine hundred, and the number is not enforced; it is noticed. They do not speak of permission to conceive. They say a life leaves and a life is lent. Quickening does not take in a full city — that is simply observed, the way the drips are observed — and after a sealing, within days, some long-waiting couple's child begins, and the midwife and the plug-maker may work the same street in the same span of lamp-trimmings. The birth happens in the shallow basins, in the cold black water the child will wade for the rest of its life. The first cry finds its gap. The census is recited and comes out right.

Work is the liturgy and everyone holds an office in it. The veil-tenders know their shingles by grain and their strains by lineage — old-white from the eastern seeps, iron-sweet from the red line — cropped with chert flakes, dried on warm stones, pressed to cakes, twisted to thread. The lamp-keepers learn flame-height palm-above-flame, feeling for the exact heat-distance their teachers felt; and when a lamp gutters out, as lamps do, it is relit from a neighbor's wick, so that all their fire is one fire, loaned hand to hand since before the molts record. The basin acolytes are the calmest people alive. Their apprenticeship is measured in molts and consists of learning to do what every child can half-do — parse the Chord into its separate notes — until they can name four hundred drips in order and hear a worn basin-lip the way you would hear a wrong note in a song you had sung your whole life. There is one of them, in her final skin, who keeps the forty drips that fall over the deep shelf and is teaching them to her granddaughter, basin by basin, a hair's width at a time. Nothing has ever needed adjusting. She adjusts. That is the whole of the calling, and everyone understands it, because it is everyone's calling writ small: the room was finished before them, and they are its finishing, kept wet.

The other lives are few enough to be loved individually as kinds. The veils are the flock and the wolf both — the crop and the slow hunger it is fenced against — and the very old, who have finished everything else, sit and watch the veil-line creep back from the lamp-glow, the only motion in the room slow enough to be interesting. The white fish come up from the sumps, and this matters to the people more than they say: the not-room sends them. Everything of the fish is used — oil for light, bones for needles and hooks — and a great catch is an event by which molts are dated. The hoppers are the room's one jest, pale and ridiculous, the only irregular rhythm in a world of intervals, and on the recitation-days the new-skinned chase them through the mat-gardens while the whole city laughs in clicks. Then the catch is divided nine hundred ways, each share no more than a taste set on the tongue — the entire city tasting one life together, rich beyond telling, because it is the only telling of richness they have.

The Eel is everything else. It is felt first by the very old, whose loose-jointed bodies are tuned lowest; then the word goes out along the whispering gallery, the one message every ear is meant to catch, and the city does the thing it has no word for. Work stops. Speech stops. The knock of shuttles, the flaking of chert, the soft percussion of nine hundred lives — all of it sets down at once, and for the length of a passage there is only the Chord, and the people standing in the water with their broad feet planted, feeling the floor of the world go round and deep with a body beneath it. Infants are held down so their feet touch the water: their first Eel, the beginning of their true calendar. Everything after is dated from it. And once — the molts record it, and the molts do not lie — it was found still at the sump-mouth, and rendered, and its oil went into the oldest lamps, where it is fed still, a drop at a time, on the recitation-days; and by the next circuit it was alive again, and circling. From this the people have drawn the only two conclusions available, and they hold both without strain: that there is one Eel and it cannot die, or that there are many and the deep sends them one at a time. Either way, the Eel is the proof the sumps go somewhere. Either way, the oldest light in the city is the body of the longest life, burning.

They sleep in turns, so the city never sleeps all at once and the lamp-lines are never unattended, and they dream the way they live — in sound. They say the sealed dream too, and that the faint resonance of the walls is their dreaming, and that to knock gently is to walk through a dream without waking it. They say the shed husks dream of growing. Some say the sealed live on in the doubled city under the black floor, where the lamps hang flame-downward and the drips fall up. It is not a doctrine. It is a way of loving the reflection.

What they fear is short to list, because so little can go wrong: that a lamp-line might fail all together, which has never happened; that a basin's note might drift, which has never happened; and the pull of the sumps, which has happened. There is a recognized temperament, the sump-eared — the ones who linger too long at the water's doors, listening down. They are not shamed. They are loved and watched and given duties near the lamps, and if one of them finally goes down on a cord and the cord comes back up empty, the city seals an empty niche and grieves in the high unanswered note of it, and the child who quickens after is said to listen before it looks.

And there is this, which the acolytes do and the people understand: an acolyte will sometimes cup the seep-water where it wells fresh from the wall, and taste it. It is the only thing that enters the room from the not-room, the only news that arrives, and it always tastes the same. Nothing has changed. The world beyond the world sends word, and the word is: nothing. They drink it like comfort.

Watch them on a recitation-day, all nine hundred gathered along the ledges and cord-bridges, the hopper-taste still on their tongues, the sealed-census recited plug by plug along the walls until every dead hand has been named. Then the wall-choir: a rhythm chosen to ride the Chord's longest interval, and nine hundred small hands striking the plugs of their ancestors in time, so the niches answer — the whole crust of the city humming like a struck hive, the dead speaking in their shaped notes, the Chord underneath it all, the lamps doubled in the black floor, and somewhere below the floor the Eel mid-circuit, felt in the feet like a second, slower chord. The room absorbs the choir the way the water absorbs the rings — completely, without change.

The room holds its note. The people are the holding.
