The census of the chamber is short, and you will make it wrong the first time.

You will count the salamander, and stop. One animal lives here; the plant is the place; everything else is weather or furniture. That first census is wrong in both directions. It counts too little, because the room is fuller of life than any ground this dry has a right to be. And it counts too much, because most of the lives down here are one life, wearing different speeds.

Take it slowly. In order of obviousness.

---

First, the animal, since it is the only one.

The salamander is the length of a forearm and pale as something that has never needed color — root-pale, cave-pale, the pale of the inside of a wrist. When it climbs a light-vein it glows; the green lives under the skin, in the cells themselves, algae keeping house in an animal. Its limbs are translucent to the bone, and the bones are green. Its eyes are the only true black below ground. Every other darkness in the room is green at some depth, but the eyes are night from before there were leaves.

It breathes against the room. In, when the chamber draws its long dusk breath inward; out, at the dawn exhale — a small fast counter-rhythm beneath the great slow one, so that between the two of them the air is never entirely still. Whether this is courtesy or engineering, no one has asked it. It does not appear to age. Nothing here is old enough to age, but the salamander carries its agelessness like a profession, like the one thing it is entirely serious about.

---

Its day has a liturgy. Morning on the weeping wall, spread flat against the cool quadrant where the light-veins converge, belly to the brightening tissue, limbs out like a swimmer's. It basks the way a lizard basks, except that it is feeding: the algae in it drink the conducted sun, and it drinks the algae, and so it eats daylight twice removed. Midday it grazes the moss-felt, and the moss is the plant growing its own younger stage upon itself, so the salamander spends the slow hours eating the room's childhood, which the room regrows by morning without apparent resentment. At dusk it stations itself at the foot of the throat, front feet together, patient as a heron, because the dusk inhale brings the world's small life down the flutes — a beetle, a moth, an event. What the digesting pools drown, it harvests. It reads the pools the way you read weather: the still ones are the dangerous ones, where the cells have sunk to the bottom to wait, and the water looks poured rather than held.

At the indoor rain it comes out from behind its rib and turns its face up into the drops, and swims, and for those few loud minutes it is only an animal, and glad.

And it clicks at the echoes. A sound like two pebbles tapped once. An hour later the ceiling gives it back as a small green patter, and it cocks its head and listens to itself, delayed and translated, and seems to find the translation acceptable. Some nights it clicks far more than it needs to. The ones who visit think it is stocking the weather — giving the room rain to practice with.

They call it the one who was already there, because it was. The first person down the throat found it basking on the weeping wall as if the chamber had opened around it. No one has seen it young; no one will see it old. There is always exactly one, in every room of this kind the dry country remembers, and it is always already there. What that means, the ones who visit do not say, because they do not know, and they have learned to be careful about asking after the room's arrangements.

---

Second, the greens. There are three, and they are one.

The moss is the plant remembering being small. The pool-cells are the plant let loose — each green, each swimming under its own power, a population instead of a tissue. The algae in the salamander are the plant domesticated, the plant keeping house in an animal. One green at three speeds.

The pool water is not water, strictly; it is a crowd. A gourdful would be a herd. This is why the rule: water from the weeping wall only, never the pools. The rule is older than any reason anyone can still repeat, which is how you know the reason was bad.

Watch the pools at midday and they watch back, in their way. Disturb the water and it glows, and the glow is not alarm but attention. Hold your hand above the surface and the cells gather in its shadow, drinking the dark you brought. At night they migrate pool to pool through the veins of the floor, and the floor pulses green at midnight, slow as sap. The salamander swims their circuit and they bunch and follow it in a comet-tail; where it sleeps, the nearest pool is brightest. It keeps them the way a shepherd keeps a flock that is also, cell for cell, its own blood.

The ones who visit say the salamander is a pool that learned to walk — that in the first days, before anyone came down the throat, the cells gathered in the middle of the floor and stood up on four legs. It is not true, probably. But the green under its skin is the green of the pools, and it has never been seen to eat anything the room did not touch first.

---

Third, the guest — because the visitor does not live here, and is the only inhabitant that returns, and returning is its own kind of residence.

They come after the big rains — twice a year, if the year is kind. Alone. Cord tied to the leaf-stem, gourd empty. The rules are older than the room: the dry country has met these before, a grandmother's grandmother watched one finish and leave, and the rules were waiting at the surface when the leaf opened, the way shade waits under a stone.

Nothing with a name is spoken inside. The visitor clicks instead — once at the throat's foot, meaning arrived; once at the weeping wall, meaning asking; once before the climb out, meaning thank you or forgive me, the clicks do not distinguish. They learned the clicks from the salamander, or from the room's echoes of the salamander, which an hour later amounts to the same thing.

They drink from the weeping wall, intercepting the slow sideways beads before the floor can have them. They cut a little fiber, sparingly, and where the blade passes the light-veins dim for a moment — a small local dusk — and the visitor waits it out with their head bowed. They build nothing; nothing built stays built. And once a season the room takes its one thing.

It is a tithe now, because the visitors have learned to offer, so that the room need not choose. The twist of cord-fiber hanging in the void was laid on the floor deliberately and was hanging by the next visit. The chert flake chose itself — it broke mid-stroke and sprang, and the air took it gently out of reach, and the visitor's hand slowed going after it, and they understood a tithe had been collected, and were easier after. The moth offered itself: it fell through the throat on the dusk breath and was taken not as a meal but as a relic, dried to paper, wings spread — the only kept thing that arrived alive. The long hair is no one's the current visitor will claim. Some kept things have owners. It is better not to know which.

At dawn the visitor waits at the throat's foot for the exhale, and the breath of the room carries them up the barbed flutes the way a fruit puts out a seed. They land in the dust blinking, gourd full, smelling of crushed stems, and for some days after, their cord glows faintly at the hem in the dark, and the children know the room is still open.

Between visits they exist here the way the dead exist in a house: as something the walls practice. The room kept their standing-place for a day. It keeps their clap forever.

---

Fourth, the sounds. They are the largest population in the room, and the only one that never dies — it only translates.

Every sound made below is still below. The afternoon chorus of snapping water columns is not only this afternoon's; it is every afternoon, stored and replayed into the new one, greener each time. The salamander's clicks come back as patter. The visitor's clap came back as rain. Nothing is absorbed; everything is filed.

And the room keeps names. At night it practices them in drip-code and slow creak, and the vowels improve by the season. The rule against speaking names inside is the oldest rule, older than the cord, and its reason is arithmetic: the room keeps what it can hold, and a name is a handle. If a name is ever spoken — a slip, a child, an endearment — the custom is to return at the next rain and say many names, all of them wrong, until the true one is buried under false ones and the room spends its nights practicing strangers.

Once, a visitor heard the drip-code fall into step with the seam's cold hum, like a lesson with a patient tutor. She did not say whose name it was getting right. She has not come back.

---

Fifth, the seam — which is not an inhabitant, but is the only thing in the room that any inhabitant watches.

Behind the rib the wall goes thin as a meniscus, and through it: a green that is not the green of leaves, with weather in it, somewhere that is raining upward. Cold comes off it, the only cold. The hum comes off it too, a bottle-note, rising a little between visits as the seam heals narrower. It is the sound of a door still open a finger's width.

Nothing has come through it since the seed. But the salamander goes there sometimes and stands before the lens, still in a way it is never otherwise still, watching the upward rain. It does not click there. The ones who visit have seen this and do not like it, the way you do not like seeing a dog watch a road.

The seed-husk hanging in the void is from no plant that grows under this sky. It may be the room's own cradle, kept. The salamander watches the seam. The visitor watches the salamander. The room watches everything, the way walls do. Everyone here is watching something arrive or leave.

---

Sixth, the census turns, because the obvious answer was the true one all along. The room lives here.

It is the only full-time resident. Everything else is its guest, its flock, its echo, or its luggage. It breathes, and the throat is its breathing. It pulses, and the double beat in the floor is its heart. It grows at night with a sound like distant fire, and the crackle is its sleep-talk. Its memory is tiered like a shrine: an image kept a day, a sound kept forever, an object hung forever at heart-height. The walls keep its own past for it — the dark lenses of swallowed chambers, the stone held mid-meal, the pale lace of the tree that was this ground's first tenant, eaten and worn now like a signature the room has taught itself.

It is weeks old. It is loud. It is practicing — rain at noon, names at night, dispersal at dawn on the bodies of its guests. The ones who visit understand that it is not a place in the world but a thing the world is briefly holding, and they behave accordingly: the way you behave in the house of someone who is packing.

---

But the census is wrong a second time, too, because all of it — leaf, throat, walls, the loud green vessels — exists to enclose the emptiness at the center, and the emptiness is the only thing in the room that is becoming more itself.

The kept things hang at heart-height, and higher, and lower, as if around a body. One a season. It is beginning to describe a shape. Ask the ones who visit what shape, and you will get different answers: a hand. A leaf. A door. The honest ones say: a guest. The room keeps objects the way you furnish a house for someone you have not met; it keeps names the way you practice a language before a journey; it keeps the salamander the way a ship keeps a cat. When the seam closes it will seal, and depart through the thin places, and unpack all of it under some other sky — the green animal, the stored weather, the husk, the hair, the almost-perfect name.

For now the census stands at this: one animal, and it was already there. One green at several speeds. One guest who returns, and is returned. A nation of sounds. Seven kept things. One keeper the size of a room. And the tenant — which is almost nothing at all, and is loved the way the dry country loves rain: by everything arranging itself around the absence.
