Nearly everything that lives here is one thing.

Open the census and the first page is a single entry: the felt. It is not a community — it is a population, a few genetic individuals spread to geological size. Where two of them meet you can find the seam if you know to look: a slight change in the black, a shallow ridge where the rhizomes of one will not knit with the rhizomes of the other, and the voles' tunnels turn along the boundary like roads at a border. Each genet began when the mud was raw — a fragment, a spore, a strand washed down the wall — so the oldest living tissue in the basin is exactly as old as the drainage, no older. Nothing alive here predates the pumps except the fish.

The felt does everything slowly enough that you can mistake it for ground. It drinks the fog. It photosynthesizes at the dim floor of possibility and banks the surplus in rhizome the color of old linen. It keeps the basin's books: every drum, plank, and footprint is entered in its skin at millimeters a year, legible to anyone with a knife and patience. Its only visible deaths are the poisoned rings of the old drum fields, and it is re-taking those at a decade per meter. Once in several years it has sex — the bloom — and the whole basin smells briefly of the idea of sweetness, and then it goes back to being ground.

The fish are the exception, the ancien régime. When the lake was drained they did what their kind had always done — kept to the deepening middle — and the middle became the Sump, and they are there still: dark, slow, growing a fraction a year, some of them older than the roads, older than the second generation of engines, older than every keeper who has ever signed a logbook. No one angles for them. This is not policy; nothing is written. But they are the last citizens of the lake that was, and you do not eat the elders. The intake screens turn to keep them out of the works, mostly; a fish found dead in a sump pit is entered in the log with its length. The owl takes one sometimes. That is permitted. The owl is a citizen too.

Under the felt, in the white mat, live the voles — the basin's livestock, its staple crop of meat. The mat is a hand deep, cool, wet, roofed with moss, and they tunnel it the way their kind elsewhere tunnels thatch; they are almost never in the open, and when they are, it is at the ditch edges at certain hours, and the owl knows the hours. Every vole is born into the engine note the way other voles are born into weather, and in bloom years they eat the pale cups and multiply, and the foxes raise more kits, and the owl grows fat enough that the logbooks remark on it. Trace any food web here far enough down and it starts at the fuel tanks.

Where warmth leaks, life concentrates with a precision you could survey. Above each outfall stands the midge column — the basin's only swarm, a shimmering rod from the black water to the lamplight, day and night, all year. A midge's whole geography is that column: born in the warm ditch, risen into the plume, mated in it, dead on the water, a hundred generations within earshot of one lamp. It is the most local life there is. And along the rising mains the felt runs subtly lusher, a darker, thicker line tracing each chord across the floor, because the lagged steel leaks a degree or two and the felt leans toward it. From the cableway pylons, the basin's vegetation draws a map of its plumbing.

Every station keeps ravens, or is kept by them. They are station-specific; the pair at one hall does not visit the next except in overhaul season, when they arrive uninvited and unerring, days before the extra crews, and the keepers say the ravens read the work orders. They work the midge column like fishermen working a seam. They ride the stack plume in deep cold, spiraling up the one thermal under the Lid until they vanish into the haze and come down furred with rime. They cache scraps in the rime gardens where nothing thaws, and the foxes find the caches, and the ravens watch them do it. Only in the quiet sector can you hear their wings — a sound like shaken canvas — because everywhere else the bass takes it. In the logbooks they appear by description adequate to name: the crooked-tail, the one that takes food from the hand.

The owl is singular. There is one, white, resident, and the whole basin is its parish. Because the drone fills the frequencies its kind hunts by, it has learned to hunt by sight and patience — low quartering over the felt, still-hunting from a perch, and perches are scarce in a world with nothing tall. It uses the pylons near the floor. It uses the tug: two kilometers from the present water, the drowned wheelhouse is the highest seat on the basin floor, and the owl sits there in the rime like a figurehead come inside out of the weather, waiting for the voles that live in the felt over the keel. When a sector goes quiet for overhaul the owl moves in, and the keepers claim you can tell it is hunting by ear again — it stoops at things no one can see. Its pellets accumulate on a warm manifold where it roosts out of the wind, and the keepers open them and count vole jaws: the cheapest census in the basin.

The foxes have solved the cold the way everything here solves it — by moving in with the machines. Their dens are hollows in the pipeline lagging, the only warm dens under the Lid, and the kits are born to the ticking of the steel and sleep through the roar from their first hour. A fox on business uses the trestle line as a road, tracks running the chord for kilometers, paws stained rust where they cross the dead rings of the drum fields. They cache what the bloom years give them; the felt heals over the caches; they dig back through the scar tissue unerringly. One at each station becomes half-known — the cross fox, the lame one — and is written into the logbook, and watched for, and grieved, eventually, correctly.

And there are the travelers. In passage season geese come down out of the moving world and find the Sump, the only open water, and stay a day, two days, and this is the basin's holiday: the word goes station to station — geese on the Sump, eleven — and every keeper who can walk out does. The ground eats the treble of their calling; you watch them call across the water and the sound arrives thinned, close, as if the basin were turning their volume down out of tact. Then they rise over the rim, and for weeks there are feathers on the felt, and one feather pressed in a logbook, and that is all. Smaller than this the record thins: springtails and rotifers in the mat, a dark sheen on the brine pools that is either mineral or alive — no one has checked; the ravens will not drink there — and once, in a bloom year, in the warm halo of a station, a bumblebee. One. Where it came from is not recorded, because no one knew how to ask.

Then the keepers, a few dozen, on rotations of months — the basin's only migrants that return. Their bodies adapt in the order the basin demands: the jaw stops aching, the sleep deepens, the drone stops being a sound and becomes a condition, like pressure. Long-timers wake in the night when an engine's load changes pitch — they say the floor turned over — and their hearing goes in a notch exactly where the engines live, so that years later, above the Lid, in the moving air, they cock their heads at conversation and cannot sleep for the wind. They read the felt each morning the way other people read news; one line of tracks is three days' discussion. They date their own history by blooms. In the overhaul months they walk in the quiet sector on purpose, to stand where the held note has a hole in it and listen to drips, to the felt creaking, to a vole going about its business.

They know the engines the way farmers know animals — each one an individual, older than anyone who tends it, with its order of starting, its temperament in cold, its specific knock — and they are not wrong to speak so. By mass, by metabolism, by the plain measure of who eats and breathes and warms the world, the engines are the largest living things in the basin, and everything else — the midges in their column, the foxes in the lagging, the bloom, the dryness that makes the felt possible at all — lives in their waste and their wake. The sun pays the felt a subsistence wage. The engines tip.

One annex keeps a cat. It does not cross the apron; it sleeps on the warm manifold in shifts with the owl, and the logbook records its kills with the same neutrality as the pellets. The permanent residents, of course, are the dead — a dozen, on the shingle bar, heeling further each decade. The felt grows thin over the gravel and gives up, and someone re-seats a marker when a quiet walk passes that way.

Stand on the rim at night and none of it can be counted from above: not the owl on the drowned tug, not the fox on the trestle, not the old fish holding still in the black water, not the keepers at their kettles, not the vole beneath the print your boot left last season. All of it kept under the dim and the skin, in warm hollows, in the lee of machines. The census fits on one page, the page is short, and nearly everything on it is there because five or six engines keep breathing. And the elk — if the elk exists — it is somewhere on the rim road now, or it is not, and if it ever comes down, the felt will close over its tracks within the season, and the largest wild thing the basin has ever held will become a rumor again.
