Begin with the ones you can hear, since hearing is the sense this place is built around.

Take a rauk from its ledge — it will permit this once, badly — and look at it. It is built like a solution. Raven-sized, but denser than a bird has any right to be, because everything in it is freighted for altitude: blood so dark it is nearly black, loaded to hold air the way a diver holds breath; a heart that can throttle down to almost nothing in the cold and come back up humming; breath so slow it hardly seems to happen, except that each exhalation is a small warm fog, and the walls are thirsty. The fur is soot-black, mammal-fine, and it smells faintly of the floor — guano-meal and warm stone — because everything that lives here ends up smelling of the floor. The wings are membrane and fold away like closing fans. The feet are for hanging on, not walking; a rauk on flat ground is a rauk out of context. The ears, hidden in the fur, are the largest thing about it for its size. And the beak is the old part: deep, keeled, edged with something that is not teeth but remembers teeth — a tool from before birds agreed to be birds, good for cracking krill-plate, for probing ice-crevices, for the endless communal grooming that keeps the colony stitched to itself.

Now multiply by forty thousand and stop thinking about individuals. Think about addresses. The bowl is a city of ledges, the ledges are a city of tenures, and tenure is identity: you are where you roost. An old ledge is worn smooth as a river-stone, the one soft thing on a mountain where nothing has had time to soften, and behind it, in the crack at its back, an old tenure keeps its dead — skulls only, placed beak-down in the dark, gone glossy from the touch of living beaks, generations deep. Nothing here is built. Some things are kept. Ask where the smooth rounded pebbles in the scree come from, the ones with no business on a young mountain, and the answer is that they are gizzard-stones, and heirlooms. A rauk finds one on the dead lakebeds far below, carries it home, swallows it; it grinds krill-plate for the rest of that bird's life; when the bird dies, the tenders sift the stone from the remains and pass it to the next of the line. A good stone works for centuries, wearing smoother with every tenant, until it is shaped like an egg and heavy with use. The oldest have spent more time inside the lineage than any bird ever does. The colony does not make tools. It adopts them, and hands them down, and in this way a little of the plateau's ancient lakebed lives at the top of the Tusk, grinding its dinner.

The air above the bowl is the colony's outer ear. The gyre never empties because the gyre is how the colony listens to the weather: wind-shift, krill density, the weight of returning foragers written in the grain of the turning — the ledges read all of it. And below the gyre stands the chord, which is not one voice but forty thousand signatures held in a single sustained intention. A rauk coming home across the black plateau does not simply land. It calls from the dark, and its ledge answers, and it lands into its own name returned to it. Sound is how you arrive: you arrive by being recognized. Which is why deafness is the one catastrophe no tenure survives. The deaf do not find the mountain — so the old and the earless stop leaving. They go down into the warm floor and become tenders, and the hum that comes up through the stone, that you feel in your boot-soles and your breastbone, is enough of a voice to live inside.

They tend by ear. Pressed to the warm meal of the bone-fen, an old rauk hears what you would need instruments to hear: which eggs are quick, which are cooling, which are calling. For the chicks call from inside the shell, out of the dark of the floor, and the tenders answer them, and when they break the shell they dig toward the answering — up through the warm dark into the larger dark, shaking off meal, fully furred, ravenous. A rauk enters the world the way it will enter everything for the rest of its life: by being heard.

What holds all of this up is the river. The sky-krill come on the high winds out of the cloud-sea below — where they spawn, nobody knows; no rauk has ever followed the river down — each one no longer than your smallest fingernail, armored in clear plate, and the updraft thickens them into a single rising current that the colony has only to open its mouth to drink. On still nights the current falters and the krill settle into ice-crevices and sleep, and then the rauks go gleaning with slivers of bone, hooking sleepers out of the cracks. At the scree aprons below the rim, the anvil-stones do their generational work, and the glittering middens of shattered plate around them are tenanted too: a pallid flea works the shards for the oil that films them. Even the colony's trash has a colony. Higher up, the rime-feathers on the windward columns strain krill out of the updraft like a filter, and a pale race of small climbers — the outer colony, who never enter the bowl — gleans the mountain's fur. On quiet days the rauks work the feathers themselves, picking their own houseclean.

And every volume of the water is occupied, because every volume here is. Each black font holds its well-shrimp: translucent, slow as the lichen, molting perhaps once in a decade. Each basin is an island, and the shrimp in it are one sister-line, sundered from the next font's line for ten thousand years, growing quietly strange to one another in the dark, some of them older than the tenures of the ledges above them. The rauks drink by right of address, and when a font's line fails, the ledge that drinks there counts it a death in the house. Note, too, where some of the water comes from: forty thousand slow breaths, each a small fog, and the cold walls harvesting the tenants just as they harvest the sky. Drink from a font and you drink the cloud-deck, the stone, and the exhalations of the dead. The oasis is partly self-watered. Some of it was chosen.

The floor hums with the small. Midges boil off the warm guano in a smoke of wings, and at the haze's edges work the climbers proper — finger-long, six-limbed, pale as cave-things but sighted, running the column-flutes like slow hands up a keyboard. Watch them cross the drip-lines and you learn the walls' best secret: the glowing vein-lichen flinches. Touch it and the blue light dims, a slow quench spreading a handspan and recovering, so that everything moving on the walls is written down in darkness as it goes. The midges feed the climbers; the climbers feed the rauks, who snap them off the flutes on slow evenings; the rauks feed the floor; the floor feeds the midges. Nothing leaves. And smaller still: the archivists' pale kin ride the birds themselves, burrowed in the soot-fur, passed beak to beak in the grooming — and when a rauk dies, its passengers climb down into the floor. The archive receiving its readers.

The archivists themselves, thumb-joint long and blind as the stone, graze the strata layer by layer, each one spending its whole life inside a handspan of floor, tasting time. They molt once a decade. Catch one and you hold a century's record in a shell: what it ate is what fell. Down where the ice-band keeps the three white years, nothing moves, but everything is present — the unhatched, the unrisen, a whole dead season kept by the cold the way a throat keeps a word unspoken. Nothing rots, so nothing is forgotten, and the pale hair-fungus threads the whole archive together, digesting the oldest bone at a rate too slow to call hunger.

The strangest tenants did not come down the wind. They came up the stone. In the wet dark of the throats, where the mountain exhales on its tidal cycle, pale filaments hang in the outbreath and strain it — breath-mats, feeding on whatever the deep sends up: sulfur, spores, the mountain's own chemistry. The Tusk seeds its own bowl. And deeper, inside the fresh rock itself, the endolithic crusts eat mineral that has never seen weather. These are the only natives. Everything else — krill, lichen, rauk, you — is an immigrant. The endoliths arrived before the surface did, and the slow growth that ticks and groans through the colonnade is, to them, simply weather. On the leeward faces the black lichen banks the whole dark year against one red hour. It is not green because green is for places with light to spend; it takes everything the Kindling gives — sixty thin minutes — and spends the year digesting it together with stone so new it sweats salts. A patch the size of your hand has had a thousand summers, each an hour long. The colony keeps the same calendar: tenure is counted in Kindlings, the elders keep the tally, and when the one hour comes and forty thousand birds go still and face the thin light, the bowl is not worshipping. It is counting.

One thing here is not a tenant. The bowl has never seen it plainly; it is known by what it subtracts. The rauks click as they fly, a constant soft interrogation of the dark, and the dark answers — column, ledge, krill-swarm, each other. It does not answer for the stalker. Where the ears report a hole, the eyes report a shape: tall, rime-pale, jointed like a limb, standing the way the columns stand, which is the wrong way for anything alive to stand. It climbs the flanks over days, out of the cloud-sea or across the dust — no one has ever followed one home. It hunts by ear. And so the bowl's answer is the hush: forty thousand throats closing at once, the alarm not a sound but the subtraction of all sound, traveling at the speed of hearing. In the hush the colony speaks with mirrors — the eyeshine cascades are its silent language, news passed lamp to lamp around the amphitheater without a syllable — and if the stalker climbs, the walls confess it, the glowing delta flinching dark wherever it touches, its whole shape written in extinguished stars. It can stand for three nights. The hush cannot last three nights; every silent hour is an hour unforaged, the eggs cooling in the floor. This is its whole strategy: it prices the silence and waits for the colony to go broke. Some years it takes an edge-sleeper and is gone. Some years the ledges find it afterward at the rim, dead of the mountain's two weathers — cold and quiet — an arm's length from more meat than the plateau holds. Then the bowl takes it the way the bowl takes everything. The archivists get it. Its pale bones go down through the strata and are filed among the tenants, and you can read its visits in the floor ever after: the thin scared bands, the failed eggs, the hush written as absence. The floor does not distinguish between its tenants and its terrors. It keeps both.

Count it all and the census comes out strange: forty thousand rauks; climbers, midges, well-shrimp, archivists beyond reckoning; mats and crusts and lichens; one tall visitor. And yet everything here arrived by one of two roads — down the wind or up the stone — and everything lives at the meeting of two rivers, the krill falling out of the sky and the breath of the deep rising through the throats. The bowl is the confluence. At the confluence the colony has accreted, the way everything here accrues, into a single thing with forty thousand hearts and one voice. When the throats exhale and the hum swells, the chord tilts, finds the mountain's note, and holds it — forty thousand flutes on one pitch, the bowl ringing like a struck glass. Call it tuning. The plug came up around an empty throat, and the throat is empty no longer. The mountain grew itself a voice, and the voice is alive, and it answers.

Ask what lives in the Tusk. The answer lives in the Tusk.
