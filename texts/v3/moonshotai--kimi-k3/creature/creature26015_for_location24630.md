Three hundred people live in the Bright, and the first thing a stranger learns about them is that three hundred is not a crowd — because a crowd is a thing made of noise and jostle, and the Quiet took both of those out of the world forty generations ago.

Watch them move. The roofscape lanes are two shoulders wide, and people pass each other constantly, but there is no pass, really: there is a *yield*, a small practiced turn-and-slide, shoulders rotating, breath drawn in, a choreography so old it has become gait. They walk ball-of-foot first — the moss-step — placing weight the way you would lay down something sleeping. Everyone's hands are occupied with something small. Nobody stands idle the way surface people stand idle; idleness here is a discipline with its own posture, called *sitting the basin*, and it looks exactly like a person watching water not fall yet.

Their eyes are the eyes of an indoor people: near-wise, patient with detail, able to read a moss-grade or a rind-thickness at a glance — and faintly unpracticed at distance, because there is no distance. The longest sightline in the world is a hundred and twenty paces. A stranger is known instantly, not by face but by feet: loudness lives in the feet, and yours give you away at the Well's foot. Children watch you from ladder-holes with the frank, unhurried stare of people who have never once been told to hurry. And before you have straightened up from the climb, the wall has already said it: *someone is down.*

They call the place the Bright. Themselves they call nothing in particular — *the people* — and mean it as a complete answer.

---

They have two tongues, and the wrong one is precious.

The public tongue is the sign-dialect, and it comes in two sizes. Small-sign is for arm's length: economical, subtle, full of shorthand worn smooth by centuries of lane-use. Large-sign carries across the chamber — because sound dies in three paces, but a glance goes the whole room. At rest hours the air above the lanes fills with crossed conversations, hands talking over the heads of people talking below them, until from a corner the whole town appears to be silently conducting weather. A conversation can be held across forty paces of open air without a single other soul being included. There is a man — every generation has one — who signs too broadly, whose small-sign sprawls into the space of bystanders. They say of him, fondly, that *he signs like weather.*

The other tongue is speech, and speech here is touch. Voices are rusted by disuse into something soft and grainy; there are elders who have not spoken aloud in a decade and are heard daily, because the walls carry what the air will not. How many people have heard your voice is the true measure of your intimacy here, and the number is always small. Mothers sing to infants, but the hush-songs are murmured against the chest and felt more than heard — vibration-songs, absorbed through bone. Lovers learn the wall-paths and whisper into the ice, and at a marriage the couple stands at opposite curves and sings to each other *through the chamber wall* while three hundred guests watch their mouths move and hear nothing. It is the most public private thing in the world, and the guests weep at it anyway, at the shapes of it.

Laughter, which will not be suppressed, is carried to the Hearth-Cell like an offering and released into the cover of the crackle — the one loudness the law permits. The Hearth-Keeper has therefore heard everyone's joy, every private sound the town makes, and keeps them all the way a basin keeps water.

The deaf of the Bright are its poets. This surprises outsiders and no one else.

---

You are not introduced by your name here. You are introduced by your water.

Every house is a drip-line — a descent of rights from one of the eleven hundred named drips to the basin beneath it — and kinship is hydrography. *She is of Ninth-Bead,* the introduction goes, *child of Slow-Returning, of the house beneath the Third Scallop.* Children recite the Thousand Names of Water as a skipping game, and the game is the land registry, and the registry is the family tree.

When two people marry, the door-frame of the marriage house gets its coat of red, and the two basin-keepers pour first-water from each line into a single new vessel, and the ledgers are joined. Nothing is created — the ice gives no new water — but inheritances are braided. Very occasionally a drip drifts a hair's width over the decades and two houses must renegotiate a bead. The town's one famous lawsuit, the Case of the Wandering Ninth, was conducted with perfect courtesy over eleven years and is still talked about at the Hearth as a golden age of patience.

Names return. When someone is laid in the Gallery, their name goes back into the pool, and the next licensed child draws it out — so every person of the Bright is the namesake of someone visible, someone you can be taken to *see*, blue and serene in the niche-light. Not a reincarnation; a stewardship. Your duty to your name-ancestor is to learn their life from the Gallery-Keeper and tell it once, aloud, at your Wind-Turn feast. Some names wait years in the pool. Waiting is not felt as a sorrow. Waiting is what the town is made of.

Births are licensed by deaths; this is the Reckoner's office, and it is the loneliest one. She is signed as *Counts-Twice*, and she counts everything — births, deaths, moss cropped against moss grown, the floors' slow rise — and she keeps the list of couples holding a turn, and she is the one who must come to a house in the days after a death and deliver joy to a door still hung with grief-moss. She attends every Last Murmur and every first wail, the only person in the Bright who hears both for everyone. There are three couples on the list this thaw. There are two names waiting.

Twins, when they come — once in a generation, perhaps — are the town's hardest arithmetic and its favorite story. The last twins are grown now: one keeps the doubled basin-line below, and one is a famous arbitrator among the coast peoples, and salt arrives from her every third thaw.

---

Everyone in the Bright is a keeper of something. It is the only profession.

The Light-Keepers climb the Well each thaw to re-cut its throat, wearing snow-goggles of mammoth ivory — ten-thousand-year-old material against the glare — and they come back down with weather in their clothes. The senior keeper is signed as *Wind-Pocket*, and children are permitted to sniff her sleeve, because she carries the smell of far. The Well-foot, where she lands, is the town's nose: the thaw arrives there as a scent days before it arrives anywhere as news.

The basin-keepers are fluent in the drip-song the way shepherds are fluent in their flocks, and they can hear a single drip off its interval the way a herder spots a limp across a valley. When a drip *stops* — rare, an omen-day, the ice clearing its throat — the year is named for it afterward. The Year the Seventh Stopped is within living memory. It resumed. But the year kept the name.

The wall-keepers are acoustic masons, a trade that exists nowhere else on earth. They scrape the frost-beards from the whisper-curves and re-polish them with hide and fine grit, testing their work with murmured phrases sent around the circuit, apprentices spending years with their cheeks to the ice learning what *true* sounds like. The craft was founded, they say, by the stayer — the outsider signed as *Came-Down*, who climbed in as a trader fifty winters gone, sat down beside Eleventh-Bead, and simply never climbed out. His ears, ruined by wind and untrained by birth, heard flaws in the walls that the born Quiet had stopped hearing. He married into the Ninth-Bead line, died old, and is niched now with a red thread worked into his moss. They tell his story at every Wind-Turn feast: *the world is loud, and the Bright is bright.*

The Rain-Tuner is the town's one artist with a season. Before each thaw she sets tuned basins — the note set by the water's depth — beneath chosen drips, and when the Soft Rain comes the chamber plays her arrangement, single soft notes falling into stone at intervals she has been composing in her head all winter. The current Tuner is signed as *Nine-Basins* for a famous early work. Applause is impossible, so appreciation takes other forms: attendance, the signing of a motif back at her, a red thread left at her door-frame. There was one arrangement, years ago, that everyone agrees failed — the thaw came strong and the rain played itself. It is remembered with enormous fondness.

The Hearth-Keeper, *Warm-Ears*, keeper of the double doors and the permitted crackle, confessor of the town's laughter and its one strong cooking-smell — she was the fleeing child, sixty years ago. The one who came down the Well from some violence the coast peoples only hint at. She grew up to be the keeper of the loudest sound in the world, and there is not a person in the Bright who finds this anything but correct.

The Gallery-Keeper, *Keeps-the-Faces*, cuts the niches — a season's work each, with flint and ivory wedges and patience — and reads the rinds the way others read rings, and leads the lineage walks by lamplight. History is a garden here, and she is its gardener.

And there is the Astonisher: tender of the Lambency, an office always held by an unhurried elder. The current one, *Green-Dark*, takes children into the dark cell one or two at a time, removes the lamp, waits for their eyes to give up, and then lets the moss return its faint green-gold to them. She is also keeper of the aurora-watch and the starlight nights, when the winter throat of the Well pours down the sky. Her whole job is wonder. It is considered as necessary as water.

---

A child of the Bright is born in the warmth of the Hearth-Cell, and her first wail is the one sound below that the law not only permits but *treasures* — the whole town pauses for it, smiles, and goes back to work. Then begin the hushing years, a tapering license of noise: moss-step by three, fluent small-sign by five, and at seven a child is trusted with a drip — one basin, hers to keep true, her first citizenship.

Their games are the curriculum wearing a disguise. Whisper-tag around the whole circumference, soundless, is acoustic engineering. The Thousand Names is the cadastre. Folding the woolly moths off the basin-rims is gentleness practice. They hold stillness contests — a springtail placed on the back of the wrist; the first to flinch loses — and they bet on the Seventh Bead, guessing which drip falls next, which is a way of learning the town's clock by heart. On cold mornings they are taken to sign good-morning to their name-ancestors in the Gallery, and they do it without any dread at all, because a grandmother who can be visited is simply a grandmother.

And at fourteen thaws, every child of the Bright is taken up the Well for the Wind-Turn.

Nothing prepares them. The Light-Keeper goes first, and then the child comes up through the skin of the world into the enormous level light, and the horizon happens, and most of them sit down very fast — the keepers call it the kneeling moment and are ready for it. Some weep at the size of everything. Some laugh, because up here laughter is free: the covenant is of the Bright, and the tundra keeps no law but weather. Youths shout into the wind, sometimes, huge cracked unpracticed shouts, and the wind erases them instantly, and the fox that dens ten paces off — signed as *Ten-Paces*, a border-cousin the town has watched for generations — does not even lift its head. The snow buntings at the lip, the only musicians who perform above the Bright daily, are equally unimpressed.

Then the choice. Come back down, changed — most do — or walk away with the season's traders to the coast and the sister-peoples.

Those who go are mourned more than the dead. This is the Bright's great inversion, and every family knows it: *death is a room; life is a road.* The ice keeps what it is given, and the dead are given to the ice, but the wind takes the living and keeps them somewhere unvisitable.

---

The departed carry ivory toggles and worked flint and the secret of the Well — calm keeps secrets very well — and they carry the town's one exportable quality, and on the coast it makes them famous. They become mediators, judges, the people sent for when a feud has gone three winters; they marry into fishing families and are spoken of as uncanny, as the ones who are not afraid of storms. Bead-letters come back in the Well-basket each thaw, carved tokens and news and salt, and in the dark months, among the four hundred lamps, some are road-lamps — lit for the departed, though no one has ever arrived by night. They are lit anyway.

Sometimes, decades later, one returns as a trusted trader and climbs down, loud-footed and weathered, and is gently re-hushed, and sometimes cannot stay, and sometimes stays, and is niched at last with a red thread in the moss, the way *Came-Down* was, the thread that says: *went to the road and came back to the room.*

The parents of the gone wear their pride like a held breath. There is a saying for it, and the saying is also the whole consolation: *the wind keeps some of ours, and the ice keeps the rest.*

---

Daily life is maintenance raised to the level of devotion, and it feeds on the surface. In thaw the parties go up: eggs at the thaw-lakes, birds and hares, berries and roots dried against the dark, fish and oil and salt and skins from the sister-peoples. Below, the larder-niches cut deep in the walls keep meat beside much older meat, and the basins give a sparing harvest of tadpole shrimp — eaten with something close to ceremony, since the shrimp are older in form than the ice, and the children are taught the litany of precedence along with their letters: *the shrimp before the ice, the ice before the forest, the forest before the beasts, the beasts before us —* and the children supply the coda — *and we are being kept.*

The flint-knapping is done topside, in thaw, in the open — the Bright's noise, made by appointment, where nothing counts. Below, in the dark months, ivory is carved instead: a toggle in the shape of a bunting, a winter long in the making. Ambition exists here, but it has been turned downward, into depth: the great biography of this town is a basin kept true for fifty years. *Steady* is the highest praise one person can give another. Steady is the only boast.

Privacy is not a place here; it is a gift other people give you. The etiquette of un-noticing is learned before long-sign. When someone needs to be alone, they go and sit among the dead, because the dead do not watch.

In the dark months comes the Kindling — the four hundred lamps lit one by one as the light fails, until the town becomes a small sky, gold suns held in the blue glass ice, the two lights keeping their old friendship. The aurora burns above the rim in perfect silence, which the people consider only proper. And the storyteller stands at the Speaking Curve and murmurs the old epics *into the wall*, and the audience distributes itself along the whisper-path, a hundred scattered ears each receiving the story privately, intimately, all at once. The epic of the Old Death is performed in large-sign on a roofstage, and when the drumming is signed — the terrible silent pantomime of the drumming, and then the fin of the ceiling, and then the remaking — the smallest children hide their eyes.

---

They quarrel, of course. They are people. But a quarrel in the Bright is conducted by withdrawal: the unsigned slight, the wall-path untraveled, the gift of privacy revoked. The gravest sanction is to be made Unmurmured — for a term, no one signs to you at all except *Counts-Twice*, and she only on matters of duty — and restoration is made by service, by cleaning another house's line with your own hands. Theft is nearly unknown, not from virtue alone but from geometry: nothing can leave, everything is known, where would you put it.

The true crimes are balance-crimes: moss cropped faster than it grows, a drip-line fouled, noise. The last category has exactly one entry. Generations back, a man mad with grief drummed on a basin with both hands, and the town sent him up within the day — mercy and terror in a single act, because the law is not superstition: *the ice keeps what it is given,* they say, *so be careful what you give it.* The epic says the town acted before the ice could answer. He is called only the Drummer, and his name has never returned to the pool.

The one fear is breath. A trader, long ago, brought a cough down the Well, and the Coughing Year filled a dozen niches in a single season, and since then the watcher at the Well-foot reads a stranger's breathing before anything else, and visitors do not stay for the dark months, ever, no matter how trusted.

---

The living are the minority of the Bright.

The niches run the length of the Gallery and begin down the Cistern wall, forty generations of faces under their clear rinds, thousands strong. And the ice holds its older tenants as citizens too, with names and habits of regard. The mammoth is signed as *the Colleague*: its exposed tusk-curve is polished by palms, because everyone touches it in passing, a greeting three hundred years old, and each ivory-worker serves an apprenticeship on the same shoulder their grandmother worked — and it will never be finished, on purpose, because to finish it would be to end a friendship. The bison deeper in is *the Inheritance*, untouched, the town's proof of its own restraint. *We are not so poor as our grandchildren,* the old saying goes, meaning: leave it. And the bird at the odd depth, wings spread, is *Still-Flying*. Children sign *safe landing* to it. *Nine-Basins* once dedicated a Soft Rain arrangement to it, and it was a good year.

When the melt gives up a spruce trunk, the wood-cutters take only what the ice has already felled, and the cut releases its resin, and for a day the whole Bright smells of a forest that has not existed for ten thousand years. These felling-days are among the rare scented occasions, and people find reasons to pass the wall slowly, breathing the vanished world.

The small tenants are neighbors, not larder. Basin-keepers read the ice-worm calligraphy for melt-news the way clerks read a ledger. Springtails are the stillness-testers of children's games. The woolly moths drink at the basin-rims and are folded off by gentle fingers, and when a stranger sits down in the moss for the first time, the whole town finds reasons to glance over — because sooner or later a moth lands on the stranger's hand, and what the stranger does then is the truest introduction the Bright ever gets. The midges that dance forever in the Sunstroke's column are considered the light's own tenants, entitled to their pillar.

And at the lip of the world above, *Ten-Paces* the fox and the buntings hold the border, and report nothing, and are reported on constantly by the Light-Keepers, whose news of nests and dens is received like word of cousins.

---

A person of the Bright chooses their niche in later life, and visits it, and keeps it clean, and it waits like a coat on a peg. When the end comes, the dying — if they are able — give a Last Murmur at the wall, and the wall carries it around the whole circumference to every ear that presses, the only broadcast the town permits; even the Unmurmured are re-admitted to hear it. Then the vigil in the Hearth-Cell warmth. Then the moss-wrapping, the laying, and the slow kindness of the rind, as over the years the face goes blue and gentle, and the grandchildren are brought to sign good-morning.

And then there is the cry. *One cry to a lifetime,* the proverb says, and the newborn's wail is spent for you at the start — but some, dying, spend a second one, a single full shout at the very end, and the town startles like one body, and it is permitted, and pitied, and honored, and the ice takes it without echo, as the ice takes everything. Others save theirs their whole lives and go into the Gallery unspent, and at their laying the town stands a little closer, the way you stand closer to something precious and breakable being carried.

Ask a child of the Bright why the ice glows, and she will tell you — in small-sign, solemnly, the way children recite things they half know are stories — that the light is made of banked sound, that the Bright is bright with everything its people never said.

And perhaps that is only a children's cosmology. But the glow is real, and the town is real, and the cries are real — and they are all still down there, under the bright empty plain, keeping each other.
