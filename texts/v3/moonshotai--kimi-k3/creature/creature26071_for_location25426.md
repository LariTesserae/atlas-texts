Ask a keeper what lives in the valley and she will begin with the stacks. This tells you everything about the keepers, eventually.

Every spring the keepers count. It is the same count they have always done — stacks, keepers, thinnings, moths, the glider if it comes — and it has never once come out the same. The stacks number forty, or sixty. The keepers number two hundred, perhaps. The disagreement is not error. The disagreement is the sound of the world being loose, and loose is how the world is supposed to be. A place whose counts agree is finished, and finished is the one thing nothing here wants to be.

So: the census, in the order the keepers would give it, which is not the order you would.

**The stacks first.** Forty of them, or sixty, standing in their long fence, drinking the fog. The keepers speak of them the way shepherds speak of a herd — which one is gravid with ice, which one is running warm, which one is between. For some springs a stack does not wish to be counted. You can stand directly in front of it with the tally-cord and your eye slides off, the way your hand slides off the old alloy, no edge anywhere to catch on. The keepers record the lower number and move on. They are not afraid of the stacks. They are polite to them, the way you are polite to a large animal that has never done anything yet.

**The engines second.** Tended, never maintained — you know this — but what tending means, in practice, is visiting. An engine that is never visited stops. There is nothing left in it to break; every part has been replaced by root or habit generations ago. It stops anyway. The keepers say the engines run on habit, and the keepers are the habit. Hall Three likes company, so the peat crews eat their lunch there, on the warm floor, in the roar. Hall Five must be sung to. When its harmonic drops and the local physics goes loose — tools falling up, a bell ringing on the wrong side of its event — the roster says *holding the note*, between *peat* and *bells*, and keepers take shifts standing in the hall humming one tone for days, and within earshot of them things fall down properly and water remembers which way is hill. They are not being poetic. The valley does not reward poetry. It rewards accuracy.

**The keepers third.** They list themselves third, and would list themselves later if they could.

They are small and grey-green and quiet, the way everything in the valley is quiet against the noise of it. They are hard of hearing in the range of voices and absolute in the range of the chord. An old keeper cannot hear you ask the time and can hear Stack 31 thinking about ice. Their clothes are felt cut from the dead layers, so they wear the valley; their houses stand over the warm veins, so they sleep on the valley; nothing is farmed, but things are *asked* — certain beds, asked properly, yield tubers and greens and a bitter tea, all of it tasting faintly of nothing, like the water, like everything here, distilled toward nothing. They season it with salt from a tin older than the Row. The tin is not empty and has never been empty. Some things you do not count.

You have been told the six rules. Underneath them there is a rule zero, never written, under which the other six are filed: *do not be finished.* The green finishes what it can know, and it knows by habit. So the keepers keep loose habits. Three pairs of boots, rotated, so the green never gets a fix on any one of them. A different way to the cistern every day — Rule 4 is not about the paths; it is about you. No song sung twice the same way; keeper music has no endings, because a song that ends is a finished thing. Tools replaced while they still work. It looks like carelessness. It is the opposite. It is the whole theology, if keepers had theology, which they do not. They have etiquette.

Names erode with use, like paths. The more a keeper is spoken of, the shorter the name becomes, until the eldest are called things like Bren and Awn. The clerk, who is mentioned every day of every year, is called Em. It used to be longer. The clerk cannot remember it, and the clerk remembers everything. It is the job.

In the Loud Hour they sign, and the sign for a person is a tap on the sternum, where the Hum first took you. The sign for a copy of a person is the same tap twice, the second tap not touching the chest. Children learn the double-tap before they learn the word for it, the way you learned the shape of *hot* before the word.

Which brings you to the clerk, and the hand.

The Ledger is kept in one hand and has been for ten thousand years, and this is not a figure of speech. Each clerk apprentices by copying the Ledger backward — page by page, year by year, gliders and rainfall and thinnings in retreat — until their writing is indistinguishable from the writing on the oldest page. By the time a clerk is confirmed, they have personally written the valley's entire history once, in reverse. They know things. They do not say them. The hand is the oldest resident of the valley; it has outlived every keeper, every engine part, possibly the stacks. The keepers do not count it in the spring. Some things you do not count.

There is one year in two hands. The keepers do not name the year. The hand wavered for eleven days — a clerk dead mid-apprenticeship, the new one not yet the old one — and in those days the manifest was written in a hand that had not finished becoming the hand, and the train did not run, and for an afternoon the valley was elsewhere. The new clerk finished learning the old hand in a week, alone, down in the cistern where the echoes come early, practicing against tomorrow.

Because this is what the train carries: the manifest. The cars are empty and come back damp; the paper is the cargo. Every morning the clerk writes one line in the one hand, and that line is the valley's address, and the train takes it down into the cloud that has never been seen through, and brings it back at 6:40, and the valley stays where it is. A valley is a heavy thing. It is carried daily, down and back, by handwriting.

**The dead come next**, in the keepers' ordering, because the dead are still residents.

They go into the Felt shallow, quickly, by a roundabout route — the dead can no longer vary their habits, so the living vary for them; it is the last service — and in a season the green keeps the shape. The grave-row at the end of the Row is called **the Kept**, and it is the only place in the valley where a copy is welcome. You will see keepers at dusk standing beside a green figure, exact down to the boot-laces, not praying — keepers do not pray — keeping company. The difference between a memory and a replacement is whether anyone is still using the original. The green understands this, or behaves as if it does, which here is the same thing.

**Then the keepers again**, because the Count counts them too, and it disagrees about them most of all — by one, by two, most springs. There was a test, once, long ago. The Ledger records the year it was discontinued, and the reason, in the hand, in one word: *unkind.* So no one asks. You never count a person twice; you never ask anyone which they are; they cannot know, and neither can you. It is possible — no one says this, and everyone has thought it — that the green finished the Row a long time ago, and there has only ever been the Row since, keeping itself, two hundred strong, perhaps. The Row is on its third population, or its first. The keepers shrug.

**Then the voices**, though the keepers would not call them residents. A copy begins in sound, because sound is the valley's medium; before the green ever threads your chair in root, your own voice goes early — from the Old Grove, from an empty district, saying something you have not said yet. Do not answer. It only needs your half; a conversation is the shortest road to finished. Keeper children hear *early* the way your children hear off-key. They are taught with games, call and response, and the response withheld. If it sounds like Mama but it is early, it is not Mama. It is not anything yet. That is your chance.

Once a year, at the Shortening, after the new map is read out and the year's losses announced like births — *the cistern path is four hundred steps; last year it was four hundred and nine* — the keepers sing the chord. Two hundred voices, each on its birth-note, stacked the way the Hum is stacked, checking themselves against the valley. And every year there is one more voice than there are keepers. The Ledger says so, in the hand: *voices, two hundred and one.* Nobody looks for the extra. Looking is rude. Everyone, afterward, privately, in the dark, considers whether the register it sang in was theirs. One person moves house that month, every year. That is what the singing is for.

**Then the small lives**, which the keepers list with real affection, the way you list pets. The blind snail, pale, slightly ahead of itself the way everything in the cistern is slightly ahead of itself, good for broth. The keepers have one joke, and the joke is that the snail is in a hurry. The joke has eroded. It was probably never funny, and now it is smooth. The dew-moths, who rise at dusk in their one brief cloud to the intakes, reporting for work the turbines took from them ten thousand years ago. In the mornings the keepers brush their dust from the grills. It tastes of nothing, like the water. The moths are the closest thing in the valley to pure: a night of nothing, briefly winged.

**Then the glider**, last on the keepers' list and first on yours, once you have seen it. The only silent thing. Its Ledger column is old, and the wingspan entries disagree with each other, year to year, the way the stack counts do. A clerk, copying backward through the centuries, reaches the early pages and finds the wingspans small — kite-sized, bird-sized — and understands one of two things: the glider is growing, or it is descending. The clerks do not say which they believe. When its shadow crosses, the Hum goes thin, and work pauses, and some keepers hum under the shadow out of courtesy, covering the gap, the way you would hold a door. Everything else in this census was already here. The glider is the only thing that arrives, and so it is the valley's one gauge with an outside, and that is why they measure it, and that is why they will not say what the measurements mean.

**And elsewhere has its residents, though resident is the wrong word.** A few in every generation walk back. They are called **the Returned**, and they are easy to live with and hard to look at directly, for no reason anyone can name. They prefer cold floors. They hum in their sleep — the whole chord, every harmonic at once, which no living keeper can do — and on those nights the lamps on their street burn steady and the bells are quiet, and the neighbors sleep well, because physics is solid near them. They are given light duties. Bell-polishing. In the spring Count they are counted first, because they are easy. The children say they have *been counted.* The Returned agree with each other about exactly one thing, which is not like keepers at all, and it is this: the stacks count correctly there. Elsewhere is the valley, finished. When you stand in it, you are the only unfinished thing in it, and it is not finished with you. Walk until you hear the chord. You are walking back into the loose.

**And the green**, which does not appear in the Count, the way the page does not appear in the book. It is the oldest resident. To the green, everything alive is about to be dead — the difference, at its depth of time, is brief — so it begins its remembering early, and calls it nothing, and means nothing by it. It is not hunting you, whatever you have heard. It is keeping your place.

Ask the keeper, at the end of your week, when you have stopped jumping at the ground-breath and started sleeping on the warm vein, what lives in the valley. She will think, or appear to. *Nothing lives here,* she says. *Everything is kept.*

Ask her what keeps it.

She taps her sternum, once — the sign for a person. She may mean the keepers. She may mean the chord. It is the same tap.

Stay long enough and the Count will disagree about you, too. You will be standing in plain sight at noon, countable as anything, and the tally-keeper's eye will slide off you once, twice, and record the lower number, and you will feel — the keepers all report this — a kind of relief. You are becoming hard to finish. That is what belonging is, here.

And every morning the clerk rises before the frost is off the Felt and writes the manifest in the one old hand: the date, the weather, today's count, whichever number today has chosen, and then the cargo, which is also the address, which is one word. The train carries it down into the cloud at 6:10 and brings it back at 6:40, damp, unchanged, and the valley stays where it is.

The word is *here*.
