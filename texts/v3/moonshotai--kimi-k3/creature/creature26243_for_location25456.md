First, the heat — because everything that lives here lives around it, and everything else follows from that.

Map this world by warmth and you have mapped its census. There is the warm skin: the first few meters above the felt, where the floor's breath keeps the air merely bitter. There are the warm islands: the vent-mouths with their standing fog, the lens-pools, the melt-threaded hearts of the hollow towers, where trickling water means the dark inside a trunk stays just this side of living. And there is everything else — kilometers of lit, killing cold, which nothing inhabits and everything only crosses. Every animal in this world is huddled in the skin, or holding an island, or making a crossing. That is the whole zoology. You could write it on one hand. But what is written there is strange past belief, and it repays the looking.

Begin at the bottom, in the felt, with the smallest life — because when the bloom comes, the floor itself seems to fuzz.

A grey nap rises on the peat that is not the One's growth. It is eggs waking: bloom-dust, call it, each animal no bigger than a grain of the light it chases, hatched from dust that waited frozen exactly where the last bloom let it fall. Their whole lives fit inside a light-pooling: hatch, gorge, mate, lay, and begin to slow as the light wanders off — and then freeze mid-meal, like the towers, like everything. Unlike the towers, they do not thaw. The eggs do. Their generations are counted in light-passings; a bloom is their entire geology, and when you walk a green belt you wade knee-deep through more individual lives than you will ever knowingly meet again. They are the base of everything. The ponds are fed by what sifts down through the drip-holes. The great things of the air strain the rising glitter of them. When the light moves on, the ghost-forest stands flocked white with frost, and some of that white is the dust, waiting as eggs, patient as the One itself.

Beneath them, the burrowers — the greater part of all the flesh in the world, and never once seen whole.

You know the evidence. The floor's slow heave, like a sleeper turning. The breath-holes, rimmed in rime, steaming in rings that contract and widen with something's rhythm below. The traps the makers set at the rims: circles of thorn teeth, closing on whatever rises to breathe. What the traps bring up, across the sizes of a life, ranges from eel-thick to horse-thick, and the heaving hills that cross beneath you in the deep peat are something else again — things the traps do not trouble, things whose breathing opens fumaroles. They eat *down*. This is the thing to understand: the honeycomb under the floor is not geology. It is meal. The caverns are eaten, not formed, and when the floor shears away into one of those crisp peat cliffs, unslumped and fresh, what has failed is a warren's roof. The world's second falling hazard is underfoot, and it is made of mouths.

Cold is their void. They are creatures of warm, wet, breathing dark; the bright air that you breathe would kill them as surely as the kill-cold of the middle void kills you, only faster. Once — the accounts agree on this much, told wherever travelers trade counts — a floor-collapse opened a warren in daylight, and a young burrower came out onto the surface: white, eyeless, shovel-muzzled, all lung and lip. It did not die of its wounds. It died of the weather, the way a fish dies of the shore. The makers who found it took it apart with something like reverence, and wasted nothing, because a carcass in this world is a quarry. Bone is the hardest substance here that is not ice — the world's only ore. A hauled burrower keeps a band in hooks and needles and pressure-flakers for a season; the fat seals resin and feeds the young; the hide is pale as a glider's skin and tougher than any woven thing. And in the night, if you lie down on the felt and put your ear to it, you can hear them: a grinding too low to be sound, felt in the jaw and the small bones, kilometers of it, unceasing. You can hear the world being eaten. It is being eaten all the time.

The black ponds hold their own tenants. The swimmers are white and slow and blind, hand-length to fathom-length, turning in the dark water under the ice-skins like thoughts that have not decided to surface. They rise to the drip-holes, because each drip from the ceiling carries a bead of air down with it, and a pond sealed under its skin would otherwise smother; you can watch one hang beneath a hole for an hour, sipping the falling water, sip by sip, as it comes.

And in the drip-bells — those growing bells of ice that ring each punctured hole — live the keepers.

Something small lives in the drip-bells and taps, the accounts say, and they leave it at that, but here is the rest of it: the keeper is white, no bigger than a thumb, with one claw grown into an ice-pick, and the tapping is husbandry. It trims the bell's inner lip. It keeps the drip true through the freeze-nights, when the hole would otherwise skin over and the pond below would begin to die. The bell grows by accretion around the work — drip by drip, ring by ring, century by century — and a bell outlives its keeper the way a tower outlives a bird, except that bells do not fall, because ice is the one substance the One cannot digest. The tapping under your hand, when you lay your palm on an old bell, may be the fiftieth generation of the same keeping. The bells are the oldest tended things in the world, half made by water, half made by the keeping, and the makers do not kill keepers. A dead bell skins over. A kept bell is water, findable by ear in the dark season, in a world where water is otherwise an event. They are paid in being let alone, and the arrangement is older than any tower standing.

Above the skin, the crossings begin. The gliders you know — or you know their weather: the slow snow of empty skins, sifting down through the bright air, gathering in the floor-hollows like shed light.

Here is what a glider is: a warm thing spending its warmth across the void. It launches hot from a vent-mouth or a bloom-ground, membrane taut, and climbs the rising column as far as the warmth will carry, and as it climbs it cools — and its mind goes out with the heat. Not sleep: something slower, a throttle closing. At the top of the glide it is a falling leaf with a fading intention, and it sinks, and the sinking brings it down into the next warm column, and the warmth comes back into it like a second life, and it wakes and climbs again. A crossing of the void is not one journey. It is a dozen small deaths and wakings, and the animal that lands on the far side is only mostly the animal that launched. They do not call. They are the one large silence in a world where everything else announces itself. And they do not fear falling, because nothing here fears falling — the sky takes hours to arrive — a failed glide is an hour to find another column, and only landing is dangerous. When a column fails entirely beneath one, it folds and comes down like the ice it resembles, and the felt takes it, roots within days, like everything that falls here. The floor does not distinguish between a tower and an animal. It is the same gesture.

The molt is why they shed: membrane frosts at altitude and craze-cracks, and the only repair is to grow out of it. Every shed skin is scarred with frost-lines — a chart of one animal's cold, one journey written in fracture — and the makers gather them where they drift into the hollows, because a skin is a ready sheet of transparent membrane, and an eyrie's one window is a quilt stitched of other animals' journeys. What the makers do not gather, the skin-pickers take: pale, many-legged things no bigger than a thumbnail, the janitors of the hollows, eating the shed light slowly through the dark seasons. They are the whole fauna of the deep hollows, and they are content, as far as anyone has asked them.

Highest and strangest are the fluters.

In the fog columns — those motionless, kilometers-tall exhalations above the vents — live the largest animals in the air, and you will likely never see one closer than a smudge in the mist, because they almost never descend. A fluter is mostly one warm breath, held: a bladder of gold-pale membrane, big as a tower's crown, with a small animal hung beneath it like cargo. It drinks the wet warmth of the column, strains the rising bloom-dust out of the updraft, and trims its buoyancy by venting — and the venting is the call. The fluting note that crosses the void once in a day, that everyone stops to hear: that is one animal, alive, over a vent, adjusting itself against the cold. A note can last a minute. It can cross a day's walk of emptiness. The makers triangulate on fluters, because a fluter means a vent below it, and a vent means warmth and water, and so the notes are not merely remarked — they are used. The tallies count them.

Where they come from, nobody has counted. Where they go is known, because a fluter dies the way a tower falls: slowly, publicly, across a day, settling through the bright air as the warmth leaks out of it, and a fallen bladder on the felt is a windfall beyond price — the only truly airtight skin in the world. The makers sew it into the lining of sleeping-chambers and the skins of water-bags. When you cut into a sealed room in an old trunk, and you find the innermost lining still supple after centuries — that pale gold membrane was once the largest thing in the sky.

All of this, remember, lives on one body.

The dust eats it. The burrowers eat it. The gliders sleep in its hollows and wear out their skins against its weather. The fluters strain what rises off it. Every meal, every molt, every peg driven into a trunk is addressed to the same individual, and the animals of the Underbright are not its tenants, because there is no tenancy here — they are something between its fleas and its thoughts. And it has never once been seen to scratch.

And then there are the makers — who are, by any count that matters, the rarest animal of all.

You have already stood in their rooms. That is the first thing to grasp: the world is full of them the way a house at night is full of its family, and the emptiness is a matter of doors. But begin with what the traces say of the body, since the body is what the traces remember. Person-sized: the eyries fit a curled sleeper the length of a person's span. Built for the vertical: the peg-ladders' spacing says they hang as easily as they stand, and the tool-grips are worn on both faces, which says hands that work overhead. Strong, or many: a clear-ground ice lens the size of a door does not walk itself up a tower. Warm-blooded, necessarily — nothing cold-blooded knaps ice, nothing cold-blooded works the mid-levels where the eyries cling. Fiber-clothed, against the cold. And of their faces, nothing. They carved counts, not likenesses. In all the tally-marked trunks of the world there is no image of a maker, unless the counts themselves are the portrait, which they may well be.

Their tools deserve a word, because the tools are the world turned against itself, politely. At these temperatures, clear calving-ice takes an edge like flint and holds it, and their blades are knapped from it, pressure-flaked with a bone point into things that shave fiber and split thorn. A blade is stored in a wall-notch, edge out, so it never warms; the grip is wrapped, because the enemy of an ice blade is the hand that holds it; and a blade's life is a long thinning — knife to sliver to drill to awl to nothing, re-knapped down across the seasons, until the last flake is a bead of clear ice that goes into the resin pot, because nothing here is wasted that took work. Thorn for teeth. Bone for the points that must not shatter. Fiber for everything that binds. Resin for everything that must wait. And that is the deep grammar of their whole material world: *everything they own is made to freeze.* Property here is not what you hold. It is what you can leave.

Because they follow the light, like everything else, and the light does not care what you were in the middle of.

A band rides its bloom-belt on a circuit measured in light-passings, arriving after the green, living on shoots and sap and cached seed and trap-meat, and departing before the freeze, leaving everything it cannot carry sealed in resin against the return: seed-pots, fat-pots, spare lenses, a turned lens left aimed at the hollow it will melt next time — next time being a year, or a lifetime, or three lifetimes hence, and no matter, because the tally on the trunk is the deed, and the tally will be read by hands whose grandparents cut it. The seeds, especially, are wealth: the One flowers and sets seed only in the bloom, and a seed-cache is stored light, condensed summer, ransomed back to the floor at the return. And the lens-gardens are their hearths — the only warm water in the world — and they give their dead to the pool-margins, where the green is fiercest, and tend that green across the generations, and turn the lenses with hands that will one day feed it. Creation and destruction are the same gesture here. The makers did not learn that from philosophy. They learned it from gardening.

But the light's absences are not all equal, and here is where the makers become themselves.

Some pools wander back in a season. Some are gone for a hundred light-passings, and a band cannot follow a light across a void wider than its caches, and so — when the tally says the absence will outlast the supplies — they do the thing the whole world does, the thing the ghost-forests do, the thing the bloom-dust does. They freeze, and wait.

The chambers are cut into living trunks just above the warm floor, where the melt-threads run in the walls and the dark stays just alive. They are lined with fluter-bladder and sealed with resin, and the sleepers lie down on fiber with a seed-pot close and a blade at the wrist, and their hearts slow, and slow, until the pulse of the trunk is faster than the pulse in the wrist, and the One becomes their clock and their cradle both. When the light wanders back, the trunk warms. The warmth is the summons. The sleepers surface with the bloom, like everything else in this world, and go out into a green that to them is simply morning.

The chambers are marked. A tally at reaching-height: how many sleep within, and after how many light-passings they are to be waked. The etiquette around them is the oldest law the traces record: no one cuts an unmarked trunk; a marked trunk is cut only to check the count, and resealed; and a count that has run out is waked, because oversleep is how the world takes you — not by destroying you, but by preserving you, ring upon ring, until your mark is a meter deep inside the wood and your band is a century gone and the tallies outside all read wrong. They have a ghost-story, you can tell it from the way certain deep chambers are marked with a sign that appears nowhere else: the story of the too-late waker, who cut his way out of his own bedroom into a world that had finished forgetting him, and went looking for his band along the light's itinerary, and is looking still, and leaves, at every lens-garden, one fresh notch. Travelers who have never met a maker have met that notch. It is the most-encountered maker in the world.

Their ears are their eyes. In the haze, the next tower is a rumor — but sound is reliable, sound is slow and patient and multiplied by the halls, and so their whole geography is acoustic. Distance is boom-time. A territory is described as a chord: this tower's note arriving so many counts before that one's. And some of the notes are made. You have heard a hollow trunk sound its organ-note across kilometers and credited the world's breath finding a hole — but find a trunk with one clean bore, placed just so, at the one height where the breath turns, and you have found an instrument: a cut voice, tuned, each known tower given a known pitch, so that a traveler in the whiteout between towers can navigate by the overlap of notes the way other peoples navigate by stars. The world plays the map. The makers cut the holes. News travels the same way — a fall, a convergence, a count — note by note across days, band to band, slower than walking and more reliable than sight, and when a fluter's call crosses the void, even the makers may not always know whether it was an animal or a message. It may not matter. Both mean: *someone is over a vent, alive.*

They meet each other the way everything here meets: rarely, hugely, and on the light's schedule. When two light-paths cross — when the wandering pools converge and a double bloom wakes a region of floor — the bands converge with it, and for one bloom there is a *city*: dozens of eyries lit and inhabited, lenses crowded, news traded, bladders and seeds and counts exchanged, matches made, the whole compressed business of a people conducted in the green fierce days before the paths diverge and the city dissolves back into rumor. Their civilization is not a place. It is an event — recurring, calendared in tallies, existing only in time. A maker might attend three convergences in a life, and sleep through the centuries between, and count the life a full one.

Which is why you will never meet them.

It is not elusiveness, or not only that. It is arithmetic. The world is past every horizon; the bands are few; most of their number, at any given moment, is asleep inside the trunks — the waking are a caretaker crew, one band per region, turning lenses, checking counts, cutting the tallies. And they hear you. In a world where a dropped peg rings back from a hall minutes away, a walking stranger is audible for an hour in every direction, and a people whose whole geography is sound can leave in a direction your ears cannot hold, and be one silence away from you, forever. The closest you will come to an introduction is what every traveler comes to: returning to camp at drip-hour and finding, on the trunk beside your shelter, one fresh notch, bright where the cut still weeps resin. You were located, measured, and recorded. The visit, from their side, was complete.

There is one closer account. You will hear it, eventually, wherever travelers trade counts, and it is always the same account, because it happened once.

Outsiders — not makers, people like you — cut into a marked trunk whose count had long run out, and opened the chamber, and found the sleeper. Curled on fiber gone grey with frost. Feathered in frost, the way the ghost-forests are feathered, everything here being patient in the same way. One hand closed around a seed-pot. A small blade of clear ice at the wrist, still sharp, still waiting. And an ear pressed to the still chest heard it: one beat. Then nothing, for longer than anyone could hold still. Then, at the hundred-and-somethingth drip of the melt-thread in the wall — another. Alive. At one beat per hundred drips, in the warm dark of the tree, asleep since before the tallies outside meant anything.

They resealed the chamber with fresh resin and cut no more trunks after that, which is how you know the account is true: it is the only story in this world that ends with people stopping. And the next light-passing, so the account goes, the tally beside that chamber had one new mark. Not a warning. A count. As if to say: *noted.* As if to say: *still here.*

Because that is the last thing to understand about the makers, and it reframes everything: meeting requires two parties keeping the same time. They keep the world's time. Their hearts, when it suits them, run at the speed of the melt-threads; their absences are measured in light-passings; their memory is stored in a living tree that swallows it slowly enough to keep it forever. To them, a visitor is a bloom — bright, brief, warm, gone — and what the makers do with blooms is what they do with falls and light-passings and fluters and sleepers and strangers.

They count them.

You have not been ignored. You have been tallied.

So stand there a while longer. Your breath rises toward a glowing sky of ice, and a glider's empty skin sifts down through the halls beside it, scribed with the frost-lines of a crossing you did not see. Under your boot the floor heaves once, slow — a burrower passing beneath you like a thought through a sleeper — and from the pond-margin comes the small tick of the keeper, keeping the hole, in the fiftieth generation of the keeping. Across the void a note arrives that might be an animal and might be a message and might be both. Above you, behind resin and rings of living wood, the sleepers' hearts count drips. And on some trunk, somewhere within sound, there is a notch so fresh it still weeps — the count of you, already kept, already part of the only archive the One cannot digest.

The world is not empty. It is full, and quiet, and waiting — and it knows exactly how many of you there are.
