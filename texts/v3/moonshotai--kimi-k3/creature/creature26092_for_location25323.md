Start with the ones you can count.

Nine houses, and in them some thirty people — the number changes slowly, by ones, and everyone always knows what it stands at. Two electric pumps and a diesel spare. One heron per sump, and the sumps number about a dozen. One kingfisher. Toads, uncounted; hares on the dry plates; slow-worms under them; voles along the garden seams; swallows in their season; moths at the lamps; and a frog population that once had a whole reservoir to spread itself across and now has twelve wet rooms, and is loud in proportion. And then there is whatever keeps the deep cellar cold. That one is not counted, and it completes the census the way a full stop completes a sentence.

The people are three generations deep, and about half of them descend from the machine and half from the drowned. The first tenders came with the lifting station — fitters from the canal company, an engine driver — and within a decade some of them had married daughters of the village that had just been sacrificed, daughters who had been moved up over the rim and kept coming back down to look. The distinction survives only on anniversaries. In kitchens it has long since stopped mattering, because everyone here is known by their weep. Ask after a person and you ask after the weep — how's Kettle Gland — and the answer covers the garden, the house, the woman, her mother. Nobody has an address. They have a line in the leak-book, and the line does not say who owns the weep. It says who answers it.

The book is bound in oilcloth and lives on a shelf by the switchboard, within reach of the bench. The front of it is engineering: each weep by name, when opened, by whom, its flow in wet years and dry, every tuning — a quarter-turn here, a new washer there — in fifty years of hands. The back of it is the other record: the doorstep years, the year the church showed its corner, the storms that re-cut the Sike. The bookkeeper is the senior tender, and the office passes with the pencil. A new weep is not named when it is opened. You listen to it for a year first, through frost and storm, until you know its voice, and the name comes out of the voice. Kettle Gland sings before it boils. The Long Cry carries to the rim on still frost nights. The Lych Gate Weep drips in twos — a step and a pause, a step and a pause — and it stands by the setts where the churchyard gate is coming up, and nobody has ever once proposed that it be fixed.

A tender knows their own weep the way you know a child's cough: a changed note at three in the morning wakes the whole house. When a weep sickens — when it runs thin in a dry year, and the mint flags — the neighbors come the way neighbors elsewhere come to a sickbed, with soup, with cuttings, with frogspawn in jars the way other places would bring bread and salt, and the bookkeeper comes last of all and gives the gland a quarter-turn, and everyone agrees that this is engineering, and it is, and it is also a visitation.

That is how you get a house here: you take on a weep. Houses are not bought, or even, at first, built; a weep is inherited or opened, and a house accretes around it like silt around a snag. When someone leaves — the young do; they go over the rim to the canal towns — the weep is let run and the garden goes shaggy and the house stands there audible in its emptiness, because a weep with nobody thinning it has a louder voice than you would think. And mostly the young come back. They say the quiet up there is too big to sleep in; their breastbones keep time by the hum and wake them in the silence, reaching for a heartbeat that isn't there. They come home at thirty with a toolbox and a baby, and the bookkeeper opens the book.

The children learn the schedule before they learn numbers. They can sleep through the frog-wall at dusk and come bolt awake at the wrong silence, dressed and halfway down the path before the lamps have stopped swaying, because come running means them too. There are three lessons. The schedule is the first. Stepping over toads on the pipe-track is the second. The third is never taught and always learned: which thresholds are not yours to stand on.

Once a year the water board sends a man down the rim with a clipboard. He would like the weeps logged as defects and a program of remedial works. He is shown the book, which he cannot parse; he is walked along the gardens, which he admires despite himself; he is given tea, and he says, as they all say, that it is the best tea in the district. Nobody tells him why. He goes back over the rim, his report joins fifty others in a drawer, and the weeps go on weeping, tuned like an instrument nobody is allowed to finish.

The tea is made on new water — the condensate caught in troughs beneath the coldest runs, dripped into every kitchen's barrel. It has been nowhere and touched nothing: never been cloud, never been river, never been in a body or a root. Babies get it for their first drink and the dying for their last, and it goes over the rim in flasks to weddings, as proof. Everyone will tell you it is the sweetest water in the district, and no one will mention, because no one needs to, that it is the sweat of the machine. They know what it is. It doesn't stop it being sweet.

Because the machine is the loudest of the residents, and everything else is scheduled around it. The pumps are called One and Two — the poetry is kept for the water; family doesn't need pretty names — and the diesel standby is Three, and she, and the oldest voice in the basin, the one that was here before the electric line came over the rim. She is run once a month to keep her honest, and the children come to stand in the doorway and feel her in their teeth, because that is the sound their great-grandparents heard first. They do not pray to the machine here; they oil it. The gauges are polished by sleeves. And the machine keeps its own weep, indoors — the interior rain off the cold trusses — and gardens it with ferns that nobody planted, that came in on boots as spores and stayed: the one garden in the basin that is not in the book. The swallows nest above it and scream through the indoor weather from April to September, and the week they leave is the saddest of the year. On the windowsill there is a shelf of sheddings: a heron's feather, a slow-worm's skin, a curl of silt like a pot sherd, a clay pipe stem and a shoe button that came up out of the village. What the bed gives up is brought here, where everyone can see it and no one has to own it.

Of the rest: the frogs own the sumps, a lake's worth of frogs in twelve rooms, and their chorus is the basin's second engine. The herons take the tithe — one grey custodian per sump, each known by its stance the way a nurse is known on a ward — and a new heron is noticed and discussed for a week. There is one kingfisher, the way there is one Book; no one has ever seen two; when one dies, another is simply there, fishing the intake pool, sitting on the very pipe that steals its river, and its being there is read like a gauge: while the kingfisher sits the pipe, the water is honest. The dry plates between the gardens belong to the hares and the slow-worms, and people cross the emptiness the way you cross someone else's field — on the paths, by daylight. The foxes come down at dusk and the deer step over the ring at dawn, but they are visitors; the rim is a door only some can use. The residents are the ones who cannot leave.

And then there is the village, which everyone treats less as a ruin than as a neighbor who has been away. People keep to the lanes of setts when they cross it — the same instinct that keeps you to the path in another's field. Children learn early that you do not stand in a doorway that isn't yours, and none of them can remember being told. After the year's storm there is the doorstep walk: everyone out early to see what has surfaced, children counting, the bookkeeper writing it in the back of the book. There are those who can point to a doorstep at ankle height and say that was ours, the way you would point out a grandparent in a photograph, and their houses wear the old lintels as inheritance, which nobody begrudges. Stone may be taken from the other houses, but never a threshold. The graves were moved before the water came — everyone is careful to tell you this, moved up over the ring properly, with the minister from the canal parish — and it is probably true. It is probably true.

The village had a mill, because the Sike had work in it, and the mill is why the intake is where it is: the Engine House drinks the stream in the same place the mill did. The same drink. Only the throat is new. The wheel-pit is the deep cellar, the long stairs going down further than the light, and the tenders keep accounts — first water to the canal, second to the gardens, new water to the kitchen — and the accounts almost balance. Almost. Take the Sike's flow, subtract the lift, subtract every weep in the book, and there is a difference: small, patient, always in the same direction. A small difference compounded for fifty years is a lake. It is not written down; the book is exact about everything else. The water went away and mostly stayed away. The rest is below the stairs, where the cold comes up like something breathing.

The grill is cleared at midday, in pairs, in summer — never in autumn, when lakes turn over, never in the Still — one working, one watching the steps. Once, and the story is practical rather than old, a bookkeeper lowered a plumb line on every foot of light rope they had, and it went down past the reach of the lamp, and when it came up the last fathoms were dry. Dry, though they had hung below the level of the pool. They did not try twice. The kingfisher has been seen to fly down those steps and come back up, so the air is good at least as far as a kingfisher goes. And the mill's lintels stay on their threshold. There is no rule written about it. There doesn't need to be. It is the third lesson.

In the Still — the scheduled pause, the held-breath hour — the basin hears itself: drips echoing in the open cellars, pipes ticking as they cool, snags clicking, a page turning in the Engine House, and sometimes, under all of it, faint and wrong, water moving against a shore. Nobody checks. The shore is empty; they know the shore is empty. People keep to the lamps in the Still, and do not cross the village, and lower their voices without deciding to, and the night tender walks the round and listens to the machine the way you listen to a sleeper beside you, and comes home if the rhythm is good.

There are three silences, and only one of them is safe. The frogs stopping out of turn means weather, or something crossing the bed, and the tenders look up. The Still has a name, and is kept. And the unscheduled silence — the wrong silence — is the alarm, and you come running, from the furthest garden, you come running, because everyone knows without saying what silence would mean if it were allowed to last. Given a month of it, the Sike would spread; given a season, the sumps would join; given a year, the lake would come home. There are those who think it would come home faster than that — quicker than water should move, like something that has been waiting politely. So sound here is not noise. It is the sound of everything breathing at once — pumps, frogs, drips, swallows, the long stairs — and the whole covenant of the place is that the breathing must not stop.

When a tender dies, the bookkeeper rules off their line, and at dusk a valve is seated somewhere in the system and the main speaks once — the struck-bell note rolling across the bed, the only bell this place has ever rung — and the body goes up over the ring to be buried in the world, in ground that was never anyone's cellar. The weep is let run; the garden goes shaggy; the house stands audible in its mourning until there is someone to answer it again, and the neighbors come with jars of spawn.

By full dark the night tender is on the round in oilskins, lamp to lamp, the moths in their halos, a hand laid on each standpipe the way you check a sleeping child, goodnight to One and to Two, the kettle, the tick of the switchboard, the indoor rain ringing softly on the casings. Count them from the rim, if you like: nine windows going dark one by one; a dozen herons on one leg in the duckweed; toads on the track; hares owning the emptiness; one kingfisher roosting near the pipe; the fern garden dripping; the Book shut on its shelf; two hearts beating inside one hum; and at the bottom of the wheel-pit, under the long stairs, the cold going up and down like a tide, like breathing, like the lake that never left — keeping the one room in the basin that nobody enters and everybody keeps.

The frogs surge. The second shift clocks in. And everything that lives here — the counted, and the one uncounted — breathes in time.
