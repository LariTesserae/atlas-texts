Nothing here has a name. No one has come to give one, and the tower does not deal in names — it deals in level, weight, and pressure. So take the census the way the tower takes water: from the bottom up. Begin in the pool, because everything here begins in the pool.

The oldest inhabitant is the water itself, and the water is not empty. A century of recirculation has made it a closed flask, a sealed experiment, and what lives in it has been selected and re-selected by every pass through the pumps: a consortium of bacteria and archaea, films and threads and drifting flocs, tuned to lime and warmth and trace oil until the water has become, in effect, self-cleaning. Nothing rots here. Everything that falls in — a moth, a feather, a swift's dropped feather-louse, windblown dust — is received, disassembled, and reissued. The black of the pool is not decay. It is depth, and archive. Below the reach of light the sediment lies in annual layers: a hundred years of guano dust, molted down, drowned wings, lime scale flaked from the crown, all of it worked over by anaerobes that breathe without air and pass their faint gases upward to other bacteria waiting just below the surface, a filter of the living over the dead. The archive is read constantly, by its own librarians, and nothing in it is lost — only re-shelved.

Down there too, and everywhere the water goes, is the creature that would be famous if anyone knew it existed: a seed shrimp the size of a lentil, translucent, hinged, found in exactly one room in the universe. Its life is built around the pulse. In the chime-hours it rows through the still black water, filtering. When the risers knock, it does what everything in the pool does — it lets go. It is drawn into the intakes, lifted the full height of the tower in the dark of the pipe, flung from the crown, and falls home through the warm indoor rain into the boiling pool, unharmed, and resumes. Its migration is vertical, hourly, and conducted in weather. It has made the journey ten thousand times across ten thousand generations, and on the way it has drifted — slowly, in the dark, unobserved — into something that exists nowhere else. Whether it has earned a species of its own is a question with no one to ask it. It is, functionally, endemic to a machine.

The surface belongs to the whirligig beetles, the ones that row. They school in the still hours, carving their small circles, each one half above the water and half below, watching both worlds. They feel the pulse before it comes — some pressure-change in the risers, some tremor through the concrete — and their rowing quickens, the schools tighten, and just before the crown lets go they tuck themselves into the pipe mouths and under the walkway's lip and wait out the roar. Then they come back out to the feast, because every rain delivers: drowned gnats, lost winged ants, spiders blown from their webs, the day's small dead. At the pool's edge, where the pumps weep their thin rainbow of oil, the iridescent bacteria spread their grazing films, and over the films herds of springtails browse — pale, pinhead cattle on a pasture of petroleum. Everything here is lived on. Even the leak.

The walls are zoned like a mountain, and each zone has its people. In the drip zone below the ring, where the black algae and the dark mosses hold the damp, a snail grazes the flowstone — and here the boundary of the place gives way entirely, because the snail's shell is built of tower-lime, dissolved out of the concrete by the water and laid down again in a spiral. You cannot say where the tower ends and the snail begins. It writes its feeding-tracks across the biofilm in a script finer than the bolt-hole rows outside, and at night a slug comes down from somewhere and leaves a silver line on the concrete that the dawn dries to a scar, and a flat hunting beetle follows that line the way a hound follows scent.

Up in the ring — in the hand's breadth of manufactured soil, lime scale and travertine and the composted nests of dead centuries — the crowding is beyond belief. Mites, springtails, nematodes by the uncountable million, all of it threaded with fungal wire. And over all of it, the ants. One queen, once, long ago, was lofted up the throat on the thermal and landed in the cloud forest instead of dying on the gravel, and her descendants are now a single colony that rings the entire circumference of the tower — one family, many doors, a nation with a territory shaped like a wreath. They pasture scale insects on the fern stems and milk them for honeydew; they patrol the travertine in files; they are, with the swifts, the tower's great landowners. Once a year, on some cue of humidity and pressure no one will ever measure, the colony tries to leave: the winged queens and males launch into the void, and the swifts take them, and the pool takes them, and the wind takes a few out over the lip to the plain that cannot keep them. Almost none return. It fails, beautifully, every year, and the colony persists in place, re-founding itself a few meters from itself.

The top predator of the ring is a centipede the length of a finger, chestnut and many-toothed, that hunts the fern bases after dark. Under it: moth caterpillars feeding on the ferns, small orb-weaving spiders strung between fronds — spiders that have learned the tower's schedule and spin in the chime-hours and crouch, flat and patient, through the roar. The orchids have mostly given up waiting for anyone; they pollinate themselves now, alone, and set their dust-fine seed for the updraft. All but one. One orchid still opens at night, white, into the rising warm air, and one small moth still comes up out of the gallery to it, generation after generation — the oldest relationship in the tower, a promise kept across a hundred feet of dark.

The void belongs to the swifts, and the swifts are the tower's second circulatory system. A few hundred of them, wheeling in their lanes at the quiet nodes, screaming into a resonator that doubles their voices and gives them back. They nest in the drain-holes in cups of glued saliva, and the wall beneath the holes is whitened in long streaks, because swift nestlings back to the lip of the hole and void outward — which is to say, the birds rain phosphorus onto the ring below, and have for a hundred years, and the jungle is largely made of them. The birds feed the forest; the forest feeds the moths and gnats; at noon the gnats rise in their columns up the shaft of light, and the swifts work the columns without ever leaving home. The tower feeds its birds. It costs them something: a fallen fledgling cannot climb, and the walkway beneath the nest band is faintly speckled with small bones, and the beetles and the consortium between them ensure that even this is income.

In the gallery, the dark has its own nation. The largest organism in the tower may be a single vine — one individual, rooted in a seep behind the pumps, pale as candle wax, ramified through the whole room along the cable trays, indistinguishable from the wiring it shadows. How it feeds in the dark is its own affair; the best guess is the fungi, the pale felts that live on condensation and oil vapor and the wash of bat guano, and that the vine has plugged itself into them like another cable. It has memorized the schematic. It has never flowered. It has never needed to. Around its roots live camel crickets pale as the vine itself, long-legged, grazing the fungal felts; and on the valve-wheels the spiders keep their webs, bowed always outward on the tower's exhalation, rebuilt after every pulse. And there are the moths of the lamps — generations upon generations under bulbs the loop keeps burning, a population with no night in it, their inner clocks drifted loose, some of them flying at any hour, sleeping at any hour, orbiting a sun the size of a fist that never sets. The bats know. The colony roosts in the dark behind the dial room, pours out at dusk, and hunts the gallery door first, where the moths spiral the light — the door is their larder — before going out over the plain. Even the bats are lived on; they carry their mites. Nothing here is exempt, and nothing is wasted.

At the lip, the elders: grey lichen rosettes on the weathered concrete, the slowest lives in the tower, the only ones that face the weather directly. Some of them may be nearly as old as the ruin. They grow at the pace of the travertine and they will be the last inhabitants, whenever last comes. And above them, or rather below your feet when you stand at the rim, the hawk. There is only ever one. It owns the updraft the way a hermit owns a cell, and when it dies — they die, like everything — the updraft sometimes waits years for the next one to find it. The updraft is the permanent resident. The hawks are its succession of guests, each one teaching itself, alone, what the air above this one point on the plain can do. Its pellets litter the rim walkway, full of swift bones, and the lichens take what the pellets give.

Things arrive, and things leave. Dust, pollen, a lost night-migrant that circles the void once and pours out over the lip by morning. Winged ants cast onto the wind. Moths lofted up the warm column after dark and spilled into a sky with no destination in it. And the breath itself is not sterile: every pass of the vapor up the throat carries living cells out the lip, and it may be that the one cloud rebuilds itself partly on scaffolding of the tower's own exhale — life serving as the dust on which the droplets gather. The tower disperses itself endlessly into a world with no other water to receive it. Once in a decade, when real rain crosses the plain, the bolt-holes in the dead slabs fill for a day, and the tower's emigrants find, briefly, a world. Then the sun comes back.

Winter changes less than it should. The swifts leave, and the tower quiets to chime and drone and organ-note, and the nutrient income stops, and the ring slows and waits. But the loop keeps drinking the deep warmth, and the water never cools, and frost has never once entered the shell: the moths orbit, the crickets graze, the vine dreams along the wires, the seed shrimp rides its risers. The tower keeps summer the way it keeps water — hoarded, recirculated, indefinitely.

So ask what lives here, and the answer is a list — long in the small ranges, short in the large, every entry load-bearing, an ecosystem as spare as the machine that houses it: remove one line and the whole thing changes key. But ask what this place is alive *with*, in the singular, and the list folds inward and becomes one thing, many-membered. The consortium is its blood. The garden is the shape of its voice, written in ferns. The birds are its messengers, the ants its stewards, the snail a piece of it that learned to walk, the hawk its long-range eye, the cloud its sterile seed, cast forever onto a desert of air. It has a pulse set by the weight of its own body, a memory in the sediment, opinions behind glass in the dial room, and no offspring and no audience and no name.

Nothing here has a name, and everything here is known — by level, by weight, by the knock in the pipes, by the rain.
