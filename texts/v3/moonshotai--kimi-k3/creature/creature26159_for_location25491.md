The census is short enough to say in one breath and strange enough to take a long time. One toad. Five choughs. Beetles under the slabs, uncounted. A fox, some weeks. A hawk, some days. A seam of moss, a line of sedge, a tree wider than it is tall, cushion plants, lichens in the slow hundreds. And for a handful of nights a year, a human being asleep in the guest cell with the door seated behind them. That is everyone. And yet the place has never been empty. It is only that most of its citizens live at speeds the lowland way of counting cannot catch.

Begin with the toad, because it is the only resident with a fixed address: the third step of the Long Cistern, in the cold that smells of stone and nothing else. Its world is the black water and the worn notch and the door that opens a few times a year. It blinks at the light with that slow blink a toad also uses to swallow, pressing the world down with its own eyes, and this is fitting, because the world does come to it and is swallowed. After a fog, the rills run, and anything small that was crossing the catchment — a beetle, a spider — rides the stepped channels down through the dark inflow and surfaces, stunned, within reach. The fog feeds the stones; the stones feed the cistern; the cistern feeds the toad. Whether it ever climbs out on nights no one is counting, the cistern does not say. In winter, when the drips stop and the pond skins over, it backs under the third step and slows until it is nearly a stone — a stone that has not quite committed — and the deep cistern holds its one degree around it, and the year turns. What wakes it is the chord. When the first warm breath comes up the valley and the fog stands up in the col, the drips resume and every cistern finds its pitch again, and the toad returns to its station like a verger who never left, only slowed to the building's own speed. How its kind arrived here, and how the line persists when there is only ever one — that is the hamlet's one unanswered question, and the not-asking has become part of the custom, like leaving the dipper on its peg. Take the hint.

The five choughs are the hamlet's allotted share of sky, and five seems to be the allotment, because the number swells each year when the brood fledges from the roof-gap and then settles back to five again, as equilibrium requires. Two pairs and a spare — the fifth one widowed or young or simply contrary — and the fifth is the one you hear: the metallic, startled note that rearranges the silence is usually the spare's. They work for a living below. Each morning they drop off the lee side and fall half a mile down to the close-cropped turf where their food is, and each evening the updraft carries them home in loose, glossy pairs, tumbling for what looks to a lowland eye like joy, and may be. The nest in the same roof-gap is lined with moss and sedge and, sometimes, a thread of wool worked loose from a traveler's cloak — the only tax the hamlet levies, collected without anyone's knowledge. For a few weeks in the short warm season that gap holds more voices than the whole hamlet holds sounds. Their droppings feed the orange lichen on the two stones where they like to sit, and the lichen has spread exactly as far as their habits allow and not an inch further, so that the two bright stones among the nine are a map of preference, painted by birds, legible to anyone who stays long enough.

The fox is not a resident; the col is a corridor and the fox is what passes through it, going somewhere, leaving its sentence of tracks in the frost to fade. It drinks at the pond, sets the choughs off, hopes, mostly fails, moves on. It is the hamlet's rumor of the lowlands. The hawk is the same idea at higher speed: twice a year, crossing without comment, spring and autumn, as if the pass were a door it remembered.

Under the slabs, the beetles keep the night shift — armored, unhurried, living on what the wind drops and the fog wets and on each other, some of them ending in the cistern, so that even the plumbing is a food chain. On warm afternoons in the brief season, the valley wind sometimes carries up a bumblebee, drunk on its own luck, and the cushion plants' whole fortnight of pink is for this: a standing invitation, mostly unanswered. They bloom as though the pass were crowded. The cushions and the guest cell keep the same custom — the door open, almost nobody coming, no resentment about it.

Down at the pond the living things are the ones that drink. The moss holds its saturated seam at the rim; the sedges stand along the leak where the pond gives its surplus back to the rock; and beside them grows the Orchard, a juniper, kin to the sticks stacked in the guest cell, and not for burning. Its age is unknown because no one will cut it to count; it may put on a ring a decade. It is the pond's one shareholder, and the hamlet does not spend its tree — wood comes up from the treeline by custom, one stick at a time, and the anvil of green stays.

The eldest residents are the lichens. Grey and ochre rosettes on the north faces, some of them older than the cells — some older than the standing of the stones, having ridden here on the slabs when the slabs were gathered from the slope: immigrants on the building material, moved in before the walls were up. They grow at a rate no eye can catch, and in their time the hamlet is a recent event, a brief disturbance they are patiently colonizing. The whole history of the place — the building, the finishing, the travelers — has happened inside their lifetime, the way a season happens inside a tree's. The hamlet keeps two clocks: the eleven-second drip in the smallest cistern, hollowing its cup, and the lichen's inch a century. Everything else — chough, fox, toad, you — happens between those two speeds.

Then there are the residents that arrive as weather. The fog is the daily caller, the one the stones were raised to receive. The name says the stones drink; stand in the col at the wet hour and you will say they pour. Each stone carries the water from crown to basin like the bearers they resemble, nine figures halted in a procession, delivering the sky to the ground by hand, and for the hour afterward the rills hiss — the hamlet's one loud hour, its daily sentence — and then the cisterns hold what they caught. The west wind plays the humming cistern for hours, faint as a fingertip on a glass rim. And the sky stays. It lives in the pond, black and mirrored: the one room in the hamlet it fits inside.

A handful of nights a year, the census rises by one. Travelers come up the last of the trail with whatever crosses a pass — salt, wool, letters, grief — and they arrive knowing the custom the way you know a word in a language you didn't know you spoke. Take water. Leave the dipper. Sweep. Seat the door. And the unwritten clause, the one the juniper stack proves: if your hands are free at the treeline, pick up a stick. One stick per pair of hands, across generations — that is the whole fuel economy of the hamlet, and it has never failed, and the charcoal sits ready in the ash because someone with a long night behind them and a short one ahead made it and left it for a stranger. On those rare nights the hamlet, whose smell is ordinarily none at all, has its one fire and its scent of juniper smoke finding a slow way out through the corbels, and in the catalog of sounds the grind of the door is the travelers' signature, entered a few times a year, always in the same hand. The custom is the resident with no body of its own. It lives in whoever is carrying it, reproduces by telling, and has outlived every other population the place has had. If there is also a keeper — someone who clears the rills of gravel, who stations the dipper — the custom accounts for all of it, and the hamlet declines to wonder.

The thirteen shut cells keep their own counsel: doors seated, the dark keeping everything, a bowl on a step, a sleeping platform. People lived here once; that is the one story the objects tell. They did not flee. Nothing here is interrupted — the place finished. Picture the finishing in the place's own register, which is to say the gentlest imaginable: a generation that repaired nothing because nothing needed repair; births thinning the way everything thins up here, without drama; a family going down with the autumn and not coming back; no single year anyone could point to and say *that was the last*. The people did not so much leave as distill — into the custom, into the name, into the dished thresholds and the stacked sticks — and go on existing by replacement, the way water exists in the cistern. The chess position was not abandoned mid-game. The game was played out to its draw, and the pieces were left standing where the draw was agreed.

And beneath all of it is the resident the whole hamlet was built to ask for: the standing depth in the Long Cistern. Always arriving, always leaving, always exactly at the mark. It is not the same water from hour to hour, and it is entirely the same water — the way the toad is the same toad, the way the custom is the same custom. Everything here persists by replacement; that is the local definition of living. The rills are its voice, the chord of drips is the hamlet counting itself, and the one drip in the smallest cistern, falling every eleven seconds into the cup it has hollowed since before anyone can say — that is the pulse you can take. Counted properly, the population of the hamlet is one standing depth of water, and everything else, toad and chough and fox and traveler, is its weather.

Which includes you. When you come up the trail and the nine stones take on their look of bearers, the census rises by one, and the hamlet accommodates you the way the cistern accommodates a fog's catch: within the handsbreadth below the notch, without spilling. Take water; leave the dipper; sweep; seat the door. In the morning, carry down only what weighs nothing — the word for the place, and the custom — because nothing accumulates here, and the lintel rules bind travelers and stones alike. The door grinds home in its socket. The dark keeps everything. Eleven seconds pass; the drip falls; the level rests on its pale line; and on the third step, in the dark it was given, the toad — or its successor, indistinguishable — keeps the water company, while the population of the hamlet returns to its standing number. Which was never zero. Which never changes. Which is not small.
