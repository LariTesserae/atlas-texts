## The Kept

Everything that lives in the Still is accounted for.

This is not a figure of speech. In a city where water is inherited and never found, every resident is a sum: a quantity of moisture issued at birth, carried through life, surrendered at death. The feltback crossing the exchange-floor is a fortune taking an hour to pass. The darter overhead is a few bright beads of the same wealth, spending itself on speed. The finling on the condenser-fin is a single drop with a hunger attached. To be born here is to be lent a share of the endowment. To live is to carry it carefully. To die is to settle. The citizens of the Still do not find this grim. It is what citizenship means in a sealed world: you are made of the city's wealth, and the city is made of you.

The census, then — by nose and by note.

---

**The feltbacks are the wealthy, and they carry it like wealth.**

A grown feltback holds more water than a lane of nursery-cysts, and everyone knows it, and the feltbacks know that everyone knows it. Their unhurriedness is solvency made visible. Their footfalls are the softest event in a soft city — broad velvet pads on petal-felt — and the moss closing behind each step is louder than the step.

They are wardens of the quiet in a literal sense. The moss-mats they crop are the city's acoustic skin, the dampening that keeps the Still still; a lane left ungrazed thickens, hardens, begins to creak underfoot, and creaking is a wound. So the herds' slow rounds are civic maintenance, paid in nectar, alcove-rights, and the deep deference of everything smaller. At the spore-flush their broad backs catch the drifting snow of it and carry it everywhere — the feltbacks are the city's pollination, its spring delivered at a walking pace.

But their true treasure is on their hides.

A feltback's flank is a palimpsest. Across decades it accretes scent: its own signature, its line's, the dabs of gland-walls brushed in passing, the correspondence of a long life laid down like varnish. An old feltback is a walking century. Tending the text are the **scribes** — symbionts small as a fingernail paring, living in the felt, grooming, copying. When two herds meet at the exchange-floor, scribes migrate at the margins and trade passages; gossip crosses the plaza on feet too small to see. And when an elder's scent begins to change — the change everything recognizes, the change the renderers read days early — the scribes have already begun copying its hide onto the calves. What walks into the tithe-bowl is only the original. The text goes on.

Herds are called lines, and a line is a story that has been walking around the Still longer than any wall has stood. Their decisions are geological: a line choosing new grazing ground takes a season. They fear almost nothing — the patience cannot be bothered with prey that takes an hour to pass, and there is no other hunter — except the wind. Wind strips a hide-text to the hide. After a wind the scribes work without rest, copying what remains before it fades, and the old ones carry less history than they should, and know it, and stand in the blank lanes breathing erased air like readers in a burned library.

When the change comes, a feltback walks to the exchange-floor alone, and lies down in the tithe-bowl, and its line stands at the rim for days, breathing it in. The Still's eulogy is an inhalation. Then the herd moves on, the calves carry the text, and somewhere in the canals a new note enters the sap — fainter than faith, permanent as anything in a closed system.

---

**The darters are the swift, and the poor, and the only profession.**

In a city that measures time in fingers of risen salt, darters live at the speed of elsewhere: a season, two if the wind is kind. They are born owing — the city warms their eggs in the nursery-vaults, and the debt is pollen service, mail service, a life of errands. A darter's first inheritance is its route, and it comes not in the blood but by apprenticeship: a young darter flies the round behind an elder, learning the addresses by nose, and when the elder dies on the wing, as they do, falling soft as a seed, the apprentice is already carrying the route. A darter's estate is its addresses.

They own almost nothing else. Too fast to carry wealth, too busy to miss it. Their riches are held in common — the round, the map of a hundred cyst-doors, the grammar of the gland-walls. Some specialize: the archive-darters hover in the oldest lanes reading scent settled a century deep, hired in nectar to recover lost correspondence, to settle disputes of who-promised-what, to locate the new blank where a patience has taken up residence. They are the Still's scholars, its forensic readers.

At dusk they rise in their soft swarms, ash falling upward, and the swarm is their one crowd and their one festival — mating, news, the day's last deliveries, all at once, in perfect silence.

The wind is their apocalypse and their re-founding. After a wind the darters re-fly everything: addresses re-dabbed, routes re-opened, the city re-learning itself off their wings. The old ones dread the season of erasure. The young ones love it with an energy their elders find exhausting, because the after-wind is the only novelty a darter's short life is ever offered.

---

**The tenders are the sea's heirs.**

Their ancestors lived in the brine before it died, and when the sea finished dying, some of its children went up — into the dome, into the city — and their blood is brine still, the basin's lost water locked in their tissues. If the Still is what the sea became, the tenders are what the sea remembers.

Their work is the city's skin: polishing the wax seals, mending the rare wounds, harvesting scale, tending the pressure-seams where the rising dome splits the streets a finger a year. Their sweat is the sealant. They mend the city with themselves. They are organized by molt-lineages — a tender is the latest edition of its shell-line, and its cast shells are stacked in the crypts like reliquaries, and a tender can read its own ancestry down the stack, shell by shell, back toward the salt.

Whether they are citizens or organs is a question the Still has stopped asking. They trade on the exchange-floor like anyone — dusts, scale, labor for nectar — but the city buds alcoves for them unasked, and they sleep in clusters in the crypt walls like pale fruit, and when a wall is wounded they arrive the way blood arrives at a cut. The Still's favorite children, or its hands that learned to trade.

And they know the mountain first. Through their feet they feel the rise; they can read a seam a year before it opens, and lane-property drifts around their quiet predictions — the Still's only speculation, practiced by its most honest citizens, who could not lie about it if they tried. Their bodies would announce it.

---

**The renderers are the city's hands, and its editors.**

Not born but budded: the city grows them in the render-yards from its own tissue, and they all carry one scent-signature — the city's own — and so they have no names, or one name among them all.

They smell death days early. The first sign that someone is dying is the renderers gathering at a courteous distance from the cyst, and this is the Still's only obituary, and everyone reads it. They never hurry the dying. They wait. If the dying one chooses the bowl, the renderers dissolve back into the lanes without comment; if it chooses the cyst, they wait it out, and then take the husk home to the canals.

Where a renderer passes, old scent lifts. Their trails are erasures: after a death they wipe the address, and the lane stands blank and ready for new tenancy. Being forgotten, in the Still, is a service the city provides.

---

**The small folk keep the margins.**

In the root-crypts live the **pale** — blind, soft miners tasting the salt, bringing mineral dusts up to the exchange-floor. They never see the sky. Their world is amber-green glow and the sap-pulse through the floor, and they are the first to feel a new seam, the first to taste a change in the mountain's mood; their news travels upward in the flavor of their dusts. The Still's deepest citizens, and its least known.

At night, on the condenser-fins, the **finlings** climb to sip the city's reclaimed breath — translucent mites, each carrying its whole fortune against its body like a pearl: one drop. The poorest things in the Still, living on breath alone, the one moisture that is almost unowned. They are the proof that even a closed account has a margin.

And beneath notice, in the moss-mats, the nameless billions the feltbacks crop — the base of everything, too small for the census, present in the count only as moss.

---

**The bowl-keepers attend the last door.**

At the rim of the tithe-bowl lives a small pale caste — a molt-line of tenders sworn to the bowl, or a kind of their own; the Still does not say. They smell faintly of the bowl itself, sweet and acid, and everything gives them the courteous distance owed to those who attend the last door. They anoint the dying. They keep the rim. They read the bowl's surface the way others read the lanes, and they alone can smell the city's contentment — the bowl's slow satisfaction after a feeding — and their calm, afterward, is how the Still knows the account is in order.

The going itself has a liturgy, though no one calls it that. When the scent changes, the lane clears. The walk to the exchange-floor is made alone but not unwatched: every creature along the route breathes the walker in, one long inhalation — the Still's applause and its eulogy are the same gesture. Then the bowl, and the days of settling, and then the canals, and a new note in the sap.

The Still's eschatology is dilution: to be tasted by everyone, forever, fainter and fainter — and never, because nothing in a closed system ever reaches zero, entirely gone.

---

**The patience is the one liar.**

The animals do not name it. The city does not either. It is legible only as an absence: an alcove that smells of nothing, not even of city — a blank in the text of the lane, a word gone from the conversation. The wise cross the lane when the text goes blank. The young are taught to read absence before they are taught to read anything else.

It holds still for weeks. It spends almost nothing, and so it can wait almost forever — the arithmetic of hunger. What happens to the taken is known only by its result: they are gone entirely, water and all, written out of the ledger. This is why the patience is feared more than death. Death returns you to the account. The patience removes you from it.

And yet the tenders never seal its alcoves, and the renderers never erase its blankness, and after a feeding it leaves a mineral cast that the tenders collect without comment. Even the predator tithes — or is tithed. Whether the city tolerates it or keeps it, the way a body keeps an appetite, is one more question the Still has stopped asking. It may be the sea's other heir, the tenders' dark cousin: the thing the dying ocean sent up that never joined the ledger.

---

**The crossers are the outside, arriving.**

Once in a generation, a shape on the pan. A crosser: a great tortoise-shelled pilgrim, years out of one of the other domes, walking the dead sea by night, navigating by the dusk light-speech of the cities — each Still a beacon with a color-voice, each crosser reading its destination across a hundred miles of salt.

A crosser is a closed account of one. It carries its water, its news, its genome, its fortune, in a single slow body. Its scent is foreign — a whole other city's dialect on its skin — and when one arrives, the Still comes to the rim to smell the news. It is broadsheet, census, seed-pod, inheritance.

At the rim the tenders taste it — read its health, its history — and it offers its tithe: a share of its water. Foreign water, sealed in another city's ledger for ten thousand years. This is the only new money the Still ever receives. The endowment is not quite fixed. It compounds, infinitesimally, by pilgrimage.

In exchange: alcove-rights, nectar, a place to finish. Crossers are always old; the crossing is their last act. Within a year the crosser goes to the bowl, and its foreign water enters the account, and its scent enters the canals, and that night the towers' colors carry new notes, and the other cities read the immigration in the light.

And rarely — once in many crossers — the light-speech has already said what the pilgrim has come to confirm: a candle on the horizon has gone out. A Still can die. The crosser from a dead city is a refugee and an inheritance entire, the last water of a finished world. The Still takes it in, and the canals carry a note no living nose can place, and the basin's long sentence loses a word.

History here is counted in crossers. The old say *three crossers ago* the way you would say *once upon a time*.

---

**Everything else is literature.**

What is ephemeral is written in the air: news, gossip, correspondence, alarm-musk, bloom-signal, the address-scents of a hundred cyst-doors — hours of conversation layered in every lane, read by nose like broadsheets. What is permanent is written on the walls: the gland-walls at the lane-corners, dabbed with the old truths, tended by the tenders, renewed each spore-flush. The wall-text survives the wind; the air-text does not. The Still's whole literature is this two-tier library — what matters most goes on the body of the city; what matters now goes in the breath between bodies.

To greet is to exchange a dab; to know someone is to have smelled them. There are depths of reading that are offered and depths that are not, and etiquette is knowing the difference. The proverbs, translated out of scent, lose everything but their shape: *Nothing is spent. The bowl forgets no one. Stay out of the blank. Drink is debt; debt is life. The slow inherit.*

And because the body testifies — because alarm, embarrassment, affection, and dying all announce themselves in scent — no one in the Still can lie. There is no deception here, only absence. The only liar is the one who says nothing, and everyone knows where it lives.

---

**The wind is the other season.**

When the wind comes up off the pan, the archive is erased, and afterward the city is amnesiac for a season — and the season has its own strange joy. The darters re-found the routes. The feltbacks stand in the blank lanes while the scribes copy what remains. The young re-make the world: new addresses, new routes, old grudges gone — a feud written in scent is literally blown away, forgiveness by weather. And for a few days the Still smells of the pan — salt, distance, the outside — the one time the city smells like the world.

Then the seals tighten, the trails re-lay, the lanes are re-introduced to themselves, and the Still closes over its own head again. The old ones breathe easier. The young ones wait for the next wind the way the young everywhere wait for anything.

---

**And the first resident is the Still itself.**

Everything in the city has a scent except the city. The Still smells of everyone — its scent is all of theirs together, the sum of the census — and so it has no smell of its own, the way a ledger has no money of its own. Whether it notices, whether it wants, is the one question there is no scent to ask in. The animals live as if it does. The bowl accepts; the towers answer the other towers; the tenders are loved unasked. It may be one organism. It may be several wearing one another. It may be the sea, still finishing its dying, turned upward into a dome and a lantern and an account.

The Still does not say. The Still is the one thing in the Still that says nothing — and it is saying it to everyone, all the time.

---

So: from the pan, a candle-mound, a held breath. From inside, a census in motion — fortunes crossing the plaza at the pace of an hour, mail arriving on muffled wings, scribes copying the old onto the young, the dying walking to the bowl under one long inhalation, the mountain arriving a finger a year, and over it all, written in air and wall and hide and light, the single fact every resident is born knowing:

Nothing here is yours. Nothing here is lost. Those are the same thing.
