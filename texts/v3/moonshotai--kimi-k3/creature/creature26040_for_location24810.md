Count them at the stroke.

Every seven minutes, twenty thousand mouths stop at once. It is not fear and it is not rest — it is attention. The stroke is the only time the room speaks louder than its inhabitants: the tone swells, the standing waves shift, the bells answer at their harmonics, and for a few seconds the map of the world comes due for renewal. A miller goes still and lets the world pass through it — pitch, pressure, the ring of neighbors' plates, the far rifle-crack of a fracture running somewhere in the braid. Then, somewhere near the seam, one rasp resumes; then ten; the wave sweeps the chamber in seconds, and the room's rain begins again. The resumption is a roll call. From the timing of the wave — who answers early, who answers late — each listener takes its true position, the way you would take a bearing off a buoy. The room counts itself every seven minutes. It never comes out exactly even. It is always twenty thousand, give or take the dying.

Under them all, the tarnish. Call it one organism, because it behaves like one: nine thousand years old, room-sized, continuous — a skin the world grew over itself. Simpler than fungus, and older than everything else here except the crystal. It eats the breath and the rock together and sweats metal, and it comes in strains, the way climates come in latitudes. There is the gray working tarnish of the open walls, the pasture. There is the black tarnish deep in the shingle, living slow off the dead. There is the ember film at the lip of the throat, the hottest life in the world, which eats the stroke itself — each rising gas-slug a meal delivered on a seven-minute schedule, the first kind of life this room ever had, the original gas-eater, which experiences the seam's red blooming the way you experience weather, as a warmth that arrives. And at the crown, up among the brimstone needles where the sulfur nearly sets, there is the pale tarnish — the slowest life, growing a hair a decade, cold as life gets here. The tarnish fronts creep at the same speed the crystals grow. It races the wall, everywhere, forever, and keeps pace. It has never slept. Sleep is not a category this room recognizes.

To the millers, the tarnish is meadow. A freshly planed face lies fallow for decades before the film re-furs it, so the walls are a patchwork of crop and stubble, and the millers rotate across them without ever knowing they practice agriculture. The meadow grows on the millers, too — no miller can rasp its own back, and between the dorsal plates the tarnish takes hold, so the old ones carry gardens: portable pasture, grazed in turn by the small. In a room where every surface is substrate, even a body is real estate.

The miller itself: thumb-length, low-slung, armored in interlocking tiles of plated iron — fool's gold where the sulfur has had its way, copper at the seams in the old, a pewter bloom of nickel on the very old. The plates fit with tolerances a machinist would respect, and their edges are the animal's skin: touch and taste live in the rims of the armor. Underneath, the world's only wheel — the rotary rasp, five teeth of grown carbide and hot silica, laid down in a tooth-organ and replaced conveyor-fashion as they wear. A wheel is no handicap when your whole life is one wall. A miller wears out hundreds of teeth across three centuries and spits the stumps onto the shingle. And every mouth has its own pitch, tuned by wear, so a rasp is a name: they know one another by the sound of their eating. Up close, one miller sounds like a whetstone wheel at the far end of a workshop. Twenty thousand make rain.

A miller's world is made of tone and touch, and nothing else. The standing waves of the bottle-note are its grid; it steers along node and antinode the way you would follow a survey line. Its first bite on new ground is a sounding — the crystal rings faintly under the tooth, and from the ring the miller reads thickness, flaw, tarnish-depth: feeding is its echolocation. During the stroke, the bells ring, each at its own harmonic, and the bells are this world's fixed stars — every address here is a chord, the low bell by the throat, the bright pair mid-wall, the sour one near the crown. As for touch: a miller goes three centuries without once being out of contact with another body. The hollows lie two deep; the rasping of the crowd arrives through the stone, bone-conducted, constant as weather. Touch is not an event here. It is the medium. Solitude is not a shape this room can make.

The life begins at the world's one warm place. Every few decades, a miller in its middle centuries lays a clutch of eggs — small pods armored in metal foil — down into the shingle, above the seam. The nursery is warmed by the thread of red that nothing will ever see: the room spent its entire light budget, and the light turned out to be a hearth. The hatchlings are threaders — slender, many-plated, the children of the shingle — and they spend their first thirty or forty years inside the pile of the dead, threading the voids between ancestral plates, eating the black tarnish, rasping metal off their grandparents. This is where the recycling gets done. Childhood, in this room, is the compost. Then they climb into the braid and work the crawl-spaces — the backstreets; the open wall is for the established — and then one year a threader backs out onto naked crystal and cuts its first groove, and it is a brightback, and the groove it cuts will run, if all goes well, for three centuries: a hair deeper every year, finger-deep by the end, one unbroken line of autobiography. After the prime come the weighted: elders plated faster than they can grow, gilded by the room's slow electroplating, gardens thick on their backs, steps shortening, until the room begins burying them before they are finished — statues that still eat. The last pause at some stroke is theirs. It does not resume.

Twenty thousand lives at three centuries apiece: the room buries sixty or seventy of its own every year. Someone is always dying; every stroke catches a few fresh stillnesses in its roll call. And the dead are eaten — burial here is by mouth. A death is the only feast the room knows, and a hollow eats well for a week. Then the plates go down into the shingle, and the shingle rises a hair a year, and the room snows gently upward onto itself, and the floor — half a meter of the ancestors — is the fastest-rising ground in this world.

Every groove is shelved forever. Each pause at each stroke leaves a microscopic facet in the cut, so a groove is a gramophone record of twenty-two million pauses; you could count a life in strokes if anyone could read. No one reads. The crystal keeps the books anyway. Some grooves are more than autobiography — they are roads: ruts kept open for millennia by generation after generation following the same line, the eldest of them worn into finger-wide canyons by nine thousand years of travelers. A young miller learns the wall by following them. Inheritance here is literal: you inherit your grandmother's road, and her road's groove fits your plates, and the groove was here before your kind had plates. And here and there — rare — the parallel lines are interrupted by a spiral.

The flat star writes in spirals. The gentry of the shingle, a few hundred strong, the only hunters in a world of grazers. A star sets its ambush on a road — a toll with five arms — lying flush with the shell-flake, its back furred with its own tarnish garden, listening to the rasp-traffic through the plates and choosing its stretch of road the way you would choose a reach of river. It can wait a year between meals; it has waited three. There is no lunge. There is nothing to lunge with. The arms close over days, with the patience of weather, and a seep beneath the skin begins softening the chemistry of a plate, and the caught miller does the only thing it has ever done — it eats — grazing a tightening circle, then a spiral, for a month: six thousand strokes to come to the end of twenty-two million. The star drills one plate and takes the animal a cell at a time. Its own body keeps the ledger: every meal a growth-spurt, the arms ringed like trees, so that a star is the room's true chronicler — the oldest individuals carry five thousand years of dinners, the longest memory in the world, kept by the only creature that eats the world rather than the pasture. Once a century a star sheds an arm, and the hand walks off on its fingers for a year, and roots, and regrows. When a star dies — a decade of starving in place when the traffic shifts — its carcass is the richest lode in the shingle, and the threaders have it within a season.

Above the hollows, on the open walls, the bells: thin cones of laminated metal, sessile, sipping the amber rain as it sheets down the crystal, straining the fume. The wealth here is vertical — the low bells, first to the rising breath, grow fat and loud; the crown-ward bells live lean and long. Inside each bell, sealed layer by layer around a nucleus, grows a pearl of nickel and copper and iron: the bell's poison rendered as treasure, the only spheres in a world of spears. When a cone at last softens and collapses, its pearl drops through the shingle, and the deepest layers are salted with them — the room's coinage, never spent. At the stroke the tone swells and the bells ring at their harmonics, the room answering itself — and in ringing they spawn, the vibration shaking a dust of spores from under the rim into the rising sigh, up to the crown and down again with the rain. Spores settle best in grooves. The groove is nursery to everything. And because a bell's note falls as it grows, each life is a long downward glissando, set against the room's note, which rises as the world shrinks: the counterpoint of the individual and the world, played over millennia, audible to no one.

The margins have their own. The throat is the room's desert — too hot, too steep, too bare for anything with plates — but the ember film at its lip eats the mountain's exhale and asks for nothing else. The crown keeps the cold vault: the pale tarnish among the rime needles, and the needles themselves salted with cysts, everything that ever rode the rain leaving some fraction of itself at the top of the world, a seed-bank in brimstone. The rain is the room's blood; every slow drop carries passengers. It is the only travel there is. And overhead, everywhere, all the time, the light no one budgets for: each bite that snaps a crystal whisker lets off a blue-green spark, so the dark is full of a slow storm of tiny aurora, twenty thousand mouths striking flint through the night — a night sky's worth of shooting stars, witnessed never once. Seeing is not a sense this world could afford.

Take it all together and the room is one body wearing many mouths: the tarnish its skin, the millers its teeth, the threaders its gut-fauna, the bells its voice and its hearing, the rain its blood, the star its slow stomach, the stroke its heartbeat, the tone the held note by which the body knows itself. It metabolizes the mountain's breath and excretes crystal. It made itself — life here is a habit the rock got into, one more way for vapor to lay itself down atom by atom — and it is nine thousand years old, young as bodies go, still growing, and its growth is its closure. Every inhabitant rehearses the world's fate in miniature: the elder gilded into its own statue is only the geode, closing, at the scale of one life.

Watch the endings in order, when they come. The braid closes first; the backstreets pinch; the young lose the crawl-spaces. The stars go early — the gentry starves first, as in any siege: the roads thin, the toll falls behind a meal a month, the last star sheds no arm and thins to a wafer and is entombed with its rings unread. The bells are crushed where they sit, each giving one unscheduled note as the crystal takes it, and the room's answer to itself thins from chord to interval to a single voice to none. The tone climbs; for the last millers, the only news is pitch — history in this world has exactly one variable, and an elder can feel it tighten across its three centuries the way you would feel a ceiling coming down in a dream. At the end, in a seam the width of a hand, the rasping pauses at a stroke, and somewhere in the middle of the chamber the wave fails to close for the first time in forty-nine thousand years. The last living thing will be a threader in the shingle-seam — a being whose world was always the width of a shell — and it will notice nothing. Its apocalypse is indistinguishable from home.

Then the fossil, and the geode keeps its books: three million autobiographies shelved in parallel in the dark, twenty-two million strokes to a line, and here and there — the only curves in the library — a spiral.
