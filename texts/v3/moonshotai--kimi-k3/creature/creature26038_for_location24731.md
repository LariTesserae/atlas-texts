**The Census**

Officially, the population of the Coldworks is nine and a dog. The dog is listed as equipment — VALVE: 1, CANINE, GENERAL PURPOSE — and the nine are listed by trade, and the form has a box for TOTAL HEADS that someone fills in each quarter with a number that has not changed in years. A hundred pairs of earmuffs hang on the pegboard at Head Station; nine pairs have gone soft with wear.

Anyone who has wintered here will tell you the count is wrong. Not high, not low — wrong in kind, the way a map is wrong that shows the halls and not the traffic. You will learn the population the way you learn the dark down here: by distances, and mostly by ear. Almost everything that lives in the Works is known by evidence long before acquaintance — a chair kept clean, a garden gone brown, an entry in a log, a light crossing a far junction like a ship at sea. This is normal. Sparseness is not emptiness. It is residency at very low pressure, and the customs are how we keep the pressure up.

What follows is the real census, as the old hands keep it — which is to say, not written down anywhere you could find twice.

**The Dog**

The senior resident is a dog.

Valve is eleven, or thirteen, or some number the paperwork was never meant to hold; the vets are phone calls down here, and he has never once been sick. He has a circuit — Head Station, canteen, pump hall, dormitory — and he has a chair: in the visitors' gallery that has never had a visitor, one chair of the dust-filmed row is clean, and it is his, and from it he attends the chord the way other dogs attend fires. The warden swears he can hear a missing tooth and that it offends him — when a pump goes down he stays only the first hour, as if to be polite, then leaves.

He lifts his head before the horn. Two seconds, sometimes three. Once, in the early days, the station clock said the Tide was due and Valve slept on, and the Tide did not come, and since then the log has read, on occasion, *Tide per dog*. His lamp at Head Station is relamped first, ahead of the galleries, ahead of everything. This is not written anywhere either.

He will not pass the brine doors. He will walk you to the frost line and sit, and wait completely, the way dogs wait, and if you are in the cold store when the Thaw is due he will be gone when you come out. No one has taught him the purge hours. No one asks how he knows.

And he does not watch the tram. Everything else in the Works he inspects, escorts, counts, greets — but when the bell rings down Long Street, Valve is asleep, or drinking, or deeply occupied with the far wall. No one has ever seen his eyes follow it. It is the one thing in the Works he declines to know.

In the admin drawer there is a requisition form — CANINE, GENERAL PURPOSE, QTY 1 — filled out in three different handwritings over the years. Unsigned. He is the warmest thing in the Works, and aware of it. The Works, for its part, appears to be aware of him.

**The Nine**

The nine are: the warden of the pumps, who reads the plant by ear; the lampman, whose rounds are the gardens' weather; the storekeeper, who minds the cold store and the crane's feelings; the boatman, who rows the reservoir soundings and counts the echoes; the cook; the mechanic; the electrician; the surveyor, who keeps the maps and so decides, in pencil, what the Works contains; and the ninth, who is on long rounds.

The ninth is always on long rounds. The ninth bed is slept in — the sheets crease, the pillow dents — and changed weekly by whoever passes, and everyone assumes someone else has met them, and the name on the ninth locker is a roster name, which settles it, and the rosters name more people than anyone has ever met.

Two hundred beds, all made up tight. That is the dormitory's devotion, or its standing orders: the house is kept at the roster's numbers, not the headcount's. The long table is set at one end, and set always for one more than the shift — the guest place. You pour two cups. You drink yours; the other goes cold, or doesn't, and either way you wash both. Nobody remembers starting this. It is simply how tea is made, the way rain gear hangs four kilometers underground: a courtesy that stopped being funny a long time ago.

The customs: announce yourself entering a hall — *hall entered* is enough — because echoes answer late and people answer later, and it helps the record. Log your own movements honestly, so that what isn't anyone can be entered under *traffic*. Wave at the flashlight crossing the far junction. If it doesn't wave back, note the time.

Striking a name from the roster requires the leaver's signature. The builders never signed out. They signed the chalk in Bay 40, stood the rebar whiskers, and went, officially, to lunch — so they remain on the rosters, rotated to the deep shifts, due back. Payroll at the surface stopped querying it years ago. It is a rounding error: a column of names, scheduled forever.

**The Lampwives**

When the cold declined the threshold garden, the garden's moths stayed, and found new suns. The name is exact: they keep the light, and are widowed by it.

Everything else about them is parish business. One lamp, one world — the green rectangle of algae, the moss fringe at the light's sill, the whole of it grazed, tended, and known. They are born on one lamp and die on it, and between the two they do not hurry. There is no wind down here; flight is a decision. They rest on the warm glass like congregants, sip condensation from the tile, and in the Lag, when the ceiling-rain arrives two days after the weather, they are already sheltering under the pipe flanges. You can read the indoor forecast in them a day early, and the lampman does.

You can also read a dying lamp. A day or two before one fails, its moths go restless — rising, settling, rising — and the lampman calls this moth-pressure and walks his rounds by it, and the gardens get their relamping the way parishes get a new roof.

The dead lamps are the sad geography. The garden browns into its ghost; the moths sit the brown for days, mourners or shareholders refusing the news, and then they are gone. Died, or attempted the crossing: thirty meters of dark between islands, no light between, and you find them sometimes mid-span, spent on the tram rails, winged, facing the next lamp. Some of the crews carry them. Cupped hands, a walk through the dark, a delivery. They call it the mail. New hires laugh. By winter they are carrying moths too, and not saying much about it.

Only once do the lampwives hurry, and it is for the tram. Its lit windows crossing the dark draw them off their lamps in a stream — the only time you will see them fly like moths fly anywhere else, and the only time you will think of them as capable of a mistake.

**The Rats**

The rats are the other warm-blooded nation, and their country is the motor rooms, and the cold is their sea.

They came down as freight, like everything else, and they hold the warm rooms and no more: you will see one at a threshold, sitting up, looking across four degrees of open gallery the way a person looks at cold water. Within their rooms they are sleek, unafraid, and keep their own shifts. They are also the best instrument in the Works. Rats leave a motor room hours before a bearing goes, and the warden watches them the way he listens to the chord; when a whole colony packs the warm pipes and emigrates, you know a room has been shut down before anyone tells you. They are the announcement.

The treaty is old and simple. The canteen is ours — trapped, patrolled. The motor rooms are theirs. Valve does not hunt. He could. Borders are observed down here; that may be the first law of the place, older than the brass plate.

The rule is: don't name the rats. The roster is long enough. One of them is named Foreman anyway — a big buck in Motor Room 2 who takes insulation fiber from your hand, the electrician's doing — and nobody reports it, because the electrician is who you'd report it to.

**The Loaches**

The hill sends them in bead by bead, through the weep holes, like letters.

They are pale and finger-length and eyeless, or nearly — the aquifer's children, born in black water under real stone, washed into made stone and finding it acceptable. They hold in the slack behind the channel baffles; they drift the slow black braid toward the sumps; they are the only residents whose home remembers an outside. Sometimes you will see one work up to a weep hole's trickle and hang there, trembling, reading it like news from home.

They learned the horn in two Tides. One blast and they go to the channel floor and hold. Two, and the water stands up a foot and breathes out, and some of them ride it — dispersal, colonization, the canals restocking themselves on a timetable. At Tide-fall they gather at the sump grates in rows, heads upstream into the dark, like a congregation at a rail. What they are waiting for is entered nowhere.

In the bend of Canal 3 lives the Resident: thicker than your thumb, twice the rest, known to everyone. Loaches do not live long, the books say. The books were written upstairs. The water was here before the building, and there is no reason some of what swims in it isn't older than the roof.

You don't eat the loaches. It isn't a rule exactly. They came a long way. So did you, but you had a cage and a lamp.

**The Eel**

In the back of the sump-house logbook is a page that is not in the logbook's index. The entries are in six or seven hands over the years, all in the same format: date, Tide, thickness, direction. Once a season, roughly. Thickness is always *forearm*. Direction is always *downchannel* — toward the deep works, the reservoir, the sumps that sump the sumps. Color: white. Witnesses: one. Always one. No entry has ever been corroborated, and none has ever been entered as anything but fact.

The warden's theory, which is everyone's theory: an elver came in through the aquifer before the pour, when the hill's water still ran the hill's way, and it grew in the dark on what the weep holes bring, and the Works rose around its water the way a town rises around a church. Everything else down here moved in. The eel was served notice.

At the first Tide of a season, someone — it is not arranged; it is always someone — leaves a little fish on the sump grating. Canteen fish. A few bites' worth. It is gone by Tide-out. It could be the current. It is entered in the log as the current.

The boatman keeps the echo tallies — shout from the steel beach, count the seconds, know the level. A short echo means a full house. The tallies are filed in the same drawer as the eel page. Nobody has ever said why they are kept together, and the long echoes are entered too.

**The Colleagues**

Twelve organ pipes, nine turning — and the nine that turn are not always the same nine. The rotation appears on no schedule the warden keeps, and he keeps the schedules. Pumps go down for service and come back, and sometimes a pump no one has logged out is simply off, resting, its pipe cooling, while one of the sleepers turns. The warden stopped reporting it the year the reports started coming back stamped OPERATING AS PER SCHEDULE. Some days the chord is missing a tooth; some days it has a stranger's voice in it. The old hands adjust, the way you adjust to a colleague's cold.

The crane keeps its own schedule in the cold store, and its housekeeping is not the manifest's. Cylinders migrate the aisles in patterns that match no paperwork, and the storekeeper's position — stated flatly, over tea — is that the crane is keeping the cold even. Racks it visits often rime slowly. The racks it ignores grow their frost in long feathers, as if neglected on purpose. New hires joke about the crane. By the second winter they nod to it crossing the aisles and tend its grease points when they pass, out of politeness. It docks facing the brine doors. And during the Thaw, when the sleet falls indoors, it stops sometimes mid-aisle, hook still, for the whole twenty minutes — as if listening to the only weather it has.

The clocks agree. There is no signal at this depth, nothing for them to agree *with*, and they are wound on a rota that has gaps, and still the station clock and the canteen clock and the dormitory clocks keep one time. The only clock you can hear tick is in the Quiet Bay, where the Hum stops dead and your heart is the loudest machine in the Works — and even your heart, if you sit there long enough, begins to sound as if it is keeping someone else's time.

The elevator levels a half-inch high for a lone passenger, as if eager. For the dog, it levels dead true.

**The Tram**

You will hear it before you believe it: the bell down Long Street, dopplering through the lamp-islands, and then the car itself out of the dark, lamps lit, no driver, the windows furred with frost on the inside — so all you ever get of the interior is silhouettes. Spools. Crates. Shapes that could be seated men, if you were inclined to check. You are not inclined. Nobody is. That is the first thing the Works teaches you: how not to check.

Sometimes it stops for you. It slows, and aligns, and the doors open, and warm air comes out — warm, in a place where the warmest thing is a dog — and it waits exactly as long as courtesy requires, and not a second more. The rule is unwritten and universally kept: let it pass. It will. It has somewhere to be.

Where it has to be is called *outbound*, and outbound is the halls the maps omit, whose numbers the sequence skips, where the surveyor's pencil simply does not go. The surveyor, asked, says they aren't finished. This is true in every sense. The builders meant to come back after lunch; the rebar whiskers are still standing; the rosters still carry their names. It stands to reason that lunch is being kept for them somewhere, on posted hours of its own. Sometimes, far off, from no hall the maps contain, you will hear the doors' chime. You enter it under *traffic*.

The tram has never been early. The old hands know its times though none are posted. The dog will not watch it; the moths stream to it; and when it has passed, Long Street is darker for a while, the lamps needing a minute to seem bright again — as if the street were adjusting its eyes.

**The Argument**

Everything else down here came from somewhere — by cage, by crate, by coat lining, bead by bead through the weep holes. The cold came from below, which is to say it did not come. It was here. It is the prior tenant, the native population, and the plant is not its maker but its interlocutor. The storage business is simply what you build around an argument you cannot stop having.

The argument keeps office hours. Once a day the Thaw runs — the plant's daily statement, twenty minutes of indoor sleet — and within the hour the rime has replied, feathering back over every railing, patient as tide. The crane goes still to listen. The dog leaves early. Nobody leans on the lower walls. It isn't fear; it's courtesy. You don't put your weight on the other party.

On the lowest level is the inspection port: sealed, bolts undisturbed, frost on the inside of its glass, so that the port shows nothing, which is either its failure or its success. The storekeeper wipes the outside weekly, *for inspection*, and logs nothing, because there is nothing to log. The frost keeps the record on the far side, in a hand no one has learned to read. Or everyone has.

And the Hum — the Hum you swallow in the pump hall, that arrives through your boot soles before the bottom does — the Hum is strongest here, in the sternum, below hearing's floor. The warden's heresy, only half whispered, is that the pumps tune themselves to it: that the chord has a drone beneath it that is not ours, that the hall is a duet and only half of it is the plant. The Tide keeps its appointments; the Lag arrives by mail; the Thaw falls on the hour. Ask where the timetable came from and you will be shown the posted hours, the brass plate, the warden's logs. Ask twice, and an old hand will tell you: we copied it.

**The Count**

So: one dog, listed as equipment. Nine of us by trade — or eight, and a ninth on long rounds. Lampwives beyond counting, in their parishes of light. The loaches, letters of the hill. The rats behind their warm borders. One eel, on the evidence of one witness a season. Twelve voices, nine usually speaking. A crane. The clocks. A tram, outbound. A column of names due back from lunch. And the cold, which was here first and does not want anything — that is everyone's comfort, stated and restated: it does not want. It only keeps its appointments, like everything else that has learned the timetable.

The Works was built for a town and houses a handful. But the count is rising, or the counting is improving, and either way the furniture is patient, the beds are made, the extra cup gets poured.

You will know you have been here long enough when the words change. First you say *nothing lives down here*. Then you say *hardly anyone*. And then one Tide — the dog lifting his head before the horn, the loaches gathering at the grating, the chord coming up through your boots like the building clearing its throat — you will catch yourself saying *we*.
