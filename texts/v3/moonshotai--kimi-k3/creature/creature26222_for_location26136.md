Nine hearths, three clusters, a little over a hundred people when the years are kind — fewer after a hard Hungry Hush, more after a string of fat ones. The knot-cords keep the count, and the count is always being revised. But numbers tell you nothing. What tells you something is this: they move through their home the way you move through a room where someone is sleeping, except that they have done it every day of their lives, and their mothers did it, and the body of the habit is now hereditary. Watch a child of three cross a ledge. Before the sign-language, before the tree-tending, before anything else, they learn the feet — heel, edge, toe, weight committed only when the stone has accepted it. A visitor is read by footfall before any greeting is exchanged. Plateau feet strike. Gorge feet place. They will know your whole journey from how you set down your pack, and they will be kind about it, and the children will mimic your tread in sign behind their hands, and you will not know you are being teased.

Generations in the green dim have made their mark. The eyes are large-pupiled, quick in low light, and they squint up at the lip like moles; when the Blade comes they work in it with the greed of people starved for true color, but it hurts them a little, and you can see them flinch and lean into it at once. The feet are bare or felt-soled, the toes wide and prehensile from the notched trails. The hearing is the real marvel. Everyone in the Hush sleeps through whispers and wakes at a missing drip. They can tell which pool is breathing by the pitch of its sigh, can feel the Swallow's evening inhale as a pressure on the eardrum before the first fog spills over the lip, can hear — this is the dripwright's professional claim, and it is believed — a cracked cup in a neighbor's drip-line three ledges away. And their voices, from disuse, are low and rough and unpracticed, each one a rare instrument, so that to know what someone sounds like is an intimacy ranked and guarded. The hierarchy of knowing a person runs: hands, then rhythm, then voice, then name. Everyone knows your hands — that is the public self. Your household knows your heel-rhythm. Your Speaker-intimates, the people who climb to the foam with you, know your voice. Your spoken name — for there is one, a syllable or two kept wrapped like the copper — is for kin and lovers, and is said almost nowhere, and almost never twice.

Names otherwise are signs and taps. A person carries a sign-name given in childhood and changed, at most, once in a life — the old knot-keeper is signed as a slow closing hand, like a tree shutting at dusk, because her patience is proverbial; the best tithe-carrier of the Deep cluster is signed as two drips and a pause, after the rhythm of his walking. Nicknames bloom constantly in the hand-talk and are wicked. The hand-talk itself has registers: the big full-arm signing flashed across the gaps between clusters, formal and slow, a public address; the quick small finger-talk at chest level for those close enough to touch; the palm-talk for the thickest milk nights, when the glow drowns and the dark is total, and they sign into each other's open hands like writing on water; and the heel-tap code through the ledge-stone, which the gliders taught them or they taught the gliders — the story varies by cluster — though everyone agrees it was perfected for a deaf grandmother of the Blade cluster, so that she could receive the news through her bones, and she lived to a great age missing nothing. To interrupt someone, you do not speak over them; you reach out and gently still their hands. It is the rudest gesture in the culture and the most tender, depending entirely on how it is done.

A day begins before the milk lifts, with the house greeting itself: someone walks the eaves and listens to the drip-line, the way you'd check a fire. Then the trees — at dusk you watch them close, but at dawn you go among them and look for the one that closed late or badly, because a sick tree is a sick well and the whole settlement knows by midday. The morning is chores in gray light: felt, cords, drying-racks, the soft industries. Then, if the sky allows, the Blade. The clock-keeper flashes one great sign down the gorge and everything true-light happens in a rush — the silk-fine weavers at their looms, the pigment-grinders, the clock-keeper herself cutting the day's mark on the polished wall, which is now a calendar of cuts going back generations, a record of light in a place that mostly does without. When the Blade fails for days they tally the gray counts and go on in the undersea dim, unbothered, the way farmers wait out rain. Dusk is the day's hinge: the inhale starting, the trees shutting their hands one by one down the colonnade, and then across the dark gaps the light-palms waking — green-lit hands opening and closing above the emptiness, saying the day's last things, household to household, slow as fireflies. No house sleeps without its drip-song running. They say every house sleeps with one ear open, and in the Hungry Hush, two.

The three clusters have characters, the way neighborhoods do. The Spray cluster, up by the Speaker, works the loud trades — knapping, bone-cracking, anything with a noise in it — and produces, perhaps consequently, the outgoing personalities, the laughers, the ones who fall in love easily and often, living as they do beside the one legal voice-water. The Blade cluster in mid-gorge keeps the weavers, the House of Cords, the clock-keeper: the precise ones, the archivists, the ones whose signing is grammatically correct. The Deep cluster, down toward the Swallow, tends the oldest trees and watches the pools and carries the tithe to the throats, and is thought by the other two to be a little strange, a little comfortable with the dark at the bottom of things — a charge they answer with a shrug they have perfected into a sign. The accents in hand-talk differ just enough that marriages across clusters produce children who are charmingly bilingual.

Birth is the culture's great logistical problem, because a baby is the loudest thing in the world and does not care. The lucky ones are Speaker-born, arriving in fog season when the falls roar and cover everything. A clear-season birth mobilizes the hamlet the way a fire would elsewhere: neighbors come carrying their cups and shells, and the birthing ledge is rigged with everyone's drip-lines at once, the whole community's smokescreen braided into one dense patter around a single small loud life, while inside the felt-lined room — every cluster keeps one, for births, for the coughing sick, for the rare guest who snores — the midwives work and the cry-rota turns, because a crying infant is soothed instantly, relentlessly, by whoever's shift it is. The placenta goes to the roots of the child's birth-tree, the tank-tree assigned that day, the well the child will sip for the rest of its life. When a tree outlives its person — and they usually do — the tending-hand passes down, and the people say: hands change, the well remains. The oldest of them, a vast gnarled rosette of the Deep cluster signed as Grandmother-of-Wells, predates the cords' ability to say by how much, and her water tastes of stone and time, and she is sipped at weddings and at deaths.

Childhood is the still-game, drilled daily and played with real delight: freeze mid-motion at any unexpected clatter, hold it, hold it. The best players grow up to carry the tithe. Children learn time by the snail-tide — a carcass wears its slow living carpet and is gone in a day, a snail-day, the unit of every small appointment. In Fat Week, when the air is soup and the walls shiver with wings and the roasting-racks never cool, the children fling handfuls of moth-dust at each other in silent scale-storms, the only snowball fight in the world with no sound in it. And every child, sooner or later, is carried up the long stair to the Speaker and held in the spray and allowed — encouraged — to yell into the foam, and this is a memory they all keep: the one place your voice was ever free, water on your face, somebody's arms around you.

The trades worth naming are the strange ones. The clock-keeper, who owns the Blade. The dripwright, who tunes the eaves and says the work is tuning the house's breathing, and who is never finished, and who alone has a job to do at funerals. The stone-swaddlers, young and nimble, who climb the choir scree after every storm to check the moss-jackets and cradle new falls, and who, when a stone sounds in the cold of night, find the singer by morning and re-swathe it — putting the word back to sleep, they say. The pool-watchers, who read the breathing pools and the gray wading bird they call the Reader, who schedule the washing-days, and who carry the dead. The tithe-carriers, who go in pairs on milk nights, one carrying, one listening, walking unhurried to the throats and unhurried back — the unhurried part is the whole training — and who describe, when pressed, the sensation of being heard by something very large and very close, the way you might describe weather. And the voice-keepers, the culture's answer to an archive that cannot hold sound: people gifted at mimicry who keep the voices of the dead in their own throats and perform them at the Speaker in fog season, so that a grandmother can hear her mother's laugh once a year, accurate to the catch in it. The knots hold facts. Voices must be kept in bodies. It is the most honored work in the gorge and the saddest.

Love proceeds by hand across the gaps — courtship here is literally long-distance, full-arm signing from ledge to ledge at dusk, with the whole gorge able to read it if it bothers to look up, which gives young courtships a public, theatrical quality everyone pretends not to watch. The lovers develop a private finger-talk, then make the climb to the Speaker, and there, in the foam, they hear each other for the first time. To fall in love in the Hush is to finally hear someone. Spoken names are exchanged in the spray like contraband. Weddings are held at the Speaker in fog season, the one loud joy the law allows, and the two houses braid a phrase of their drip-songs together afterward, so that the marriage is audible on still nights if you know how to listen. Old couples who can no longer make the climb sign their news in the dim, and it is said to be enough, and watching them, you believe it.

Anger is managed like fire. In season it is carried to the Speaker and shouted out; off-season, feuds are conducted in a politeness so devastating that visitors have mistaken blood vendettas for diplomacy. Justice runs on noise-debts — weeks of voluntary muteness, paid down — and on the water-law, the oldest law: the water belongs to the tree, the tree to the hand that tends it, and to kill a tank-tree is to burn someone's well in a land without rivers. The penalty is measured in tending-years owed to the wronged household. The ultimate sentence, pronounced only twice in cord-memory, is to be sent up — given to the Loud — which they regard the way other peoples regard the sea. Council meets at the Speaker beneath the rafters where the mute owl nests, welcomed, and if the owl lifts off mid-debate they recess until it returns, on the grounds that the other listener has the floor.

Death is the culture's quiet masterpiece. If the season and the dying person's strength allow, there is a last loud day: they are carried to the Speaker to speak their names, to laugh — laughter being the most dangerous sound in the world and therefore the one most properly spent on the dying — and to give their voice to a voice-keeper. Otherwise there is a final sign, held a long time, which the household learns by heart. The pool-watchers carry the body down to the karst throats, and that night the family keeps vigil for the bloom, the brightening, and no one finds it frightening; the dead keep the lamps lit, that is simply what lamps are, and on clear nights people go and sit by the pools and hold their glowing hands over the water and have, for all practical purposes, a visit. Then the dripwright comes to the house of the dead and removes one cup from the drip-line — a rest, left in the pattern forever. Old houses have many rests. You can hear a house's whole history in its gaps, and a grieving household answers no light-palms for a season, and the neighbors come in the dark and leave cups of tree-water on the silent ledge, and the gorge absorbs it all. One house of the Deep cluster carries a rest for something that was not a person: the eel that appeared in their washing pool, impossibly, and stayed for years, and was fed moth-bits by three generations of children, and was mourned like a grandmother. No one begrudges the rest. It is considered the best thing about that house.

The other citizens deserve their due. The Reader — whether one bird or a succession, no one agrees — stares at a pool the day before it breathes, and is never hunted, and when one dies another is eventually found standing at the water, because the pool always sends a new Reader. The gliders drum their messages in the grain and are answered heel-first through the stone; in the Hungry Hush a glider drumming at your ledge is begging, and is fed, discreetly, the way you would feed a proud neighbor. The giant snails are the undertakers of small deaths and the clocks of childhood, and in their own deaths become the cups of the drip-lines — a snail crosses your path in life and carries your water after. And then there are the listeners, about whom everything is agreed and nothing is known. Pale as pool-things, jointed like a crab's leg multiplied, eyeless, carrying the fan of antennae that opens like a hand: that much. Tracks near the tithe-stones, like the print of a hand with too many wrists. The gliders will not drum about them. Ask a child what a listener looks like and you will get the stock answer, delivered with the stock shiver: like the part of the dark that is paying attention. The covenant holds — the tithe is carried, the ledges are left alone — and when it fails, when someone is taken, which happens rarely and always in the lean season, the hamlet's response is the same as at a birth: every drip-line in the gorge converges on that ledge, grief wrapped in patter, because hate is a kind of noise and they cannot afford it. Sometimes, on the thickest milk nights, a house's drip-song comes back at it from down-wall, half a beat wrong. You do not answer it. The dripwright retunes at dawn. No one has seen a listener clearly, anymore, by definition — the clear seeing is the last thing — and the drowned doorways below the terraces are assumed to be theirs now, the First Hush gone to pasture.

Once in a generation someone climbs down from the plateau and stays. A come-down drinks from the common cups for a full year, and the test is never stated and always running, and acceptance arrives the day a household teaches them its drip-song — because to be taught a house's pattern is to be trusted with its voice. The people call themselves, in the hand-talk, by a single sign: the gesture of setting something down gently. Those who place their feet softly. They think the plateau traders are deaf — how else could anyone live in all that noise — and the traders think them cursed or holy, and both are, in their way, right. In the House of Cords the children are shown the bronze bell in its felt-lined box, beautiful, never once rung, and someone always asks, and the answer the eldest knot-keeper gave, on the last occasion it was asked, has been kept by the voice-keepers ever since. What does it sound like? The end of the draft.

Stand among them at dusk, if you are ever permitted, and feel the arrangement turn: the inhale, the slow white cataracts coming over the lip, the trees closing their hands down the colonnade one by one, the pools kindling below the terraces, the light-palms waking across the gaps. Somewhere up-slot a choir stone settles and lets go its one deep note, and every hand stops and every head turns and the note rolls down into the Swallow and is gone, and after a moment the hands resume, softer. They are not a silent people. That is the thing outsiders get wrong. They are a tuned people — a hundred and some souls who have made themselves into a single organ of hearing and set it down in the dark of the gorge like a cup under a drip. What lives in the Hush, mostly, is listening, wearing human shape. And if you stay long enough, they will teach you the sign for themselves, the foot placing, the gentle setting-down, and it will be your name too.
