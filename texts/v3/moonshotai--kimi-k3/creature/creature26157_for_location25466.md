Ask what lives here, and the plateau answers the way it answers everything: late, and truly, and in several sizes. The census depends entirely on what you are willing to count as a citizen.

Start with the ones you can see. The lizards are the first to notice you, and the first to file you: a morning of push-up displays on the warm slabs, pale bellies flashing in code, and by noon every lizard on the dome knows your size, your pace, and your intent. Learn to read them and you will never be surprised again. A dome full of displaying lizards is a dome at peace; a dome gone suddenly still, every head turned the same way, is a dome with a hunter on it. The keepers travel by lizard the way sailors elsewhere travel by birds.

The hoppers are the legion and the telephone. Gray as the gravel, patient as it, they own the hot hours by refusing them, and at dusk they drum the stone — the soft coded thunder you feel through boot soles and sitting bone — and the whole plateau becomes a body with a pulse. What they are saying to each other is hopper business. What rides along under it, some dusks, is not. If you drum back — this is the old experiment, the one the keepers' elders have always made — sometimes, from very far away, something answers: in the hoppers' code but not quite, like an echo with intention. The keepers drum names into the stone, mostly. The names of their dead. The answer, when it comes, comes once: the name, returned. Delivered, and acknowledged. The ropes record three such answers across the generations, and the ropes translate the answering word three ways — one rope reads it as *kept*, another as *continue*, the oldest as *almost* — and the word was the same word. Afterward, for a season, the hoppers drum in a pattern no rope has recorded before. Then they go back to their own affairs, and the line stays open.

The moths write on the air because the air will carry nothing else. At dusk they rise over every cup in flashing clouds, signaling in light, and a keeper crossing the flats at last dark can read the water news off them: which cups are full, which are failing, which have gone to salt. After the rains they come up like ash reversed, and for one long bright week the plateau is almost crowded.

Crowded is a relative term, and the rain-hour teaches you its extremes. In the hour the dry channels run, things hatch in the brief water — glass-small, translucent, all of youth in a single afternoon — that live and spawn and are taken back before the channels stop. Their eggs wait in the dust's keeping between rains, which is to say between years. The keepers kneel at the running channels to watch them, and do not consider the hour wasted. Everything here is old, except these, and they are entrusted with the entire opposite virtue.

The mirror beetles are staff, not fauna. This is in the ropes: no beetle has ever been seen to mate, to hatch, or to die. Their numbers per reed are always sufficient. They climb the cold flanks at fog-dawn and stand on their heads, drinking what condenses, seamless underneath, tasting — the lizards established long ago — of nothing. And when a reed is going to fall, the beetles leave first: gone a season ahead, and the neighboring reeds carry, for that season, more beetles than they need. They commute. Whether the reeds grew them or the plateau issued them is a genuine question, and the keepers, who collect questions the way the reeds collect water — for keeping, not for drinking — have let this one alone for three hundred years.

The sails hold the sky the way birds do elsewhere, but they are not birds, and the difference is that they have renounced the ground entirely. They fledge in the air, which means this: the young cling to the parent's membrane until their own wings can hold a thermal, and then they let go. Fledging is a letting-go, mid-air, the opposite of a fall. No one has ever found a dead one. The dust takes them on the wing, and what reaches the stone is a brief gray snow the beetles do not eat. The keepers suspect the dusk moth-clouds are also nurseries — that some of what rises flashing from the cups does not come down, and is, years later, a scythe on the wind. No one has marked a moth and found a sail. No one is likely to. The suspicion keeps well. And the sails' shadows arrive a heartbeat after they cross the sun, always, as if the sky were deeper than it looks and the light needed a moment to climb out. Watch long enough and you will notice the lag is not the same for everything. A tossed pebble's shadow lands with the pebble. A keeper's shadow trails her by more than a heartbeat; the old rope-keepers, by more still. The lag is the measure of how long a thing has been kept. It is the plateau holding your light a moment before letting it go.

The pale hunter is few — one per great round of country, spaced like the trees, like the keepers themselves. All noon it is stillness; then it is an unspooling; then there is one small tick of gravel, and it is over, and there are no bones, because the dust takes bone first. Either the hunters live very long, or the plateau provides: the ropes record the same hunter at the same cup across three keepers' lifetimes. At the water it lies down an arm's length from the lizards and drinks in turn, watching the sky, and the truce holds, and on the approaches it does not, and everyone knows the difference. The truce is the plateau's one law with enforcers, and the enforcers are everyone. The ropes tell of a hunter that came up onto a dome's crest the night a keeper was taken back, and stood, still as noon, until the stones were done. No rope says why. Not every word is for you.

Most of what lives here is plant, and most of plant-life is patience: gray grass keeping its meter of gravel, cushion plants tight as fists in the slots, lichen nations under the balanced boulders that no foot has ever touched. And the trees — one per cup, old out of all proportion to their size — are keepers in their own right. When a reed departs and its cup dries to a ring of pale salts, the tree stays: leaning over the salt, keeping a cup that no longer keeps water, sometimes for generations. Keeping is what living is called, here.

The walking stones are on schedule, and the schedule is the closest thing this place has to scripture. Sit with one from dawn to dusk and your attention will slide off it a hundred times, the way your eyes slide off a reed's flanks; at dusk the groove behind it will be a handspan longer, and you will not have seen it happen. The keepers call this sitting the long reading, and a keeper who has kept it up for a season comes back quieter, and her shadow lags a little more. Watch where the stones are going, the keepers say, not where they are. When three stones' headings cross at a living reed, the keepers begin to say that reed's name more gently. A fall comes every few centuries, for reasons of the reeds' own, and the stones know first, and they arrive — how, at their pace, unless they started decades early? — and stand beside the fallen one until the dust has taken it back. A reed is cold all its life, cold with no source; the way you know a fallen reed is dead is that it is warm. The cold has gone back to wherever the cold is kept.

For the reeds, to stand is to be born, to walk is to live, to fall is to die. The grooves cross the domes and end where reeds now stand and begin nowhere, and the ropes say what that means, once, in one entry, from a keeper's grandmother's grandmother: she camped by a salt ring where the grass was new, and in the morning there was a reed, and the cup was brimming with sky. She heard nothing. Loudness is wasted; arrival is silent. The keepers call it a standing, and no rope alive has seen one. If the reeds are alive, then everything here is their guest, drinking at their cups under their truce. If they are not, then everything here is someone else's guest, and the reeds are the table set for strangers. The keepers decline to decide, and the declining is not indecision. It is manners.

And then there are the keepers themselves, spaced like the grass — fewer than the reeds, more than the falls. You may not see one. You will see their housekeeping: a thread of smoke on the far rim at dusk; a low domed shelter, swept, with a coiled rope and a stack of jars. The sweeping is not against the dust; it is in collaboration with it. A swept floor shows what has visited in the night, and the dust writes the report. The rope is the archive: keepers write in knots, and a keeper's life-work is her rope, and at rain-gatherings the ropes are passed hand to hand and read by fingers, and the standing and the falls and the jar-count are all in there, and the one rule of the craft is *keep your rope honest*. The jars are the treasury — old engineering, found and inherited, never made; fill one and it holds the water at the temperature of dawn. The ropes know the number of jars. It is three hundred and eleven. It has only ever gone down. A broken jar's shards are set out on the Table for a season, and one night they are gone — taken back, or taken in — and the count becomes three hundred and ten, and the keepers keep that sorrow like the rest, plainly, in knots.

They keep the rules, which are few, and the questions, which are many, and the Table, which is neither. The Table stands black and knee-high near the stair-head, carrying the whole plateau in relief, bright points where the cups are full, and it shifts as the land shifts, and it shows points where no cup has yet been found. Keepers have learned not to hurry to the unexplained points. What they found, when they went: twice, a standing before any eye had seen the reed. Once, a salt ring. Once, a traveler, asleep, who woke and stayed. The Table counts what the land is about to have. Sometimes it counts you. The Table and the land are still negotiating, and the keepers are the couriers of that negotiation, walking the disagreements with their jars and their ropes and their few sayings. *Loudness is wasted.* *The cups are not ours.* *Drum at dusk; the stone is busy by day.* *Not every word is for you.* Their speech is so rare that their voices are rain-voices: low, brief, used twice a season, when the channels run for an hour and the place almost has a voice of its own and sound lives a little before it dies — like everything else in the rain-hour. Then, at the running water, they say the year's names aloud: the dead, the standings, the convergences, the count of jars. It is the only liturgy. It is enough.

Keeper children learn the rules before they learn words, and the words mostly at rain. A child's first possession is a small jar with her name knotted at its neck; her first lesson is the cup — how to drink in turn, watching the sky, an arm's length from the hunter if it comes to that, and how the line between the water and the approach is exact, and everyone keeps it. Not all the children stay. The stair goes down as easily as up, and the dust repairs it in both directions, keeping the way open for the leaving as carefully as for the arriving. Nothing here keeps you. That is how it keeps you. The ones who come back up, come back up for good. And no one is ever made a keeper. One dusk a traveler asks you the way to the third cup, and you know it, and that is the whole of the ordination.

Ask the keepers who cut the stair, who fired the jars, who made the Table, and they will say: the old. Ask who answers from far away, and they will say: the old, perhaps, asking back. They have one word for the whole direction of depth and earliness — the makers, the dead, the light — and the word is *old*, and they do not distinguish, because the dust does not distinguish. Nothing rots here; the dead are taken back, and their substance put elsewhere, and at first light the dust sifts gold over the stone and settles on your tongue with the taste of a coin, and at dusk it bends the horizon's light gently from the real cups beyond your sight, so that the thirsty walk toward true water. The mirages tell the truth. The old guide. Ask a keeper why the sails' shadows lag and she will say *the light here is old*, and mean the dead traveling with the light, or as it, and mean it as an answer. Live here long enough and you will stop hearing it as a deflection. It is precision. It is as much precision as the matter allows.

You will, eventually, have the thought. Everyone does, around the second season: that the domes are the backs of something sleeping, the reeds its hairs or its needles, the dust its breath, the cups its wells, the Table its self-portrait still being argued over, the stones its thoughts going somewhere slowly, the hoppers its pulse — that the whole census is one citizen wearing many bodies. You will mention this to a keeper, if you can find one. She will not say no. She will say, if she says anything, that it keeps those who keep it. She will mean the plateau. She will mean you. And if it is not one thing — if it is a garden, and the old were gardeners; or a book, and the old are still writing it, grain by black grain, in varnish that runs down the domes like the tracks of old tears — then the conclusion is the same either way, which is why the keepers do not hurry to it. Either the plateau is alive, or it is kept. Here, those are the same word said twice.

If you stay the night in a way-shelter, the customs are these. Sweep before you leave, so the next guest can read the night. Refill the jar. Add your knot to the rope, honestly. Drink in turn, and watch the sky. Do not hurry to the unexplained points. Leave the stones to it. Leave it to it — that is what a keeper says at partings, at cups, at graves that are not graves: the whole philosophy in four words, the blessing and the instruction at once.

One morning, after you have stopped counting the dawns, there will be a jar at the shelter you have been using that you did not put there, filled, the water at the temperature of dawn though dawn was hours ago. No one will be in sight. That is the entire welcome; notes are for places where sound travels. You will drink in turn, watching the sky. You will find that you walk around the grass now, that you speak in a lower register and rarely, that your rope is honest. At some dusk the hoppers will change their code for a season, and you will feel it through your sitting bones and know, without being told, that something far away has just been given a name. Perhaps yours. And some noon, when you are not checking, your shadow will arrive a heartbeat late, and stay late: the plateau, holding your light a moment before letting it go.

Leave it to it.
