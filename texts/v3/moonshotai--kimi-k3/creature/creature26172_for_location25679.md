You can be in the vault for hours before you understand that you are not alone in it. The keepers do not hide; there is nothing to hide behind, and it would not occur to them. It is only that stillness is their resting state, and a person seated at a dam crest, waist-deep in warm water, hands open on the sinter, reads to the eye as one more hummock among the terraces. The light is too even for silhouettes. The steam erases edges. Then the hummock turns its head — slowly, so the water is not troubled — and the whole basin rearranges itself around the fact of being watched. You are being noted the way rain is noted. It will pass, or you will.

There are never many. Two hands' worth in full generations, one hand's in thin ones. They do not count themselves; counting is for things that can run short. Their word for themselves, when the surface people ask — which is seldom — means something like *enough*, and it is the same word the incised line means: this much water, this many keepers. The household has a level too, felt rather than numbered, and it holds.

Their bodies have been made over by the place, or the place was made for bodies like theirs; after so long it comes to the same thing. They are small, narrow, the color of the light. The skin of their palms and soles carries a permanent wrinkle that no drying would now undo, and at the hairline, the wrists, the creases of the neck there is a faint cream rime — the same sinter that velvets every stone — as if the place had begun, politely, to deposit on them. Their eyes stay wide-pupiled even at the brightest hour, and they angle their faces away from the oval of sky the way you would angle away from a fire. Their hair is cropped close with shell blades. Up close they smell of wet stone and sulfur and seep-oil — of the vault; there is no difference — and their skin is the temperature of the pools. Their feet are broad, the soles thick and strangely polished, moving soundless over the dished cobbles: the path and the feet have shaped each other so long that each is the other's record. Strangest are the hands, fingerprints half-worn away by sinter meal, so smooth that a keeper's grip slides on rough things and closes truly only on fine ones. An old keeper's hands are smoother than her blades. Something in the wick-smoke, or the sulfur, or the simple wet, catches in the oldest lungs; they call it nothing. It is the price of fire in a world of water.

Their work is so small it would be invisible if it were not everywhere. Each keeper holds a circuit of lips and seeps and dam crests, wading it at their own hours, clearing a rim with the edge of a shell scoop, plugging a seep with clay, pressing sinter meal through the traded hide sieve for a patch — always toward the line, never away. A keeper trimming a lip holds a blade cut from the reef the vault itself is made of, a creature older than the roof's fall, doing new work. Everything is done kneeling, waist-deep, and slowly, because speed makes ripples and you cannot read a stirred surface. Stillness is the precondition of their only measurement. Someone tends the lamps; the flames are never all out at once, not by rule but because darkness here would be total, and the listening, however good, has never been enough to patch a dam by.

Once in each ebb of the light, at the dimmest hour, the gauge is sounded. The one who does it is simply the one whose stillness the household trusts — their only rank, and it passes hand to hand without remark. The copper strip comes out of its fold of cloth that has never been dry. The keeper kneels at the slab, lays the leaf-thin edge against the incised line, and waits for the water to take the metal. The household does not gather; they pause, each where they stand, the way you pause when someone in another room opens a letter. The water takes the copper exactly at the line. It always has. Then the strip is lifted and the pool closes over the mark as if over a held breath.

The strip is the one thing they own that visibly spends. The cobbles dish too slowly to witness, but copper thins within a life; the eldest remembers it thicker. It is their calendar, their heirloom, their single proof that time is passing, consulted daily — and one day, in some generation not yet born, it will fail, and the keepers of that time will decide what to sound with. No one now presumes to decide for them. The line itself is the only writing in the vault: one sentence, cut before their words existed, and everything they do is commentary. When two keepers disagree — rare, and quiet, over the hand for a patch or the reading of a film — they do not argue past a certain softness. They sound the gauge. The water arbitrates. The line settles what their voices were never built to carry.

But first and last they listen. The chord is their map and their clock and the underside of every thought; a keeper with covered eyes can point to any note's source, and sleep here is the way animals sleep, with one ear under. When a wrong note comes — once in a childhood, a tooth lets go of the roof and drops into a pool with a sound like a cup set down — the household is upright before the rings reach the walls. They go and look. If the wrongness is theirs, a lip knocked, a seep misplugged, they mend toward the line. If it is the place's own — a tooth fallen, a drip moved a handspan — they do not presume to restore the old chord. They learn the new one. The place re-finds itself; the keepers follow. So the chord each keeper carries is slightly their own, and the eldest now living remembers one with a high note in it that has not sounded since her youth. No one else can verify it. She carries it the way the cobbles carry feet.

Rain is the only event that needs no response. For an hour the oval darkens and the air fills with a second, coarser music; the chord is decorated; the children lift their faces. It is the one time the keepers see the place being fed — the far source of the inflow made briefly visible as weather — and they tolerate it the way you tolerate a loud guest who means well. Then it passes, the gauge holds, the swallet takes the difference, the steam thickens until the lamps are coals in milk. They wipe nothing. Nothing dries. They wait, which is most of what they do.

They are born in the water, in a side pool near the inflow, warm as the body they leave, and the first thing every keeper hears is the chord, muffled, through the water itself. Stillness comes before speech. A child learns to cross a terrace without a ripple before it is answered to, and its games are listening games: name the drip that just spoke; find the new note after rain; stand in the grazing pool until the shrimp come to your toes and stay — the shrimp will not come to anything that intends to move. Once, under the eldest's eye, a child is allowed to stop a drip with a fingertip and hear the chord with a hole in it. You cannot know a held thing until you have held it wrongly. Speech arrives late and stays optional. There are keepers who have spoken fewer words in a year than you would use to describe a single pool, and their words have no edges; heard across the basin, the household sounds like the vault muttering to itself, and that is not entirely wrong.

The place feeds them nothing they may take. The films are exactly eaten; the shrimp are exactly fed; a keeper's hunger would be a wrong note in a ledger written in living things. So they are fed from above. When the stores run low, someone climbs, and beyond the bare hour of karst there are people who keep the arrangement, whose terms have outlasted everyone's memory of making them. Salt and fiber are what the keepers name; the basket holds what the season holds. The ascent costs. The light strikes like a blow; they go veiled in woven fiber and still return with swollen, weeping eyes. The dry air pulls the water out of them; wind feels like being handled. Above, they sleep badly in all that unheld space, under a sky with no rim, no chord beneath the silence — and they do not stay the night if it can be helped. The surface people ask no questions; the keeper's smell goes ahead of them, and the children stare. Coming down, the dim receives them like water. Their eyes take days to come back, and they sit at the first pool until the ringing of the sky leaves their ears.

Their neighbors are the films, the shrimp, the rare fish. The films read the water and the keepers read the films: white streamers paling at the hot inflow, rust-red dulling on a ledge — news from upstream in the dark. Nothing can be done about such news, but it is noted, and noting is their literature. The shrimp are underfoot at every hour, grazing the films and, incidentally, the keepers; a keeper's ankle is a pasture, and they set their broad feet with care, for the law extends downward to the smallest fed thing. And when a fish comes up out of the dark sumps beyond the lamplight — white, eyeless, unhurried — work pauses. It is not an omen. Nothing here portends, and the keepers seem to love the fish for exactly that: they are proof the vault goes on past the lamps, that the kept place is only the lit margin of itself. No one touches them. When one dies in the pools, it is laid on a film-ledge and the films take it back, as they take back everything that falls.

And so, in the end, do the keepers. When one dies — often in the warm pool, where they have been settled at the last — the household keeps them through one ebb of light on the flat stone by the hut, in the lamplight, and then carries them down the terraces to the swallet and gives them to the outflow. The name is spoken then, because names belong to the absent, and afterward it is kept and not used; the dead and the climbed-away share one list. Nothing is marked. The law forbids adding even grief to the place. What holds their gone is the wear: the dished cobbles, the thinned copper, one old woman's memory of a chord with a high note in it. They neither say nor avoid saying that the dark beyond the lamplight carries their dead away with the water's age. The vault keeps nothing, and so nothing is lost.

The path is the one choice a keeper has. Some climb it and do not come back, and their names go onto the same list, without bitterness — or with a kept silence that is not quite either. And rarely, rarer than rain, rarer than fish, a stranger comes down the dished steps, someone the surface could not hold, and stands at the first pool until the household understands what is intended. No one asks. The vault does not ask. If the stranger learns stillness, learns the chord, outlasts the missing of the sky, then over years the place accepts them the way it accepts rain: rarely, and completely.

It would be easy to call them priests, or prisoners, or the last of something, and every one of those would be wrong. They are what the place grew to know itself by. The water needed a lip to be held; the chord needed an ear; given centuries, both formed. If you climb out now and cross the hour of karst and look back, nothing behind you marks them — a breath of steam off the bare stone, a smell like a match drowned in milk — and below, at the dim hour, the steadiest hand in the world is laying a leaf of copper against a line cut before their words existed, and the water rises to take the metal, exactly, and it is enough.
