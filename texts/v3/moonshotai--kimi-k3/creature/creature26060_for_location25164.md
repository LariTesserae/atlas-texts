The first settler was the felt, and it is still the landlord. Everything else in the Well leases a few vertical centimeters of air above the surface; the felt owns the surface itself. It came in on the wind or in the water, spores with no opinion, and found a valley-sized plate of condensation kept at exactly its temperature, and it did what life does with an unguarded resource — it became the resource's geography. Now it holds every wall and pipe-wrap and underside in slowly shifting territories: rust giving way to charcoal along a moisture gradient, amber advancing a few centimeters a year into bone, the violet-black claiming the deep corners where the drip is steadiest. Some of those territories are single bodies. A corner of amber felt in a warehouse on the canal grid may be one organism, unbroken for a century, living partly on the city and partly on the stacked millimeters of its own dead — a resident that is also its own ancestral neighborhood. Press a palm to it and it presses back, a degree or two above the wall. It is the only inhabitant that is also the ground.

The meadow is grazed, and the herds — get close, get your eye to within a hand's width — are busy with the whole business of being herds. The isopods move like weather that has decided to walk: a front of thumb-sized grazers, dozens abreast, antennae touching, tasting, voting. A herd crossing a plaza is a multi-day event, with stragglers and molts and deaths, and the molts stay behind in pale drifts along the route, and the route's later travelers eat them. The Well wastes nothing, least of all calcium. Springtails live in the felt like plankton in a sea; breathe close to a worked stretch and the air carries a faint peppery smell, which is them. The silverfish pour away from shadow like something spilled. Their line was old before the city was surveyed, and they have outlasted their menu — the paper, the glue, the starched collars — without ceremony, the way a family outlasts a fortune. The roaches range from sequins to plodders heavy enough to hear, and the audible ones walk like safecrackers, setting each foot down as though paying for it. In the Well, noise is a debt that comes due.

Moth larvae work the felt in lace, leaving their grazing trails as pale filigree. The slugs come out at dusk in slow caravans and write the city over in slime that dries to silver by the brighter gray of noon — a nightly script of crossings and recrossings in a cursive nothing reads, unless the rats read it through their feet. Over every sump the midges dance in their dense humming balls, whole populations whose lives run to hours, so that a single evening above one sump is a complete history — founding, golden age, decline — and then the bodies drift down onto the black water, and the fish rise.

Do not call any of it quiet. The quiet is a property of your ears, and your ears own only a narrow band. Above it, the rats conduct their entire social lives — courtship, pedigree, quarrels and their settlements — in a register the felt doesn't even bother to drink. Below your easy notice, the toads work the low end. The bats, because the felt eats high sound, have deepened their voices over generations until some calls dip into hearing as a soft tok, like stones clicked underwater: the only bat-sound the Well lets a person keep. Moth wings tick on lamp housings. Web strands carry the pluck of small struggles. In the true-rains the felt itself releases a low fizz — ten million springtails jumping at once. The city is loud. It is loud in currencies you don't hold.

The hunters hold the next floor up. Geckos own the city's inverted ground: every ceiling is their prairie, speckled, patrolled, inherited. A gecko is born upside down, hunts upside down, dies upside down, and falls only once. They commute the gradient — out across the warm ceilings to hunt, in to the fin edges when the heat loads up — their whole climate a few meters and a few degrees wide. The spiders hold every right angle ever built, and a spider is an address more than an animal: web layered on web, gray with wing-scale, until the corners accrete strata readable like tree rings — here the fat moth years, here the lean. Centipedes the length of a hand patrol the gutters, tasting the world for vibration; speed is their exemption from the rule of quiet, because they are gone before the sound of them arrives. Dragonflies quarter the canals through the bright-gray hours, matte-winged and unhurried — the air is so warm and thick that flight in it is half swimming, and they loaf along like gulls over a harbor. At the sumps the toads sit in rows and pulse, and on some nights their hum finds the pumps' rhythm and the whole low city throbs at one rate, machine and throat, a single slow conviction that comes up through your shoes.

The water is inhabited past its carrying capacity, which means the water is fed from above. The pale catfish stock the canals far beyond what the canals produce, and the difference is made up by the sky's thin manna — moths knocked from the cones, midges drifting down, dead bats, roaches that miss their footing, anything, ever, that falls. Nothing edible reaches the bottom. The canals have no detritus, only delayed meals. Walk a canal path and they rise to your footfalls, because vibration has only ever meant one thing, and the surface ahead of you dimples with soft deliberate kisses — an escort of mouths, pacing you at a stroll. Some are old, scarred, barbels gone gray, longer than your arm: fish born into a city already empty, grandparents to fish born the same way. Under the bridges, where the calthemite hangs and the dusk doubles itself, live the palest of them, the night-within-night shift, nearly eyeless, all lip and listening.

Each living lamp is an island. Its cone of moths is its own — settled generations ago, drifted since, until the moths of one plaza differ faintly from the moths of the next, wing by wing, an archipelago with kilometers of dark water between ports. Wing-dust drifts beneath the housings like snow that has given up on melting. When a lamp dies — and half are dead, in a pattern no one chose and no one reads — its moths do not die with it. They emigrate, over nights, across the felt-dark, a refugee stream with dust for a wake, arriving at the next lamp as strangers. At dimming the cones boil upward into the general storm, and the bats unspool from the flues and the hollow fins in rivers that pour upward, and carve it. Where the rivers sleep, beneath the flues, the guano lies in drifts with its own citizenry — beetles that have never known a world that was not guano, and would find one unlivable. The dragonflies hand the air to the bats at dusk. It is the only shift-change the Well observes, and the only one it needs.

And then the citizens. The rats inherit. The runs along every wall, glossed by fur and time to a finish no caretaker ever achieved, are older than any rat and will outlive them all — a second street plan, rat-scaled, laid faithfully over the human one. A rat is born onto its run the way you were born onto your street. Something rides the freight crawler, in any case; the crawler does not care; the commute between districts is free and runs on a schedule older than any lineage's memory of anything else. No rat living has seen a person, nor did its grandmother's grandmother. The vacancy is not a vacancy to them. It is simply more city. To a rat, the arrangement is evidently intentional and benign: the pipes exist to be drunk from, the fins to make the cool places, the lamps to gather the moths, the pumps to hold the bass note of the world. The Well is not a ruin to its residents. It is a world with no unanswered questions.

The machines hold residency too, and seniority. The crawler is the oldest continuous citizen — older than anything with fur, older perhaps than the oldest fish. The felt has never colonized it, because it will not hold still, and so it moves through the upholstered dark half-bald and gleaming in patches, headlights burning, on a schedule no living thing set. Its beams drag a cone of moths through the sleeping districts, and the moths that drop feed the roaches along the route, and the rats trot the curb of its passing. It is the Well's only parade, and it never ends. The pumps are less residents than the residency — the heartbeat everything else lives inside. And in the control room, the one live screen updates for no one: the last clerk of a dead office, faithful without hope. Its only audience is the geckos that hunt across its glow. The one screen in the Well that is ever watched, and watched for all the wrong reasons.

The cats are the last almost-domestic things, and they have kept the shape of pettable animals the way the city has kept the shape of a habitable one — exactly, and for no one. They are lean, silent, solitary, and their territories are drawn not around food, since rats are everywhere, but around cold. In a basin held just past blood-heat, cold is the only scarce good, and the machines make all of it. A cat's map of the Well is a map of cool refuges connected by hunting runs: the sweating lines, the fin rows, the cold rails. It drinks from the bead-lines on the pipes. It sleeps against the cold side of the world. There is not a cat living that has been touched by a hand, and yet they sit the warm rails over black water in the posture of windowsill cats everywhere, watching the surface breathe — faithful as the screen, obliged to nothing.

The owl in Stack Four is the rarest resident, and perhaps the only one of its kind. No census exists, and the question cannot be asked in any register the Well answers. It holds the widest territory of anything here: the whole night sky of the basin, the lamp-constellation laid out below it like a chart, the bat rivers bending beneath. Nothing keeps it. The rim is one climb away, and the world beyond presumably has mice. But the Well is stocked and quiet, and warm thermals rise off the stacks, and so it stays. A few times a year, usually at the true-rains, it calls — once, twice, the loudest free sound in the valley, a note with no prey in it and no warning, carrying street after street through the deadened air farther than any sound should go. Whether it is calling for another owl or out of the old habit of the species, nothing here can say. Nothing answers. Or something does, from very far off, some other stack, some other dusk — faint enough that the owl itself might take it for memory.

Count it, then. The felt that is also the ground; the herds and the lace-makers and the poured silver; the multitudes loud in other bands; the upside-down sky of geckos; the addresses of spiders; the escort of mouths; the archipelago of lamps; the rivers of bats; the polished inheritance of rats; the cats at their cold wells; one owl; one crawler; one clerk of a screen; one heartbeat of pumps. And whether you add a final entry depends on how you count, because the basin inhales air and exhales water over the rim, and it has a heartbeat, a temperature, a metabolism fed on its own past, and a voice held at the edge of hearing — and from the rim road it looks like a held breath because it is one, and everything that lives here lives inside it. The last line of the census is the only one that no longer matches its description: one vacancy, kept at exactly the scale of the thing that made it, the way a drip-line keeps the shape of the drip. Nothing waits for it to fill. Waiting needs a watcher, and the Well has none, unless you count the cat, and you should not. The water ships. The lamps gather their moths. The screen reports to the geckos. The basin holds its breath, and everything inside it breathes.
