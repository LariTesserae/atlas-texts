Officially, nothing here is wet. Unofficially, there are the people.

They are the wettest things in the world, and they have arranged not to mention it. Their skin takes the gloss like everything else, and after a generation or ten it has begun to hold the gloss — a faint iridescence at the temples and the backs of the hands, a sweetness on the breath that the cellars of the night air briefly betray. In the coldest hour before the line, your breath shows, just once, as if the body were telling on itself. Then it is gone, and you are glossed again, and decent. The plain is deepest around the Grafts — this is measurable, and the wrights measure it, and do not publish. The groves tilt, over decades, a degree or two toward the towns. Toward the breath of the towns. The sward takes what you set down, and it has never made an exception, and it is most patient exactly where the people are densest. Everyone knows what it is patient for. No one says it. It is the only thing the Underlight is loud about, this not-saying.

They call themselves tenders, all of them, whatever else they are — it is the answer you get at waystations and on trams, from still-masters and children: *I tend.* Fewer than a hundred thousand of them, across five living towns and forty kilometers of interval, which means the species here is outnumbered by its own ground. They learn the posted rules as a skipping-rhyme before they can read: *the day is a public work; nothing rests on the ground; fire is licensed.* They learn the unposted rule by example, in the hush, the way you learn any language. And they learn the hooks. This is a people of hooks. Every child is given a first hook at naming, and by six can hang a room — coat, cup, lamp, boot, book — because the floor is a neighbor with an appetite and you do not feed it your things. A grown tender's hook-pattern, the private geometry of where they put their life up, is as distinctive as a signature, and is used as one.

Their voices stayed behind in some wetter world and they have not missed them. Conversation on the plain happens at library volume, and on the open sward it often doesn't happen at all: the tenders keep a full hand-creole, plain-sign, fluent enough for herding, weather, gossip, and mild philosophy. The Grafts keep time in soft bell-codes rather than horns, and the codes have dialects — a Sixth Graft evening sounds different from a Ninth. Their music is the hum-choir: voices tuned to the grid's bass note, chords built on the drone of the engines, felt in the sternum more than heard. *Loud* is their mildest obscenity and their most serious diagnosis. A loud person is a hazard, like an unlicensed flame. And when the grid flickers in storm season, a whole Graft holds its breath — you can stand in a crowded hearth-house and hear the fire think — and nobody, not the youngest child, says anything until the hum steadies. It is the closest thing they have to a universal prayer, and it is entirely silent, and it is for the light.

Over them, under them, and out along the wire walk the Wardens, one order with three walks. The High Walk keeps the engines: you reach it by the rim stairs, switchback galleries cut into the young stone, and then you are out on the catwalks — a thousand kilometers of slung walkway following the slow spiral from rim to crown, a web hung in amber noon with the plain a kilometer below your boots. High wardens live aloft for weeks in sky-wards, little hung huts between the engine housings, and their hook-work is legendary, and they come down with what the Grafts call the far look — a mild permanent squint of perspective, an awkwardness in rooms. They keep the current-books, in which every engine's appetite is entered daily, and the books have one column no outsider has ever had explained, because the arithmetic of the grid does not quite close. The trusts bill for fuel oil and the Grafts pay the light-levy, and yet the grid has never once gone dark, not even in the thin ledger years. The baseload comes up the deep cables. The wardens do not lie about this. They simply enter it, quietly, in their quiet column, and keep the day.

The Long Walk is the storm walk: causeway wardens dragging grounding chains the length of the trestles while violet fire crawls the wires and the sward's blades stand on end, bleeding the sky's temper into accumulator jars, whispering to each other over the crackle because even at the height of a storm the Underlight does not raise its voice. And the Deep Walk keeps the galleries — the mines, by the trusts' accounting; the approaches, by the wardens'. More of that later, because the Deep Walk is where the people of the Underlight trail off into something else, and it deserves its own pace.

Between the wardens and the plain stand the wrights, who are not engineers and not priests and will correct you, gently, if you call them either: they are diplomats. Every Graft is a negotiation conducted in candlewood and iron over generations, and the other party is the ground. The wrights graft and train and prune; they cut doors where the growth permits and grieve where it doesn't; they read the sward's slow draft and revise the maps each year the way other people revise almanacs. A wright's apprentice plants a candlewood whip at twelve and plans to retire inside its crown, and this is considered a normal timeline, not a poetic one. They keep the grief too. The gaps in the numbering — First, Third, Fourth, Seventh — are theirs to tend: four armatures still standing on their piers out in the plain, bright as the morning they were bolted together, because nothing rusts here, nothing dims them, nothing takes them down. The sward refused them. Why it refused is the oldest question the wrights have, and they ask it the way you ask after an absent relative, annually and without expectation. At the First Frame there is a small keeper's house, and in it the order's one museum of the other chemistry: a copy of the Descent Books, a sealed ampoule, and an iron nail in a lacquered case, orange with rust, venerated the way you venerate a knife that killed someone you loved. Schoolchildren are brought to see it. It is the only rust most of them will ever see, and they press close, and speak low, even there.

The tenders proper work the interval: herders on oiled skids, gliding the polished trails behind the slider herds at the herds' own pace, because the herd sets the speed of the road and the tram timetable is merely a courtesy the herds extend. Moth-yarders at the waystations draw wick-moths with caged ember-lights and gather the shed parchment wings — no moth is harmed; a moth is a neighbor; the wings moult on their own schedule — and every letter in the Underlight is written on moth-wing, and every tick-line operator learns to read through the soft interference of wings roosting on the wire, a literacy like listening to one voice in a warm crowded room. The ember-glands, gathered from the winter-dead, make the cold-lamps: the only unlicensed light there is, flameless and blue, a bead of it sewn to every child's coat. Miners and surgeons swear by them. Children simply love them, the way children everywhere love carrying a small tame star.

And the rendering trusts do what no one else will say aloud needs doing. Nothing rots here; everything is rendered. The cold blue creep in the digesters takes the plain's dead — beetle, slider, sward-trimming, person — and gives back fat and fiber and fuel oil, and the stacks breathe warmth into air that otherwise never leaves cellar temperature, and the stack-wards are rich and warm and cough a little, the paraffin cough, and count it a fair price. The trust chemists work in lacquered gloves, and this is why: the digesters return one more thing, a few clear milliliters per body, a hazard with a glyph, the old poison itself, sweating out of everything that ever lived. It is the most controlled substance in the world. The chirurgeons dose it by the drop for the deep fevers; an heirloom ampoule is a dowry; and the wardens requisition the rest for the art they do not teach: first-firing. Every engine carries, sealed at its ceramic heart, a thimble of water. That is why the noon is amber. That is why the light falls like weather from some older sky. You cannot make a true day, the wardens have found, out of the dry chemistry alone — the lamp wants its drop of the old world, and so the day-engines burn above the plain like ten thousand tiny memories of a sun that things once fell from. The dead are rendered before the ground can claim them — practical, yes, and slightly a kindness, because the alternative is weeks of watching — and a measure of every rendered person goes to the grid, oil and drop alike. Ask anyone where their grandmother is. They will look up. The tenders call it the dear light, and on death-days the families go out at dusk and watch the engines being cupped, and say the name once, quietly, into the coming dark.

They live, then, inside a held breath that never has to be released. Their food is fat made elegant: marbled slider, cultured creams, fat-breads baked at the licensed hearths, sharp resin-wine from fermented sap, still-heads that could take paint off a wright's patience. They sleep hung — hammocks, slung cradles — though every Graft has floors, because the habit of the hook is older than the floors. Their houses know them: a room that has held a family for twenty years leans its walls a degree toward their breath and glosses a little richer where they sleep, and the wrights say this is sap-flow, and the families say the house is fond. At night they gather at windows for the moth-rise, when the wick-moths go up fooled to the ember-points and their wings patter together in the swarm, and grown tenders weep at it, sometimes, without knowing why — a homesickness with no address, an old song the blood remembers. The custom is to mourn upward: into the hands, never to the ground. The ground has appetites, and grief is made of the wet stuff, and you do not feed your grief to the world. You lift it.

Once in a generation the held breath becomes a sound. When the survey stakes have gone out far enough and a new chamber stands raw and dark beyond the Front, the Grafts build an engine — they build it together, a house of copper and ceramic carried in procession on a skid-train forty kilometers long — and the wardens bring the drop, and the chamber is first-fired, and a noon is born where no light has ever been. For one hour at a first-light, and only then, the Underlight is permitted to be loud: the hum-choirs open their throats, the bells strike full, children scream with joy into the new light while the fresh stone ticks around them like a vast clock clearing its throat. Elders measure their lives in first-lights. *I saw the Eighth's chamber lit* is a complete biography.

The Front people are the culture's long nerves — surveyors alone with their sleds and stake-books, beetle-handlers working the seams alongside the auger beetles, half-tame colleagues who tick in their lacquered pet-boxes in every schoolroom in the Grafts. And the Deep Walk people are its taproot. They come up from the galleries quieter than they went down, with the look — the listening look, as if attending to a voice just beneath the one speaking to them — and they go down again, and again, and some petition to stay: the gone-down, who tend the approaches on deep pension, whose letters come up the tick-lines shorter every year, until the wardens enter them, quietly, in a roster nobody is forbidden to read and everybody respects. What they report is warmth, and a pulse felt in the chest, and a sweetness in the air, and every root in every gallery leaning the same way, like iron filings around a pole. No one has reached the bottom. The wardens have a name for what is down there, though they do not use it officially.

Their catechism, such as it is, fits in a whisper: *It warms; we light; it grows.* The warmth comes up the deep shafts and rides under the vault as the kites' updraft. The light goes down as food. And the sward gives back everything else — oil, fiber, ground, world. Whether this is gardening or digestion, stewardship or symbiosis, the wardens decline to rule. The quietest of them hold a further doctrine, the one that explains the airlocks with their waxed felt, the door-watch kept at the shaft's cold mouth generations after anything last came through, the hazard glyph on the ampoules and the drops sealed in the engines' hearts: that a seed is the thing before, and what a seed waits for, in every older world, is water — and that the old poison did not stay behind when the First Families came down. It came with them. It is them. The tenders walk the plain carrying it in their blood, gram by gram, the last of the old chemistry kept like a pilot light, and the sward leans toward their breath, and takes what they set down, and waits. The heresy and the hope are the same sentence, and the deep wardens say it only below ground, where the pulse can be felt in the soles: *we are the weather it is waiting for.*

So ask what lives in the Underlight, and the honest answer depends on your scale. A hundred thousand tenders, hung and glossed and soft-spoken, keeping their hooks and their herds and their quiet — or one animal, forty kilometers wide, wearing the people the way a tree wears its orchards, growing its world a kilometer of root at a time. The two answers have stopped competing. The children settle it, as children do, with the question every child here asks eventually, pointing at the scaffolding, the unstopped Front, the half-written map: *Is it finished?* And the answer is the same from wrights and wardens, still-masters and herders, up on the catwalks and down in the approaches, a benediction and a schedule and a lullaby all at once —

Not yet.
