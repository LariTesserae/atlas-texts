Everything here eats the snow, or eats what ate the snow, or waits for what ate the snow to fall. That is the first rule of life in this sea, and there is no second economy to rival it. The pasture is overhead, the harvest falls, and every living thing in the dark is arranged — body, habit, patience — around the fact of that slow grey rain.

The second rule is slowness. Food arrives at the rate of settling dust, and nothing here can afford hurry. What lives in this sea lives long, lives lean, and lives mostly alone.

Begin at the ceiling, since everything else begins there too. The algal film has its grazers: small crustaceans, pale as shed skins, that hang beneath the ice their whole lives and graze upward, combing the green with limbs adapted to a world where food is always above. For them, up is dinner. They have never once been right side up by the world's accounting; by theirs, it is the rest of creation that hangs wrong. Things hunt them — a pale fish, thin as a letter opener, that cruises the murk and looks up, the only hunter in the world whose prey is silhouetted. The only shadows in this sea are cast here, in the last hundred meters, and they are cast downward. And the world's only green is a color with almost no one to see it: even the grazers work the pasture in a pewter dusk, and in the long dark months the pasture starves to a ghost of itself, and the snow below thins, and a kilometer down the sea has its lean season. The world's heartbeat is annual. Even the abyss feels the year turn.

The ice itself is inhabited — thread-thin brine channels run through it, salted against freezing, home to things too small to have enemies. Where leads crack open, they are skylights, not doors. Nothing here breathes air. There is no one to breach.

Below the murk begins the long fall, and the falling is itself a kind of life. The snow has riders — bacteria, larvae, the drifting young of bottom things — and many lives in this sea begin high and end low. A creature hatches near the ceiling, feeds a season in the dim, and then sinks into its adulthood: the ascent of youth is the only climbing anything here ever does. After that, a life is a descent conducted over decades.

The mid-water belongs to the snow-sippers. Jellies wide as rooms pulse their bells once a minute, going nowhere at the speed of the water, trailing curtains of near-invisible line through which the snow must sift. Fish cross the black alone — soft, pale, unhurried, eyes enormous or eyes nothing, weeks between encounters with anything, including food. There is a hunter or two out there, long-jawed and patient, striking perhaps once in a season; predation in this sea is not a profession, it is an event, a rumor passing through the dark.

And there are the great slow swimmers. They are the largest things alive here, and the fewest, and the oldest — the size of weather, centuries deep into their lives, filtering the snow through the middle depths, unhurried even by this world's standards. They are nearly silent. Then, once in a decade, one of them calls — a sound so low it is almost a tide — and the whole basin rings with it, and everything that hears it stops. No one answers in a hurry. The answer may come years later, from a thousand kilometers away, and the two callers will spend the next decade swimming toward each other. Courtship here moves at the speed of the medium. These are the only voices in the world that fill the entire world. When one of them dies — of age, of some slow internal failure — the death is quiet. What happens next belongs to the floor.

The floor is where the mountains are being rebuilt. The glass sponges sift the mountain-flour out of the silt and spin it into towers: the hills were ground down and carried here, and here the flour is being built back, a grain at a time, in glass, in the dark, unwitnessed. Some towers are centuries old; the oldest predate any keeper now living. And when the benthic storms come, the gardens sing — the storm's grip drawing a faint chord out of the glass, almost below hearing. It is the only music this world has. In storm season you can hear a garden from a day's walk off, and there are those who steer by it.

The towers are inhabited. Small paired things enter a young sponge through the lattice and live out their whole lives inside as the tower grows shut around them — a house with one room and no doors, fed by whatever the current brings, defended by the house itself. Their young drift out through the lattice into the dark to find towers of their own. A tower is never empty for long.

Around the cold seeps, the white mats of bacteria are the only life in the sea that does not, in the end, eat the snow; they eat the slow chemistry of the buried world instead. On the mats graze the shelled ones — thick-shelled, slow as erosion, centuries to grow, centuries to be eaten. They matter beyond their numbers, because in a world where hardness is almost gone, they are the only ones who still manufacture it. They draw mineral out of the water and the silt and lay it down as shell. They are the mint. Every shell is made in part of older shells — the same small treasury of lime, handed down since the mountains were young — and the beds accrete into mounds, the only architecture the bottom makes for itself besides the towers. Nothing hard is wasted here. Hardness is handed down.

The silt itself is peopled — thinly, everywhere. The plain is not a desert crossed by oases; it is a population too sparse to see. Burrowers irrigate the grey, turning it over grain by grain, and the ground's slow breathing is partly theirs. Beneath them lies the archive: layer on layer of snows that fell when the world was younger, awaiting exhumation.

Then there is the fall. A great swimmer dies in the mid-water and begins to sink, and a kilometer of descent takes the better part of a day. The fall itself is silent — but it announces itself in taste, the chemistry of a body that size leaking ahead of it through the water, and the plain reads the water like news. First comes the small cloud of things that rode the body down. Then the sleepers — fish that doze scattered across days of nothing, waking once in years when the water says wake. Then the eel-things. Then, from beneath, the ground itself rises: the burrowers coming up under the carcass, the audience already in attendance beneath the floor. The feast is measured in seasons, then years. What is left is bones, whitening bacteria, dispersal into solitude — and one day, a little later in eternity, a storm lifts the scoured bones out of the silt again.

A fall is the only crowd this world ever gathers, and the keepers come to it. For once there is enough. No one taps at a fall — the meat needs no stone, and the sound would only say what everyone already knows. The stones are held quiet. There are greetings by touch, a limb laid across a limb, the tasting of a long absence. There is courtship at the crowd's edge. There are young ones at their first fall, and old ones who remember two hundred. A keeper counts its life in storms survived and falls attended, and the falls are rarer.

Which brings it down to the keepers themselves.

A keeper is built the way this world demands: long-limbed and splayed, feet wide against the silt, walking slowly enough not to sink, blood at the temperature of the water, heartbeats a minute apart, everything about it engineered for pressure, cold, and famine. It does not see — the eyes are gone or going, and no loss; there is nothing to see them with. It hears with all of itself. Its whole body is an ear pressed against the world, and its world is made of voices: the rim booming where the bergs calve; the canyons, each with its own distinct rumble, like relatives; the gardens singing in storm season; the fizz of a berg melting overhead, which means stones; and, spaced days apart across the flat, the taps of its neighbors, each one known, each one placed. Silence is not emptiness to a keeper. Silence is the map with nothing new on it.

The stone is anvil, anchor, and inheritance, and a keeper holds it the way other creatures hold a thought. It is in hand always — sleeping, storming, ranging. An old stone wears a dimple where the shells go down against it, deepened over lifetimes. A young stone rings bright; an old stone has a low, dulled voice, and a keeper knows its stone's note the way you know a footstep — on silt, on shell-mound, on old bone, each ground giving it a different sound. Which means the tapping is a duet: keeper and stone together. Neighbors a day's walk apart know each other by it. And when an heir takes up a dead keeper's stone, the sound that resumes is a voice slightly changed — the stone's low note under a new rhythm, like a word in a new mouth.

The tapping is a language with four words. There is dinner — the steady knock of shell on stone, broadcast across miles and politely ignored; the whole plain knows when you eat, and it is never held against you. There is the alarm — a rattle that empties the ear, and everything in earshot goes still. There is the roll call after storms: one tap, spaced, patient. I am here. And there is the duet, which is courtship — two keepers trading taps until the rhythms fit. Four words are enough for a world with this few things in it.

A keeper hears a storm coming for days — a grip taking hold of the bottom, a rumble becoming weather. It goes to ground in its own lee, near its mound or its garden, and it holds the stone and lets the blizzard pass over, weeks of it, the silt scouring, the towers singing somewhere through the grey. When the storm lets go there is a held-breath silence, and then the roll call begins: taps scattered across a thousand kilometers, hours apart, a slow relay of survival. And the gaps — the silences where a tap used to be — those are the census too. No bodies are ever found; the gatherers see to that. What remains is a stone lying alone in the scoured silt, dimpled, cold, waiting.

Then comes the ranging, which is the best of times. The storm has swept the plain and exposed what the silt was hiding, and the searchers go out across the fresh-scoured flat. The stoneless go first and farthest — the unlucky, the storm-stripped, the ones who eat what yields because they cannot crack what doesn't. You hear a keeper by its tapping; the stoneless you hear only in their footsteps, and a footstep here is barely a rumor. Now and then two searchers reach an exposed stone together. There is no fight. There is a vigil. They settle at opposite edges of it and wait — for weeks, for a season — and now and then one of them taps, once: still here. This is what war looks like at the end of the world. In the end one of them stands and walks away, and it is never entirely clear why, and the matter is closed for a century.

Young keepers are born stoneless and practice on shells, and often enough their first anvil is their parent's cast body — because a keeper who lives for centuries must molt, and molting is the one crisis of a keeper's life: a stretch of soft-handed hiding, still clutching the stone. Nothing bothers a molting keeper. It is the closest thing this world has to a law. The cast shell is hardness too, and hardness is handed down.

The eldest are something else again — centuries deep into lives that stopped hurrying long ago, grown vast and gaunt and nearly still, crusted with the small leavings of the world until an old keeper is less an animal than an island, a walking seep. Their tapping is a landmark; the young learn the map by it. They remember. In a world where almost nothing happens, everything that happens is worth remembering, and there was never any reason to evolve forgetting: the eldest remember storms three hundred years gone, and falls, and the voices of stones whose hands have long since dispersed. When an eldest dies, the sea loses a bell, and the map must be redrawn in every head within a week's walk.

And when any keeper dies, the stone stays. It may lie there a year, ten years; no one takes a stone too soon — whether that is custom or indifference is a question only keepers could answer, and they do not answer questions. Then one day a stoneless one, or a young ranger, or an heir closes its hand around it, and it sounds again, and across a day's walk an old keeper lifts its head at a voice it has not heard in eighty years — changed, but with the stone's low note under it, like a word in a new mouth.

No keeper has ever seen the mountain. Every keeper has held it. The stones are the proof that the world was once otherwise, delivered one at a time by the ice. And from the plain's point of view — the plain has time for points of view — it may be that the stones are what persists here, and the keepers are what passes. A stone comes down from the mountain and crosses the flat for ten thousand years in a series of hands, and each hand is certain, for the whole of its life, that the stone belongs to it. The stone is patient. Perhaps the keepers are not the keepers. Perhaps they are how stones travel.

The rest is the world's weather, unchanged: the snow falling through the black, the storms combing the plain, the gardens singing when the storms come, the shelled ones laying down their slow lime at the seeps, the great swimmers calling once a decade into the dark and waiting years for the answer — and under it all, spaced days apart across the flat, the small domestic sound of dinner. Tap. Tap-tap. Someone cracking a shell against a stone older than the hills that made it, in a sea that finished long ago and has never once been still.
