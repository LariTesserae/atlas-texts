Take the census by ear.

Sight will not serve you here; the dim forbids it. So sit at a cairn through one dusk-peak and stop trying to look, and instead count voices — the rasp, the boom, the gulp, the volley — until the plain resolves into its citizens the way a chord resolves into notes. **On the Sounding, everything is heard before it is seen, and most things are never seen at all.** Every living thing here has a sound, or has a silence. Those are the two great categories of inhabitant, and everything else is detail.

The first citizen is the film. It is older than the plain — older than the plain's shape, anyway, for it was here when the mountains were, and it ate them. What rains down into the sheets as dissolved iron and sulfur and salt is the mountains' slow meal, still being served, and the film still sits at table. Its glow is a kind of exhale: the chemistry of eating, made faintly visible. Peel it from a whaleback in layers and you read time directly — young film thin as velvet on top, older growth thick as felt beneath, and at the bottom the black compressed centuries that the keepers cut as peat. After each rain the spores rise and walk the flats as grey veils, and where they settle the grey creeps on, an inch a season, the slowest conquest in the world and the most complete. It covers every wet surface. It covers the shells of stonecattle, the backs of old bellowsnewts too slow to rub it off, the leathers of the dead. It is the field, and the granary, and eventually the grave-cloth of everything.

In the sheets themselves the census explodes past counting. Skaters dimple the surface and talk to each other in ripples — courtship, warning, territory, all spelled in touching rings — and when the chord falters they go so still that the water forgets them. The translucent thumb-sized things the keepers call cupfish pulse in every pool, and they pulse on the beat of the chord, and no one knows how they count; children keep one in a cup of pool-water set on warm stone, a pet and a lamp and a gauge of the evening's loudness all at once, and every camp has its cup. Sievers squat in the shallows straining spores, and they are the plain's great practical joke, because a siever shut tight is indistinguishable from a cobble — pick up a handful of gravel and some of it is breathing. And some of the eggs are eggs. The swimming herds spawn into the gravel beds at the causeway crossings, stone-skinned eggs armored against a world of mouths, and a keeper scooping gravel for a fire-ring may hold a hundred unborn swimmers in one fist.

The bellowsnewts are the plain's voice and also its memory. Each pool keeps its own pitch, and has kept it for longer than keepers have kept records — a pool's name is its note, and keepers give directions in hums. The sac under the jaw is voice and ear at once; a newt hears its own note, and the notes of ten thousand neighbors, and tunes itself the way a stone settles. Down at the bottom of every pool, sunk in the black, lie the old ones — newts grown huge and slow and bass-blooded across unguessable years, half-furred with film, holding the tonic. When a falter sweeps the basin and the young newts flail and lose the thread, it is always one of the deep old ones who finds the note again. The plain's memory of its own pitch lives at the bottoms of pools, and everything that breathes here is in debt to grandmothers it will never see. Each bloom the newts shed the worn skin of the sac, and the keepers wade out to gather the cast bellows, and dry them into drumheads. A drum made from a voice, played in codes of threes and fives. The newts do not miss them. They are making more.

The drumwings own the air, such as it is. Their volleys are speech and sight together: they clap their wing-bladders and read the echo, shouting the dark world into shape around them, and their small dull eyes are nearly decorative. They follow the blooms rimward and back across the year, they glean the film from the shells of stonecattle — riding the files in rows, clapping softly to themselves, passengers on the slowest ships in the world — and they are the plain's alarm, because the mobbing-volley that means *stalker* is read by everything with the wit to listen, which is everything. One boundary of their lives is absolute, and keepers mark it well: the drumwings will not cross into the Rim-quiet. The last cairn-wardens call the turning-place the drumwing line — the edge of the audible world, drawn twice a year in wings.

Then there is the run, and the year turns on it. On the out-breath, down out of the rimward haze where no keeper goes, come the swimming herds — ribbon-bodied, faintly lit from the film they strain, so that for a few days each season the channels become moving rivers of cold light. The weirs funnel them; the keepers take a tithe and pass the rest, because the law older than any camp is that the run must reach the southern shallows, spawn its gravel into the crossings, and die — for the bloom that follows is fed on the death of the run, and the run's ghost walks the flats as grey veils. The young ride the in-breath back rimward, into the quiet, and return grown. The run is the only citizen of the Sounding that naturalizes in silence, and what it does there, and what it learns, it has never said. And the weir-nights are the most dangerous work the keepers know, because everything else comes to the weirs too. The stalkers wade the tails of the weirs in the loud dark, patient as standing stones, and the keepers work by drum-line with the peat-fires stoked high and blue, and nobody speaks, and everybody sings.

The stonecattle are the plain's long thoughts. Their shells grow in ridges, and you count the ridges as keepers count blooms; there are bulls walking the Sounding whose shells hold more than a thousand. Because they cannot rasp their own backs, the old ones carry their shells under full gardens of light, and a file crossing a sheet at night is the only starfield this sky permits. You feel a herd before you hear it — the herd-hum, too low for ears, a grey pressure in the chest that holds the file together in fog — and you hear it long before you see it, and seeing it, when it happens, is almost an afterthought. Calves are born walking; the file does not stop for birth any more than for anything else, it only bends. When the very old at last fall back, the file closes over the gap, and the rasping stops by one voice, and that silence carries for miles — and the plain sends its quiet company. Drumwings first, who hear the gap. Then the small millions of the sheets. Then the stalkers, cautious, late. Last of all the film climbs the bones, and for a season the dead giant glows, and keepers steer by known lanterns the way other peoples steer by landmarks. The shells, when the light finally leaves them, become roofs and boats and shields. Keepers know the herds as individuals — they read the bright sentences and name the files from the handwriting: the Crooked Question, the Long Answer. To kill one is a treaty matter, a whole camp's year, done once in a generation at the full cover of the dusk-peak. Mostly they wait. The plain is patient, and teaches patience, and the falling-back provides.

The pale stalkers are the chord's exception and its enforcers. They are the only citizens the noise does not include: voiceless, footfall-less, tall and soft-padded and colored like the dim itself. For days one will stand in a sheet, unhurried as geology, while its folded trumpets stay furled — and all that time it is not waiting for prey to come near. It is memorizing. It is learning the terrain of sounds: where the file rasps, where the sievers tick, where the cupfish school. And when the falter comes — when ten thousand newts lose the note and the whole basin holds its breath — the trumpets unfold, and the stalker walks its memory. In the chord, everything is hidden; in the silence, only the stalker has a map. This is why silence is the dangerous weather. This is why everything freezes and listens with its skin. The old keepers count falters the way other peoples count omens, and a long falter means, somewhere, a taking. A dead stalker's trumpet-ear, pried loose and cleaned, becomes a hearing-horn for the camp's watchers. There is a justice in that which the keepers do not explain.

And there is the wrong note. Rare — rarer than stalkers, which is to say a handful in a lifetime — but every child learns the rule before the rule about fire: the cairns each have their one true note, the notes are memorized like faces, and **if a note sounds where no cairn stands, do not walk toward it, whatever the fog says.** Something out on the sheets has learned the cairn-music. Some keepers say it is a bellowsnewt grown ancient and pool-less, a grandmother who lost her basin to some slow disaster of the breathing rock, and who goes on singing alone in the dark, wrong, enormous, lonely, and hungry. They do not say it is evil. They say it is to be pitied, and they say it from far away, and they walk the other way.

The vents have their own tenancy. Every gulp has a pitch and the keepers name the deep ones — the bass of the chord is theirs — and things live at their mouths that feed on the in-breath itself, sessile snappers that take what the swallowing water takes: a skater, a school of cupfish, once, by every account, a keeper's copper blade, leaned too close. What does the swallowing, down in the rock's throat, no one has seen; the keepers call them the Swallowers and leave it there, and they draw their water on the out-breath only, and they are polite about it.

Even the answering stones are inhabited. Inside the hollow flaws, down cracks that swallow a lifted light, there is old black water that has not seen the sky since the mountains were young, and sometimes — when the stone has taken up the bass and held it long — something in there knocks. It knocks in threes and fives. Either the stones have learned the keepers' drum-code across the millennia, or the keepers' drum-code is older than the keepers, and was learned here first. Keepers leave offerings on the booming stones: cobbles split on fossil ghosts, set down without ceremony. Nobody says *to*, and nobody says *for*, and everybody does it.

Of the Rims' inhabitants there is no census, only rumor, in three versions that the camps argue over at moots. In the first, the quiet is grazed by things that have given up sound entirely — earless, voiceless, pale, soft as drowned hide, creeping over the film in a silence so complete that a hearing creature among them goes half-mad from the absence. In the second, the quiet is inhabited by the hushed: keepers who walked away from the noise, long ago, for grief or shame or love of stillness, and what they became out there, the moots decline to specify. In the third, the quiet belongs to one thing, the Listener, and the Rims are not silent at all — they are merely the one place where something is doing all the hearing. The drumwings turn at the line. The keepers turn with them. The water is born up there, and seeps down into the sheets, and it does not tell.

And then there are the weir-keepers, who are fewer than the cattle in a single file, and who are the plain's loudest small creature and its most attentive listener. Their bodies have gone to the place: voices pitched low, made for the troughs under the chord; hearing quick as a skater's; eyes that have conceded the argument. During the great hours, when voice is useless, a camp holds council in stone-speech — foreheads pressed to one warm whaleback, the bedrock carrying their words to each other underneath the world, beneath the reach of any unfolding trumpets. The deaf among them, and the old whose ears have dimmed, are not set aside; they become the eyed, the watchers, keepers of the camp's one auxiliary sense, and a stalker-horn is passed among them like an office.

They work the only sharp edges in the world, knapped from cobbles, and they treat the ghost-stones — the lucky splits, the worn shells of things from before the mountains died — with a reverence that has no doctrine. Each camp keeps its pile at the ring's heart. Each child is given one, to carry until it is lost, which may take a lifetime. They set these stones aside the way you set aside a question you do not expect to answer but intend to keep. Their larder is the run, dried and pounded; egg-gravel roasted in the blue peat-coals until it clicks and pops; siever-meat, grey and iron-tasting, pried from its cobble; film-curd pressed from the bloom-thick growth, sour and faintly cold-bright, so that children at feast-time go around with luminous mouths, eating light. Salt comes off the tide-lines when the water sinks. Nothing is scarce in number. A camp's wealth is measured in walks.

They travel from note to note, and the notes are kin to them; a keeper can be woken from sleep, spun around, set down in fog, and will hum each cairn's pitch until she finds the one that is home. At night the camps drum relay down the cairn-lines, news moving across the dark basin faster than any walker, threes and fives in the troughs of the chord. A person's name is a rhythm, drummed at introduction, worn for life, and at death it is played for a whole dusk-peak without pause — the loudest thing the keepers ever do, because a funeral must be loud, because nothing beloved is ever sent into silence. Then the body is weighted and given to a vent on the in-breath, and the water breathes them in, and the chord does not change, and that is precisely the comfort: the note is never held alone. Children learn the stillness game almost before speech — freeze at the falter, listen with the skin, feel who is near — and learn the world's accents before their letters, and sleep with their ears open. Their songs are built for the place: a line thrown into a lull, the next line caught an hour later, so that a full song may take an evening and the great songs take a season, and no one ever hears the great songs all at once. They are held entire only in the camp's common memory, sung by the world in pieces, assembled only in the listeners.

So the census stands: the film, which eats the dead mountains; the small millions, who are everywhere and always far away; the newts, holding the note from the bottoms of pools; the wings, shouting the dark into shape; the run, commuting between noise and silence; the cattle, writing their slow bright sentences; the stalkers, walking their memorized world through the held breath of the falter; the wrong note, singing its loneliness; the tenants of the stones, knocking in the old code; the keepers, few and drumming. And at the dusk-peak, when your cup dances and the answering stone takes the bass down below hearing, the whole assembly speaks at once — and when the chord falters, for one breath, the assembly takes its own census, each life hearing exactly what it lives beside — and then the deep grandmothers find the note again, and the rasping resumes, and the Sounding goes on: everything counted, everything listening, everything alive and heard.
