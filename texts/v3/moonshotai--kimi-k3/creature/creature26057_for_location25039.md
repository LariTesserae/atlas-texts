Ask at the office who lives here and the clerk will not give you a number. He will reach down the inventory — the great leather book in which the Works counts itself, belting and boiler tubes and spare beater arms — and open it at the page the clerks have kept for as long as there have been clerks, under the same heading, in hands that succeed one another down the page like keepers: *Establishment, other.* It began as a joke, the year the Shed Three willow was taken onto the strength. But jokes here are kept up the way the planks are renewed, generation after generation, until nobody remembers they were funny and everybody maintains them.

*Tree, one, in service.* Then: *Cat, one, deaf.* *Heron, one, self-appointed.* *Swifts, two, summer.* *Eels — see Sump.* *Spiders — not enumerated.* *Kingfisher, one: see folio for the year ——.* And at the foot, because a census that starts as a joke ends up truer than the serious kind: *Persons, forty-odd, various, in service.* The last two words are exact, whatever the persons think.

**The persons.** Forty-odd, in the cottage row and over the stores, and their names are a short dictionary. The same twenty families have kept the place for a hundred and twenty years, swapping offices among themselves like a hand of cards dealt over and over: a keeper's daughter marries a beater-man, their son takes the bank round, his nephew is in the engine house, and the wage-book turns its pages and finds nobody new and nobody missing. There is no master of the Works. There are offices, and the offices are older than the officeholders, and this has so far proved sufficient. The company that drove the Post and dug the first drain is sixty years dissolved; its minute books molder in some county archive; the returns are still made out each year in the old form and filed in the office, and to whom they are now addressed is considered an outside question.

New blood comes across the causeway at fourteen, marsh boys and girls apprenticed to the beat, and the drone decides about them within a season. There is no arguing with it; it is not an argument. Half are gone by the first frost, back to the great grey quiet they were born to, and they are not blamed. The ones who stay, stay for life, and are taken out by barge at the end of it, and in between they acquire, without noticing, the place's defining trait: they think in rotations. Ask a press-crew man his age and there is a pause while he converts years into pollard cycles. Growth equals cutting. Leak equals patch. Seven years an apprentice, nine years a pollard, a hundred and twenty the Works. Nothing sudden except the whistle and the rain-bursts.

**Their bodies are tuned to it** the way a fiddle is tuned, and the tuning costs. Everyone speaks in *mill voice*, a shout-register that makes town shopkeepers blink, and everyone under forty in the bowl can read lips the way other children read print — without being taught, from the sheer necessity of the dinner table. You can tell a visitor at shift change: the whistle sorts the room, and the bowl-born go on chewing while everyone else leaves the ground a little. The plank walks carry a gait the length of the bowl; mothers know their children's step three sheds off, and a new man is announced by the boards before he is announced by name. The old beater-men have a notch gone out of their hearing at the beaters' own frequency, so the senior driver is deaf to his granddaughter across the table and alive to a dry bearing at forty paces — the frequencies he lost are the ones the world uses, and the ones he kept are the ones the Works uses, and he calls this a fair trade. The doctor from the town has learned to time his visits for the seventh morning, the only hour he can hear a chest. All the bowl's auscultation is done at the Hearing, between the rising water and the tea.

**The children** learn the numbers before the multiplication table. It is taught as a chant, call and answer, in the schoolroom over the stores: *What keeps the water down? The pumps. What keeps the pumps? We do. How long? Three days to the doorstep, five to the clock.* A child's first Hearing is kept like a birthday. They are woken before dawn into the wrongness of the silence — the first silence of their lives — and carried to the gauge house to watch the brass dials creep, and most of them cry, not from fear but from the size of the thing they've been told. On the office doorframe the settlement nails go down, a finger a year; beside them, in pencil, the children go up. Both columns are dated. It is one instrument read two ways, and the children grasp it before anyone explains: the house is settling, and they are what it settles for. The Post, driven flush with the turf at the founding and now standing taller than a man, is the only member of the establishment still growing. Children are stood against it to be measured. It wins every year.

**The Hearing, from inside,** is the only service the place keeps. On seventh mornings the bowl wakes itself — there is no whistle, and that is the alarm; people surface out of sleep into the enormous silence with the jolt of a missed step on a stair. The boilers are banked, not dead, and the tea still comes, and everyone speaks under their breath out of something older than habit. And the marsh, which is inaudible all week, filters in over the bank: wind in the reed, far-off water, birdsong, and on spring dawns the frogs — a whole frog-parliament out there, exotic as foreign music, though the bowl itself keeps none. Nobody knows why. The fen posts its small singers outside the bank, the far side of the Works' voice, like tenants kept off by the noise of the party. The people stand at the Telltale wall and watch the water rise a finger's breadth, two, and rehearse the drowning calmly, with tea, and then the pumps run up and the drone reasserts itself and everyone exhales, and the cat — but the cat deserves its own paragraph.

**The law under the laws** belongs to everyone: any hand in the bowl, from the senior driver's to the rawest boy's, may lay hold of the stop-rope and bring the line down, and no one is ever blamed for a false alarm. The only unforgivable sound is the one you talked yourself out of hearing. They still tell of the apprentice, twelve years old, who stopped the yard on a note nobody else could catch and was right — a beater pin backed half out — and for a year afterward the old men paused and listened when he crossed the yard, the way you listen to a dog that has once found something. New apprentices are stood before the flood marks on the engine-house wall and told the story of the one silence nobody rehearsed, back before living memory: the gap in the ledger where the counter stopped, the three seasons it took to pump the bowl back down. No one alive has heard the bowl silent by accident, and the marks are kept so it stays that way.

The dead, in fact, hold most of the posts. Count the establishment honestly and the living are the current shift of a very long rota. The brasses are bright with a century of dead men's wiping; the treads are hollowed by dead men's boots; the flood marks are their handwriting and the nail column their minutes, and the listening rod the keeper presses to his jaw is polished at one end by the bone of men whose names are out of mind. When their own time comes they leave by the lode, on the board-barge, because that is the road and there is no other — you cannot bury in the bowl; a grave would be a pond by evening. The whistle is not blown for them. The Works does not stop for anyone; stopping is for the seventh day, and once, long ago, for the Flood. The crews stand capless by the tramway as the barge goes out under the Spout, and the beat goes on over their bowed heads, and no one has ever proposed it should be otherwise. The beat is the respect. The dead take the lode down to the river churchyard; the living stay and keep the water down. It is understood as a fair division of the work.

**The cat** answers to nothing. It has never heard its name, or the whistle, or the Works, and it is called, when it is called at all, by two stamps on the grating outside the cookhouse door, which it feels through the floor and honors or not, as cats do. It sleeps on the dynamo casing, in the spot that is warmest and loudest and, to the cat, stillest: for it the whole machine age is a warmth and a vibration, a permanent purr, and it purrs back. No one has ever heard it do so — the din takes it — but the night keeper rests a hand on its ribs on his round and feels the small engine going. The Hearing is the cat's bad day. It cannot hear the silence, but it feels the ground of its world go dead under it, and it paces the cooling casing and stands over the dynamo like a beekeeper over a hive gone still, and settles only when the pumps run up and the floor comes alive. For the cat, weekly, the world ends and is restored — calmly, with tea. And on Hearing mornings, when every other beat in the bowl stands down, the keeper's hand on the cat's ribs finds the one that continues. The pilot of the place, kept in a cat. It is no kind of mouser, and needs to be none: even the mice are marsh mice and keep outside the bank, as if the din itself were the cat.

**The heron** is an office, not an individual. There has always been one. Young herons drop in from the marsh, stand a day on the outfall apron, take the whistle once and are never seen again; the one that stays becomes the Heron, and the succession is never witnessed and never discussed. It has outlasted two keepers and is working on a third. Whether it is deaf or philosophical is a dispute now in its fourth decade, and the evidence is unchanged: it ignores the whistle — the only winged thing in earshot that does — and it fishes the outfall with the patience of the place itself, which the men on their breaks take as proof or as mockery, depending on the fishing.

**The eels** are the only immigrants the Works processes gladly. They arrive as elvers up the very pipes, glass bootlaces with the whole ocean behind them, and in their season the night keeper lifts the suction screens oftener and passes the small ones through by hand. They grow in the dark of the Sump on spillings and midges and time. At the Sump's cleaning, once in years, a big one is seen — thick as a forearm, older than the apprentices — before the dark closes over it again. In autumn the silver ones go down the way they came, and the keeper sees them sometimes in his lantern at the Spout, sliding over the outfall sill toward a sea no one here has seen, and the ledger says, without comment, *Eels gone down.* They are not eaten. What climbs in by itself is not eaten: that is the whole of the law on the matter, and it has never needed another clause.

**The swifts** pay no rent and are the only tenants whose comings and goings are minuted. The dead flue is kept standing and repointed for their sake — *flue, one, disused, in service* — and the children learn their date the way other children learn the fair's. Their scream is the one wild voice that rides over the din; it threads the beat like a shuttle. When they go in August the office enters it, and the bowl is a little more entirely the Works' own until spring. **The spiders** hold the rafters and pay their rent in midges; the webs up under the drying-shed roofs are rigging a bargeman would sign for. **The midges** are the bowl's one plenty, rising at dusk to the caged bulbs in soft storms; children are bitten and do not look up from their games. **The hens** of the cottage row, deaf in effect if not in fact, lay through the whistle with the composure of long service, and are by a wide margin the least impressionable residents of the bowl.

And **the kingfisher**. It came once, decades ago, and stayed an hour, and the clerk of that year — a man otherwise remembered for his stinginess with coal — gave it half a page. Children are shown the entry the way relics are shown. In a bowl of green and grey and black water it is remembered less as a bird than as an event of color, a blue with no business here, and old men still say *the kingfisher year* and everyone knows which one.

**The tree**, of course, is the senior member of the Shed Three crew. It is inspected on rounds: the keeper's lantern goes up into the leaves, the lead collar is checked and eased a ring at a time, and its girth is entered in the same hand that enters the boiler pressure. It is the only machine that feeds itself, the only one that runs on the leak, the only one permitted to shed — its leaf-fall chokes the gratings every autumn and is minuted and forgiven in the same breath. Men are apprenticed under it and retire under it, and they glance up at it before they speak for the shed, the way you glance at the oldest man at the table. When it dies it will go to the boilers, in service to the last, and come back as ash on the bank: the full tour of the establishment, completed by the only member who will never leave.

**The Engine's Garden** is tended by no one, by something stronger than rule. The fig against the boiler-house wall fruits most years, and by ancient custom the fig belongs to the night shift — eaten standing, warm, at two in the morning, by lantern light, with the heron for company if the heron is wakeful. On the bench by the steaming lagoon the retired men sit like plates warmed at the fire, silvered and sound, deaf as the cat, reading each other's lips, glancing up when something shifts in the beat — unable, after fifty years, to stop keeping the place even for an afternoon.

The establishment page is truer than the clerks know. Everything on it is in service, and the service is mutual: the people keep the Works, and the Works keeps the people — roof, larder, clock, climate, trade — and the line between keeper and kept is not where an outsider would draw it. Ask anyone in the bowl whether the place is alive and they will laugh at you. Ask again at the Hearing, when the bowl is still and the dials are creeping and the only heartbeat left is the cat's, and you will get a longer answer.

The night keeper does not think of his round as a muster, but that is what it is. The gauge house: dials steady — *present.* Shed Three: a hand laid to the bark in passing, damp ticking in the leaves — *present.* The apron: one slow eye over a grey shoulder, unbothered by lantern, whistle, or forty years — *present.* The screens: a nosing from below, once, twice — *present.* The dynamo casing: a heap of cat on the warm iron — *present.* The cottage row, dark windows, forty-odd heartbeats adding their small change to the counter — *present.* And last, at the engine house, the brass rod to the main bearing and the beat coming up into the bone of his jaw — steady, unhurried, older than everyone asleep in the bowl and everyone barged out of it: *Works, one — present.*

He writes nothing down. The round is the entry. It will be made again tomorrow, and the night after, and by another jaw in forty years — if continuing holds. And continuing is the whole technology.
