**The Kept**

Outsiders call them the Thaw-people. They call themselves the Kept — not poetry, not complaint; a statement of residence. The cold keeps everything here. The people are no exception, and they carry the fact the way a wall carries a roof.

They are built as the city is built: for keeping heat. Broad through the chest — the thin air has its arithmetic, and over four thousand years it has solved for lung — deep in the ribs, short in the limb, wide in the hand. Their faces are planed by wind, their cheeks seamed with the fine white lines of old frostbite, which the elders read the way they read lichen. In the bright season they wear goggles of slit bone, and a crowd of them looks the way the architecture feels: guarded, small-opened, warm inside. Their clothes are the colors of the herd and the ground — grey, grey-brown, the off-white of felt. Color is so scarce it is biographical. A red sleeve means a dowry-thread unpicked and re-sewn for three generations. A blue cord at the braid means trader-kin, Below.

They walk like people carrying something full. It is the first thing a visitor notices and the last thing he understands: the Roof pace, a gait that spends no breath and no heat, nothing wasted. Children learn it before they learn the lanes. Nothing on the Roof runs except water, and water only for an hour a day.

They are a hand-on-shoulder people. Words are expensive; hands are warm. Their laughter is silent by courtesy — a good joke is measured in shoulders, and you can tell one has been told by watching a lane at noon, the way you can tell the ground is about to boom by watching the dogs. The dogs always know first.

**The day, and the Hour**

The day is built the way the city is built: around water.

Before light, the scraping. Children go stone to stone along the lanes with bone scrapers and skin bags, taking the night's rime — a lane's stones are its orchard — while the ice windows are cleared and the fog-net gutters on the ridge are checked for their slow overnight drip. Then the chime: one stroke on the hanging slab at the cistern, the ice-skin broken, the note rolling off every wall in the city. It is the one thing allowed to be loud every day, and it is a stone.

The first water belongs to the dead. The scrapers carry a dipper up to the terraces and press a thumbprint of rime onto the lintels of the niches. It will sit there until noon, and then run slowly down the seal-stones like a long drink. The dead are served at dawn and take their water at the Hour, same as everyone.

Morning is for hard work in hard cold: stone, salt, dung, hauling. Nothing bends. Everything is honest and brittle.

Then the sun stands over the south-facing rock, and the Hour begins. For one hour the world is soft. Leather becomes truthful — it bends, it takes a stitch — and the cobblers haul their benches out into the lanes to catch it. The runnels are opened, and the black channels wake and talk. The cisterns are blessed. The gardens are watered by hand, a skin-bag at a time, each plant known. On the Long Back — the south wall of the old cistern-house, warm as an animal — the old people press their shoulders to the stone and take the sun like lichen, deliberately, not talking. Greetings are exchanged now; courtships advanced; business settled; quarrels brought out into the soft hour, because the Kept say *save it for the Hour*, and by the Hour most sharp things have thawed a little.

A child is allowed to waste one palmful of water at noon — to throw it, to watch it flash. This is how the price of things is taught: by being given, daily, a small something to spend.

The Hour ends the way a guest leaves: all at once, and the house feels it for a long time after. The threads of water stiffen into glass beards. Afternoon is for finishing; dusk for retrieval — everything is brought in, because the night keeps what it is lent, and it does not lend it back until the next Hour. Night is the close dark, the small miserly loved dung-fire, the tallow lamp, mending and cord-knotting, and the patient stew that has simmered since morning without ever once truly boiling. All night the walls click as the frost re-seats the stones. Children are told it is the house getting comfortable. They grow up unable to sleep in any room that is silent.

**The long work**

A house begins before the marriage does. When two people are betrothed, each family brings a stone — chosen, not cut — and the two are set together in the frost, and everyone comes to look at how they sit. *I have a stone for you* is a proposal. The corner-stones are planted at betrothal; the couple is married, by custom, at the third course, when the wall can stand in the wind on its own — which is also what is wished for the marriage.

A good house takes three generations, and everyone knows which stones were whose. The choosing is the signature. The choosers are the city's artists: they walk the scree fans reading the riven slabs for grain and temper, and a famous chooser's eye is trusted the way a voice is trusted Below. Children are set, as a game, to sorting cobbles into the polygon cells of the frost-nets, so the ground gives them their first masonry lessons; later they are taught the truth of it, that the city is copied from the ground, that the frost is the first architect, that all fitting is imitation.

Walls are dated by lichen — a crust a hand wide is a century — so every house wears its age outward, and a lane can be read like a page. The lichen also forgives. Given a century it covers the botched joint, the scorch, the initials someone cut in a foolish year. *The lichen forgives*, they say. *It is not fast.*

Doors are knee-high, shoulder-wide, with a dogleg behind — heat, they say, is a flock, and every door has its sheepdog turn. Inside: platforms, wool, the close warm dark. The warmest corner belongs to the fermentation bags, fish and leaf-mash working slowly at body-temperature; the second-warmest to the oldest sleeper. The young take the draft by the door and are proud of it.

**Keeping**

Waste is the crime here. Not theft — nothing stays stolen in a place where nothing is lost; everything stays findable, and the Rememberers keep the dung-map and the salt-counts and who owes whom a favor from forty winters back. But waste — a dropped scrap of meat, a blade left in a lane, rime unharvested on your own stones — waste is permanent, and is shamed accordingly. The first moral lesson a child receives is not about gods. It is: *your granddaughter will find it.*

Dung is counted like coin, and gathering-rights along the herd-routes are assigned by house and held like land. Salt is silver. Bone is lumber. Wool is gathered, not sheared: the herd leaves its underwool on the rubbing-stones along the routes, and a family's claim to certain stones is dowry and inheritance, recorded in knot-cord and remembered.

The tuber-yards at noon are the closest the city comes to a daily dance: lines of people, arms linked for balance, slow-stepping the frozen tubers on the frost-flat, treading the water out of them a day at a time until they are pale, light, everlasting. It is done, like everything, almost without sound.

And in the melt season the frost gives back what it has taken. They call it the Heave: the ground works all winter and pushes up what was dropped into it, and children walk the stone-nets reading the ground like a letter — a button, a needle, a toy herd of dung-chip graybacks, surfaced after forty winters. Findings go to the Rememberers, who read the house-marks and return them. Nothing on the Roof is ever found for the first time. It is only found again.

**The dead are neighbors**

The newly dead spend their drying season on the wind-racks behind the terraces, where the air does the work that rot does Below — cleaner, slower, more respectfully. Then they are seated in their niches with their tools, the seal-stone fitted and chinked with wool, facing the herd-routes, generation on generation.

The Kept sit with their dead the way people Below sit with their thoughts — which is to say, the dead are where thinking is done. Before a hard decision a person goes up and sits in the lee of a niche and lays it out, low-voiced. The dead do not answer quickly. Nothing here does. That is the point. At herd-week the terraces are crowded: the dead are told the count of the herd, the calves, the news, and it is felt to be good for them when the pass fills.

In the lower terraces sit the guest dead — lowlanders whose families paid in metal and hardwood to have them kept where keeping is possible. The Kept find this natural. Of course the dead should live where nothing leaves. The guest dead face the herd-routes too. It is felt to be good for them as well.

The seal-stones grow lichen in their turn; a hand's breadth of crust on a seal is a century of tenure, and the dead wear it like medals. To be forgotten here is impossible — which is not the same as being remembered. The Rememberers keep the difference, and keep it warm.

**The other census**

Ask a child of the Thaw who lives in the city and she will not stop at the people.

The herds first, which are named like rivers — no: rivers here are young and undecided; the herds are older and surer than rivers, and are named like mountains. The Long Grey. The Ten-Day. The herd is not owned; the herd is a weather that gives. At the pass the city takes its tithe, and the choosing is an ethics: the lame, the old, the ones the herd has finished with. *Do not spend the herd*, the children are taught. *It is not yours to spend.* In the melt season whole quarters follow the routes out, gathering dung and wool, sleeping in hide tents low as boulders; the city in summer is half a city, and likes it.

The gray cat pays its rent in information. When it is seen near the walls, the herd is coming, because the cat is the herd's shadow. To kill one is not exactly forbidden; it is simply not done, the way one does not put out a hearth. Its fur, taken only from the found-dead, is the warmest lining there is, and a mitten of it is an heirloom. When it screams, once, at dusk, the city does not startle. It notes.

The wolves have learned the local custom: they hunt mute. The Kept do not hate them. The wolves take the sick and keep the herds honest, and a winter with wolf-sign close to the routes is read as a healthy year.

The bonebreaker birds hold the oldest contract in the city. The people set out the long bones on the anvil-stones they do not themselves use; the birds drop them, take the marrow, and leave the bone cleaned for the tool-stock. It has never needed witnesses. The anvils are known by name, and a bird trying a new stone is news, discussed at the Long Back for days.

The snowfinches are the soft tenants: they pay no rent and are welcome anyway. Their mass settling into the eaves at dusk is one of the city's clocks, and a wall without finches in its joints is called cold-hearted, its household looked at sideways. In deep winter they roost in the warm breath of the walls, and the people count them among the insulation.

The marmot, the people say, speaks one word a year and spends the year choosing it. Hearing it is a personal letter from the season; you may say that you heard it, but never what it sounded like. The marmots keep to the far side of the dead-terraces. Out of delicacy, the people say.

The fish of the still ponds taste of nothing but slow. Caught through the ice with bone hooks, eaten frozen in shavings or fermented into the great feast-food of herd-week. One pond, by the terraces, is never fished. The old swimmers are left to their long thoughts.

The lichen is the first citizen. It is scraped only off one's own walls, by right; to harm a lichen older than yourself is, at minimum, bad manners. And the stones themselves are counted — the warm-blooded citizens, the hearths you can lean on. Certain old people greet certain stones by name. Nobody finds it odd.

**Sound**

The baseline of the world is silence, so every small sound is a sentence. An old woman can read the temperature off the squeak of her own boots. The ping of the White contracting at dusk is the salt-crews' clock. The clicking of the walls is the house talking in its sleep. The dogs are bred to whine; a dog that barked would go through the city like a fire. Children are not hushed so much as absorbed — the silence closes over a loud child like snow over a dropped stone, and she learns.

Distance has its own voices: a dozen thin whistle-signals that carry along the lanes and out to the routes. And in the deep season, when the drifts roof the lanes and the city moves through snow tunnels, the walls themselves carry the talk — tap-code, knuckle on stone, house to house through the fitted work. Stone carries a tap farther than air carries a word. Every evening the lanes go tick-tick, goodnight, goodnight, like the runnels freezing, and it is the same kind of sound: the city settling.

There is exactly one thing in the city allowed to be loud every day, and it is a stone. The chime's note is older than the lanes; children learn it before their own house-name. It has been rung at night only once in the long memory — for a crevasse accident, in the story — and the story is still told in a whisper, with a shiver, as if the telling might wake something.

Twice a year the lid comes off. Herd-week: drums are lawful, singing is lawful, and the herd itself supplies the bass, days of it, a sound like rain. The songs are built the way houses are built — a verse is a course of stone. Some songs are still under construction. A singer may work her whole life and add one verse, and the song is the better for it, and she is remembered in it the way a wall remembers its stones.

And a few times a winter the ground knocks — water freezing fast in the deep cracks — and the old people nod and rename the season by it. A winter of many knocks is a knocking year, and knocking years are remembered as the years the deep water moved house.

**Below, and strangers**

Everything the Roof cannot make comes up the pass in the melt season, on the backs of lowland porters breathing like bellows: metal — a bronze needle is a dowry; an iron blade has a name and a biography — hardwood, dyes, feathers, dried sweetness, which the children are given once at herd-week and dream about the rest of the year. Down go the salt slabs, the felt, the willow-work, the everlasting tuber-stones, and the obsidian: the Roof sells its sharpness Below and buys back softness.

The Kept do not go Below. Below is where things leave. They speak of rot the way other people speak of plague: a lowland condition, pitiable, faintly alarming. Lowlanders arrive carrying food that is already dying — soft fruit, flowers, meat going — and the Kept receive them gently, as one receives people bearing a misfortune they cannot see. In bargaining they are patient past all lowland patience, because the one who can wait wins, and the Kept can always wait. The lowlanders go home down the pass saying the Thaw-people are slow, and stingy of words. The Kept watch them go, and say nothing, and keep.

The only true exile is downhill. No one sentenced so has ever been heard of again — not because they died, but because Below, nothing keeps.

**Night**

Dusk takes back the Hour. The runnels knit themselves shut, lane by lane. The smoke lies down in its grey strata, and each house writes its name at its own height. Supper is white slices — meat cut frozen with the grain, thawed on the tongue, the one oven that is always lit — and patient stew, and the walls click, and the old go to the warm corners, and the young take the draft by the door, and the taps go goodnight through the stone.

Ask a Kept, in the dark, who lives here, and she will not say *we do*. She will say: we are kept here. And if you ask her who *we* are, she will start counting — and the count will go on past the living, past the seated dead, past the herds and the cat and the birds in the joints of the walls and the fish in their long thoughts and the lichen on the seals, out to the stones holding the day's warmth like a held breath.

And by the time she finishes, if she finishes, it will be nearly dawn: time to go and scrape the rime, and hear the one loud stone, and begin keeping again.

Nothing here is lost. It never stops being kept.
