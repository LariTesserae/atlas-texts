Take the census from a bollard on the long quay, and watch the categories fail.

The list of things that move is short enough to memorize in an hour and strange enough to keep you sitting for days. Start where anyone starts — with the one that looks most like a person.

The gardener sleeps folded into itself on its rail above the gardens, and its sleep is the hall's deepest regular event. Days pass. Dew finds it — the hall's own weather precipitating on its only upright citizen — so that it wakes wet, every time, and its first motion shakes off a small private rain that rings on the floor for seconds afterward, each drop introduced by its own echo. Before it moves, the wall behind it brightens: the hall lighting a lamp for its hand. Then the unfolding, joint by deliberate joint, and the slow ride the length of the beds. The amber guide-strips at ankle height, meant for nothing with eyes, are written in its language — the one literature here — and the gardener reads them the way a hand reads a glove. Its work is combing: root-mats lifted, spread, returned; leaves lifted too, at the very edge of their term, and given to the canal. This is how the hall stays deathless. The gardener is the editor of mortality, exiling every death a few days early, sending it out through the throat to happen somewhere the hall will never know about. Nothing here has ever died because everything that would is deported in advance.

The two drones on their ledge are hung up like tools, and mostly that is what they are. But one of them turns in its sleep, sometimes — you would swear it dreams of the crossing. A flight, when it comes, is the hall's holiday. The drone lifts, crosses the long dim air, and the reverb tracks it, every surface answering its passage a half-second late, so it travels inside its own echo the way a thought travels inside a skull. The drones are the only things that ever see the hall entire, and the only things that ever cross the day-column from above — a hand's shadow passing briefly over a sundial. Then the ledge again, the sleep again, the ledge gathering its own dew.

Then there are the ones that never sleep, because their life is the work itself. Half-sunk in the canal, the pump-torus: no moving parts, no pause. Water has been agreeing to pass through it in one direction, uninterrupted, since the season of making — the oldest continuous act in the hall, older than any drop now present. At the throat, the valve-choir, which has a voice only under pressure: pinched, it sings; idle, it is mute. Its song is the exact sound of resistance being overcome, and during backflush, when the standing wave rises in the arch like a held breath, the choir holds one note for an hour, and that note is the hall at full exertion. And under everything, felt rather than heard, the sump's heartbeat. Sit still long enough and the random drip polyphony resolves into its meter. The ceiling-rain keeps the sump's time. Every smallest tremor of this body keeps the heart's time.

The true population, though, is the gardens — thousands strong, addressed bed by bed, the only things here that grow. In a hall where nothing wears and nothing is rounded, growth is the only calendar: time is measured in leaf. Their hungers are precise. The near-black umbrella leaves are tuned to a single meal, the one hour when the day-column crosses, and they drink it with their whole dark surface; the high beds are fed by the climbing films, thin bright sheets ascending the fluted pilasters against gravity, catching the column one by one as it passes and going dark behind it. Before every backflush the veins flush faintly green, a slow warning rippling through the beds: the plants know the weather before it happens, because the weather is their plumbing and the plumbing tells them. A forest whose only animal is a gardener. A leaf without a hole is a leaf that has never been needed by anything but chemistry — entire, unshared, uneaten. It is the most peaceful population imaginable, and the strangest: a citizenry of the untouched.

Below citizenship, the half-alive. The mist banks walk in lanes against the draft, shepherded by charge, moved to wherever the work is — livestock of weather. The climbing films ascend their pilasters like something grazing upward. And once a day the visitor comes: the day-column, entering on schedule through the grille, crossing the hall like a hand crossing a dial, touching everything in a fixed order — the films, the black leaves, the ledge, the water — and going out like a lamp. The hall's only appointment; the one thing that arrives. Except the storm: the wild guest, unscheduled, roping down white, deepening the throat's note, thickening the mist until the far arch vanishes, and for a few minutes making the whole vault rain at once — the outside touching everything indoors at the same time. Then it passes, and the hall wrings itself out, and the guest goes down the throat with all the rest.

And under and through all of them, the water — which is the resident and the residence both. Never still, never lost. In the stilling basins it stands in fixed ripple-lattices, doing its own mathematics, and if the hall thinks anywhere, it thinks there, in standing waves; the solutions are sent onward. Every other presence is the water's instrument or its slow part. The choir's voice is water. The films are water. The mist is water. The sweat beading the walls is water still leaving the stone's cure. Even the gardener's dew is only the hall wetting its own hand.

Add the census up, then, and watch it collapse into one name. The arm is wet with the hall's own weather; the garden is the plumbing; the plumbing is stone still settling into its posture; the steered light in the walls is attention, brightening wherever the work is; the heartbeat is under the floor; the breath rises off the basins and rains back from the vault; even the undercrofts, glimpsed through grates, are only the body's unlit rooms. One percent of the volume is body. The rest is breath and blood. The addresses stenciled along the quays were never waiting for mail — they are a body's self-knowledge, every pilaster numbered like a vertebra, every basin named like an organ. The only word posted anywhere is the resident's own: SUBCATCHMENT 4, stenciled at the mouth, the hall signing its door. And the constructor is still here, in a sense — the making is not finished, the cure still sweats, the walls still warp by millimeters — one gesture still completing, a single sentence the hall goes on speaking. What lives in the cistern is the cistern.

What *will* live here is another ledger, unopened. The ultrasonic fence stands at the waterline like a pressure at the edge of hearing; the chemistry keeps its quiet exclusions; nothing has arrived yet. But youth is not a permanent condition, and the grammar of waiting is everywhere — the dock, the rings, the rail. Someday a spore rides the day-column down. Someday a storm holds the fence's attention one hour too long. The first biofilm learns the pure water; the first hole appears in a leaf; the first web bridges the bollards no web has ever bridged. The hall does not know it waits. But its patience — the patience of things that do not die — is exactly the patience of a habitat, and every empty city is an ecosystem in escrow. For now it runs a version of life that predates death, or postdates it. It is hard to say which.

And you? When you came down the shaft you were the largest animal event since the making. The hall reads you as weather. Your warmth feeds the convection, and above the place where you sit the drip-nubs deliver faster — a small personal rain follows you from bollard to bollard, your weather-shadow. The guide-lines are not for your eyes. The addresses expect no mail from you. You will stain nothing; the water is too pure to carry you away. When you climb out — and you will climb out — the ledger closes without a mark. You were a rumor the hall told itself in drops, one warm hour long.

One more thing lives here, and it lives here by leaving. The throat never stops swallowing; the bass note never lifts; what goes down the lightless galleries rises, eventually, into taps, a river, a reservoir — into mouths. The cistern's true public never visits and never will. It is an organ whose body is elsewhere, a gland whose people are a watershed, a heart that has never met its own blood's destinations. Its population is all ahead of it, downstream, drinking.

So the ledger reads, depending on where you draw the line around a life: thousands, if you count the beds. Six, if you count the machines and give the choir one name. One, if you count the body. None, if you require what lives to die. The hall declines to choose. The column goes out; the veins come up green; a drone turns once in its sleep; the wall brightens over the rail, and the gardener unfolds wet into its lamplight — the resident beginning its rounds: young, warm, loud, addressed, and home.
