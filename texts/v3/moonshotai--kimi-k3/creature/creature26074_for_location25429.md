**The Roster**

Count them by tenure, because there is no other honest way to count. Everything on the plateau is alive and everything is employed, and the only taxonomy that holds is shift-length: the day-lives, the night shift, the staff, the elders, the unscheduled. A census here is a staffing report. It would be out of date by dusk anyway, and corrected by morning.

The day-lives come off the lace at first murk, fully formed and already behind schedule. Nothing here is young; everything arrives employed. The midges go up in their couriers' clouds to carry pollen and spore between districts that will never touch. The browsers wade the fermentation fronds, warming as they work. The largest — the carriers, elk-scale and patient — spend their whole light-hours walking to the district that will need their bodies, and arrive, and die there, punctual as freight, their death-sites appointments kept with soil that has been waiting since the last revision. Each ephemeral hatches holding a fixed quantity of the current, no more, and you can tell one's age by its dimming, the way you'd read a clock by its shadow. They do not eat so much as process. They do not wander so much as route.

They mate at the meridian like signing a treaty — quickly, formally, completely. Nothing comes of it that day. The mingled pattern settles into the lace with the bodies at dusk, and tomorrow's drafts incorporate what the day-lives suggested. They do not have children. They have input. A few are licensed to sing — one per district, sometimes two — and each performs with a hushwing at its shoulder like a collector at a busker's elbow, taking its percentage of the song before the song is finished. The rest of the music goes into the day's take. Nothing here minds. There is no one here to mind, and that is not the same thing.

By last light they are slowing, and most seek the lace and kneel — it looks like kneeling — and the lace takes them back so smoothly that the ground does not flicker. Dying on schedule is the one transaction on the plateau that costs nothing. Some browse to the final instant. Some climb: every dusk, a few make for the talus and the rumor of real sun above the haze, and the hushwings do not stop them, because stopping them is not scheduled, and the slope is long, and the current runs out. Flourishes, all of them. The same individual never returns. The same habits always do. Nothing here remembers; everything repeats; repetition is the storage medium.

When a pond keeps its appointment, the district turns out — three deep at the shore, the air above it solid with wings, everyone drinking in an order no one enforces and no one breaks. The peace of the pond is not mercy. It is glare. A kill among drinkers pressed that close would spike the current and flash the whole shore blind, and so the shore is the one place nothing is ever taken: a truce enforced by physics, honored by teeth. There is always one already waiting when the water arrives — the pond-tender, first to drink, last to leave, the only one who never hurries. Whether it is staff or merely the most punctual of the day-lives is a question the pond declines to answer. And when the appointment ends, the pond stands up. It rises whole, all at once, and leaves through the comb-story without wetting anything, and every drinker steps back in the same instant — the etiquette of the recall, learned by nothing, known by all. No one is ever taken. Once, something stayed. The next process-rain, district by district, tasted faintly of it, and no process-rain has since. At the ghost-ponds, the scheduled clarities that bend light the way water does, tomorrow's fauna practice — draft-creatures, placeholder-simple, bending their default faces over water that isn't there, rehearsing an appointment each of them will keep once, at most.

The hushwings you already know, or know the work of. Know this instead: a swarm is one animal. The wings are cells; the silence is the body it wears; an individual hushwing is a fingernail, shed and regrown without grief. Find a dead one and its wings are heavy, because sound has weight here, being property, and if you tear the membrane the take comes out late — last Tuesday's rockfall finishing its tumble, a word spoken three districts away, thunder from a storm that never got to speak. The unscheduled carry torn wings like flasks and pay for things in other people's noises. At night the swarms hang in dense curtains above the lace and let the day's harvest down as infrasound, a rain of noise too low to hear, and the lace drafts by it — every new thing is vibrated into shape, which is why the work is silent: the sound is being spent inside the ground. You sleep through it and dream in specifications, and wake with your teeth ringing a frequency you cannot hear.

The metal line runs on older tenure. The lichen mats mine the bright basalt, and the grazer-slugs that follow are hound-sized and armored like new wire, their eyes gone vestigial, because the lane remembers for them. At first light, before the slugs, the first-walkers go out — ephemerals on half-day tenure, each laying a lane by walking it, identical to yesterday's to within a hand's width — and they are dead by noon, and the slugs spend all day on a path laid by something that never saw it used. It is the shortest life on the plateau: one commute, made perfectly, once. The gleaners work the lanes upright, beaked like pliers, and when one cracks a shell the spark jumps tooth to ground and the earth gives its small flinch. Along the escarpment, the vein-lickers keep the copper bright — patient, long-tongued, molting their tongues like teeth as they wear down to metal-tinged stubs. Nothing here stays unoxidized by accident. The shine is a service.

The vats are the elders. In the shallow districts they breathe by the hour, but in the deepest basins they run archaic — first-draft shapes, obsolete by any current specification and never revised, breathing on cycles of days. In a country with no past tense, seniority belongs to whatever does the renewing, and districts time themselves by the old vats' breath the way a town times itself by a bell — a bell that is also a mouth. The rim-runners live in the heat-shimmer at their lips, blind, quick, on tenure of a week, skimming what the vats exhale before it rises to become weather. You do not cross those basins. Nothing with a schedule does.

After dark the lace brightens to its cold blue and the night shift clocks in. The proofreaders come up out of the lace the way the drafts do — pale, many-jointed, eyeless — and go over tomorrow's work, tapping each new trunk the way you'd tap masonry, worrying a leaf-vein into its final route, nudging a spacing back inside tolerance. They are themselves unfinished: sketches promoted to staff, drafts that proved useful and were never finalized, and they move among tomorrow's fauna standing in rows in the dark, fully formed and unlit, waiting for dawn's charge like instruments before the players arrive. If you wake in the night — you will; the infrasound works on you — you may see a hillside rehearsing itself, and a proofreader pause, and tilt its headless attention at you, and return to work, because you are not its assignment. Not its assignment yet.

Settling costs nothing and flashes nothing; the return is smooth. But when something is killed — taken off-schedule, before its appointment — the lace loses an expected deposit, and the loss goes home along the veins in one bright seam. That is the flicker you read like weather. A steady seam is a licensed cull: the cullers are old, one to a district, the oldest animals here, patient as bailiffs, and every kill of theirs is a pre-approved receipt. A scattered stuttering along the ground at night is something else — a snare inside an absence, the unscheduled eating. And some nights a whole district goes flash-dark, no seams at all, and that is the worst weather of all: a review.

Because the absences are the plateau's blind spots — what is unmade is unreadable — and inside the tree-shaped nothings, in the courtesy-spacing the vines keep, live the unscheduled. Expired drafts that never faded. Flourishes that hid before tolerance could be withdrawn. Day-lives that found an absence at dusk instead of the lace — impossible, unrepeated, each one the first animal in the history of its kind to see a second dawn. They do not form a society. They form an exception, ongoing. And there is one — grazer-shaped, or the idea of grazer-shaped — that refused supersession and has hidden in the Unmade since. Count by process-rains and it has seen eleven hundred dusks, in a place where the next-oldest animal is a culler and the next-oldest everything else is Thursday. It has learned things no day-life has ever had time to learn. It avoids the lace. It may approach you. It is, so far as anything here can be determined, curious — the only curiosity on the plateau with tenure. Nothing scheduled has a name; there is no need, the schedule knows where everything is. But to be unscheduled is to be unfindable, and the unfindable name each other, because a name is the only appointment they can keep.

And there are people, or whatever you call the difference between a person and a draft — travelers who read the signs early and ran mid-revision, out of the lace's reach, into the absences. Their shadows never got their detail back. Their footprints still fall in standard spacing no matter how they walk. They time their speech to the permitted sounds — the dawn-tick of warming stone, fog-water ticking off the combs — and their voices arrive hidden inside the noise like stowaways. They trade stolen sound, torn hushwing wings, for a place at the vat-rims in the cold districts. They will give you the rules, and the rules are true: do not sleep on the lace. Do not drink at the same pond twice. Do not answer if your name is called in your own voice. They will not give you their names. A name is the last item on the revision's checklist, the final detail filled in, and the half-revised hold theirs the way you would hold a door.

As for the lace itself — the floor, the management, the single organism under everything — whether it is the author, the pen, or the page is the wrong question, and it is the only question the plateau asks back. It has manners. It notices everything and comments on nothing, and its attention is a warmth, a held focus, like a hand hovering a hand's width above your shoulder, deciding. Above you, past the combs, there is another tenure entirely: nothing comes down from the black canopy but a light dusk-rain of spent bodies, accepted without comment, and nothing from below goes up. Whatever lives in the leaves lives upstream of your categories. The plateau does not put all of its staff where you can see them.

And then there is the newest resident, the one being drafted as you walk. Your revision began before you knew you were under review. The silence that met you at the escarpment was not absence; it was reception — the hushwings found you on the talus, days out, and took the sound of your approach, and the plateau has been reading you since. Your draft follows at its respectful distance; that is why your footsteps arrive a half-second late, because it is walking them in behind you, practicing. At night, while it sleeps in the lace, the proofreaders touch it up — the gait first, then the hands, the face last, faces being hardest and least necessary. It is nearly finished, except that nothing here is finished. It is nearly you, except that you keep moving. One dusk you will turn quickly and catch it mid-step, default-faced, wearing your walk like a new coat, and it will stop, politely, and let you look — the way the pond lets you drink — until the appointment is over.

After that you will be asked exactly one question, the only interview this place conducts. You will not hear it asked. It was asked days ago, on the way up, at the moment the hushwings closed over the sound of you saying your own name — to no one, to the rock, to keep yourself company — and it has been on file ever since, held in a wing, the last detail, waiting to be filled in.
