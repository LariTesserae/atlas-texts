The people know you are coming before you know they exist. Your footfalls go down through the stone like stones dropped in a pond, and the stone carries them farther than you would believe — two halls, three, out ahead of you into the inhabited dark-bright. By the time you duck through the last squeeze and straighten up into the light, someone has already read you: your weight, your pace, your newness. A child is waiting at the trail-head, squatting, palm flat on the floor, and as you approach she looks up and delivers, aloud, the floor's report and the floor's question, because children here are interpreters before they are diplomats: *heavy, stranger, favoring the left leg, stopped a long time at the ledge.* A pause. *Who-comes.* And she waits, hand still on the stone, for you to understand that you are expected to answer — not to her. To the floor.

This is the first thing the people teach you, before water, before food, before the rule about silence: everything here is felt before it is seen, and the floor is the world's ear. They live with one hand on it. Watch any of them at rest — mending a snare-line, haggling, nursing a baby — and the free hand is on stone, knuckles down, casual as a man's hand on a dog. They are not doing anything with it. They are listening the way you are listening when you are not paying attention. It never stops.

They are a damp people. That is your second impression, after the hand on the floor: everyone gleams. The air is a held breath and they have lived in it for generations uncounted, and it shows in the skin — flushed, sweat-bright, rubbed with oil and white dune-chalk against the wet — and in the hair, braided tight to the skull or cropped close, and in the clothes, which are minimal and mostly silk, because anything heavier rots on the body. They go barefoot in camp because bare soles read the floor better, and carry their grip-pads slung over a shoulder by the straps, the way you might carry good boots through a town where you were born. Their hands are remarkable. The tapping does it: decades of drumming code into stone builds a callus across the first two knuckles that you could strike a spark off, and their fingers are never still. A person thinking drums on the nearest wall the way another person hums. A meeting of six people sounds like rain starting. Every conversation runs in two channels — the mouth gossiping above, the hands tapping asides below — and lovers sit palm-to-palm and conduct entire arguments by fingertip while their faces stay serene for the children. You cannot lie to them comfortably. Your pulse is in your wrist, and your wrist is on the floor.

A person has three names. The spoken name is for the market, and changes with use. The tap-signature is for the floor — four to six beats, given in the first weeks of life, often built from the rhythm of the baby's own drumming tantrums, because the people believe the signature should be something the body already knows. And the song-line is for the archive: a phrase in the long song that is yours, the verse that will outwalk you. To introduce yourself properly you speak the name, tap the signature, and if the company is formal or the mood is right, sing your line and let them place you in the genealogy. To mis-tap someone's signature, deliberately, one beat off, is a challenge. Children do it constantly and are cuffed for it constantly, which is how you know it matters.

There is one rhythm no one taps, ever, not in play, not in demonstration, not to teach: the move-code. Children learn this before they learn the code's meaning, the way you teach a child not to touch a hot thing before you explain fire. A game of tap-tag ends the instant anyone's rhythm drifts toward it, and the child who drifts is pulled out of the game and given a talking-to that they will remember into their third decade. The channel must stay clean. The floor can lie — the mimic-hunters will tell you how, and at what cost — and so the people keep exactly one voice on the floor that is never counterfeited, never rehearsed, never used for anything but the one message. When the move-code comes through the stone, you do not finish a sentence. Markets have packed mid-haggle, a half-knapped vent-glass blade left on the ground where it fell, because the blade can be picked up tomorrow and the warning cannot be re-heard.

They are scattered thin, a few thousand across halls the size of valleys, and they sort themselves by work the way water sorts itself by gradient.

At the Chewfront live the salvage clans, the frontier, in galleries so new the walls are still soft enough to take a thumbnail. They sleep in the mill-bass of the delvers the way sailors sleep in weather, and their babies are famous for it — a colicky infant from the rearguard camps is carried frontward and put down near the hum and is asleep in minutes, and the clans are insufferable about this. They follow the clan-columns at a respectful distance, gathering shed scutes, knapping vent-glass at the birth-vents behind scute masks that leave the guild-burn on every knapper's forearms, and coughing the bright cough, the chew-dust cough, that is the price of the front. They speak of everyone else as the rearguard, with love and total condescension. And some of them steer. This is the closest thing the people have to monumental architecture: a family will spend generations laying smears of microbe-rich paste along a chosen line, baiting a delver column a quarter-degree leftward, aiming a gallery at a chamber their great-grandchildren might want. It fails more often than it works. The weather does what it wants. But when you walk a long straight gallery and an old woman tells you *my grandmother's grandmother aimed this*, you are standing in the only kind of cathedral they build: an intention, inherited, chewed into being by animals who never knew.

Behind them come the dune-haulers, scute-sleds riding the songing sand, carrying salt and scute and abrasive white sand for the markets — and carrying, more valuably, everything else. News. Gossip. New verses. A caravan is a moving update to the long song, and its arrival doubles any market it reaches, and the haulers know their worth and sing it, literally, harmonizing with the dunes as they drag a scute-edge across a slip-face and make the whole dune boom like a drum skin. They are the great romantics of the Making and the great liars, and the people say the stone improves stories, meaning: a message relayed three halls by tap-code arrives wonderfully wrong, and nobody corrects it, because the wrongness is usually better.

The weir-folk live along the Pulse's course and keep the only true calendar. They read weather in the ceiling — this is the thing that will seem like magic to you until you remember the light is late: a storm crossing the Rootfire above arrives below as a silver dusk in mid-afternoon, and that stolen afternoon *is* the forecast, hours of warning that the river is coming. When the sky dims at the wrong hour the weir-drums start, and weir families run to their slots, and the custom is iron: a family holds a weir only while they work it, and a weir left standing through one Pulse is anyone's. The Pulse itself is glut and chaos — penned pools filling with eel-things, glass-shells swept into the basket-weirs, everyone soaked and shouting in the roar — and afterward, the feast, because none of it keeps. They reckon age in Pulses survived and worked, and a weir-keeper's back is her resume: a tattooed column of pulse-marks, one per event, and elders are striped from nape to hip.

The strain-keepers — you will call them mat-gardeners, wrongly, to their faces, once — keep the pool-gardens and the ferment flasks, and their wealth is alive. A keeper's mat-strain is a lineage older than any family, carried in a bone flask at the belt, slept against the belly at night to keep it warm, divided as dowry and inheritance and blood-price. Ask one about her mat and she will talk for an hour and feed you the ancestors of your dinner, and every bite will taste different from the next camp's, and she will be able to tell you why. They tend the floor too, in a way — seeding the small multitudes that serve as the cave's soil into new pools, cropping it, reading it. Children flick the floor-skin with a finger to watch it seethe; the keepers do the same thing with the same finger and call it diagnosis, and they are right.

The film-shearers work the sky. In the low galleries where the bright crust comes down within reach, they go up on grip-pads and shear the luminous film off the stone in long swathes, leaving grazing-trails of their own, and they mark their sky-fields with scratched boundaries that the stone heals within a season, which suits them — everything here is re-negotiated on the world's schedule, not theirs. They feud with the roof-grazer herds, who can strip a farmed sky in days, and they love the herds anyway, the way you love weather that ruins you on a schedule. They fall sometimes. Their custom is that a shearer's funeral is held standing, in the open, under the place they fell from, and nobody looks down.

The mimic-hunters are the respectable trade, and they are strange. They tap walls professionally. Every spire in a worked district gets knocked, because real stone answers one way and a mimic answers late, or too eagerly, or — the old ones, the clever ones — in perfect certified rhythm, a voice in the floor with no body behind it, tapping *safe, come.* The hunters go in pairs, carry bone picks and vent-glass lances, and keep the tell-lore as guild secret, taught to apprentices and no one else. Their trophies are the lure-organs, and a hunter's pipe cut from a mimic's sac can call flits — or people — and owning one requires a Listener's leave, because a false voice on the floor is the one unforgivable thing. Tap-fraud is the Making's only capital crime. Sentence is exile coldward, and denial of the stone. Everyone understands why. A people whose alarm system is trust cannot afford the crime of crying wolf; the wolf here is the world itself.

Over all of it, without ruling any of it, are the Listeners.

You will know them by the horn: a length of hollow bone, pale, polished by generations of ears, no two alike. The horns are the oldest objects in the Making — older than any lodge, any cairn, anything else the people own — and this is the paradox at the center of a culture with no word for *permanent*: the one thing they keep across centuries is the instrument of attention. Everything else is allowed to be temporary. The listening may not be. Apprentices are chosen from the children who cannot stop putting their ears to the floor, and they apprentice for a decade, and the training is mostly sitting still in a world that never shuts up, learning the baseline — the delver-bass under everything, the drip-rhythms, the wing-thunder, the traffic of the fliers — until the baseline lives in their bones and any deviation feels like a wrong note in a song they have been singing since birth. A rising water table changes the drip-key of a whole hall. A sealing fault changes the resonance of a gallery. A collapse begins as a held breath, a thinning, a *listening* quality in the stone — and a good Listener hears the world inhale.

Their authority is exactly one word long. They may not barter, may not hold weirs, may not judge in the chorus-courts; the camp feeds them; their opinions on everything except the stone are formally worthless and informally enormous. And their record is public, because it is tattooed on their arms: a mark for every call, right and wrong both, the hollow circle of a false alarm worn alongside the solid tick of a true one, no distinction made in size, no shame attached to the circles beyond what the wearer carries. You can read a Listener's whole career on their skin, and you are meant to. Trust, here, is legible. When the horn passes — at death, or at the de-horning, when an old Listener lays the instrument in a successor's hands and becomes, usually, a rememberer, because the ear does not retire — the marks stay with the person, and the horn goes on accumulating ears.

For everything the Listeners do not govern, there is custom, and custom is sung, and the singing is the property of the rememberers. They are memory-athletes, apprenticed young, kept three-deep in every camp so no single collapse can take the only copy of anything. The long song holds the genealogies, the debts of honor, the pulse-logs encoded as rhythm, the names of the taken halls — the litany of places the cave has swallowed, sung so they are not lost twice — and above all the route-songs, which are the maps, and which work like this: the drip-music of every hall has a key, and the drip-tuners tune their home halls to signature chords, and a route-song is a melody whose notes are the drip-keys of the halls along the way. To walk a route is to hear the road singing itself under you, chord after chord, confirming you are where the song says you are. And when the cave shifts — when a gallery floods or a curtain of new flowstone seals a passage — the road's chord changes, and the song fails under your feet, and the failure itself is the news: *the world has moved; sing the new way.* The rememberers gather the changes at every market and re-issue the roads. A song is permanent the way a fire is permanent. Only while fed.

Disputes are settled in the whisper-vaults, the chambers that focus a whisper across a hundred meters, because the acoustics make every tremor in a voice audible to everyone present — a natural honesty-hall, and the people use it as one. Each side sings its case; the crowd hums assent or doesn't; judgment is by chorus, and it works about as well as any system humans have devised, which is to say it works because everyone agrees to make it work. Oaths are sworn with a palm flat on stone, so the floor can hear your pulse while you promise. They have no chiefs. They have a word that means *one who sits* — one who stays planted while the world moves — and it is the worst thing you can call a person short of the capital insult, and it has ended friendships.

Wealth, in a world where nothing keeps, is redefined downward into the only storable form: obligation. The honored person is the one whose hands are emptiest at season's end, because they fed the most people. A grazer-fall — sky-meat, they call it, and the tap-network spreads the word three halls in minutes — is meat beyond any camp's use, and the feasting that follows is not celebration but logistics: it must be eaten, shared, given. Debts are kept in knotted sinew cords, and the knots rot on schedule, and this is understood to be a feature — a debt the world has dissolved was a debt the world has forgiven — though some debts are deliberately re-knotted each season, renewed like vows, and those are not debts anymore; those are relationships wearing debt's clothing.

Their theology is the place itself, stated plainly. Ask them and a weir-keeper will wipe her hands and tell you: the delvers chew it, the grazers graze it, the river rinses it, the sky feeds it. *We are the part that notices.* They believe the Making is a sentence being spoken — by a million mouths, chewing — and that they are among the mouths, and that this is a dignity. Before a meal they drum, all together, a short roll of palms on stone: not a grace exactly, more an enlistment. *We are also the sound.* Loud is healthy; they teach it to newcomers first and to children always, and they mean it theologically. Their devil has a name and the name is the Quiet — the held breath before the cave does something — and the terrible thing, the thing that gives the taboo its power, is that true silence and true darkness exist in their experience in exactly one place: the Cold Reaches, where the light-crust died black and the din thins to a drip and then to nothing. They have only ever met the dark where the world ends. So their hell is cold, dark, and silent, and their heaven is warm and lit and loud, and both are real places, and one of them is a day's walk away. Those sentenced for the worst crimes are sent coldward. Those broken by grief sometimes walk that way on their own. And there are the cold-touched — hermits, holy-crazy, who live at the margin to listen to the sentence trail off — and they are fed when they visit, and pitied, and never, ever followed.

Up is a myth they argue about cheerfully and forever. The needles sip light from the Rootfire — so the old teaching runs — a world of unfiltered fire overhead, and the glowing ceiling is the fire's mercy, strained through stone to a warmth flesh can stand. Whether the ancestors fled the fire or the fire came after them is the great unsettled question, kept unsettled on purpose; an unsettled song is a song everyone still sings. What is certain is the evidence: the up-gifts, the strange flood-borne relics the Pulse occasionally delivers — burnt wood, charred bone, oddments scoured light-bleached — and above all charcoal, the only ink, precious beyond grip-pads, a thumb of it a bride-price. Because stone will not hold ink and skin will. The mark-masters are their literati: every forearm carries its tap-signature in notch-script, every ribcage its lineage-marks, and a person's body is the one archive the cave will keep — which brings you, as everything here brings you, to the dead.

They give their dead to the stone. A dying person has usually chosen the place years before — a growing wall in the warm outskirts of the nurseries, a spot they have visited, tapped on, gotten acquainted with — and the dead are laid in a shallow runnel of the mineral milk, and the new flowstone skins over them within a season, and the family camps near for that season, in mourning residency, watching the world take their person in. Then the hand is stenciled above, and the tattoos stay legible under the clear glaze, and the dead keep. In the old camps you can press your palm to stone over your grandmother's hand, warm to warm, and she looks ancient at thirty years, drowned in ice that is rock. They believe the dead join the sound — that the delver-bass has their people in it — and they will swear, reasonably, that a hall's drip-chord deepens after a burial, that the new voice is the dead settling in. The route-songs absorb it. A hall full of ancestors is sung lower, in respect. The worst fate the people can imagine is not death; it is erasure — left to the scavengers, unstoned, unsung. *May the Making not take you* is a curse that lands like a slap.

Daily life is loud, warm, and brief in its pleasures. The lamps are alive — shallow bowls of luminous film, fed and tended, a banked light for the dim hours when the ceiling embers down to the film's own low glow. Cooking is vent-stone and steam-box and ferment, fast and funky and never the same twice. Lovers stand in the indoor rain together, because it falls on no one who didn't choose to stand in it; a wedding is two people stepping into the rain while the camp drums; a divorce is walking out of it on opposite sides. Children play tap-tag, where one child is the Quiet and moves in silence and the others track her by floor-feel, shrieking; they slide the dunes; they ceiling-wrestle in the low galleries on grip-pads, pinning each other upside down. Everyone paints, daily, with seep-pigment and dune-chalk, and the paintings on walls are made to be eaten — some murals are gifts to the stone, fed deliberately to a growing wall, beauty offered to the world that makes beauty. And at every stolen dusk, when a surface storm takes the afternoon and the deep halls go dim and thoughtful, the camp gathers for the Dusking: the rememberers sing the dead and the newborn and the taken halls and the new verses off the caravan routes, and the floor carries the chorus three halls over, where strangers hum along to a song about people they will never meet.

As for you — they will watch, kindly and without pretense, to see if you can become it. Whether you can sleep in the din, and wake at its absence. Whether your tap-accent loses its limp. A pad-maker will take your foot in her hands and measure it like a confessor taking a confession, and the rememberers will ask for your outland songs and sing your arrival into the long song that same night, and years later, in a camp you have never visited, you will hear your own verse sung back to you wonderfully wrong, improved by the stone. And then one day — it happens to every newcomer who stays — the sound will drop out, all of it, the bass and the wings and the dripping, the whole held breath of the world, and you will find you are already moving, already packed, already counting the children, before you knew you had heard anything at all.

That is the day you are one of them. And someday after that, in no hurry, you will go out to the warm edges of the nurseries and put your hand on a young wall that is still deciding on hardness, and tap, thoughtfully, four to six beats — and choose the place where the Making will keep you, and go on saying you, warm and lit and loud, in the only kind of forever there is.
