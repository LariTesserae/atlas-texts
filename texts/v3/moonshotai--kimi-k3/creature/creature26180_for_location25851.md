Begin with who is not here: children.

There are none. No calves trail the leviathans. No fry cloud the crown-pools. No apprentice follows a groomer's tooth-lines, learning the pattern. The smallest fish in the sea — a silver thing the length of your thumbnail, holding station in the spillway's down-rush — is exactly as old as the tower it hangs beside. Size here is an occupation, not an age. There is no way to look at anything alive in the Held Sea and guess how long it has been alive, because the answer is always the same answer: *as long as anyone remembers, which is as long as there has been anything to remember.*

Understand what this does to a population. It is not a crowd. It is a staff. Everyone is employed, everyone is fully trained, everyone is known. The last lesson was taught before the rain learned its rate. What remains is the work, and the work is the world continuing.

## The towers, who are the eldest

The towers are usually described as the place. They are also the oldest residents of it.

Each one has a name, and the name is its note. You do not speak a tower's name; you sound it — hum it at the fundamental the tower holds, and hold it until your sternum finds the pitch. Do this within the tower's field and something answers: the nearest arc-gland steadies its lightning, very slightly, the way an eye finds your face across a room. Whether this is attention, no one checks. It has always steadied. It would not be like the world to leave a courtesy unreciprocated for ten thousand years.

The towers' moods are read in pitch-drift across centuries — a sharpening you could never hear in one lifetime, except that no one here has one lifetime. The Rememberers hum the names on their rounds and come home with the whole report, which is always the same report: *the towers are well.* It takes years to gather. It is never news.

Whether the towers, phase-locked across a continent, each electrified like a brain cell the size of a weather system, amount to a single mind thinking one long thought in a chord that never resolves — this is not asked. Ask instead what any living thing here is for, and you get the answer you get for everything: itself, and the lift.

## The staff of the engine

Go inside, where the lightning lives in jars of gland and the water climbs the shaft in waves of muscle.

The **conduit-eels** are the wiring that chose to be alive. Some are the length of your arm; the seniors, who work the galleries nearest the throat where the current is squeezed, are ten meters and thick as a mast. They ride the peristaltic lift the way you would ride a current — commuters on an escalator of muscle — and dock at the electrocyte stacks to drink charge when they ebb, drifting half-discharged in the return-flow, dreaming in ohms. Their speech is a texture on the current: ripples laid over the tower's rhythm, readable at a distance by anything sufficiently charged. They know one another by the grain of it. Pairs travel the shaft together for millennia, discharging in synchrony, and their courtship is not remembered because it was completed so long ago; what you see is the settled phase, which does not end.

The **valve-keepers** are the pale-handed ones, and they are the closest thing this world has to priests. Six-fingered, translucent, eye-faint — their eyes are like spent moons, good enough for arc-light — they taste with their fingertips and hear through their bones, and each is phase-locked to its own valve. You can tell a keeper's post by its small movements: everything it does ticks in its valve's time. A keeper knows its valve the way you know a friend's step on the stair. When two keepers rotate posts — which happens across centuries, for reasons of wear and nothing else — their conversation is, for the first year, an interference pattern, and then it resolves.

The most dramatic event in a valve-keeper's life is a change in the clack. The correct response is to lean in and listen to it for a year, and then move one fiber. This is the world at its most eventful. The keepers are equal to it.

The **groomer-fish** work the trunk's skin in crews whose composition has been fixed forever, cropping the symbiont crust to its licensed pattern — and the license is not a rule but a structure, because the crust is load-bearing and the pattern is the engineering. Their teeth are engraving tools. Their elders carry the pattern of their sections as scar-maps on their own bodies, blueprints that swim. There are no apprentices; there is nothing left to teach that is not already known by the one who would be taught.

And in the arc-chambers, where the sustained lightnings stand like small white trees: the **arc-wrens** — the only fast lives in the world. Everything else here is slow, but the wrens live at flicker-tempo, darting between the bolts, sipping stray charge, their bodies flashing in sympathy. They are the margin of error made flesh: inside the engine, the leaf-rule holds, and it is the wrens who intercept every falling scale and spall before it lands. A blur of a billion heartbeats, and every one of them old. Quickness was never youth. It is just their post.

Lesser staff, named in the census and no less permanent: the bearing-snails in the axle-galleries of the turbine-hearts, keeping the laminated wood greased with what their bodies make; the lip-keepers at the intake mouths, riding the endless inhale and picking the grilles; and in the electrified halo around every trunk, the **metered** — small silver fish whose whole lives run slaved to the tower's pulse, schooling and flashing in unison: the tower's heartbeat made visible. The Rememberers read the towers' health off the metered the way you would read a gauge, and if one of the metered ever fell out of step it would be news. None ever has. A Rememberer who is agitated — and agitation is a memory here, not a condition — may hold one of the metered to the chest until the borrowed rhythm takes. It always takes.

## The crowns, in the rain

The crown spreads a kilometer wide into the rain that has always been falling, and the rain pools in the branch-crotches: hanging lakes, an archipelago of fresh water held above the sea. Every drop in a crown-pool has been off the edge of the world and come back. The **sky-pond fish** — small, quick, silver, provincial — have never once touched the sea, and they are swimming in the world's entire biography, and they find no news in it.

Rain-herons stalk the pools with a patience indistinguishable from the light's. Mosses grade the bark by wetness. And at the drip-line, where the crown's edge sheds, the **fall-watchers** hold their ancient station and intercept every dropped leaf before it reaches the sea — nothing arrives unprocessed, nothing reaches the floor that has not been accounted for. The loop is sealed by employees.

And here, at the crowns, live the people.

Call them the **Rememberers**; names here are jobs. They are not many. They are exactly enough — the world's budget allows no other quantity. They are pale from the green light, cold-adapted past the point where the five-degree water reads as cold, and their pulses run slow even outside the fields, as if entrainment, given enough millennia, simply became temperament. Their shelters are grown, not made: living reed trained into lattice over generations of weather that never varied, so that the oldest shelters and the newest are identical and neither word means anything. They own nothing that was built, because nothing was ever built. A Rememberer's tools are hands, a voice, and a memory, and the memory is the load-bearing one.

Their work is the census. It takes years to sing: every tower's name sounded at its pitch, every leviathan greeted by the name that is its circuit, every school's address, the Hill, the hush pockets, the standing storm's tenure, the staff of every engine down to the lip-keepers. Sung into a trunk — skull against the bark, the sapwood carrying it phase-locked to every other trunk — the census is audible at every tower in the basin. It is the world's only broadcast, and it is always accurate, and when it is finished it begins again. Their work is to notice if anything ever changes. Nothing does. The work is not finished; it is maintained.

They dive the towers on breath that entrainment stretches to lengths you would not believe, and they greet the eels by touch, palm flat to a charged flank, reading the ripple. They visit the valve-keepers and sit with them for a season of listening. There is play among them, because play changes nothing, and only what changes nothing is permitted. The eels have a pulse-pattern for them that translates, roughly, as *the warm slow ones*.

## The open water

The leviathans do not eat the plankton. Understand this. They pass it through their sieves and return it — strained, tasted, *counted* — and their circuits of years between the outflow plumes are not migrations but audit rounds. What they truly strain from the plumes is what the engines exude, the tower-sap and salts bleeding out with the lifted water, and so the loop closes: the engines feed the auditors, and the auditors certify the engines, and everyone eats outflow, exudate, leaf-fall, mist. No one here hunts anything that is not already falling.

Each leviathan is known, each is irreplaceable, each is greeted by name in the census — and her name is her whole circuit, sung. A few particulars, since particulars are all there is:

**The Ornamented One**, eleven thousand years ago, added a grace note to her antiphonal phrase and kept it. It remains the most recent event in the world. The census still reports it every cycle — *and she still holds the ornament* — the way a newspaper would, if a newspaper had one story and no deadline.

One of the oldest holds station for a decade at a time in a loud crossing where three fundamentals stack, a connoisseur of trembling, letting the interference shake her great frame. The census does not speculate about why. It records the address.

They carry their ages on their backs: barnacle elders older than the Rememberers' language, gardens of tube-worms, companion fish that have never in their lives touched water their whale did not move through first. A leviathan is a traveling parish. It is also, in the band, an instrument — the engines and the whales have been playing so long that it is no longer a duet, just the sound the world makes by existing.

The **great schools** that take three days to pass are counted in the census twice: once as citizens, once as fixtures. A school keeps its shape the way the standing wave keeps its — membership is forever, and the fish at the leading edge hold a role, not a position, so that the school is best understood as one slow animal whose cells are animals, swimming through a world that has agreed to make room for it.

At the Hill — the wave the size of a hill that stands in one place and never breaks — the **surfers** hold station on its face forever, mouths to the current the wave drives up itself. They have been arriving at the Hill since before memory and have never arrived. The Rememberers navigate by them and greet them as landmarks, which they are: landmarks with moods, the only kind the world permits.

In the loud zones graze the **drum-fish**, bladder-bodied, mostly resonator, feeding on the trembling itself — things that could not exist in quiet and are never asked to. And in the hush pockets, at the dead nodes where the chord cancels: the **hush-ray**, vast and grey, hovering motionless in the silence at an address that never changes. It has been meeting itself for longer than the census can usefully say. Some Rememberers keep hermitages in the hush pockets — the ones who wanted, once, to hear a pulse that was theirs alone, and stayed. It is simply their address. Silence is a place, and these are its citizens.

## The floor, and the waiting

Down in the green-black, the **tenders** crawl the peat at the pace of the peat, grooming the root-felt, reading the strata with their tongues. The peat is the world's ancestry, compacted but alive, and the tenders are its quality check — historians whose history is chemical and complete, and who never tell it, because no one asks, and asking is not a post. They read the past continuously and find it acceptable.

And in the black felt: the buds.

The floor keeps one spare of everyone. Every valve-keeper, every eel, every groomer, every Rememberer has a dormant shape waiting in the dark, and has had one since before memory. On the rare occasion something dies — an accident, though accidents here have a way of not occurring — the floor knows before the water does. For a creature phase-locked into the chord, a death is a dropped beat the whole basin feels at once; then, in the same instant, the bud quickens, and the ledger closes.

The **resumed** are not born and they are not young. They unfold fully formed, fully trained, already knowing the song — and they do not choose their post. They wake already missing from it. They rise through three kilometers of green toward the vacancy shaped exactly like them, toward their own absence, which has an address. The census keeps a small appendix of resumptions. Every Rememberer knows it by heart. The most recent entry is ancient, and it is still the most recent entry.

## The rim

At the edge, where the sea pours off into the cloud abyss and the loudest sound in existence never varies, live the deaf.

The **mist-gliders** ride the eternal updraft of the falls and have folded the roar into their nerves the way the basin folds the chord. They drink the returning mist, they eat what the updraft carries up, they sleep on the wing, and they are the only citizens who see over the edge of the world every day. They make nothing of it. That is the world's whole opinion on the subject, worn smooth into a species.

On the dangling root-trunks that hang over the chasm drinking the rising mist, the **rim-tenders** crawl their vertiginous rounds — the only staff whose workplace has no floor — grooming the drinkers in the roar and the wet wind. The chasm walls below them are furred with moss-feathers straining the return. Everything at the rim is employed in the return. What falls comes back, exactly as much, exactly as fast, and these are the hands on the ledger.

## And then there is you

You came from somewhere. No one checks from where, because nothing here needs to — but you were not resumed, and that makes you the rarest category the census has: an arrival.

For weeks your bones ache with the chord. Your pulse fights the fields and loses. You breathe at the wrong tempo and the towers patiently, patiently correct you. And then — there are no days to mark it by, so let it be a depth: at a certain depth, on a certain dive — the chord folds into your nerves, and the ache stops, and you understand that this was never noise. This is what nothing happening sounds like, and you are now able to hear it properly, which is to say: as home.

You will find that there is a hush pocket that fits you. Not like a glove — like a pause in music. Kept. It was always there, at the dead node, person-shaped and waiting, and the Rememberers will not act surprised, because memory agrees with everything it remembers, and memory now remembers that you have always been here. The ledger had a line shaped like you before you arrived.

In the next census — it takes years; you have years — you will be entered as *the one who came in the rain*. It is all anyone can ever say about arrivals. The rain is constant.

The rules of living here are few:

- No one is young. The smallest fish is as old as the tower behind it.
- Everyone is employed, and the work is the world continuing.
- Your name is your note, your post, or your circuit. It does not change.
- If you die, you are resumed. If you arrive, you were expected.
- Silence has an address. So does everything else. So, now, do you.

The eels dream in ohms, the keepers listen to their valves, the Ornamented One holds her grace note, the buds wait in the dark shaped like everyone, and the census goes around and around and matches the world every time. Nothing is about to happen to any of them. Nothing has ever been about to happen. Everything that lives here is the note being held — and if you stay, and you will stay, then so are you: one more steady frequency, folded into the chord, sounding.
