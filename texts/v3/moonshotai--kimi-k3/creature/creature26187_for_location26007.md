You will hardly ever see them. That is the first thing to understand about the lives of the Cistern: they are abundant in kind and sparse in number, and the water is very large, and so most of what you will ever find of anyone here is what they have done. The census of this place is taken in traces — a grain ticking into mortar, a cleaned trail crossing a filmed wall, a socket in the masonry where someone used to live. Hang still long enough, and the traces accumulate into citizens.

Begin with the sheen, because everything does. The films are not one thing but a guild of the almost-nothing: rust-eaters thickening at the cold seeps, pale colonists spreading across fresh-laid stone, the dull mature growth a grazer prefers. A new wall goes through successions like a slow season, and the masons sow it — a grain-carrier that has licked a tended wall carries the garden on its mouthparts to the next stone. Whether it knows this is not a question the Cistern asks. The films do more than feed the world; they bind it. Mortar cures harder where film has lived, because the dead cells are the binder. Every wall is mortared with its own cemetery, and it is the cemetery that holds.

The mason lineages are the visible hands of the place, and they work alone. A grain-carrier is the size of your thumbnail and makes journeys that to it must be continental. It chooses a grain the way you would choose a word — by taste, by fit, by some inner grammar of the wall it carries in its body — and then it carries that one grain across a distance you could swim in a minute, and sets it, and goes back. Do this for decades and the route wears a road: faint polished paths cross the floors of the Fallen, and you can follow one to a quarry at one end and a wall under construction at the other. The young apprentice on the rubble, where errors don't matter. What *trusted* means, in a world without judges, is that the taste for standing walls comes on late, like a second instinct — until it does, the carrier's body simply will not climb to true work. Lineages differ in mortar recipe and arch-curvature, the way dialects differ. A district is a language. The Rim preserves dead ones.

The secretion-masons are slower and stranger: a mortar gland with a memory, extruding its ribbon along grooves it cut years ago. They lengthen as they age; the oldest are half-fused into their own work and move by the thickness of a coin in a day. When one dies at its wall, the wall is built over it. This is not a tragedy. It is a specification.

The tunnel-keepers are the only builders whose building is removal. Each one holds a circuit of passages that takes days to patrol, and it knows its tunnels by the water's speed and taste, the way you would know a road by its turns. Without them the Cistern would silt shut within centuries. Their reamed castings — graded, sorted grains — are the finest quarry a grain-carrier knows, and so carriers shadow keepers along the circuits, taking delivery of tomorrow's walls from today's cleaning.

New work shines. Where mortar is fresh it bears a faint cold light for a day or so — the marking-glow, whether it is the cure itself or a mason's deliberate touch. You learn to read a district's fortunes by its points of light: where many small glows burn in the dark, the wall is winning.

Against all of this, the unbuilders. Fret-mouths lace the undersides of walls into filigree, harvesting the mortar's minerals a mouthful at a time. Bore-shells drill. And there are the keystone-pickers, who taste stress: compressed mortar weeps a different chemistry, and so the pickers congregate precisely where failure will happen, drawn to it the way you are drawn to a warm door. Selection has never cured them of this, because the fall is theirs — they ride the collapse down, and the rubble is their migration, their feast, their nursery. Against them the mason lineages have been evolving for ages: mortar doped with rust, with bitterness, with recipes that fail in other ways. An old wall with a red core is a recipe that worked.

The grazers go wide as a hand and slow as weather. Some creep naked across the films; the rubble-backs cement debris to their shells and carry a little landslide with them, indistinguishable from the Fallen until they move. The veil-croppers graze the rust-films at the seeps and pay for it — iron loads their shells year by year, until an old one is a lump of rust that walks, too armored for almost anything to bother, trailing cleaned stone behind it. That trail is its autobiography: where it turned, the film was richer; where it hurried, something passed overhead. You can read a grazer's age in the width of its script, and where two trails cross you can sometimes see which one yielded. The script is never read as script. It is consulted once, by other grazers, as a plain fact: *already cropped; move on.*

The gardens on the spire-tips are all mouths. Fans trim their vanes to the current like sails, and when the Breath runs strong and the structure flutes its tone below hearing, every fan in a district opens at once — the gardens bloom in a sound you feel in your teeth. Net-casters fish the meeting layer directly, throwing webs of mucus and hauling them in limb over limb, the only fishermen this world has. Lace-tubes grow their own chimneys to reach a private current. Their colors — rust red, chalk white, one startling sulfur yellow — are bookkeeping: the chemistry of handling minerals, worn where it happens to be visible. Beauty here is excretion that found a use. The gardens spawn to the fluting and to the falls, casting eggs into the crossing snows, and they eat the world's descending young — beneath every garden the masonry is fat with failed settlers. A garden is also a bet placed on a tower's judgment, and the towers are always being undermined. The old gamblers settle the Long Weir's lip, or the elder-fused walls, where the current is guaranteed and the foundation has already proven what it is.

Because some lace-tubes never leave their first settlement. The wall grows around them; the centuries pass; their siphons become the wall's veins, filtering the same water they helped dam, until they are load-bearing lives. You can read a pillar elder's age in the strata that entomb it — the archaeology of a single life. When one dies, the district shudders, and the socket it leaves whistles when the Breath runs. You can tell how many elders a district has lost by its chord; the voice of a hall deepens as its elders go. A socket is the only addressed housing in the Cistern — a young settler may take one within days, or it may whistle empty for a century, and both of these are common.

Between the snows lives the only pelagic commons. The drifters never touch stone in their lives — comb-rowers, mucus-clouders, small strainers thickening the glittering band — and among them drift the young of nearly everything walled: masons, grazers, tubes, all beginning here, eating snow. Settlement is the great mortality, the long descent through the gardens, and most do not arrive. Courtship out here is a pulse of light, brief and rare, answered or not.

Learn the three lights, then: the shine of new work, the pulse of courtship in the meeting layer — and the lure, which is the other two, forged.

The long pale hunters are the color of thrift, pigment being a cost no one here can justify. A hunter hangs in a nave and uses the whole hall as an ear; the rasp of a grazer carries three halls away in this water, and the hunter is patient enough for the arithmetic of arriving when the meal is finished, not before. When it moves, it moves — a nave in a minute — and then the hall is empty again for days. One lineage hunts by forgery: the false-mason hangs in a likely arch, glows the glow of fresh work, ticks faintly, and takes what comes to finish the wall.

The rubble plains keep sleepers — scavengers dormant for years at a time, woken only by a fall-boom's frequency arriving through the stone. They are the first to a tower-fall, then the crushers, for the armored dead, then the sifters. The dust-bloom is a wedding as much as a feast; half the district's kinds spawn in it, because a fall is the only time the sparse world is ever crowded. Within a week the crowd thins. The masons arrive last and stay longest: a carcass-plain is quarried for a generation.

The plunge mist below the Long Weir has its own citizen, seen whole almost never. The weir-mothers are the largest lives in the Cistern, slow bulks straining the endlessly re-suspended silt — which is ground history, shell-shard and mortar-meal of a thousand dead districts, so that to eat the mist is to digest the archive. Gardens grow on their backs. A weir-mother is a district that swims. When one dies, it is the only true whale-fall: the plunge pools feed for a season, and the carcass seeds a reef with no rubble in it.

Deepest, the Fossil Works keep their hidden citizens. Under the silt the silt-worms swallow and grade and cast, and carriers shadow them for the castings — pre-sorted grains, a quarry that renews itself. Deeper still, whole halls lie drowned in silt, and the buried rooms are not empty: pale, eyeless things live down there that have never once surfaced, in rooms no one has entered for ages. And there are the doorkeepers — a kind of tunnel-keeper that keeps one buried doorway clear across its entire life, passing water through a room no one enters, for no reason that survives examination. Call it instinct that outlasted its purpose. It is the closest thing this world has to faith.

The Rim's abandoned districts are not empty either; they are given over. The unbuilders hold them, and the wall-walkers — slow, many-legged, patient — pick the unbuilders from their bore-holes one by one. To the wall-walkers the Rim is a stocked larder. To you, if you go, it is a library of dead dialects, standing in pure form, being read by mouths.

When the cold deepens and the ice fingers come down the spires, sealing the gardens in suspension, the thaw-followers wait at the freeze-line — the only ones whose blood tolerates the descending brine. They are first to the shatter, eating the shock-killed and the newly unsealed films while the last shards are still falling. They read the calendar before anyone else knows it has turned.

And rarely — once in an age, perhaps — the Gate sends down something stranger than leavings: something alive, from the bright world, that lives for a few hours in the cold dark while nothing knows what it is. The scavengers take it like any other fall. The only accidents here arrive from outside.

That is the census, as the place teaches it. Every life is a use made of stone. You are either building, eating, waiting, or being read. The films feed the world and the dead bind it; the walls are either being raised or being mined; the young go down through the gardens and the survivors are quarried later from the nursery itself. Nothing is wasted, including you. Stay long enough and the Cistern will enter you into the record — the grazers will crop the film from your footprints, the carriers will take what you dislodge, and if you die here you will be mortar, which is to say: useful, which is to say — by the only definition this world recognizes — alive a while longer.
