The loudest acre has a freeholder.

He is an old grey bull seal with a horse's muzzle and a neck written over in scars, and the scars are the deed. He has held the high point of the rock for nine winters, through every audit the gales conduct, and he holds it the way you keep a fire — by lying on it. Everything on the acre is ranked, and the ranking is public. Below him the younger bulls hold the middle ground by negotiation and teeth. Below them the cows in their season, the pups in theirs. The auks pack the waterline like a working waterfront, shoulder to shoulder, shouting. The cormorants stand along the southern edge in a black juried row, wings held open to a wind that owes them nothing. The gulls take whatever is left and are content, because gulls have never once needed rank. The constitution of the rock is short: keep what you can hold, and hold what you can keep dry. Every storm revises the map, and after every storm the freeholder is still there, a tone heavier, asleep in the exact centre of the only dry thing in the world, and nobody discusses it.

The birds are the only tenants with dual citizenship. The auks go off the rock like dropped stones and come up out of the dark forty metres down with silver in their beaks, having flown — there is no other word for it — between the kelp trunks, through the moving columns of light, working the same water as the cod. Whatever happens over the crests, the rock knows within the hour; the auks are the wire. The cormorants go deeper and stay longer and come back with nothing to say. And at dusk the shearwaters arrive from the open sea to batter the boiling surface with their wings, the only tenants who pay their rent entirely in violence and are gone again before full dark.

The majority of the population is never seen at all. The snapping shrimp hold every crevice of every stone, an address density no city achieves, each one in a room just its size, each room with a door. At the better doors a goby stands watch, touching the shrimp's antenna with its tail, reading the shrimp's alarm the way you read a dog. The snap that makes the endless crackle is a claw closing fast enough to boil the water at its tip — and in the dark of the crevices, every snap is also a spark, a real flash, brief and blue and unwitnessed. The valleys are full of tiny lightning. The crackle you hear from above is a standing census: every crevice answering present, every hour of every day. At slack water, when the race lets go and the kelp stands straight, the whole region reports itself occupied.

The herring are one animal with ten thousand hearts. The school has routes older than the beacon, habits that no single fish carries and no fish needs to: when to rise, when to fold, which shoulder of which dome to round. It is the region's weather and its bank and its news, and everyone draws on the same account — the seals corkscrewing through the columns, the auks punching in from above, the whales straining it through the passes, and beneath it all the cod, the householders, hanging unhurried in the shadow of the school like men under awnings, waiting for the rain to feed them. In the dark months the cod drum, low in their swim bladders, a sound like someone knocking to be let in.

The freight cars have tenants, and the tenancies run long. In the third car off the grade lives a wolffish the length of a paddle, and he has lived there for thirty years, which is to say since before the youngest bull on the rock was born. His door faces up-current. On his doorstep lies his whole account of himself: urchin spines, crab shell, mussel valves, a midden he has never once been moved to clean, because it is accurate. He hangs facing the flood, swings with the ebb, and at slack he hangs like a held breath, watching the same view he has watched since the kelp was young — the bright thread of the rail, the grade going away into the green, the cod crossing like slow traffic. Nothing about the view has changed in his lifetime. That is why he is old. A fish gets old here the way a stone gets round: by staying. In the last car there is a pair, and this winter there are eggs, a pale clutch lodged deep in the dark, and the male lies over them through the cold months, fanning, refusing meals, keeping the only warm-blooded appointment a cold-blooded animal can keep.

The switchyard has a keeper.

She is an octopus, and the transformer is her den, and the office has never once been vacant. Not in living memory, and not in the midden's memory, which is longer: the pile at the door is an archive of appetites going back through keepers no one counted — crab carapace, urchin test, mussel, whelk — and the archive has a shelf for things that are not food. A brass knob. Three glass beads from the old insulators. A gauge face, kept and kept clean, its needle dead since before any current keeper was hatched. Each new tenant rearranges the collection to her own mind and leaves the older arrangements underneath, so the switchyard has an archaeology of taste. In the evenings she keeps one arm on the cable. It is the only warm thing in the valley, and it hums, and she tastes it — that is what her arms are for — and what the hum is to a creature of touch and patience is not knowable, except that she has never once let it alone. She will hold the office for two or three years, which is the length of a life, for her. Then the eggs, then the long guarding, then the drift of her children out on the tide, and in due course the transformer is occupied again, and no one has ever traced where the applicants come from. It is the only job in the region. It has never been advertised and never been empty.

Some tenants are seasonal and some are smaller than the leases they hold. The whales come through the passes on the right tides of the right months, and one of them — a female with a notch out of her fluke like a bite taken from a coin — has been coming for more years than the beacon has burned, and her voice is a resident even when she is not, arriving long before her body and staying long after, folded into the cold water like a letter kept in a drawer. The crabs that work the old trawl net on the saddle have been demolishing it for decades, board by board, and will never finish, because a crew does not burn its own boat: the net still fishes, and the catch feeds the demolition. In the eelgrass the pipefish stand head-down among the blades, thin as the blades, the meadows' discreet lodgers, present in the census only by their crackle-free silence. The one vacant address in sixty kilometres is the cobble mill over the crests, where nothing can live because nothing can hold still — and even that is kept. The gales come in and turn the beds like a keeper turning a garden, and the round stones roll, and the purring goes up through the water, and the range polishes its empty room.

Below the last of the light, the chasm has its own tenant.

She is a sleeper shark, five metres of her, and she cruises at the speed of an hour hand, a slow circuit of the fault-line like a caretaker walking a fence. Her eyes carry the small white tassels of the parasites that ride her kind, worn like tags of office. She takes what the storms send down — nothing falls here that is not met on the way down, and she is the last of the meeters — and in the loudest sixty kilometres of cold water on the coast, the eldest resident makes no sound at all. She moves through the crackle and the hum and the drumming and the race like a held breath moves through a room. And she is old enough — just — to have been born before the water finished rising. Whether she remembers the coming of the sea, the whaleback valleys still open to the sky, the dam when it was young and loud and dry — no one can ask her, and she would not say. She keeps it the way the sea keeps everything it is given: not carefully, but permanently.

One cormorant sleeps on the beacon housing, every night, the only warm dry perch in the region that is not the rock. The light comes on beneath it at dusk and burns for no ship in particular, and the bird does not keep the light. It keeps the light company. That is most of what keeping is, here.

Take the census at slack water and you will hear it, because the census is the sound: the crackle of the uncounted, the hum the keeper tastes, the drumming of the cod under the school, the freeholder's breath on the high point of the acre, the wolffish fanning his eggs in the dark of the last car, the sleeper turning, soundless, at the bottom of the chasm. The range is finished, but it is not empty. Nothing finished is ever empty. It is handed down. And when the tide turns, all of them go back to work at once — the maintenance crew of a completed world, none of whom has ever been told that is what they are — over the oldest, stillest bones there are, every one of them carrying somebody living.
