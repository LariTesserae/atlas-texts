# The Longer Inventory

The room keeps a short inventory: one water, one light, one garden, one wall, one trough, one bird, some days. This is the longer one. Nothing in it is large. Most of it is slow. All of it is specific — the pot does not support life in general, only life in particular, and everything that lives here is here on the same two conditions: make your living from what the thread brings down, and take the light as it comes — as an appointment, not a condition.

## The films

Below everything else, the films. Every surface the water touches, and some it only dampens, wears a skin of life one cell thick — too thin to see, present everywhere. They eat what the rain remembers: peat stain, pollen, the dissolved ghost of sheep-dung, all of it fallen on no one three days ago and lowered forty feet on the wire of the thread. The whole room is fed on crumbs from a table it cannot see. No one has counted the films; they are uncountable in principle, the way a color is uncountable, and they are a color — the black of the walls is partly them. The moonmilk in the alcove may be their masonry, laid down and dried across decades on a chemistry no one watches; whether it is alive or only made by the living is a question the room declines to answer. Everything else in this census grazes the films, sleeps above them, or is them.

## The natives

In the pool, the natives: a few dozen white amphipods, eyeless, the length of rice grains. They were born in the dark and they will die in it, and nothing between those two darknesses will ever have been seen. Eyes were an expense; their ancestors spent them. What they have instead is time — they do everything slowly, live years at it, molt by season, brood by year. They know the pool the way a body knows its own house at midnight: every rimstone dam, every slab of the trough's footing, every inclusion. They walk across the bronze ring and graze the film from its patina. The offerings are their pasture; their mouthparts are the only hands that still handle the gifts. Their famines are long and their feasts are small — a drowned midge let down the thread, three days dead, is a season's news — and they are built for exactly that proportion. Nothing hunts them but the bird. When the bird walks the bottom it is not, to them, an animal; they have no use for the category. It is a local catastrophe, a weather with a beak, and when it has passed they resume — which is all they have ever done: resume.

## The garden

Everything about the garden is old. Each moss cushion is a heap of its own ancestors, green only at the very crown, growing over its dead a handspan a lifetime. The hart's-tongue keeps its tongues for years; the liverwort spreads its platters at the speed of the stipend. For that is what the garden lives on — a stipend of light, the grey seep all year and the one gold appointment at the year's height — and it spends like an institution with a small endowment: carefully, nothing it cannot afford. The fall made it and keeps it — the thread makes the mist and the mist makes the garden — and ten paces off there is nothing, bare black stone, because the room spends its whole allowance in one patch, and the garden is that patch.

And past the light's edge, in the dark niches, the goblin's gold runs the boldest account in the room. Its cells are built like little lenses. Each one gathers what stray light the dark contains — light no other plant could live on — bends it inward onto the green, and holds it. What glints back is the savings, returned. Like the land above, it is a miser; like the land above, its wealth is hidden, pocketed, kept underground. It glows for no one. Bring a lamp and it answers — the only thing in the room that gives your light back to you greened — and when the lamp goes the constellation goes with it, and the moss keeps its gold in the dark, which was always the arrangement.

## The threshold

At the twilight of the entrance, where the dark is not yet entire, the spider keeps the border. One spider: an orb-weaver, alone, and the aloneness is part of the office. She hunts by touch, as everything here is done by touch; her web is known whole by no one, least of all herself — she knows it from inside, the way a hand knows a glove. The roar does not trouble her. It is her weather, constant, and she has the knack the ear takes an hour to learn: she hears inside it. A midge straying down the funnel in summer, a moth taken in autumn before the torpor — the footfall arrives to her like a word spoken in a library, and she goes to it across the shaking dark. Her egg sacs hang like white beads on a thread, a necklace in a dark no one dresses for. When they hatch, the young climb to the lip, pay out silk, and go where the wind over the fell goes. The room keeps her and exports her children. She is the only resident whose whole life is the threshold itself — fed from both sides, belonging to neither, mending her invisible web at the edge of the loudest silence in the parish.

## The guests

The moths are pure guests, and give nothing but their presence, counted: two or three. They come down in autumn with the moor's summer inside them — ivy blossom, dropped fruit — fold their wings into the shape of a dead leaf, and burn that summer all winter, one slow degree at a time. The disguise is wasted here; there has never been a leaf in this room; they are camouflaged against a hunger the room has never held. Their skill is stillness, months of it, hung on the wall like tools. In spring the wall sheds them, they go out to the sallow, nothing in the room knows they came, and the count drops by two or three.

The bat, some winters one, has solved the roar by resigning from hearing. It furls its ears, folds itself like a shut umbrella into a ceiling crack, and slows its heart to a few beats a minute; between beats it is nearly geology. It has chosen the safest sleep available anywhere: no owl hunts down the slot, no weasel, the cold never changes, and the roar — which erases every footfall — erases all footfalls equally, hunter's and hunted's, until there is nothing left to fear but the flood, and the flood announces itself musically, pitch by pitch, in time. Some winters none comes. Then the crack stands empty, the year's inventory runs a line short, and the room is patient about it.

## The citizen

And then the bird. The dipper is the only citizen — the only one for whom the room is a place among places. It owns the commute the way the dead's feet owned the footholds: down the funnel, through the wriggle, in the black, by memory, the roar growing in its bones, until the chamber opens and it lands on the trough — it lands where the light lands — and bows. The bow is what dippers do. Here it reads as liturgy, and the room, which permits no voices, permits the bow.

Then down the stones and into the water and along the bottom, alive inside the roar's own element. Underwater it blinks its white membrane, a signal lamp going on and off in the dark pool. It feeds. It picks the natives off the stones one by one — so the room's only daily citizen is also its only predator; attendance and catastrophe arrive in the same body, on the same feet, some days.

In late winter, when the moor above is silent, it sings — a long sweet warble the room shreds at once, a voice passed through a torn drum. But its call is another matter: flint-struck, narrow, hard, pitched by a million years of whitewater to sit in the roar's one thin seam, and the room that erases all voices carries this one. For the length of a call the room has two voices. Then one again.

And it leaves — that is the thing. It goes up the shaft into the squall or the padded silence, carrying a bead of the pool on its back and an amphipod in its crop: the room's only export that returns. It knows both rooms, the loud one and the quiet one. Nothing else does. The spider knows the threshold; the natives know the pool; the dead know the stone. Only the bird knows the difference, and it makes the difference daily, twice daily, all its life — the small bright hinge the whole place swings on.

## The dead

The longest residents are the dead. They live here as shapes worn in stone. Their feet are in the dished hollows at the heels' intervals; their grip is in the handhold at the squeeze, worn to the shape of a grasping hand — every descent is a handshake; your hand goes into theirs, and fits. Their patience is in the three cups at the lintel, a thumb apart. Their foresight is the trough, set waist-long exactly where the light's foot comes down at the year's height — they kept the appointment often enough to learn it, which means they kept it for centuries. And their giving is in the pool: flint, bone, clay, amber, bronze, settling grain by grain into the siltstone, kept now by the rock they were given to. They are not a presence the way the bat is a presence. They are a presence the way the flowstone is: finished pouring, still gleaming. Of the whole census they are the most numerous and the least countable, and they are still receiving: the natives graze their ring, the bird lands on their trough, the light keeps their appointment. The room keeps them the way it keeps the silt lines — as a record it has no intention of finishing.

## The largest resident

And then the one all the others are inside of. Apply to the room the tests applied to everything else in this census, and it passes them one by one. It holds its temperature: five degrees, wet, constant against squall and drought and season — warm-blooded, at five degrees. It breathes: on a falling glass it exhales, on a rising one it drinks the moor's air down, and on the coldest mornings its breath stands over the slot as steam, the only breath the empty fell ever sees. It drinks, and has a pulse, and they are the same act: the thread, wrist-thick, three days behind the sky, and it has never once stopped — that is the promise and the heartbeat both. It feeds: rain, pollen, peat, a drowned midge, a thrown bead. It digests: the trough going down grain by grain, the offerings inclusion by inclusion, on the room's own schedule. It grows: a page a century on the walls, a grain a decade in the gourd. It keeps a diary: one line of silt a generation, knee-high, waist-high. It speaks — the standing note, the furnace hiss, the high fizz — and it is the only resident that sings its own weather in advance: when the outlet chokes and the pool rises, the great voice climbs, pitch by pitch, the throat tightening, and anyone inside hears the flood announced musically before they see it. And it pays attention. Whatever enters is counted. That may be the whole of its inner life, and it may be enough: an attention so slow and so total that to be counted by it is to be kept.

It is mortal, on its own scale. The thread that feeds it is also unroofing it, grain by grain; in some tens of thousands of years the chimney will open to the sky, daylight will stand in the room all day, nettles will inherit the garden, and the standing note will have no bell to stand in. Until then it lives here, holding its five degrees, saying its note, keeping count: one water, one light, one garden, one wall, one trough. One bird, today.

## The last entry

The last entry in the census is struck out within the hour, and knows it, and comes anyway. A person, for the length of a visit, is the largest thing the room contains, and the deafest. What you say here is kept nowhere — the room's privacy and its law — and what you are is kept exactly as long as you stay: counted on entry, held in the loud dark, released. The room gives you one thing to carry out, and it is not an object. It is a ringing that fades by supper, the cave's voice staying in the body after the body has left the cave — the same arrangement it makes with the spider's children on the wind and the bead of water on the dipper's back, except that yours walks itself home across the padded moor, hearing for one evening exactly how quiet the world is.

By the fell's edge the ringing is gone; the last export is spent. Behind you, under the nettles in the funnel bottom, the count goes on without you, true whether or not there is an ear to refuse it: the films and the natives and the border-keeper, the guests asleep on the wall, the dead in their stone, the garden spending its stipend, the great resident holding its temperature and saying its note — one water, one light, one garden, one wall, one trough, some days one bird, forever or nearly.
