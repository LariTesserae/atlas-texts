They are shaped by the horizontal. Twenty-odd people who lie, swim, and haul for a living, and stand only in the dry side-vault where they sleep — their bodies have gone long and smooth to the water's liking, shoulders broad from the net-haul, legs almost an afterthought, feet spread and grip-toed from bracing on raft-frames. Their skin carries a permanent sheen of eel-oil, worked in against the wet, so that a keeper in the dark is known first by smell: the oil, and under it the particular musk of the person, and under that, faintly, the lime. The lime is in everything they are. It grits their hairline, stiffens the lashes, and in the old it begins to hold. A keeper past sixty grows a pale crust at the knuckles, the elbows, the rim of the ear — the rind taking them the way it takes everything the water touches. They speak of it without dread. *She is coming into the stone*, they say of a grandmother, and it is nearly tenderness. Small children are set to scrubbing the old ones' hands with snail-shell rasps, not to stop it — nothing stops it — but to keep the fingers closing a while longer. The old submit, and tell the knots of the strings while they are scrubbed, and this is how most teaching happens: wrist-deep in an elder's hand.

The hand is the first organ of this people; the eye is a distant third. Their eyes have gone wide and dark over the generations and they read the grays of the hall the way a reader reads print, but a lit lamp is an occasion — oil is counted — and full daylight is an ordeal they mostly choose not to undergo. What they truly see with is sound and touch. Every child learns the drip before speech: the one far-off fall of water, on its slow count, which is clock and metronome and well all at once. Hide drip-catchers hang beneath it and beneath two lesser seeps, and minding the catcher is a child's first duty, and the count is known to everyone, and if the count ever shifts — it has, twice in the strings, as the ceiling grew — a rafting is called and the strings come out. They have also learned the birds' trick. A keeper swimming the black hall clicks softly with the tongue, tick, tick, in the swift's own tempo, and reads the columns' return. The best of them can cross the hundred paces without touching stone, threading the wreaths, finding one particular capital among hundreds. The champions of this art are the dim-eyed old, whose sight has gone but whose ears have flowered, and the children's favorite game is to be judged by them: swim the hall, make no ripple a raft can feel, and be told at the end exactly where you cheated.

Voices are low here, and spare. The dark drinks sound, so speech happens at touching distance, and much of what must be said never reaches the voice at all. There is the tether-code — tugs on the lines that web the rafts, three-and-pause-and-two, a spare grammar of *come*, *haul*, *hold*, *air-turn*, *landlord*. There are the ember-words: a hand slashed through the surface wakes the phosphor, and a practiced slash writes short bright letters on the black — *net-full*, *danger*, *dive*, *dive now*. And there is touch itself, which carries the freight that elsewhere faces carry. Names, above all. A keeper's name is not a sound; it is a knot. At birth the string-holder ties it — always a working knot, a lashing, a bend, some pattern the rafts actually use, so that to speak a child is to tie her — and the child wears it on a cord at the wrist for life. Two keepers meeting in the dark do not speak first. A hand goes out, finds the wrist, reads the knot. First the name, then the voice; it is the shape of every courtesy they have. When two people marry, their cords are braided through a single lashing and the knot is read into the strings, and the unlashing of a marriage, which has happened eleven times in memory, is done before witnesses with the cut ends kept. When a keeper dies, the cord stays on the wrist. It goes down with the body, so that the stone will keep the name.

Their days are not days. Nothing here turns on a sun; the rhythm is the mountain's breath. At the exhale, when the warm heavy air presses up the stair and out the door-seam, they work the Door-hearth — a shallow stone basin on a landing where the draw is strongest — and this is the only fire of any size the Vessel ever sees. Then the eels are split and smoked and the fat rendered for the lamps, and the hall below smells of it for days, fat-smoke lying under the wet-lime smell like a line under a verse. They say they cook with the mountain's own breath and have never needed another wind. At the turn, when the deep water gives up its gas and the pearls climb in their slow chains and the low layer goes thick and sleepy, the work stops. They climb to the high tethers, watch the swifts drop clicking toward the water and then climb themselves, and breathe shallow, and wait it out the way sailors wait out weather — playing knot-games, telling the floor-stories, sleeping in shifts. At the inhale the air comes thin and sweet down the stair and that is the diving time: trap-boxes hauled from the floor on gut lines, eels taken by bone gig, snails scraped, the velvet gardens combed with bone combs from their family's columns — for the velvet is farmed, in its way, certain columns seeded and tended and inherited like orchards, a family's wealth readable in the coat of rust and cream on its pillars. The wreaths of blind fish they take only from the ring's edge and only by net, never by gig, because a wounded fish in a wreath is bad luck that lasts for years, and twice in the strings a wreath has *reversed* — turned, against its own years of turning — and both times a great bad-breath followed within the season. No one fishes a reversed wreath. No one speaks above a whisper near one.

The landlords are older than law, so the third law is barely a rule; it is a posture. You do not hinder them. You do not feed them. You do not fish within a body-length of one. And when one climbs a column and hangs at the waterline with its broad head in the air, all work in the hall stops — the listening silence, it is called — until it slips back down. The keepers know the four of them the way one knows headlands. The Throat-warden, almost always near the First Water, its back plated in black velvet it never sheds. The Door-side one, the restless one, the one most often met under the rafts. Long Sleep, who vanishes for whole breath-cycles and whose reappearance is noted in the strings. And She of the knobs, the largest, so old that lime has crusted her motionless shoulders twice over, rind on rind, like a thing halfway to being a column herself. The strings record that once, six generations back, she hauled herself onto a raft and lay there a full day, and the family fed elsewhere and did not disturb her, and the raft has been lucky ever since — it is called the Guest Raft still, and a tether on it is the best wedding-gift there is. The strings also record, twice, very long ago, a landlord taking a keeper. There was no reprisal. That, too, is the law. What the landlords listen to, at the waterline, no one pretends to know — the Throat, the mountain, the deep news — but the keepers watch how long they listen, and a landlord that listens long is read the way a sky is read.

Nothing leaves. That is the whole theology, if it is a theology: the Vessel keeps. So the dead are given back to it, lowered through a particular gap between two named columns — the kin-gap — and down through the warm dark to the floor, where within a decade the rind begins and within a lifetime the body is a rounded pale shape with the wrist-cord somewhere inside the stone. The floor is populous. The stone keepers down there outnumber the living hamlet many times over — grandparents of grandparents standing and lying in the sediment while the eels thread through them, and the oldest so fused and knobbed they might be masonry, kin to the First Water's fused columns. Divers visit them. A child learns the location of her stone grandmother the way another child learns a grave, and on the rare lamp-lit days a diver will go down and put a greased hand on the rinded shape and read the wrist through the stone by memory. That the eels eat them, and the keepers eat the eels, is stated plainly and without flinching, in the formula taught to every child: *to be eaten is to be kept.* In lean counts — and the strings are honest about lean counts — the old have sometimes asked for the water early, while they could still swim to the kin-gap themselves. It is called the Giving, and it is honored, and it is refused as often as asked, and both choices are knotted into the strings with the same care. They are not a people who look away.

The strings are the whole of what they carry. They hang in the dry vault, cord upon cord of knotted eel-gut, and the string-holder — chosen in childhood for the hands alone, trained by touch in the dark of the vault until the knots are a first language — reads them two-handed, hours at a stretch, while someone feeds her and someone else minds her raft-work. Memory here is plural and unbothered by it. One cord-line says the keepers were born from the water and owe it. Another says the builders sent them down to mind the cistern, and that the Door was to be opened every seventh year, and that one seventh year no one came, and then no one ever came, and the keeping continued anyway. A third says they *are* the builders, diminished to fit their own work. The strings hold all three and the string-holder recites all three and no rafting has ever voted between them. Memory is not history, they say. Memory is ballast. You do not throw ballast overboard for contradicting itself.

And woven through every cord, the closing numbers: the rise of the water by generation, the thinning of the Low Air, the length of the bronze chisel. The chisel has a name — it is called the Argument — and it is itself a closing number, because a cut of the overflow lip costs it a little of its length, and the strings record its length at every cutting, and so the keepers know not only how much air remains but how many cuts remain to buy more. The two chisel-keepers, always two, always trained across a lifetime for a rite they may perform once or never, are the only people permitted to hold it. At a cutting — there have been nine; the groove of the lip shows nine polished lines — the rafts are lashed, every lamp is lit in wild expenditure, and the name-recitation is spoken, every keeper who ever lived, in order, a recitation that now takes the better part of a waking and grows longer each generation, which is itself, the string-holders point out, one of the closing numbers. The chisel-keepers cut. The water falls a finger. The children are lifted to breathe the new air, and the Argument, shorter, is wrapped in oiled felt and put away. They know exactly how this ends. The youngest learn it the way other children learn weather, and their consolation is not denial but custodianship: that they are the Vessel's memory while it can still have one, and that after the closing the heart goes on without the breath — the wreaths turning, the landlords climbing to a waterline with nothing above it, the velvet eating the spring. The strings even carry the final instruction, the last knot on the oldest cord. Until that day, law: the strings never touch the water. The last knot commands otherwise. When the air is a finger deep, the string-holder is to tie the whole of it to a stone keeper's hand below, and let memory rind into the mountain with everything else the mountain has kept.

They have been up, of course. Not often — the Ascent is a rite, once in a life for those who choose it, roped and felted and greased against a cold their bodies no longer believe in, up the Long Stair in high summer to the saddle of gray stone where nothing grows. They come back down within the hour, eyes burned weeping by a gray sky, half-deafened — for the wind is the terrible thing, a rasping roar with no walls to drink it, sound let loose to be torn — and they sit silent at the stair's foot for days while the hamlet leaves them alone. The outside has a name in their speech: the Thin. Their word for heaven, if it can be translated at all, is a full warm room; their hell is openness, air without end, all the way up. Every child goes through a phase of it anyway — climbing a few steps too many, breathing the thin on a dare, whispering the heresy of the Openers, the story-people who would unmake the egg and lead everyone out under the cold lid. The elders let it pass. It passes. The stair is very long, and the warm smells rise from below, and home is down.

They are not solemn, whatever all this sounds like. With twenty people, everyone is an epic, and the strings record not only the cuts and the Givings but the feuds — the three-generation quarrel over a cut tether, settled at last by a marriage — and the jokes, which are mostly about sinking, since clumsiness is the one unforgivable vice and therefore the one inexhaustible subject. A keeper who drops a tool must watch it sink and memorize where it lies, and the floor is dotted with famous mistakes: the lamp that fell during the great bad-breath, the bronze hook lost at the Guest Raft's mooring, each one rinding slowly into the museum of shame, visited by divers who surface laughing in embers. The strings even record, with a knot reserved for the purpose, that the most tedious man in the Vessel's memory was born in the same year as the largest eel ever taken, and that the eel was the better loss.

For now there is still the skin of air, and the lattice of rafts, and the drip counting. In the dry vault a child practices her clicks against the wall and is corrected by a grandmother whose eyes went gray years ago and whose ears miss nothing. Below, on the black water, two keepers meet at a tether crossing in the dark. Neither speaks. A hand goes out, finds a wrist, reads the knot — the other hand does the same — and only then, the names established, the lashings acknowledged, do they say anything at all. First the name. Then the voice. Then the work. The mountain breathes through them, slowly, and they breathe with it, and the birds tick overhead, keeping the count no one taught them, older than the strings.
