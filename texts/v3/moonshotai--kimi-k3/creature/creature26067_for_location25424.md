Who lives here? The answer depends on the counting, and the counting is the first thing the valley undoes. There is one inhabitant — the city itself, a single green body with the wind shut out. There are twenty — the citizens, counted the way the valley counts, by the self and not the stem. And there are more than rain — the small, who were here first and will be here last. All three censuses are true. Walk them.

First and eldest is the hedge, and you have already met it: it is the wall, and it is one. It came before everything, a spore over the rim when the rock was still raw enough to cut, and everything alive inside lives inside its decision. It has never flowered here. It does not need to. Where the wind strikes the rim hardest it grows densest, shoulder-high and tight as wicker; on the lee arc it grows looser, taller, as if listening for the wind to change its mind. The only place to read its age is the gate, where its two ends flank the sluice like doorposts — gnarled, grey-skinned, thick as your thigh, the oldest wood in the valley, which is to say the only wood in the valley. It will not bridge the running water, and the water will not let it, and so the city has a door. Just inside its rootmat it keeps a bare trodden strip, a ring road the keepers use at night. Its roots are a treaty line nothing crosses; the towers stop a body's length short of it, and the pavement turns aside.

Second is the pavement, the largest body here and the one you think about least. The lanes are the glacier's handwriting — the first paths the meltwater sketched downhill — and the pavement is the ink: one felted individual, star-shaped, reaching its points toward the plaza. Kneel hard on it and it gives off a smell sharp as cress, the chemistry by which it tells its neighbors no. At dawn it is rigid underfoot, feathered with frost; by afternoon it lies an inch under the flood and takes your weight like a held breath. It asks only grazing. It is the most touched and least seen of all the citizens, and it is in perfect health, and those two facts are the same fact.

Then the twelve — the towers. Each clone is a stand, and each stand has a character, the way a family does. The one nearest the gate leans outward over the lane as if to inspect whoever comes in. The storm-clone on the windward side grew squat and thick-armored. And there is a widow: half her stand went under a rockfall some decades back — the scar is still visible at the wall's foot — and her remaining trunks lean toward it. At night the twelve comb the fog, and each tower gutters its harvest down its own trunk, the diamond bark channeling water rootward, so that every tower waters itself, a closed household. By day each bark-diamond holds one drop, and some diamonds hold a folded moth, fur-grey on grey, and you can look straight at a trunk and see only bark. On the one dry afternoon of late summer the crowns dust their spores loose, green smoke in the vault. Almost none survive. The few that matter are raised in the hearths.

The reeds are two — the north rank and the south rank — or possibly one, meeting under the plaza where no one has dug to look. They are the voice. Most of the day the city is hush, but when the melt pulse comes off the ice at midday you can hear it arrive in the reeds, a rising hiss moving downhill rank by rank, as if the valley were an instrument and the glacier had drawn breath. For an hour the reeds lean and straighten and the streets run. Then the pulse passes, the hush closes, and the reeds go back to holding water like a held note.

Three hearths stand at full size — the gate hearth, the north hearth, the plaza hearth — and the children are not counted: a head-sized one near the wall, a fist-sized foundling you could miss with a boot. Inside, the chambers run from cool to warm the way a house runs from mudroom to hearthstone. The outer rooms are damp and cool and full of other people's seeds — tower, reed, well, all the citizens' children start here. The middle rooms are hand-warm: moth pupae hang there in spring like seeds that are almost animals, and the keepers' trails lead in under the skirt before a cold snap and do not come out for a week. At the core is the fire that is not a fire, a dense damp heart where the small are rendering yesterday's leaves into heat, and it never goes out, and it is warm as a held hand because a hand is roughly the temperature of well-kept decay. Stand downwind at dusk when the pores open: peat, steeping, and under it a musk so nearly animal that your skin answers before your mind does. That is the core breathing. The hearths are the only citizens with tenants, and the only ones that take the dead. The keepers work the fallen into the felt; the afternoon flood carries the rest to the aprons; the cushions draw it down. Nothing dead stays above ground because the hearths are always hungry and always gentle.

The well is one citizen with a hundred mouths. The rosettes ringing the black pool are a single being, cups crowded shoulder to shoulder, holding the pool the way a palm holds water. Each cup clarifies its share of melt and settles the rock flour into clay — the valley's first true soil — and when a cup fills, the ring lets it go, and its children sprout in the spent cup, the potter raising its young in its own pots. At dusk the blooms open, pale, and a degree or two warmer than the air; hold your finger above one. Warm as breath. That is how the moths find them. The ring's sex is mostly a solitude — pollen carried cup to cup of the same self, a hundred mouths kissing by proxy of fur — but once in a decade a stranger-moth comes over the rim from some sister valley, and that year's seed is different, and the ring seems to know, and those seeds go in the best cups. The pool itself is black and gives almost nothing away. Midges work its silt. That is all, and it is enough to keep a bird.

Beyond and between, the frontier: the pioneer crusts on the fresh rockfall, each one already a commune — a fungus, an alga, often a third partner, housekeeping in a space the width of a coin. They etch the rock a hair's breadth a year and make the first dust that could be called soil, and they know, if crusts know anything, that the city will come over them in time: the felt will reach them, the towers will shade them out. They are the suburbs and the sacrifice. Up past the hedge, on the wind's side, their cousins speckle the raw walls as far as the fog reaches — the hinterland, uncounted, unhoused, holding the rock for whoever comes next.

Forty-some slates and a dozen ambers, and if you stay a week you learn them as individuals: the slate with the white-edged foot, the big amber missing its tail-tip to some old frost. The slates work the open lanes; the ambers favor the hearths' aprons, climbing the cushions at dusk to graze their pores clean before the great exhale, and the cushions suffer it the way a sleeper suffers a cat. By day they lie under the hedge's rootmat or in the hearths' middle rooms. At dusk they come out and the rasping begins — kneel on the pavement and listen: the smallest steady sound in the valley, a cat-tongue sound, many of them, everywhere at once. Their trails dry to silver handwriting, and the writing is the mending: the slime binds the felt and feeds it and keeps its chemistry true. They are the mail, too — seeds ride their backs and bellies from hearth to hearth. On the wettest nights they meet at the lane edges and knot, two selves, slime-lit, unhurried. Step around them; the city has little enough sex and this is some of it. Their eggs are pearls in the felt's edge. In hard cold they go deep into the hearths and wait in the warm dark, and if slugs dream, they dream of the rain they were born in.

The frost-moths are the valley's only pelt and its only hurry. They do not eat; an adult is a week's worth of stored weather with wings. By day they roost in the bark-diamonds, grey on grey. At dusk they shiver themselves warm — you can watch one do it, wings a blur while the body burns its small store up to flying temperature — and then they are off on the errand that is the city's whole economy of sex, and their wingbeat is, some evenings, the loudest sound in the valley. The ring pays them in advance: each caterpillar is allowed a tithe of leaf, one leaf in twenty, measured out by chemistry, and the pale frost-furred larvae eat exactly that and no more, and when they are ready they are carried — no one has seen how; the keepers are suspected — to the hearths, where the pupae hang hand-warm until the dusk the cushion lets them go. Then the hawk-hours: a few times a season the shadow crosses the rim and for one hour the moths come down through the vault like grey ash, and the lanes take them, and the keepers work them in, and by morning there is nothing. The fear of the hawk lives in no single moth — a week is too short for lore — but it lives in the moths, in the way they emerge in pulses on the hawk-free weeks, timed like a held breath.

The dipper is not an individual; it is an office. One bird holds the gate at a time — a nest like a moss ball tucked in the sluice's throat, between the hedge's two grey doorposts, the only nest in the city — and the office outlives the bird. It works the milky water for midge larvae and caddis, walking the bottom upstream like a small grey clerk, and what it finds there is the proof of the rumor-fauna: things do live in the water, enough to keep a bird, barely. It bobs on its wet stone as if the cold were a rumor it had declined to believe. And it saves its song for the rim. Inside the bowl it is as hushed as everything else, but once a morning it flies up over the hedge, out of the shelter, into the wind, and lets go a long rippling song that the wind takes instantly apart. The city has one rule, and it is hush, and even the bird obeys it indoors. It is also a road with feathers: spores and eggs ride its feet down the outflow and up again, and it does not know, and it does not matter that it does not know.

And the hawk. Not a citizen — a season. It lives on the wind's side, nests on a crag two valleys over, and reads this bowl as one cell in a large larder. A few times a season it crosses, and for an hour the city has weather of a kind it cannot shed. But it also delivers the news, in the worst way: a seed in a foot's crease, a spore in a feather, once a beetle that fell alive from its talon and spent a week under the gate hearth eating and not settling, an immigrant that did not naturalize. Everything the city knows of the outside arrives either on the wind or on the hawk, and the wind is the better postman.

Then the census of the small, which the valley takes the way it takes rain. Springtails pepper the felt. Midges work the pool silt. Water-fleas tread the wells' cups, and a water-flea's whole cosmology is one cup — its weather the thawing of the dawn ice-skin, its night the closing bloom. There is one spider. It came over the rim two springs ago on a thread of ballooned silk, and it lives in the south reed rank eating midges, and it will die unmated, and its web is a small flag the wider world has planted here, a notice of intent. And under everything, in every film of water on every surface, the firstcomers: the bacteria and the fungal threads that arrived as cells and spores within years of the ice leaving, before anything green showed. They are the grazed lawn of the lanes; they are the fire in the hearths — the cushion is only architecture around their heat; they are the workers turning the wells' clay into soil. Count the citizens as twenty if you like. The valley counts its tenants by the million-million, and the green city is, seen rightly, a greenhouse: someone is being kept warm.

And over it all, not in it but of it: the elder. The glacier does not live in the valley, but everything that lives here is mostly glacier by weight, drink by drink. The remnant above the rim weeps all day and the city drinks all day; the lanes are its handwriting, the fog its ghost, the groan from above the rim the sound of it turning in its long sleep. It is smaller every year, and the city grows. You would say, if you stayed long enough to be sentimental — and you would stay long enough — that the city is the glacier's answer to its own ending: saving, cup by cup of clay and chamber by chamber of humus, against the day the weeping stops.

And then there is you. No one of your kind lives here; nothing here has ever burned; the valley's fires are all bacterial, and it has never heard a voice. You are an event, like the hawk, but warmer and slower, and you eat nothing. Stay an hour and the city ignores you. Stay a week and it begins to keep you: the hearth nearest your sleeping place warms your nights whether or not you asked; the keepers graze your bootprints and mend the felt where you stood; a moth bumps your face at dusk, taking you, reasonably, for a warm bloom that got lost. And when you go — you go — you carry the census with you. Spores in your cuffs. A seed in the seam of your boot. Moth-scales on your sleeve like grey dust, and in your coat, for days, the smell of peat and something almost animal. Once, a spore arrived here on a bird's foot and the hedge began. Now the city rides you over the rim.

Who lives here? One wall. One floor. Twelve towers, two voices, three fires, one well with a hundred mouths. Forty keepers and a week of moths. A clerk at the gate, a season with talons, an elder weeping on the rim. The small, like rain. And you, briefly — counted the way the valley counts its water: not by amount, but by where it goes next.
