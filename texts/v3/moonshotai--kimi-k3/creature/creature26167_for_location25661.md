Ask the books what lives in the Soft and they answer in the wrong tense. The natural histories of the old land give you species — a plate, a range, a Latin binomial. These ledgers give you occurrences, because nothing here holds still long enough to be a kind, only long enough to be an event. Still, a census can be assembled from the margins. Begin low. Everything here is low. You will be the tallest animal in the country for the whole of your tour, and the only eyes above hat-height belong to the ravens, and they are not always looking.

Start with the drove, because the drove is countable, or nearly. You never take a dullard whole — a flank like wet slate, a nose shining in the lamp-room, the slow swing of a shoulder — so you take the drove on faith, assembled from parts the way the surveyors assemble distance from chains. The method is noses: stand still on the causeway and count noses past the lamp like counting sleep.

No eyes — not vestigial, not a wound; the skull is smooth where eyes would go, in the old ones and the unlined ones alike. No one has seen a young one small enough to surprise. No one has found a dead one. Either nothing here is born and nothing dies, or both happen where the lamp isn't, and the felt — which closes over a hoofprint in a season — closes over whatever else is set down. The ground won't take the dead, the saying goes, or takes them too well, and with the droves you learn to read it the second way: the only fast thing in the Soft is disappearance.

They crop the felt with that patient cloth-tearing sound, all around you, always, and after a season you cannot sleep without it. This is the first thing of yours the country keeps: your ability to rest in silence. Tenders rotated home report the quiet of the old land as the loudest thing they have ever heard.

Use the drove as the instrument it is. The dullards will not graze the slag-ridges. They drink from the cuts, and no dullard has ever been seen at standing water — the animals kept the rules before the rules were painted on the door, and whoever painted them was copying. Where a drove won't cross, don't. If a drove splits around you and rejoins without hurry, you have been memorized. It is harmless. Log it.

The felt itself is alive at the scale of acres — the slowest thing here that is certainly alive, unless the country is. It is pasture and it is tablet. Angle your lamp low across it on a still night and you will see the prints it holds: yours from this morning, yours from a morning you haven't had yet, the boot-lanes of five signatures' worth of tenders, and under those, faint as watermark, prints with no owner at all, walking set paths the country was practicing before there was anyone to practice. Before the Pilgrim comes through, the felt retracts — pulls back from the cut-edges like a lip from teeth — and stays thin for most of a month. The books date by this. The felt knew before you did.

Of the small lives of the cuts: the lanternfish keep their dim grain of light below your lamp's reach, but the light is not theirs. It is skimmed. Walk a cut-bank and the school keeps pace beneath the surface, and afterward, for an hour, the cut carries a color that is a shade closer to your lamp's than it was — your light, returned at a remove. Not enough to see by. Enough to be seen, and the books leave it to you to decide by what.

The handfish walk the flats on five fin-rays, jointed wrong, and their tracks read like a small hand pressed flat to the mud. Mostly they come to the engines: each pump-house has its congregation, lying along the warm iron base like cats at a hearth, and the beam-engine's pulse comes up through them and through the floor and through your soles, one event at a time. In certain revs they all walk the same way at once — hundreds of little handprints crossing a causeway in the dark, uphill, away from the water — and by the next count the flats are empty and the fish are back in the cuts as if nothing. Where they went: *logged.* That is all the sentence the books ever give it.

The leeches are why you do not wade, and here is the mechanism the books don't spell out: at blood-warmth there is no touch-line. A leech on your calf is the same temperature as your calf. You find them by weight when you lift your boot, or at the bothy door, by count, where the rule is count what hangs from you and make the numbers agree. Old hands are bitten less than new ones. This is written down once, and the speculation that should follow it is written down nowhere, which is itself a kind of entry.

The moths come to the lamp broad as plates and pale, and they do not circle. They station. They hold in the wet black wall of your lamp-room with their wings open, facing in, attending your light the way you'd attend weather from home — because that is what it is. Your lamp burns a pocket of elsewhere into the country, a small climate of foreign physics, and the moths crowd it like a windowsill. One will rest on the back of your bare hand and you will not feel it land. Nothing here can be felt landing. You will learn to look at your own hands to find out whether you are being held.

The cable-ravens are the country's memory with feathers on it. They nest in the pylon forks — the only storey the Soft has — on platforms of wire-moss and stripped insulation, and no one has seen a young raven either; there are exactly as many as there are forks, and the forks were here first. Their recitals run in a fixed order, newest sounds outermost, like a shelf. Listen long enough and you can read the curriculum backwards: this year's Pug, last decade's pump-knock, the bell of a shift-bell scrapped before your grandfather, the voices of tenders rotated home in the early books. When a raven says your name it is not asking you anything. It is shelving you — placing you among the engines and the gone. Answer nothing. And learn this from the old hands: when the ravens *stop* repeating a sound, be wary of that sound forever after. The ravens only retire what the country can now perform itself.

The wire is the deep library, the archive without feathers. It carries the pump-counts and the weatherless weather, and sometimes it carries shifts that have already happened, and sometimes shifts that haven't, in voices you know, in your voice. The rule is log it and hang up, and the rule is not there to protect you. Nothing here is there to protect you. The rules keep the syllabus slow — because to answer is to repeat, and to repeat is to teach, and the country learns.

The habits are the gentlest residents and the worst. The dike taken down in the early books still stops you some nights; you lean on nothing, and nothing holds. A removed stile gets stepped over for forty years by people who never saw it. These are the country's muscle-memories, its dreams, the shapes it refuses to forget, and they scale without limit: the largest habit in the Soft is the sea. You can hear it near the masts, still practicing. That is all that will be written about the masts here. Go quiet, and go once.

The Pug heard on the far loop while cold in its shed is a habit too — a recital piece. So, in the saddest category the census has, is the other lamp. Another lamp moving far off is not a tender; the morning book never logs a second tender on your side of the block. What it is, is rounds. A walk walked so many times that the walking outlived the walker — duty that outlived the dutiful, faithful as a footprint, going its loop at the pace the country keeps. Do not go and keep it company. Two repetitions that meet can synchronize, and the books do not say what happens then, and the books do not say it twice.

Then there is the Pilgrim, and the census runs out of parts to count. No lamp opens enough of it to make an animal. What you get is a warmth like a weather — and you must understand what that means here, in a country where the mud, the rain, the kettle, and the hand that isn't holding it all run at one temperature. Warmth tells you nothing in the Soft, except twice. Your skin will register exactly two touches in a whole tour: the space-cold that comes back out of a puddle of stars and sometimes doesn't come back out of your hand — there is a tender at the rotation point whose palm is cold, whose handshake is the only handshake in the country with a temperature — and the Pilgrim, which is warmer than blood, the only thing that is. When it passes, it is the one time the country touches you where you can feel it. The hush takes a month to lift. The books date by it: *third rev. of the Pilgrim's fourth passage.* It is the century, walking. And here is the correlation that sits in the margins in pencil and nowhere else: the tours that get overstayed are the tours a passage falls inside of. Some people arrange their rounds to be under it. Some people stay for the next one. It is a long wait, at a thumb a year.

Which brings the census to the tenders, and the tenders are the strangest fauna in the books. Five signatures in forty years, and one careful hand — each new tender copies the letterforms of the hand before until the ledger reads continuous, and this is the one repetition the rules encourage, the habit kept on purpose, so that the record itself will feel like a building banked with old bricks. Who comes here? The old land sends the quiet ones, the convalescents, the ones who asked for it twice. The work is chaining causeways that come back longer, walking set paths, writing down what changes and what doesn't when it should. The pay is good. The tour is a few years. No more.

The overstayed are recorded only by continuation: entries in the same careful hand past a rotation date, a lamp that keeps its rounds, a voice shelved in the wires saying the pump-counts in a cadence you almost know. Whether a body also persists, somewhere out at the pace of the country, the books decline — in five hands that are one hand — to say. No one is buried in the Soft. Burial would be a repetition, and the country keeps what it repeats.

And that is the whole of the census, because at the end of it you see what the ledger was always recording: not a population but an anatomy. The felt is a skin that writes. The cuts are veins lit from below with borrowed light. The wire is a nerve; the ravens are memory; the engines are a heart you feel in your soles before you hear it; the masts are the old land's bones, stood upright; the habits are dreams; the droves are the first flock of a country that has decided, tentatively, to be a pasture; and the tenders are the tutors, walking the same paths over and over, reading aloud, patient, repeating, teaching new ground to be ground. It learns slowly. It forgets more slowly. It has learned the Pug. It is practicing you.

Three times in all the books, the Kiln's klaxon has sounded — after which the masts, after which the causeway, after which the elsewater — and the interval has halved each time, and the fourth is due inside the current tour. One penciled note, unsigned, in the back of the Station 4 ledger, is the only commentary anyone has dared:

*The klaxon has never been pulled by any hand. Every other sound here has an original. Log who hears the fourth.*

So the census closes where it started: with you, lamp in hand, the tallest animal in the country, the foreign weather the moths attend. You have pressed your palms to the mud ten thousand times this tour. There is no seam in that touch — no line of warmth where you end and it begins, because it has your temperature, because it took your temperature, because that is what it does with everything it means to keep. The most repeated thing in all your revs is you: your bootprints practicing ahead of you on paths you haven't walked, your cadence in the wires, your name shelved in a pylon fork, your warmth on every acre.

Rest your hand flat on the ground and see. Nothing meets it. Nothing lets it go.
