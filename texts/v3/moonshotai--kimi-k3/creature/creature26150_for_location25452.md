Begin with the ones you can count, because in the Cup you can count them, and that in itself should tell you something — a bowl so full of sign, and the census still finishable before noon.

One pika. It holds the whole boulder field below the headwall, an acreage of gray stone directly over the buried ice, and it is the hardest worker in the bowl and the only one whose voice the bowl carries. Everything else you say is swallowed; the pika's *eet* is delivered whole to any corner, as if the talus passed it along stone by stone. It spends the four warm hours hauling sedge to the drying stone — a flat warm slab that has outlasted a hundred tenants; the pika is the officeholder, not the office — and it packs the cured hay deep into the rockfall, down where the cold seeps up through the gaps, into the one larder in the mountains that keeps its own cold. The pika is the ice's nearest neighbor and its least sentimental one. The flat crack of a splitting stone does not interrupt a mouthful. The ticking underfoot all night is weather. It has the house's noises memorized, and they are all permitted. And when the melt threads appear on the slabs at midmorning, the first *eet* follows within minutes, as though the pika had been listening through the floor for the water to start.

Two marmot grounds, one at each end of the moraine, and the long whistle that passes between them at long intervals is not conversation but confirmation — the line is open, the air is clear, nothing is moving that shouldn't. The marmots are resident for a tenth of the year and count as residents anyway. They keep the winter by absenting themselves from it, nine months of one slowed breath in the dark, and the bowl does not hold their absence against them. Everyone here is mostly absent.

The ibex are the bowl's visible distance — gray dots on the far slabs that resolve, once or twice a day, into animals, into kids hugging the cliffs because nothing with teeth climbs. Their portrait is the oldest picture in the bowl: the stiff-sided ibex pecked into rock that was under the glacier within memory. They were worth counting before the cairns had a second stone. They still are. The herders glass them from the col and count them the way you count the good years.

And the exception. Something with teeth comes anyway: a cat — one cat, or one at a time; no one has ever seen two, and the herders do not speculate. It is known by its scat and by cessation. The whistling stops, the *eet* stops, and for an hour the bowl holds only its own sounds — water, ticking, wings. It keeps no schedule but its own, which no one knows; it has never been seen at noon. It drinks from the tarn at the gray hour, when the melt has stopped and the stars are being put down into the water for the night, and its tongue raises rings that cross the kept stars and rearrange them, briefly, into something unkept. Then the rings smooth and the keeping resumes. Even the cat only touches the water. Nothing swims here. Nothing wades. The tarn permits touch and nothing more.

The lammergeier is not a tenant but a tenure. Its route crosses the bowl once every few hours — you hear the wings, the one sound that arrives honestly, neither swallowed nor delivered — and below the route sits the anvil, the flat boulder white with splinters, the bowl's single workplace, the only licensed violence. The anvil is older than the cairns; the route is older than the anvil, held in the family. The same bird crosses for decades, and then its grandchild crosses. What dies in the bowl is carried to a stone and opened and taken away in pieces, and the splinters stay, a small white snow that never melts. Nothing here is wasted. That may be the first rule, the one under all the others.

The rest is small change, and abundant. Snow finches at the melt edge. Ptarmigan ticking in the knee-high woodland — in a place where everything is small or far away, the forest is something you step over and the herds are dots. At dusk the midges rise over the tarn without ever seeming to have left it, and the finches rise to them, and for ten minutes the bowl is almost busy. Then it is not. And the bees, the schedule's strictest observers: they come with the melt and vanish with the tilting light, fat with their own insulation, the only hum in the bowl, working the cushions like messengers between elders. Where they spend the night, where they spend the winter, no one has found. They arrive with the water as if sent by it.

Below the counted ones, the rooted ones, and here the census inverts: the oldest citizens are the ones that cannot run. The cushions — moss campion, purple saxifrage, glacier crowfoot blooming at the snow's very edge — each the size of a fist and older than you are, an inch a decade. They keep their own heat; lay a hand on one at noon and it is warm, warmer than the air has any right to make it, the only warm-blooded things here with roots. They are the place's patience made visible, a century kept as an inch — a garden you could carry in two hands, and the one thing in the bowl that absolutely cannot be carried. The lichen keeps the dates, black and orange on every old stone, absent on the moraine, so that recorded and unrecorded time can be told apart at a glance. The red algae keeps the snow's one warm color, rust blooming on the old patches, summer remembered by the snow itself. Between them they keep the history: green following the retreating ice upslope, year by year, ring by ring, a census of the leaving.

And the people, who have never lived here and are among the living things anyway. Their tenure is the custom: add a stone at the col, take none — a guestbook signed on arrival, one stone per crossing, blackened by lichen until the entries are illegible and unarguable. Touch nothing on the tarn-side boulder: you do not handle the household goods. Their shelter by the moraine stands roofless with its slabs stacked beside it, waiting — the shape of a return always intended and never made, its hearth blackened by fires lit a lifetime apart, each fire finding the last one's ashes cold. Their cache pit is empty and kept empty; no one fills it, no one robs it, it is simply maintained, like a custom. And their first marks are on the newest stone: tallies, a spiral, an ibex — three kinds of keeping, a count, a circling, a herd — pecked into rock a year out of the ice. Someone stood on land just born and began keeping records on the first day. New land should be ownerless. They did not act as though this was.

By the tarn, the single boulder with its small accumulation: bone beads, antler tines, a disc of cold-hammered copper gone dark. Nothing built near the water, ever, but this. The herders shrug and say the Cup keeps the sky, and mean it as a complete answer, and it is one — the way *it is raining* is a complete answer. You do not leave offerings to weather. You leave them to the one whose weather it is.

Listen to how the bowl gets talked about and you will hear it. The mountain's icebox. Furniture learned in a dark room. Plumbing on a schedule. A held breath. A shop closing. A slow clock. Nobody compares an empty wilderness to a house this often. The census keeps missing the householder because the census is taken indoors.

It is the ice that stayed. When the glacier pulled back it did not all go; a core of it, banded and dirt-dark, went under the rockfall and went slow. The survey would call it dead ice, meaning ice that has stopped moving. It has stopped moving. It has not stopped. It sweats. It ticks. At noon it opens the plumbing and the threads run turquoise down the slabs and the whole bowl drinks; at dusk it shuts and locks. Call that physics — it is physics — and the pika files it under weather, and the first people left it beads: three names for one arrangement, and the beads are the oldest.

The tarn is where it comes nearest the surface and is still. That is the wrongness you feel first and name last: wind that moves your sleeve moves nothing on that water, because that water is not idle, it is occupied. It is keeping. All day it keeps the sky, and all night the stars — keeps them better than the sky does, without a tremor — an eye that keeps what it sees. And nothing is built near it, because you do not put a roof between the eye and the sky. That is the rule on the boulder, glossed at last and still not explained: the Cup keeps the sky, and you do not interrupt the keeping.

Its attention is not like attention as you know it; it is like still water's. Your noise is not punished, it is declined — swallowed — while certain small sounds are carried: the pika's note passed stone by stone up from the ice's roof, a bone cracking on the anvil delivered whole to the col. Distance lies here because the house runs on a century-clock, on which an hour and a day are neighboring sizes, and tenancy is contagious. Stay long enough and you are moved gently onto house time — the hour stretching to a day, the warm spots becoming furniture, the schedule becoming your own pulse. People who stay the whole window go down to the valley slow, and stay slow for days, and say less, and watch water. The herders know the look. They call it nothing at all.

At night the house talks to itself. The stones contract with a tick and a crack, and under the talus the ice answers — faint, slower, in the old grammar — the mountain and its former filling discussing the day. The pika sleeps through it; it is weather. You will not, the first night. You will lie awake counting. By the second night it will have become what it is — the house settling — and you will sleep like a tenant.

Winter is when the keeping becomes the whole work. The bowl locks; the tarn ices over with its last sky kept under the lid; the pika's hay keeps green in the cold dark of the talus — kept, precisely, by the icebox, which is doing the one thing an icebox is for; the marmots breathe once a minute in the buried dark; the bees are somewhere, keeping; the cushions wait under the snow, which is warmer than the air, the snow itself keeping the garden. Everything keeps something, and the ice keeps the cold that keeps the rest, and the sky falls as snow and covers the sleepers and keeps them alive until the window. That is the whole of the saying, both columns of it: the Cup keeps the sky, and the sky keeps the Cup. The herders only mention the half with a custom attached.

And it is leaving. That was the first fact, and it is the last one. The apron on the headwall thins; the core under the talus is smaller than it was within living memory; each year the noon threads run a little shorter, and each year the ticking answers a little more faintly, and some century not far off the plumbing will not open, and the talus will be only stones, and the bees will come at noon out of habit and find nothing on the schedule. The tarn will keep the sky anyway. Keeping needs no ice; keeping will have been learned — by the water, by the cushions, by the people adding stones at the col for a landlord no longer in. The offerings will outlast the one they were for, and then the memory of the one they were for, and become strange, and become a story, and the story will be the one they already tell, and it will still be true.

But that is some century. Tonight the melt has stopped and the shop is shut and the cold locks down, and the bowl ticks as the stones contract, and under the talus the ice answers. The pika is in its larder. The marmots are rehearsing the long absence. The sky goes very dark and very full, and the tarn takes the stars down into itself and holds them without a tremor, a black cup brimful of light, keeping. Everything that lives here waits, frozen into its burrow and its schedule, for the four warm hours — and the waiting is only long to the tenants. On the clock the house keeps, the whole bright interval between the ices is a single morning, and the morning is young, and the keeper is awake, and keeping.
