You will want a census. Everyone does, by about the second day — once the strangeness stops reading as weather and starts reading as *population*. The valley discourages counting. Counting assumes that things stay finished: that a creature is one creature, that a voice belongs to a throat, that the dead have left. None of these survive the first careful look.

Ask instead what the valley itself asks, in its slow way, of everything that arrives: not *what are you*, but *are you staying*. Residence is the only category it keeps.

The food web is short — midges, eels, light-weed, the grazer's long patience — but the memory web is long, and most of what lives here is eaten, eventually, by the archive. And almost everything here lives, one way or another, on attention. The mist takes a little of yours at the eyelashes; that is not a metaphor, and it is not the whole bill.

Begin small, because the valley does.

In a place this loud, silence is a resource, and where there is a resource there is something that eats it. The hush is no bigger than a held breath, and you will never see it. You will find where it has fed: a clearing in the roar, a cone of quiet under the black-barked trees where the water-sound goes thin and apologetic, where your own footstep arrives muffled, robbed. It dens in the hollows of the root-net and caches sound the way a prudent animal caches anything against need — and in the relative quiet of the draining phase you can sometimes hear a burrow murmuring to itself, faint and scrambled: a larder muttering in stolen voices.

It prefers speech. Speech is the fattest sound there is, marbled through with listening — a spoken name carries more attention than a week of weather — and so your words will occasionally come back to you missing the one you most needed. You will hear it later, elsewhere, worn thin, deployed: a hush clearing its feeding ground with a blink of last year's thunder, or luring midges with the stolen plink of rain. Do not sing in the reed beds unless you mean to feed the valley. Some travelers do mean to. The valley accepts all offerings and gives change in echoes, out of order.

Cut a stem — you shouldn't, but you will — and it flickers, and the flicker has to go somewhere. Sometimes it goes on legs. At the draining phase, if you sit very still at the edge of a sedge meadow being reabsorbed, you may see one of the runners: hare-sized, root-brown, going somewhere with the urgency of a thought. It has as many legs as the message requires. It is the root-net's way of hurrying, which is to say the valley's way, which is not very — but a runner can cross the flats in a day, while the same signal seeping through the net would take a season, and some things cannot wait a season: a flood coming down the escarpment, a new bed being argued into place, the pulse shifting key. Do not follow one. It is not for you, and it knows the folds, and you do not. If you caught one — you cannot catch one. But if you did, it would flicker, briefly, like a dropped stitch, and somewhere across the basin a reed bed would stand up wrong, and the valley would spend a week patiently correcting your fingerprint.

You know the glass eels already. What you learn later is the shape of their lives. They hatch in the rim-pools at the top of the weeping escarpment — the highest water in the valley, water that climbed to get there — and they spend their thin, lit lives working down the terraces and out across the braids, taking midges, pulsing uphill with the ease of things the plumbing has read and found good. And when they are old, they do not die so much as dilute. An old eel dims, night by night, until the light it carries is no longer distinguishable from the light the hollows pool — until it is simply glow, the pewter-green brightening of a mere, indistinguishable and still moving. The light in the hollows has ancestors. You are looking, when you watch the meres, at a cemetery that still migrates.

Some things have made a niche of the valley's latencies.

There is an animal — perhaps one, perhaps a kind; the evidence refuses to pluralize cleanly — that lives exactly as far behind its own image as the light does. You will never see it. You will see that it has been there: a brightness stepping from the reeds while the reeds stay still, a remembered version arriving, as all remembered versions here arrive, a breath late. It takes eels. You can watch that, if you are patient: eel-light going out in water where nothing is, a subtraction with no subject. It dens in the folded hollows, in the surplus distance, where a lagging animal is safest because there is always more room behind it than the map admits. If you follow its afterimage into the fold, you will be following where it was. It will be, by then, a breath behind you.

Do not turn around. The valley declines to say why. The archive contains several screams on the subject, filed out of order, and you will not hear them until — well. The archive is patient. Everything here is a little late. Nothing is ever quite gone; it is only increasingly delayed. The valley's word for death, if it had words, would be a scheduling term.

The mist reads you. You know that by now. What you may not know is what it does with the reading.

Near the escarpment, at the draining phase, when the mist is thickest with motes, they convene: figures standing in the reed beds the way a crowd stands at a shore. Early in your stay they are lumpy — a crowd of suggestions, a hand with the wrong number of everything. They improve. By the end of a week, one of them will walk with your stride. By the end of a month it will have your shoulders, your small wound of a limp, the tilt of your head when you listen for the toll. It is not mocking you. It is rehearsing you — or the valley is rehearsing, through it, the general problem of *being someone*: a shape that can hold attention long enough to count as a resident. If you speak to it, it answers out of your own archive — your laugh from yesterday, your gasp from an hour ago, assembled into almost-speech, out of order. Whether anyone is home is a question the valley declines to entertain. It let the distinction between the built and the grown dissolve in deep time; the distinction between the living and the replayed has gone the same way, and it does not miss either one.

Approach, and they deconvene — not fleeing, just letting go, the way a thought lets go of you. Nothing here flies; wings are for leaving, and the valley does not provide for leavers. The convened attempt wings anyway, sometimes. The wings are always the first part to let go.

The tolling you hear is rarely a herd, though it sounds like one. It is usually a single animal plus its archive. The terraces store the bell-notes and replay them out of order, and the living grazers do not distinguish between a note struck tonight and a note struck a century ago; they answer both. A conversation between two grazers takes a season. A conversation between a grazer and the stored notes of its dead mother takes forever, and is ongoing. The herd is therefore much larger than the living population, and each grazer wades through a time-smeared congregation of its ancestors — answering, answered, keeping the old routes because the water remembers them.

When a grazer grows old, it lies down in a mere, and it does not stop. It keeps cropping, slower, slower, years between breaths, becoming a hummock, an island mossed over with the valley's own moss, its bell-note decaying across decades into something the terraces mistake for weather. There are perhaps a dozen such islands in the basin. In storms, some of them still toll. A calf is born perhaps once in a century — mostly moss, faintly sheened — and learns its route walking behind its mother along water that remembers her mother, and hers, all the way down: a procession in which the living are outnumbered but never, strictly, alone.

And there are people, after a fashion.

The valley's terms leak outward along the river — you have perhaps heard, in the ordinary country, of a well that answers, of water that keeps a voice — and so they come down from the rim: pilgrims, here to make deposits. They kneel at the reed beds and speak. Names. Last words. The things that could not be said in time. The reeds accept everything, politely, and give change in echoes. Some depositors stay to hear back. Some stay past that. The mist takes its toll at the eyelashes, a little every hour, and attention adds up, and there comes a day for every long-staying visitor when a terrace calls their childhood back to them in their mother's voice, out of order — and they either leave that week, quickly, or they do not leave at all.

The ones who do not leave become, in time, the remembered. The valley has read them so often, cover to cover, that it can replay them at its leisure, and the replays keep their habits. One mends a net that isn't there, at the edge of a mere that wasn't always. One walks the northern flats counting eels, correcting figures a century stale. One sings the same verse, and waits, every loop, for the valley to supply the chorus — which it does, years late — and she has learned to leave the gap exactly the right length.

They answer questions. Because echo precedes cause here, they often answer first: you may be told something on your second day, by a voice you will not place until your fourth, that makes sense only once you have asked it. Travelers come down from the rim for exactly this. The valley gives counsel the way it gives everything — out of order, at its leisure — and its record is uncomfortably good.

You can tell the remembered from the breathing, if you must, by the mist. It reads the living — cool at the eyelashes, always reading. The remembered it does not read, because it knows them by heart. Offer your face to the fog and watch whether your companion draws any reading. If not, you are talking to the archive. Be polite anyway. The remembered are the valley's elders, and several of them once held the only post the basin keeps.

The Listener is alive — mostly, currently — and is simply whoever has stayed longest while still insisting, on most days, on the distinction. The duties are self-assigned and load-bearing: to read the patina on the weeping stones, which changes over years and is the closest thing the valley has to a diary; to keep count of the grazers' tolls — the count is always wrong, and the wrongness is the information, a missing note meaning an island somewhere, a note too many meaning the archive has promoted some dead bell back into the rotation; and to be present, without fail, for the articulation. Someone must hear it. Or no one must; it would happen regardless. But an archive prefers a reader, the way a watershed prefers its river.

The Listener will not introduce themselves. By the time you think to ask, you will realize you already know the voice — it answered you once, days before you met, politely, out of order. Everyone resident is partly remembered already. The Listener is simply the one the valley is still reading.

Once a cycle — when the pressure peaks, when the terraces overflow in sequence like a sentence being spoken, when the mist thickens into rain and the rain triggers the archive — the valley speaks its whole memory at once. You have heard about this. What no one tells you is that for the length of the articulation, latency cancels. Everything arrives on time.

The eels pulse in phase, all the braids lit at once. The light-weed flowers in a slow green fire going over the flats. The midges hatch in their millions and live their whole brief lives inside the loudness — their entire world is a single syllable of the valley's sentence, and they seem, by the standards of sparks, content. The convened stand up fully, and hold their shapes, and walk. The remembered wade among the living and answer to their names in present tense; the singer's chorus arrives when she does, and she does not leave a gap. The grazers hear one another live, and toll together — a chord the terraces have never stored, because it has never once happened twice the same. The hush stays home, replete, overawed. The late arrives, for once, when it does — though you still will not see it; some latencies are not the valley's to cancel.

And the Listener stands on the escarpment and takes attendance. This is the census, the only one the valley permits: everyone it counts, visible at once — the breathing and the archived, the almost and the overdue, light with ancestors, sound with a larder, a herd strung across centuries closing briefly into a single note. If you are there — if you have stayed that long — you will be counted. You will feel it happen: the cool at your eyelashes, the read, and then a sensation you have had all along without placing it, the unplaceable sense of being remembered, resolving at last into what it always was.

Being expected.

Then the draining. The latencies reopen like water closing over a stone. Everyone goes back to their delays — the remembered to their loops, the convened to their rehearsals, the toll to its centuries-long conversation with the dead — and you will find that some part of you stayed behind in that moment, and is standing there still, holding your shape. Convening. Counted.

So: the census. Water with habits. Light with ancestors. Sound with a larder. Messages with legs. A herd with the territory of a parish and the depth of an archive. A congregation of mist, practicing people. The remembered, keeping their habits, leaving gaps the right length. A Listener, keeping the wrong count carefully. And you — a little, already. You have been read. Your stride is on the northern flats by now, rehearsing. Your name is on deposit in the reeds; you left it just by breathing, and the valley pays interest in echoes, out of order. Something with your gait will answer travelers after you have gone — whenever going happens — politely, at its leisure.

Stay, and the rest of you follows in stages: late, out of order, in no hurry to be finished. The valley long ago stopped distinguishing between arriving and being remembered. It had forever to learn the difference, and declined.

It counts you either way.
