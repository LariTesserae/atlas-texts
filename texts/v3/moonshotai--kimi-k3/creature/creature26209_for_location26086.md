**The Census**

The census of the Kessel is taken by bell and returns, at last count, four hundred and some souls — a consensus rather than a fact. Ask what actually lives in the hollow, though, and the count lengthens, and the Bell-House keeps the longer version the way it keeps noon: by agreement, in pencil.

Start with the rung. To be a citizen of the Kessel is to be rung, like a street: when a child is born the bell is carried round the house within the week, and the household holds its breath, and listens. The child is not expected to answer. The house is expected to answer for the child, and it does, if the family has had the sense to breed on finished ground, and everyone pretends this was never in doubt. They are a slow people, the townspeople — slow not from cold alone but from longevity, since the creases leak time and a childhood here lasts as long as two elsewhere, and the old grow very old without particular distress, and the town's manners are the manners of people who expect to know one another for a long time. Arguments are conducted at a murmur and resolved, if at all, at the pace of the Settling. There is a feud on the Old Flat now in its third generation, over a wall both families remember differently; both are right; the wall exists in both tellings; the wall is fine; the families are fine; the feud is considered a form of warmth. Warmth, in fact, is the local wealth. The rich of the Kessel are not loud about it — they are warm about it. You can read a family's fortune in its frost-shadow, in whether the inside ice on its windows lets go by midday. Peat is hoarded like coin and given like jewelry. Draft-timber, though plentiful and light and free for the salvaging, is never burned: it burns beautifully, and warms some other room. The town tried it once.

The trades follow from the place. Bell-wardens walk the rounds and survey by ringing and sit on the committee that declares noon. Corner-wardens plant cornerstones for grandchildren and read the hum, and a cornerstone that refuses a site is moved without argument — you do not argue with a cornerstone; you apologize to the site and try elsewhere. Rim-wardens keep the bowl's edge, where the distances shorten, and meet the travelers who arrive an hour before they expected to, and teach them the litany over broth: trust the damp, knock and wait, never follow a late echo, do not count the houses. Cistern-keepers harvest the crease-water and keep the taste of unwritten in clean stone tanks. Salvors work the Creases for draft-timber. Stone-cutters take the town's small annual tithe of true stone from the Old Flat, cutting in the coldest weeks, when the stone is most itself, testing each block by ring. The Delayed Well has a warden, hereditary, mostly ceremonial, whose entire duty is to refrain — the post's one written instruction is *do not ask it questions*, and the pay continues. Every inhabited street keeps a kettle going; this is close to the local definition of inhabited. The kettle is the town's second instrument, after the bell, and the more reliable of the two: a street that has swallowed its bell may still be boiling water, and a street that boils water intends to stay. Children learn the two rains apart by taste, the way children elsewhere learn birdsong, and they are taught the counting-games with the ends cut off — there is a skipping-rhyme in the Kessel that counts the houses, and it is always stopped at nine, because the rhyme has no reliable end, and a child who chases it is called home. Moth-week, in the brief summer, is the town's one festival. It is a survey. The wardens work double rounds while the vellum moths are out, because a wall wearing moths is a wall that exists in more than one telling, and the town's idea of a celebration is finding out where it is.

The dead are the one class of inhabitant the town lets go. They are buried in the ring-true ground of the Old Flat, one draft deep, so that they remain the dead of one world only. A grave is rung once, at filling, and the hoped-for answer is solidity. Nobody says aloud what the other answer would mean; the Kessel is a place where the unsaid stays fresh.

Then there are the twice-told. A crease closes around you, or a room finishes with you inside it, or you pass through the wrong older doorway on a cold still morning, and afterward you are slightly plural. You remember a brother the town does not. Your shadow, at certain slants of light, consults a different sun. You are seen, very occasionally, in two places, and you dislike it more than the witnesses do. The twice-told vote once, by courtesy, and they make the finest wardens alive, because a twice-told warden feels a crease the way a struck bell feels its own overtone — from the inside, as a kind of recognition. A few of them were made not by any crease but by a failure of courtesy: they heard their own voice return late, and they answered it, and it answered. They are well. They are asked not to describe the conversation, and, being of the Kessel, they do not. On still nights the wardens give their streets a wider berth, out of respect, and log it as weather.

The Creases are populous, if you count the way the town declines to. Figures pass in the corners of streets — the population of other tellings, going about errands that do not concern this one. Children name the recurring ones the way children elsewhere name the moon: the Blue Woman, the Rain Man, the Autumn Draft's one pedestrian. Most are like draft-rain; they wet nothing. But some stay. A lodger arrives the way a wall arrives — gradually, undeniably, and colder — and over years takes on solidity the way stone does, drinking warmth, until one census the bell is carried round and they answer with it, and are rung, and the census book inserts them with a small caret, the mark of a count amended. Some of the oldest families on the Old Flat are careted families. It is never mentioned. The Kessel is a town of people who arrived; the only difference is the direction. There are no ghosts in the Kessel, and this is not reassurance but taxonomy: a ghost, the wardens hold, is a person persisting where they are not, and the town has a word for that already, and a procedure, and a caret.

And there are the remainders — the counters the count changed. Do not count the houses, the rule says, because the count changes, and counters, on occasion, change with it, and a person does not always come back from the counting as quite the person who went, and then the town must agree to recognize whoever did. There have been four in living memory, and memory in the Kessel is long. All four are or were employed by the Bell-House, since the census, by unbroken tradition, is taken by someone it has gone wrong for. They count carefully, in pencil, and they are the only people in the hollow who are never surprised.

Of the animals, the foremost is Plumb. He has never been young. Kittens resembling him appear from time to time, unwatched-for, and are left alone, and grow up, and are Plumb. The wardens use him as an instrument: carried, in a basket he tolerates, to a disputed corner, he will either sleep — the corner is sound — or sleep and also be elsewhere, and then the street is chalked and roped and he is given fish, though never the local kind. Two households on his route each believe themselves his owner, and both are correct. The crease-snails the children keep in pockets are the poorer instruments: a snail carried into the Creases will tell you, by withdrawing, how deep the draft runs, and if it withdraws entirely and does not come out for a season, it is not hibernating — it has more room in there than you do, and it knows it. A child with a snail in a pocket has, in a sense, more pocket than coat. The pale fish of the Lower Rooms turn over once a year, and the wardens note the Turning in a ledger, and nothing follows. Once — the ledger is exact about the year — the Delayed Well gave up a fish of a kind seen neither before nor since; it was returned, and the ledger records it as the Erratum, which makes the Erratum, in the strictest sense, a former inhabitant. The snow buntings keep the rim and are counted separately, in pencil, out of respect. They are the census of the outside world, and their refusal of the bowl is held to be the wisest thing anyone knows about birds. The rowan in the Bell-House court is counted, affectionately, as a resident — the half of it that is here. The other half is in an autumn that does not concern the census.

Of what lives below — what the fish are commas in the sentence of — the policy is silence, and the silence appears to be mutual. The plumb lines that did not stop were, by agreement, never let go of, and what is down there has never once pulled. It is patient. The Kessel respects patience above all the virtues, being made of it. Whether the well's patience, or the pools', counts as an inhabitant is a question the committee has declined to schedule, and the agenda, near the Creases, frays.

And then the count turns inward. Old hands feel new work in their teeth before the hum reaches the air. The cold is the cost of a metabolism; the hum is the sound of a body at work; the mist that pools in the streets at night is an exhalation, and an exhalation implies a breather. Nothing in the hollow is haunted — the wardens are flat about this — the town is not haunted, it is occupied, by itself, and it is an unhurried tenant. Asked what lives in the Kessel, a warden gives the consensus answer: the Kessel. Everything else — rung and twice-told, careted and remaindered, furred and finned and winged and one (1) cat — is lodging.

So the true census, which is kept in pencil and never read aloud, runs roughly: souls, rung, four hundred and some; souls, twice-told, a dozen, more or less exactly; lodgers, number withheld pending; remainders, four, employed; the dead, excused; moths, one week; snails, declined to count; fish, several, and one; buntings, respectfully declined; echoes, unanswered by policy; the well, not asked; the cornerstones, pending; the town — one, unfinished, unhurried, here. And everyone who lives in the hollow knows what the census is really for, and it is not counting. It is the bell, carried round on its rounds, and the held breath, and the listening for the short clean note and the small silence after it — the sound of everything in the Kessel answering, so far, in one voice, at one temperature, on one street at a time: still here. Still here. Still here.
