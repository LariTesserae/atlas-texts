You will want a census, and the table will not help you take one. Everything here hides by stillness, and so the population of the Quiet reads by traces: a silver trail across a fin, a line of dust on a well's rim, one white underwing feather snagged in the scrub at the height of a bird's alarm. You could walk a week and see nothing move. You would be wrong by five orders of magnitude. The rule for counting here is the rule for living here — nothing persists on the table that cannot pay its two taxes, silence and patience — so count what is slow, count what is mute, count what is mostly water, and you will have counted nearly everything.

Begin at the wells, because everything here begins at the wells. Each rosette cup is a pond you can span with two fingers, and each pond has its population. The glass snails come first: fingernail-sized, translucent as the drops they drink, so that if you hold one to the light you can watch it swallow — a dark comma of a gut moving through a body made of kept water. They grow the way the cushion-moss grows, in rings too fine to tally in a human lifetime, and a big snail, a grandmother snail, may have been grazing the same rosette since before the charter lapsed. Below them, smaller still, live the well-mites, pale as flecks of mica, and in the silt at the bottom of each cup lies the valley's standing reserve: cysts, dust-dry, patient as the land itself. These are for the rain. Rain comes four times a season, logged like a comet, like a birth, and when it comes the wells bloom — a brief glass carnival of fairy shrimp, hatched, fed, spawned, and gone again within days, leaving the silt reseeded for the next visitation. The keepers used to walk out bareheaded on rain nights, the one night the rule relaxed by a single notch — never clapping, never that — and let it hit them. The logbooks' word for the hatching is the same word they used for festivals. It is the valley's only carnival, and it is as silent as everything else, and it is over before you would believe it happened.

The dew-moth you already know by its work. A hand's span of white, flying at first light as the mirrors loose the frost, and flying the way everything here flies — slowly, at the pace of snow falling upward, on wings whose loose scales make no whisper the listening house can find. Watch one work a row of fin-scrub and you are watching irrigation: the scales shed from its wings dust the blades beneath, and the dusted blades shed their dew the faster, down into the wells. Pollinator and watering can, yes — but also partner. The scrub blooms at dusk, when the fog comes in, pale small flowers opening into the vapor, and since the valley has no voices it has perfumes instead: the night air carries the bloom-scent the way other places carry birdsong, and the moth reads it from a field away. Its caterpillars feed at night, soft-mouthed, and their droppings fall into the wells and feed the snails, and the dead moths fall into the wells and feed the snails, and the loop closes without a sound at any point.

The watchbird is the valley's sentinel, its clock, and very nearly its only voice — except that it is anatomically mute, a bird with the apparatus for song and nothing to say, the syrinx worn down by selection to a keepsake. It is grey, thrush-sized, and it hops the fin-tops at dawn in ones and twos, and its alarm is a flag: both wings opening to show the pure white beneath. One flash is a stranger. A held flash is something worse. And the signal relays — a flashing bird is answered by its neighbor's flash, and that by the next, so that news crosses the whole basin in a white wave at the speed of light, in a valley that long ago gave up on the speed of sound. The keepers' slates record a partial lexicon, three handwritings' worth of careful guesses. The bird nests in the fin-trees, in a cup felted with stolen filter-moss, and here is the detail that will stay with you: the chicks beg silently too. No gaping shriek. When the parent lands, the nestlings tilt back their heads and show mouth-linings of pure white — the flag again, flown from birth, the first word of the only language the valley permits. They eat dawn moths on the wing, glean snails from the blades, drink from fin-tips. Pairs hold territories for decades, and generations of keepers logged them like equipment: the north-slope pair, the cistern-field pair, outlasting the hands that named them.

The pale lizard is equipment in the logbooks, almost literally — entries list it among the fittings, and you will understand why when you see one on a pump casing, motionless enough to be a casting, its skin gone the color of dull alloy from a lifetime of basking on dull alloy. It drinks from the drip-line, eats what the moths leave behind, and on the coldest nights it lets itself into the pump housings through the service gaps and sleeps among the warm manifolds: the valley's only hearth-guest, welcome because it is silent and because it asks for nothing but waste heat. Individuals are known. The pump-nine lizard appears in the logbooks across sixty years and three hands, and either it is one very old lizard or a succession of them keeping the same post, and the books do not care which, and neither, you suspect, does the lizard.

Under all of this, in the knee-deep dark of the cushion-moss, runs the slow majority: springtails, mites, a pale threadworm the length of an eyelash, the whole economy of decomposition conducted at the valley's pace, which is to say at a pace that barely qualifies. Nothing is wasted here because nothing is fast enough to waste. A moth wing lies on the moss for a season before it is taken apart. Iron lasts centuries — which is why the loud engines still lie at the west end under their green burial, rivets and all, evidence awaiting a jury of moss. And the pumice itself, the fifty-square-kilometer wick, is inhabited: film and sheen and microscopic graze down in the pore-space, the largest biomass on the table and the one you will never see, the sponge alive.

The machines belong on the census too, and after a week you will enter them there yourself without being asked. They are the valley's megafauna. The fin-stacks are sessile grazers, radiating cold into the night fog, each ringed by its own pen of enriched growth. The mirrors are a flock — ten thousand heliotropes turning to the light without a sound — and some of them have been standing so long that the scrub has grown up through their gimbals and they turn anyway, half citizen, half fixture, tracked by sun and rooted by moss. The crawlers are the great herbivores, a season to a field, and when one halts mid-row for reasons of its own the keepers do not repair it. They wait. A crawler's idea of haste is a week. The pumps speak in the valley's only voice, the hum you feel in the sternum and cannot hear — a heartbeat, if you want it, under fifty square kilometers of held breath. And somebody minds them. The obelisk's shims are bright cement, months old at most. A gauge-glass somewhere has been polished. A path through the moss shows the parted line of felt-soled passage, leading away and never, when you look for it, toward.

Which brings you to the keeper, the census of one, perhaps. The hut stands open: stove, boots, slates, the logbook with its entries stretching from daily to yearly, its handwriting changing three times in a century, its final page eleven years old and four words long. *Yield steady. Quiet holds.* You can read that as an ending. The valley does not seem to have read it that way. Somewhere in your first days — the third morning, say, or the fifth — you will find a slate leaned against your pack, in a tidy hand: the courtesies, the schedule of fines, a small drawn map of the cistern fields, and at the bottom, last, where a voice would put it last too, *welcome*. The valley greets you in writing. If you stay the season you may never see the hand's owner; or you may, once, at dusk, across a field — a grey figure, felt-soled, standing at the pace of the crawlers, watching you the way the table watches everything, without hurry. By the listening house there is a row of white-painted stones with cushion-moss planted on the ground beneath each, the eldest graves green to the tops of their markers, the newest grave dated eleven years ago, and the shims on the obelisk are newer than that. The company is gone. The wage is gone. The keeping continues the way moss inherits stone — because it is there, and it is quiet, and it works. Whether the keeper you don't see is the last of the line, or a successor who came up the penstock line from the lowlands where the taps still open, or whether the function has simply passed into the plant itself and the valley now keeps its own books, the census cannot say. The census only notes that the maintenance is current, the courtesy is current, and the human entry in the ledger stands at one, or possibly zero, and the valley is unbothered by the difference.

It will offer you one monster story, so that you can say you heard one. The old logbooks mention *the visitor*: a broad track in the ash, found on mornings after the deepest fogs, never seen in the making, speculated over in three handwritings across sixty years — a strayed animal from below the rim, a machine off its line, once, wonderfully, *something old in the fog*. The resolution is in the log too, in the third hand, dated mid-century, and it is the valley's whole character in one sentence: the visitor is a glass snail, crossing the ash at night on a foot slick with condensate, and the broad mysterious track is the width of a fingernail, written in silver. Even the mysteries here resolve into something small, slow, mute, and mostly water.

Because that is the true population, in the end, and the last entry in your census: the water itself, the guest everything else serves. It is the only inhabitant that arrives and departs — pouring through the cols at dusk in slow white bodies, laying itself down across fifty million square meters, gathered bead by bead all night by leaf and fin and ceramic alike, the made and the grown doing the same work by the same patience, and slipping away before dawn down the penstock, a river that has never once touched the ground. Everything else on the table — snail, moth, bird, lizard, mite, moss, mirror, crawler, keeper — is a way of slowing the guest down, of keeping it one hour longer, of counting it. At dusk, when the light goes from white to pearl and the fin-stacks begin to cool and the first beads form indistinguishably on steel and leaf, take your census then. Everything is awake. Everything is at work. A white wing opens somewhere and folds again — not alarm, only a bird settling — and a moth rises into the fog, and a snail you cannot see drinks a drop you cannot hear, and the drop that falls the length of a hand into its cup falls, as always, without a sound, because no sound is what it is for. The valley keeps its books in two columns — what was gathered, what was spent — and every living thing on the Bright Table is an entry in both.
