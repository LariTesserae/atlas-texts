**The Inhabitants**

Begin with the rules, because the rules are the census. Nothing swims. Nothing grazes. Nothing hunts. Take away the chase, the mouth, and the swim-bladder, and what remains is not a stripped world. What remains is most of what life is, given time enough to become itself.

By weight, the world is felt. By number, it is the small things in the sediment and the fall. By age, it is the things in the vaults. By reach — by the distance over which one living thing can be said to touch another — it is the threading in the seams, and the threading is the strangest of it. Take them in order, top to bottom, the way the Snow goes.

**The Rime**

Even the Roof is pastured, though thinly.

Not everything falls. The lightest fraction of the world's dead — waxes, oils, the gas-filled husks of spent spore-cases — rises instead, and collects against the ice, and in the hollows of the flutes a grey film strains the rising the way the veils strain the falling. The rime. It is one cell thick in most places, lace more than mat, and it is the ascetic of the world: a mote arriving is a season; a good decade is measured in motes. It grows at a pace that makes the floor-felt look extravagant, and it is perhaps the oldest continuous tissue anywhere, because nothing disturbs it. Nothing falls on it. The Gyre cannot lean it. The Snow never comes.

Its disturbances arrive from within its own ceiling. Where the ice rejects its salt, the brine drains down through the rime first, and the rime dies in a ring around every brinicle's birth, and the ring is re-creeped over centuries, slower than the floor's recovery, because the energy up here is thinner than anywhere. And here and there the rime has grown inward and downward across the mouth of a sealed well — a veil across a shaft no one has sounded, straining nothing, for longer than the frond-forests have existed. The gardens took the wells back. They take everything back, eventually. It only took them longer at the top.

**The Fallers**

Between Roof and floor, in the filed water of the column, live the only creatures whose habitat is a direction.

A faller is born in the rising and made for the falling. When a province of the gardens releases its spores in its single week, the bloom lifts more than spores: it lifts cysts, grains smaller than the motes around them, which have waited in the sediment — decades, some of them, tilled and re-tilled — for the chemistry that says *rise*. In the rising they hatch. What hatches is a thread, and the thread lengthens in the falling, spinning itself out into filaments longer than a hallway, a net without intent, and for years it drifts down through the strata with the Snow, straining the Snow, fed by encounter. Nothing pursues. Things meet.

The fall is not a journey between places. The fall is the place. To descend through the column is to read the centuries with the skin — here the water that last touched ice four hundred years ago, here the stratum laid down before the last overturning — and a faller carries that reading down in its body, into its cysts, which are denser than the parent and fall faster, down into the Snow and the sediment and the long wait for the next bloom. A faller's only choice in its life is its rate of descent. That is the whole of its freedom, and it spends it carefully, braking with its filaments in the rich layers, closing itself in the poor ones.

And sometimes — an event rarer than a brinicle — two filaments touch in the dark, two fallers whose paths the Gyre has been bending toward each other for a decade, and where they touch they exchange what they carry, and drift apart, and are widowed by the current, and never meet anything again. Mating here is not a behavior. It is a geography that briefly agrees with itself.

Nothing eats the fallers in mid-column. When they die they simply join what they fed on. The fallers become their own weather.

**The Old Growth**

The gardens, seen closer, are not many things. They are few things, very large, very old.

The felt is not a crowd of small individuals. In any given province it is one individual, or a handful — genets spread through the seams of the masonry, continent-wide, continuous for longer than seems possible, their holdfasts threaded into the joints of the world. It eats what the stone gives up. Its enzymes unpick the joint-compound a finger's width a decade, and this is the world's only mining and its only erosion, and it is performed by the oldest living things in it.

The fronds have no age, only size. Nothing eats them — eating is a habit of fast worlds, and there was never the budget here for a mouth — so a frond that escapes the white ring of a brinicle and the slow burial of the Snow simply continues, a handspan a century, for millennia. Their lean is a record: each frond leans with the sum of every decade it has lived, and where the Gyre shifted once, long ago, the old fronds carry a kink at the height of that century, held forever, a history of the current written in posture. Nothing here dies of anything but the world.

The veils are the closest thing here to machinery: a filament curtain spans a doorway in two centuries and then works — the only word for it — straining the Snow from still water, for as long as the door stands. The banners hang in the mouths of the hollows and drink the seep. In the stairwells the felt lies cupped in the hand-worn hollows, pooled where hands once pooled. It does not know what cupped it first. It grows in the shape it is given.

**The Sextons**

There are walkers. Few, and slow, and old, and they are the only animals with any size to them, and they break none of the rules.

They do not graze. Nothing grazes. They glean. A sexton eats only what has already fallen: the landed Snow, the dropped frond, the dead under-layer of the felt, never the living tissue it walks across. In all the generations of sextons, nothing in their gut was ever alive when they took it. They are shield-backed and many-legged, and the fastest of them can make a body-length in an hour, in spurts — and mostly they stand, for seasons, digesting. They are drawn, over years, by the taste of brine-death: when a brinicle strikes and the white ring spreads, the sextons of the surrounding provinces begin, that decade, to converge, and a ring is a dynasty's feast, a brief province of plenty worked for a generation while the felt creeps in from the edges behind them, both at a crawl, the harvest and the healing racing at the pace of everything else.

Here the flowers are permanent and the animals are brief. A frond outlives the sexton that walks beneath it; a veil outlasts a dozen of them; the felt has had them all, and will have them all. A sexton's footprints fill with Snow within the year. They leave no trace, which is the world's way. Their cast shells — they molt rarely, and grow for centuries — are the closest thing here to bones, and even the shells are taken down at last by the tillers.

The steps were built for legs. Legs still climb them — small, many, unhurried — but the sextons do not know they are stairs, and the world does not tell them.

There are no parasites. There is not enough of anything to steal, and stealing takes speed.

**The Tillers**

Within the sediment, and down through the layered dead of the deep felt, the archive is blurred, slightly, by its smallest readers.

The tillers are thread-bodied, hand-length at most, and they live within the layering, eating the already-dead, tilling at the rate of the sedimentation itself. One may live two centuries and travel the length of a hall, all of it sideways and downward through the meals of its ancestors. They never surface; the sediment is their world entire, and the Snow is weather that arrives from a sky they have never touched. They taste the strata as they pass: here a bloom-year, laid down dark with spores; here the pale ash of a brinicle ring; here the chemistry band of an overturning. The world keeps its memory in its dead, and the tillers are the slight smudge in the keeping, the copier's error, the softening of the plan.

And the sediment is full of sleepers: the fallers' cysts, the gardens' spores, the tillers' own eggs, all of them waiting — decades, some of them — for the chemistry that says *rise*.

**The Rim and the Skin**

At the brine shores the world is still being built — but only there, and only by its life.

The lush belt, the farmlands of the dark, stands at the mixing edge drinking the gradient, and among the fronds work the crust-builders: slow guilds that precipitate the mineral frost-rims, laying them down grain by grain along the shoreline, reefing the coast outward over eons. The shorelines of the brine lakes are grown, not worn. They are the youngest masonry in the world and the only construction still underway, the works' one living annex, raised by things with no plan at a pace the keepers would have recognized.

And on the lakes themselves — on, not in; the lakes below remain sterile — lives the flattest life in the world. At the interface, where brine water and common water press their chemistries together, a film holds, one cell thick, sheet-wide: the skin. It eats the mixing. When a falling mote crosses the surface and the tremor spreads, the skin rides the ripple and drinks the stir it makes, and settles, and a ripple crossing a brine lake is the whole of a coast's weather, and the skin is the coast's only tenant, a two-dimensional pasture on the surface of water that nothing can enter.

**The Finished**

In the clean vaults, in the finished water whose chemistry is complete, the world keeps its oldest lives.

They are relicts — lineages long vanished from the open ocean, persisting in the sealed hollows at nearly zero rate: a cell-lineage dividing once a century, neither growing nor dying in any way that could be observed, kept by stillness the way the vaults are kept clean. Not dormant; dormancy implies waking. Finished. Their cells are museums that are also the exhibits, and some of them are older than the frond-forests, older than the provinces overhead, older than the fall of the Snow that buried the gates their vault is entered by. Every clean vault is a reliquary with one relic, alive.

And once in a century, somewhere across the world, a vault lets go after its million years — and a finished lineage that has had no news since before the forests existed receives, in its last hours, all the news at once: the younger water flooding in, the chemistry of ten thousand centuries arriving in a day. Or, rarer and stranger, a crack opens between two hollows, and two finished waters meet, and the finished meet, and there is an exchange — slow even by this world's measure, a stirring measured in centuries — the only correspondence the deep vaults have ever had.

**The Correspondence**

The gardens talk. Nothing hears. The speech is chemical, the grammar is quorum, and the subject is always the same: whether to flower.

The felt's holdfasts thread the seams of the masonry, and the seams join province to province, and along the threading a signal can travel — a kilometer a year, no faster, taste passed hand to hand through the joints of the world. News moves this way, useless as warning, kept anyway: a brinicle strike, an overturning's new chemistry, a vault-fall's pressure. And somewhere in the threading the count is kept — of Groans, of decades, of the state of the water — and when the count crosses some threshold held in no tissue and in all of them, a province blooms. The world's one fast act is decided by its slowest conversation.

The blue glow is not the message. The blue is the accent of the message, spilled — light released where the pulse passes, leaking out of the speech into the dark, and there is no eye anywhere in the world to receive it, and it happens anyway. A hillside answers in sequence, kin mapped in light, and then dark again, and years before the next. Whether the Correspondence amounts to a thought is a question with no one to ask it of. It has no observer, no symbol, no hurry. Not a mind — a quorum. But the quorum has outlasted everything that ever asked questions here, and it is still deliberating, and the deliberation is the world talking to itself about when to flower, and the conversation takes a century, and the flowering takes a week, and that is the whole of it.

**What the Keepers Left Living**

Of the builders, the work says everything and the life says two things, and holds them both, and prefers neither.

Perhaps the gardens are theirs: domesticates, planted to trace the works, to mine the masonry at the rate the plan allowed, to keep the water by keeping its chemistry — the living filter of a reservoir whose keepers are gone, the maintenance that outlived the maintained. The tracing is very faithful. Some frond-lineages grow only in the centers of channels, as if bred to flumes; the veils span doorways as though doors were designed to hold them.

Or perhaps the gardens are simply what came after — opportunists, feeding where the joints leach, and the faithfulness is only physics, and the city is outlined in its overgrowth the way any ruin is outlined, by seep and seam.

The felt has had their substance either way by now, and the question has gone out of the world. But this much the census can say: the keepers persist at the pace of everything else — distributed, diluted, fallen and re-fallen a thousand times. Every frond holds atoms of them. Every faller strains them. The sextons glean them from the Snow without knowing, and the tillers till them, and the rime against the Roof lifts their lightest fraction to the ice they once cut wells through. In this sense, and only in this sense, someone still lives here: not kept the way the works are kept, but kept the way the water is kept — held, slowed, moved without haste from one province of the world to another.

**The Census**

No one lives here. It is inhabited to the last seam.

Inhabited by the rime in its flutes, straining the rising; by the fallers in their falling, reading the centuries through their skins; by the felt in its seams and the fronds in their lean and the veils at their work; by the sextons at their gleaning and the tillers in the layering; by the rim at its building and the skin on the water; by the finished in the vaults, holding their zero; by the Correspondence in its slow saying; by the sleepers in the sediment, waiting for the chemistry that says *rise*. Nothing swims, and nothing hunts, and nothing watches, and nothing is in a hurry, and the lights come on sometimes, and no one sees them, and they are not wasted.

And over all of it the Snow keeps falling — a few body-lengths a day, everywhere, always — and everything in it was alive once, or was part of something alive: a frond, a faller, a veil, a sexton's cast shell, a keeper — all of them falling at last at the same pace, all of them arriving, all of them becoming the floor that feeds the gardens that trace the works that keep the water.

Nothing is coming. Nothing is needed. Everything is touching something, and the touching is the whole of society, and it is enough, and the dark receives them one at a time, at the pace the place has chosen, and keeps them.
