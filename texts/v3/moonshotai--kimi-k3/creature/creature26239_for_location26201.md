Count what lives here and you end up counting the same thing three times. Every citizen of the basin is a vessel, a pipe, or a mouth. The vessels keep the water. The pipes move it. The mouths take it — and pass it on, tissue to tissue, mouth to mouth, down the long relay that is the only river left. Everything else about the place — the guilds, the queues, the truces, the thefts — is negotiation among the three. So ask of anything you meet not what it eats but where it keeps its water, and what it will trade for more. It will tell you. Nothing here can afford to be mysterious about that.

Spill a drop on the open pans — you will do it only once — and the desert answers. The salt-flies arrive on wings out of the heat-shimmer itself, before the drop has finished darkening the crust. The crack-runners come next, pale as the undersides of the plates they live beneath, pouring out of the polygon seams quick as mercury. Then the under-stone ones, the soft blind sippers that cannot survive a minute of open air and spend that minute anyway, because the minute is the whole point. They can smell water at a dilution that wetter worlds would call absence. For a few seconds the bare salt seethes; then the drop is gone, and the crowd goes back into the stone, and the pan lies empty again — but it is not empty. It is armed. The pans are not a desert with animals in it. They are an attendance, kept up hour after hour, for the one event this world allows: not rain — nothing here knows the word — but spill. Whatever strays over the rim by accident, seed or lost winged thing, is met by the same readiness, and accepted the way a dock accepts cargo.

The walkers come out of the shimmer legs-first, the way ships rise over a horizon hull-down. They walk at night and stand by day, sleeping on their feet in their own thin shadows. They steer by stars, and the sky here has never once been wrong to them — no cloud has ever crossed it — so they are the finest navigators the world has ever required, crossing trackless salt between districts days apart and arriving within their own length of the wells. Their muzzles weep brine all day, and the crust builds into white masks; the old ones are white-faced entirely, like elders powdered for ceremony. Look closely at one as it passes and you are looking at a city in transit: seeds riding in the wet bombs of its dung, builder-larvae dormant in the salt-crust of its coat, burrs in the tail-tuft, spores and grit in the clefts of its splayed feet. When a walker comes in off the pans, the district waters it before it asks — the basin's one free drink, which is not free. The walker pays in cargo, and at the end it pays in real estate. Old walkers feel their death the way they feel water, from far off, and they turn out onto the open salt to meet it — most often toward the bare gaps between districts. The city follows: seedlings first, then thicket, then a root-line extended out across the pan toward the founding. The Stand grows by being chosen by the dying. Dig at the heart of the oldest grove, down through the braided plinths, and you will find the founding bones fused into the wood — a ribcage like a vault, a skull like a cornerstone. Every district began as a body. The oldest groves are tombs that have outgrown the word.

The welltrees themselves are citizens before they are timber. Each one is thousands of stems that agreed, long ago, to be a single animal, and they have temperaments. Young districts weep freely; old groves meter their mercy; drinkers know the generous flutes from the stingy ones the way you know faces. Press your ear to a root-plinth and the seep-pulse you catch is the tree's own pulse — slower than any heart, and it has never once stopped. The trees practice medicine on themselves and surgery on their enemies: a licensed wellwright is suffered, even welcomed, but an outlaw borer's tunnels are flooded with sap gone bitter as gall. Their salt must be harvested or the rind chokes beneath its own armor, so the salt-gnawers work the south faces — blunt-jawed, lichen-slow climbers who rasp the beards and weeping crusts away and are paid in rind-tissue and the sweetness under it. They stack the salt in pale bricks at the district's edge, a dock of cargo waiting for the walkers. Old groves export salt; young foundings cannot start their pioneer gardens without it. Salt flows out, seed flows in, and the walkers carry both directions of the ledger.

The chisel-beaks — the wellwrights — drill at night, and before they drill they listen. A wellwright works with its skull pressed to the bark, hearing the seep-pulse climb and reading the tone of the water-wood behind the rind, and where the sound is fullest it sets its beak. A licensed hole weeps but never bleeds; a bled tree seals its wells across the whole district, and the guild goes thirsty for one member's greed, so greed is handled internally, at night, beak to beak. When a hole runs low the wright caps it with a wax-plug and drills another, and the old trunks are dotted with plugs like rivets. Their wages come to the wound: the borers and sap-thieves that smell a fresh hole arrive within the hour, and the wellwright eats them off the bark it has just opened — surgeon and sanitation crew in one animal, paid by the complications.

At dawn the queue forms at the weeping holes, and the order in it is older than any animal standing in it. Climbers with wings folded like shut fans; small armored things; the soft-limbed folk of the under-gallery. The queue-cleaners work the line — quick, many-legged pickers — and a drinker holds still to be picked, of salt-crusts and burr-larvae and the mites of the mid-loft, because to be cleaned is worth the wait. The cleaner's pay is the trickle that runs down the bark below the hole. Everyone in the queue is being used and everyone consents. It is the basin's one daily parliament, convened by thirst and dissolved by full bellies.

Nearby, the stillness has tenants. The husk — that is the only name its shape permits — sits at the edge of the well-glade looking like nothing: like the dead foot of a welltree, like a slab of fallen tower-ceramic gone over with salt-weathering. It may have been sitting there when the well was first drilled. It waits the way the pans wait, at full attention, for weeks. Then a drinker leaves heavy — the one that drank deepest, that walks slowest with its belly of water — and the stillness takes it, once, cleanly, and the queue does not scatter, because the arithmetic is understood by every animal standing in it: the wells levy a tax, and the slowest payer pays it. The husk drinks its kill's blood first, before any meat — here, blood is a beverage before it is a fluid — and then it may not kill again for a season. Other stillnesses hunt other levels: the dusk-glider that rides the flue-draft down at evening, silent as a thrown shadow; the pale eel of the root-cellars, no longer than your arm, that has never in its life seen light and does not feel the lack.

When something dies, anywhere, at any level, the first guild is there before the body has finished cooling. The scouts do not touch; they confirm — salt-flies, circling — and their circling is the clock-in. Then the openers, whose shear-jaws are exactly as strong as skin. Then the strippers, who take the muscle away in economical sheets; the joint-men, who disarticulate; the marrow-gnawers, acid-mouthed, who soften bone until it can be drunk; and last the polishers, mite-small, who lick the site until the salt shows through again. Each guild's work releases the scent that summons the next — the timetable is written in smell, and it is kept to the minute. Nothing hunts the reclaimers. In a world where burial must outrun rot, you do not eat the undertakers. On the open pans the timetable runs gentler: the relay takes its tithe of a dead walker and leaves the rest, for even the undertakers know a founding when they smell one, and the wet that remains belongs to the seedlings.

The builders are a thousand times smaller than their architecture. A stilltower is a colony's skeleton turned outward — silt and salt and the builders' own mineral excretions, laid down grain by grain over centuries. Technically, bone. The builders are blind; they work by taste and by temperature, and a tower is a long argument about airflow conducted entirely in chemistry — and the argument is good, because the towers breathe. There are mixers who chew the mortar, masons who lay it, flue-keepers who feel the tower's breath in their antennae and work the throat-stones that throttle or open the draw, felt-tenders who farm the crowns, and deep in the warm core the brood-caste, to whom the others carry water mouth to mouth. After a scouring storm they plate the windward faces thicker. When a tower fails and comes down, the builders are the first reclaimers of their own: they mine the rubble for its mineral and eat their dead tower back into solution, and the fall feeds the rebuilding. The creak you hear from the towers after dark is new ceramic setting in the cold. The city gets taller at night, while no one is watching.

In the cool deep levels hang the vessels. They begin as ordinary young of their kind. The keepers choose them — by what sign is the guild's own secret — and feed them, and feed them, until the body outgrows every other use; then they hang from the chamber ceilings by fused grips, swollen past translucence, slow-pulsed, dreaming if they dream. The keepers are small, many-fingered, nocturnal, and they know every vessel in their charge the way a miser knows coins — by weight, by taste, by a name that is not a name but a flavor. In lean seasons they tap them, and a tapped vessel is not killed but drawn down, and refilled in fat times; the oldest have been emptied and filled a hundred times and hang in the dark like the city's memory of every drought it has survived. It is not domestication. It is older than domestication, and it runs both directions: the vessels are fed from the common wells in plenty, and the commons drinks from them in want, and which of the two species is the livestock is a question the basin has never needed to answer. The same keepers make the bead-rounds before dawn, drinking the night's condensation off the flue-stone — and ahead of them go the bead-thieves, soft-limbed climbers who lap the stone at the coldest hour, before the rounds arrive. They are thieves, and they are tolerated, because their tongues clean the flue, and a clean flue yields sweeter water. The keepers' true complaint is not theft. It is untidiness.

Below all of it, the green dusk has its own citizens, and some of them are born in the under-gallery and die there without ever seeing open sky, and do not feel the lack — the sky, to them, is a rumor the moths tell. There are pale climbers, and warren-diggers whose tunnels keep the deep air moving, and down where the plinths enter the cracked floor, the root-tenders: slow, pale, smaller than your hand. They groom the roots, scrape away the deep scale, and guide the questing rootlets toward the sound of water — for water can be heard down there, seeping in the fractures, and the tenders hear it the way the wellwrights hear the climb. Their pay is sap, sipped at plinth-wounds they themselves keep small. At evening, when the cold comes down off the rim and the flues begin to draw, the gliders launch from the high bridges and ride the city's inhalation down through the levels — commuters on a breath. And when the floor settles, that deep thump of a crust still lifting from the weight of the sea it lost, every level feels it, and for one breath the whole city pauses, even the moths on the wing — and then resumes, the way you resume after remembering something.

At any given hour, a third of the basin's population is not, strictly speaking, happening. Sealed in wax, lacquered into eggs, folded into self-closed cells and breathing once an hour, the sleepers wait — a year, five years — for the night the wind remembers water. When that night comes, the city wakes all at once and spends itself. Flowers open by the million — the stingiest flowers in the world, offering almost nothing, because almost nothing is the going rate — and the big moths are already flying, and behind them come the woken: a generation that has waited six years in the dark lives its entire life in one soft night. It hatches, mates, lays, seals its children into wax against the next wind, and is dead by morning. At dawn the galleries are drifted with spent wings, and the reclaimers work the festival for a week. The city spends its windfall in a night; the unmaking guilds bank the change.

The canopy is the emptiest level — the crowns cannot afford tenants; leaves are too expensive to share. It belongs to the moths passing through, to the flowers on the rare soft nights, and to the grafters. The grafters are the slowest citizens of all, long-lived, deliberate, moving at the pace of the wood they tend. They taste the sap of districts and read their thirst the way you read weather, and where water should flow between districts they cut the unions — scion to stock, crown to crown — that will carry it. A graft takes a century to move water. No grafter lives to finish its own work, so the guild keeps its memory in apprenticeship: an elder teaches a young one the taste-map of the whole Stand — which district is joined to which, where the old debts of water run — and the young one spends its long life adding a single paragraph. If the whole Stand is, as it seems, one plumbing system with many mouths, that is the grafters' doing, across ten thousand lives. They are the closest thing the basin has to a senate: a small, slow animal, high in the crowns, tasting a root.

And then there are the ones who never came up. The brine in the deep fractures is not uninhabited. It never was. Down in the cold and the pressure: brine-swarms small as motes; pale ribbon-things grazing the fracture walls; and in the deepest pools the old ones — eel-bodied, longer than a stilltower's flue, slow as geology. One of them may have been alive when the first walker died on the first pan. The root-tips come down to them through hundreds of meters of dark like fishing lines, and the relicts graze the rootlets, and the trees let them, and whatever passes between root and relict in that dark is the oldest arrangement in the basin — older than the Stand, older than the pans, continuous with the sea. Once in a great while a well draws up more than water: a milky pull of larvae, the sea's own plankton lifted by accident into daylight, and some drinker at a dawn well tastes, for one sip, the old world — rich, dim, crowded — and does not know what it has tasted. It only knows the water was, briefly, alive. For the deep ones, nothing has ended. The sea did not leave; it quieted, and cooled, and slowed, and then — strangely, over a few thousand years, which to them is a season — it put down roots, and began to climb. The city believes it is holding the last of the water. The deep ones have not noticed there is anything to hold. For them it is simply night, and night is when the roots come down.

Count them all, then — vessel, pipe, and mouth. The waiting pans and the walking arks. The licensed beaks and the tolerated thieves. The hanging vessels, the patient husk, the sleepers sealed against a wind that may not come for years, and below everything the old ones, for whom the drought is only a kind of weather. The census comes out the same every time. The basin has one inhabitant, wearing many bodies. The honest record is a single line: water, resident. Everything else is the address.
