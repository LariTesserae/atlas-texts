The largest animal in the world is five millimeters long.

It is an isopod — white, eyeless, built along groundwater lines: flattened, many-legged, all antennae and caution. There are a few hundred of them, and that is the entire animal census. The rest of the world's inhabitants are two confirmed films of bacteria and a third that is rumored, plus the uncountable. Everything else is weather.

They arrived the way everything arrives here: as eggs, carried down through fractures too fine to see, in the same water that built the room and still breathes through it. Then the ark sealed behind them. Whether it still takes passengers — whether a new clutch occasionally washes in with the seep — no one knows, because no one can stand in the room to look, and the isopods are not telling. What is known of them comes from glances. You could watch the pool margins for an hour and see two.

They live in the cool lanes. The chamber's surfaces run from skin-warm to kettle-hot, and between those extremes lies a band, rarely more than centimeters wide, where wetness and temperature briefly agree to be survivable: the pool's south rim, the seams where seep water lands before the room reclaims it, the places the warm ceiling-rain reaches first. The band shifts with the breath, and the isopods shift with it. Everything alive at this address lives on a gradient — the films on theirs, the isopods on several at once — and the room itself lives on the deepest gradient of all, the difference between the aquifer and the still-cooling mountain. The residents inherit the landlord's condition.

Their whiteness deserves a thought. Cave things lose their pigment because pigment is for light, and caves have none. This cave is bright — bright enough, at mid-exhale, to read newsprint by — and it makes no difference. The eyes went long ago, in the dark aquifer, before the room existed; and with no eyes anywhere in the world, pigment buys nothing even now. So the isopods cross black glass like small white declarations, conspicuous to an audience that has never existed. They molt as they grow, and their shed skins — translucent, perfect — are the only litter in a world without dust. Some wash into the splash zone and are taken by the silica: the room is casting its own fossils in real time, a five-millimeter record in opal for whatever drills here in some later age.

What the isopods have instead of light is the rest of it. They do not hear the room; they are touched by it. The breath is their weather — the inhale drawing the water films thin, the brief warm rain ticking down, the exhale's fizz and heat spreading up through the margin into their band. The hourly thud arrives through the shell like the world changing its mind. And then there is the bell. When a quartz seam lets go, 2,300 hertz rings through rock and water and five millimeters of animal alike; for twenty seconds the world says one pure thing, and for a minute afterward the dripping from the ceiling falls in time. The drips entrain. The water keeps the beat until it forgets. Whether the isopods do too — whether grazing pauses, whether some small rhythm in them locks to the decaying note — is precisely the kind of thing no one will ever know, because knowing would require watching, and watching requires standing inside. It would be stranger if they were the only things in the room that didn't keep time.

Time is what they have most of. Thirty years of breath is perhaps a few dozen isopod generations — enough for drift, in a population of hundreds, to work; enough for selection at the hot margins to have begun its slow editing; not enough for the work to be finished. They are becoming a thing with no name that lives nowhere else, and the room is on schedule to die before the becoming is done. This inverts the usual order, in which a habitat outlives its lineages. But the species was old in the dark aquifer long before the blister inflated, and the blister — a few centuries at best, from first sip to silence — is the short-lived party here. The isopods are not natives of the room. They are natives of the dark between rooms, spending a bright century ashore.

Beneath their feet, the first film. The rust-orange comma at the pool's south rim is hand-sized and mostly not alive: each iron bacterium extrudes a twisted stalk of rust around itself and moves on, and the patch is the accumulated trail of abandoned sheaths — a district of empty houses, living only at its working edge. It grows at micron rates, layer on layer, and may be as old as the breath. Section it, and you might count the same thirty years the splash-zone couplets count: the same diary kept in a second hand. It eats a gradient — ferrous iron rising in the seep, a whisper of oxygen descending from above, life exactly where the two meet, which is why it has a rim, a margin, a shape. And it signs the surface. The wet meadow above, with its rust-stained seeps, is this same chemistry or its downstream echo: the chamber's handwriting, legible from the air, spelling nothing more specific than *warm water carrying iron*. The room cannot be found by looking. But it does, in a small way, rust in public.

The white veil does the air handling. Tissue-thin, draped at the warmest vent, pressed against the upper edge of the survivable because that is where the food rises, the sulfur oxidizers eat the sulfide at the source — which is why a chamber with volcanic plumbing and a boiling pool smells like a hot radiator in a stone room instead of a struck match. Between them, the veil and the rust patch set the atmosphere: they scrub the oxygen that seeps down, consume nearly all the sulfur, and leave the air as steam, carbon dioxide, and clean mineral warmth. The air of this world is a biological product, made in decades, by two patches small enough to overlook. A planet ran that experiment once at full scale and took an eon. The room is the experiment done small, and fast, and unobserved.

Perhaps there is a third film somewhere. Perhaps not. The census of this world is permanently approximate, and below the films it dissolves entirely: every bacterial mat carries its viruses, which are certainly the majority of the world's inhabitants by number and will never be counted at all. The room is strange this way — knowable to a fraction of a hertz from kilometers away, unknowable at micron scale from any distance. The macrophysics broadcasts. The biology keeps its counsel.

But the census may be taken at the wrong scale. The question the room keeps posing is whether the thing that lives at this address is *in* the chamber, or whether the chamber is in it. Consider the claims. It drinks rain and works. It holds its temperature across seasons with a thermostat made of nothing but solubility. It heals its wounds in days. It keeps a diary in banded stone and a time standard in a tone stable across years. And it grew — it deposited its own wick and tuned the geometry as it went, which is not endurance but development. Birth, metabolism, homeostasis, repair, memory, maturation, a voice, a scheduled death. The checklist fails only on the two items its authors care most about: boundary and lineage. The room is not a bag. It is a knot — the wick continuous with the aquifer, the steam continuous with the ceiling rain; any line drawn around it leaks. And it makes no copies. Chambers do not beget chambers. The mountain does that.

So the scale shifts, and the full census reads: a few hundred isopods, two films and a rumor, uncountable viruses — and, one scale up, the circulation itself, the dome's long warm argument with its rain, of which the chambers are cells or blooms: the places where the metabolism briefly runs dense enough to glow and ring. The room does not contain the life. The room is contained in it. And the scales recurse — the isopods ride the films, the films ride the chamber, the chamber rides the dome, and the dome rides a planet that is itself a heat engine with a thin film doing chemistry at a temperature boundary. The arrangement at the pool's south rim is the planetary arrangement done small, one of them lit from outside by a star, the other lit from inside by its own work. That may be why the room reads as recognition rather than anomaly.

All of which returns to the light. It never goes dark, and nothing there can see. The light does no work for the residents; it is waste made visible, beautifully disposed of, and it stays home — absorbed by black glass, doubled in black mirrors, spent as a whisper of warmth. What leaves is the note. The room's voice emigrates kilometers through rock to whatever might be listening; its light is for no one and goes nowhere. The only witnesses the chamber has ever had are blind, and what they witness is pressure, taste, and the bell. Nothing in the room was ever arranged for viewing. That is why it is clean.

The end is already scheduled. The silica wins its slow argument with the vents; the breath shallows; and the census closes roughly in reverse order of loudness. First the note fails — one day, between one hour and the next — and the drips lose their beat, and the seismometers kilometers away register an absence, which will be the entire obituary. Then the lights go, because the light was only ever work, and the work has stopped. Then the temperature band slips. The veil starves and mineralizes into the stone it fed beside. The rust patch stops at its last micron and becomes a thin orange band in the silica record — the diary closing on a line of rust, the diarists written into the final entry, buried in their own binding. Some of the isopods may wash back down the fractures into the dark between rooms, where the species was old before the chamber was young. The population of the world turns out to have been one deme of an older, darker nation. The room was their bright century, and the century ends.

And somewhere else in the cooling mountain, a crack reaches the water table, and a blister sips and flashes and begins to glow. The same aquifer connects the whole foam. The eggs arrive within a few years; the films seed from the same water; a rust comma gathers at some pool's south rim, and eyeless grazers cross it, white on black, conspicuous to no one. The light comes on — sourceless, clean — for no one, which is whom it has always been for. And the note finds its pitch and holds it, and this time, somewhere in the dark above the dome, there may be listeners who know what a tone like that means.
