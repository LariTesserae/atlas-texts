## The Tenants

Take the census.

It starts easily, because the categories are few. Nothing with a mouth. Nothing with wings that stays. Nothing with a voice — and nothing with ears, either, so the loudest room in the world is silent to everything that lives in it. If you count only the kind of life that moves, the census ends here: no one. The crater is empty, and loud about it.

But stay. Count the other kind — the rooted, the threaded, the dissolved — and the count slows, and then it does not end, because it turns out to be nearly everything.

---

The oldest family lives inside the mountain. Down in the fractured rock, where the rain goes down and the steam comes back up, in the warm wet dark of the plumbing, there are beings too small to see that eat the mountain's breath directly — hydrogen, sulfur, iron, the raw chemistry of the exhale. They have never needed light and never will. They were here before the garden was a spore, and they will be here after the lake. Every vent is tenanted at the throat, zoned by heat more strictly than any posted law: this kind to exactly here and no farther, that kind a hand's width out, the border a matter of two degrees and never disputed. The benches of sulfur and salt and rust are the mountain's own deposits, but the tenants farm them, grazing gradients of temperature and acid the way pasture gets grazed — and some of the color of the fumarole field, the brightest of it, is not chemistry at all. It is population.

Everything else arrived by air.

There is no other way in. The crater is an island on top of a mountain, and every green thing in it is an immigrant or an immigrant's child. Spores are light enough to circle the world; the wind is indiscriminate; a thousand years is long enough for even the unlucky ticket to land once. They are still arriving — every cloud that pours through the notch carries applicants, spores and dust and the odd winged thing blown up from the world below, which will not last the night. Watch the succession run down the floor and you are watching the boats come in, in order: first the dust-dark crust of single cells on the new ash, stitching the surface together like a scab; then the gray lava moss that can live on bare rock and prefers it; then, behind it, the deep moss, building.

The deep moss is the true architecture. A meter of it, and only the top hand's breadth is alive; below that it goes brown and sour and cold, a reservoir more than a soil, the cloud held in storage. Everything with roots stands in it the way pilings stand in harbor mud. The moss is the citizenry and the city at once. The trees are only its tallest tenants.

And all of it runs on the gloom. The rare blade of midday sun is wasted on the garden — the fronds have gone black-green, tuned to pewter, taking the dark as their portion and asking for nothing brighter.

Within the mattress, scale plays tricks. The clubmosses stand in stiff little forests a hand high — complete forests, canopied, self-important, going about their business beneath the business of everything else. Their ancestors were trees the height of towers, the kind that made the coal measures; this is what the dynasty has come to, knee-high to nothing, and it seems untroubled. The filmy ferns keep to the seams with the liverworts, their fronds a single cell thick, translucent as wet paper — you could read through them, if there were print and light. They are the most precarious tenure in the crater: they can live only where the wet is absolute, and they do, staking everything on the cloud's word. So far the cloud has kept it.

The tree ferns are the water towers. Each crown is a drip-catcher; each trunk a shaggy tenement of root and moss and smaller ferns; and beneath them the drip goes slowest and steadiest — they make their own weather down there, the cloud's rain delayed an hour and delivered in fat drops. Nothing about a tree fern hurries. They measure time in fronds.

The elfin trees are the landlords, and they are burdened past reason — their own leaves the minority of what they carry, the rest fern in the crotches, moss on the bark, moss on the moss, vines threading crown to crown until the canopy is a single stitched fabric with trees inside it somewhere, like wire inside plaster. They flower, and nothing comes. There is no one to carry pollen; what seed sets, sets rarely. So the garden renews itself the slow way — layering, runners, spores — and the same few bodies have stood here a long time, and their children are their own limbs touching down and taking hold. When one dies it does not fall. The neighbors hold it. The standing dead are still load-bearing — still residents in the only sense the architecture cares about — while the moss climbs them and digests them in place, year by year, standing. Tenure here outlasts life by decades.

---

At the warm margins live the only citizens of the furnace district: the algae, strung in orange and emerald threads through the seeps where the water would scald a hand. Each color keeps its own distance from the heat, zoned by degrees like the vent-throats, touching the frost with one edge and the furnace with the other. When the pools ice over a body's length away, the threads persist in the bleed of warmth — the garden's farthest outposts, its frontier with the engine.

And over all of it, the law of height. The gas lies in the hollows, heavier than air, and everything that lives here knows — the way a town knows its flood line — exactly how tall it may be. The canopy's contour is a contour of breathable air. The pale bare zones are bare not because nothing tried them but because the gas smothers root and frond alike, and the garden stands back from them the way the living stand from floodwater: close, lush, and precisely one meter away. At the very margin, where the poison thins to a breath, the green runs furious — the garden feeding on the edge of the gas, as close as it can stand. It is the one rule every resident obeys. It was never posted. It did not need to be.

---

Then there are the kept.

The hollows hold what falls into them, and what falls in does not leave, and does not even fade. Fronds keep their green for seasons. And the birds — the rare, tired, high-crossing birds that chose the wrong rest — lie folded in the still gas, intact, their colors kept like pressed flowers. One of them, if you kneel at the margin and look down, is built all wrong for lying still: long scythe wings, a narrow body made to sleep on the wind, a migrant that crossed the rim one night and settled into a stillness it mistook for calm. It is the only thing with wings that ever stayed. The archive grows by an item every few years, and it is as close as the crater comes to memory. Everything the hollows take, they keep. They are not particular. Kneel no closer.

---

Which leaves the question of the largest resident.

Call nothing alive that isn't. The mountain is an engine; nothing here violates anything. But look at what the census has to work with. A metabolism — rain in, steam out. A heartbeat — the drumbeat in your sternum, machine time, stroke after stroke. An appetite — heat, pressure, chemistry, perfectly ordinary, never sated. A voice — the only voice in the place, and the loudest in the world. And a garden: a culture of green kept warm and watered in its own exhaust, zoned by its breath, fed by its ash, the way a terrarium is kept — except that no one built the tank, and no one tends it, and it runs anyway. Either the mountain keeps the garden, or the garden has grown itself around the furnace the way moss climbs a standing dead tree. Which of those is true, the crater does not say. It just runs.

So count it one way, and the census is long: thousands of kinds, layered and tenanted, every surface spoken for, the living stacked on the living stacked on the dead still holding them up. Count it the other way, and there is exactly one inhabitant — the stitched thing, moss to fern to tree to cloud to vent, one body with a furnace for a heart, growing in the dark, drinking the mountain's breath, holding up its dead out of habit. The crater does not have a garden. The crater has a tenant, and the tenant is the whole of it.

The census, kept short, the way the crater keeps its rules:

Everything here arrived by air, and everything lives on breath and stone.

Nothing eats. Nothing rots the usual way. The dead hold up the living; the living digest the dead; no one falls.

The air is layered, and every resident knows its height.

Nothing has a voice but the mountain, and the mountain is not alive.

There are thousands of tenants, or there is one. The difference is bookkeeping.

---

It will not keep; tenancies here don't. One wet year and the floor will hold water, and the standing dead will finally lie down, and the archive will dissolve into the lake it was always becoming, and new tenants — small, swimming, indifferent — will move through the drowned rooms. Or the wall will fail through the notch, and the dark will drain, and the garden will die of weather it never knew, out in the open, in the light, like something beached.

But that is later. For now, the crater has no name and neither does anything in it, and the census does not need names. It needs one line.

Fully tenanted. No vacancies. The place is full.
