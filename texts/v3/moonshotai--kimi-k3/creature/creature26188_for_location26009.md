**The Census**

No one has counted them, and no one will, because the counting animal would have to stand outside the sound, and there is no outside the sound — not within the basin. You do not census the Choir. You listen to it. The hum is the census: every life in forty kilometers of paper and wax, summed into one note. Take the note apart and it comes apart into lives.

**The note, taken apart**

Closest to the ear, the hum is feet. Uncountable footfalls on comb and paper, summed past hearing into a dry whisper, the static of passage that never stops because nothing here ever all stops at once. Beneath that, the chewing — the sound of the place being made, always being made. And beneath everything, deepest, the wing-fan.

At every flue and vent stand the fanners: wasps and bees who do nothing but face the moving air and beat their wings, driving it, steering it. Convection does the hauling — the basin's own heat lifts its breath up the chimneys — but the fanners tune the flow, and they have stood at their stations so long that the paper beneath each one is polished to horn by the feet of ten thousand predecessors holding the same stance. At dusk, when the drone shifts pitch, that is largely them: the shift change made audible, day crews folding, night crews taking the beat down a tone. The Choir breathes once a day, and it takes a working population to breathe.

In the chimneys the note breaks open into clicks — the cave-birds ranging the dark — but in the deep halls the hum is one thing again, and everything alive down there lives *inside* it. The drone is weather and cover both. Courtship happens under it, hunting happens under it, flight and murder and birth all conducted inside the note the way fish move inside water. Predators know this. The centipedes, long as your forearm, hold still for hours and read the floor through their legs — the party line of the whole district, every footfall arriving like light bending around a stone. Nothing in the Choir is silent except on purpose. Stillness is a skill long before it is a rest.

**The skin-keepers**

The paper wasps are not one nation but dozens, divided by material the way human builders once divided into masons and thatchers. The crust-wasps lay the white clay-loaded paper that bleaches in the high sun and splits every frosty night. The interior crews work in grey pulp. In the old galleries there is horn-paper, dense with mineral, laid by a lineage that chews stone-dust into its spit.

The frost-splitting means the crust is resealed every single day, and the mending is the basin's first sound: before the steam rises off the chimneys at dawn, the chewing swells. All construction follows the drip. Paper needs water, water falls before dawn, so building has tides, and the tide comes in the dark.

This is where the rule lives: to be still is to be papered. The skin-keepers do not distinguish between substrate and sleeper. Anything unmoving long enough is foundation. A moth that rests too deep wakes pinned by the wing; a lizard that dozes through dawn is a cornice by noon. The walls are full of the still — the slow, the unlucky, the once-curious — and the sleepers who survive are the ones with strategies. There is a moth that spits itself a cell each dusk and chews its way out each dawn, spending its entire adult life being reborn from its own house. The ants nap in shifts of seconds, thousands of small sleeps summing to a colony that never closes its eyes. And the mass itself has not slept — not once, not for an instant — in a hundred thousand years.

**The heat-keepers**

In the Wax Vaults, the warmest air in the basin, live the stingless bees, and they are less a population than a climate. Some of the deep lineages barely fly; they walk the combs from pot to pot, saving their wings for the one night a year wings matter. The honey in the oldest pots has gone black and bitter and strangely medicinal, and there are thieves that specialize in it, and guards that specialize in the thieves.

Here also work the resin-masons — the crews who answer the unanswerable intruder. Anything too large to fight is embalmed where it stands, coated in propolis mid-stride, sealed mid-lunge, so that the vaults hold centipedes arrested in the act of striking and spiders frozen over their eggs like caretakers of amber. Walk the Ghost Margin far to the west and you will find whole chambers of this, mineralized: rooms of fossilized intent. The habit is older than the hills around the hill.

Beyond the vaults the heat leaks outward through comb and backs, and all along the gradient sit the tenants. A frog glues its eggs to vault-wall paper and lets bee-heat do the brooding. Beetles park their young at the warm seams like loaves in an oven. In the deep Choir, cold is measured in distance from bees, and the bees are the only sun the dark has ever risen to.

**The seam-lords**

Where light leaks in, the weaver ants run the world. They stitch living leaves directly into dead paper, and the leaves live — fed by drip-water, held by silk — so that the Rind is a sewn meadow, pasture without soil. On the pasture graze the herds: aphids, tended and milked and moved leaf to leaf by their keepers. The only green herd in the dark world belongs to ants.

Light is a leak, and a leak is property. When a vault collapses and a new shaft opens, the nearest weaver colonies *run* — the old hands call it the green hour — and the first silk laid across the seam decides ownership for years. The Rind is the only battlefront in the Choir that shines.

Every shaft gathers its congregation. Huge-eyed moths queue in the white column of light like bathers at a spring. And from above, on the crust, a bird has learned to fish the shafts, peering down the Choir's windows and taking what queues. Everything in the basin wants, at least once, to stand in the window. Everything that does is seen.

**The still people**

In the Web Halls the spiders span whole chambers with acre-sheets, and the sheets are instruments. Wind off the flues strums them; the spiders read the building's weather in the chord — the strengthening exhale, a distant front, a collapse three halls away arriving as a change in the tuning. They signal by plucking, a language of notes played on the thing they live on and eat from.

Here stillness is camouflage and motion is confession, so everything that crosses the halls has learned to move only when the sheet is already singing. The web-runners are roaches that travel on gusts, stepping in wind-rhythm, so the sheet reads them as weather. There is a beetle that has learned to pluck *empty* — one false note meaning nothing here — and it lives longer than beetles should.

The floor is silk and carcass-mulch a meter deep, felted, because the webs are rebuilt over and over on the same spans and the dead are simply incorporated. The mulch has its own guild — springtails, psocids, undertaker mites — and in the corridors beyond, the velvet worms hunt by glue and patience, their spit the only net that works where everything is listening. The spiders wait for weeks between meals. Patience here is not a virtue. It is an organ.

**The drowned quarter**

The cisterns sit at the bottom, drip-fed, black, the only place the basalt of the original mountain shows through — the world's last unmade surface. Under it the blind swimmers turn their slow circles, pale and eyeless, orienting always to the inflows, sipping whatever the drips carry down: spores, mites, the drowned small. Above them the glow-worm larvae bead the ceilings in blue-green constellations, and their snare-lines hang like rigging, and the swimmers rise sometimes to strip a catch from a fallen line. The only indigenous light in the Choir is a lure. Here, light either leaks or it lies.

On the wax ledges sit the pale frogs, and their whole lives are gathered into one hour. In the deep night the exhale thins to a seep, the drone falls to its floor, and the condensation begins — the drip. Into that hush, every frog in the drowned quarter sings at once: a chorus slotted precisely into the building's single daily pause, in the only hour when there is both falling water and quiet to sing in. They drink then, mate then, lay their eggs then, on ledges above the rising black. The rest of the day they sit and are silent and are not papered only because the ledges are theirs.

A pale cricket station-keeps beneath a reliable drip and defends it. Drip sites are owned, fought over, inherited. In the drowned quarter, a good drip is an inheritance, and the drip-lines on the ceiling are older than the families beneath them.

**The blind farmers**

Deepest and hottest, the leafcutters keep their gardens: the fungus in pale terraced sponges, weeded, fed, tended, luminous at the edges. The cultivar exists nowhere else on earth and cannot — a hundred thousand years of selection in the dark has made it as dependent on the ants as they are on it. The gardeners execute thieves: there is a mold-cricket that steals sponge and is hunted for it relentlessly. And there is a beetle that smells exactly like garden and is suffered to live, grazing the edges, neither livestock nor pest — a tolerated fiction, an old agreement no one remembers signing.

The garden's edges shine, foxfire-green, and the farmers are eyeless. A candle in a room of the blind. Perhaps the glow is a memory of the surface, older than the dark it lights. Perhaps it is for someone who has not arrived. The Choir does not explain itself.

**The commuters**

At dusk the basin's one true rush hour: the bats pour out of the chimneys on the strengthening exhale, and the cave-birds come clicking home against the current. The lanes are laid down by physics and obeyed without exception — the outflow rides the warm rising core of each vent, the inflow hugs the cool walls. The chimney-throat at dusk is the busiest mile of air in the world, and it is pitch dark, and almost no one collides.

In the vents themselves nest the chimney-birds, their cups glued to the walls with pure saliva — nothing cut, everything secreted — and their eggs are incubated in part by the building's own breath, so they time their breeding to the season of strong exhale. The massif is their hearth.

Then, through the night, the rain that falls upward out of the world and downward through the dark: guano, onto floors that never see a leaf. Whole ecologies stand to receive it. Molds bloom on the fresh fall by dawn. Dung-crickets work the piles. And there are flies whose entire adult existence is a single night's deposit — hatch, mate, lay, die — a generation per rain. The commuters are the basin's mouth. The Choir does not go to the forest; the forest is flown in, nightly, and dropped.

**The weather with teeth**

The army ant fronts are forecast in the floors. Days out, the drone gains a hiss, and every species in the path runs its protocol: flee, freeze, climb, seal. The front itself crosses like a weather system, days in passage, everything fleeing before it or being taken. At its edges hunt the front-runners — crickets and beetles that have never seen daylight and work exactly like the antbirds of the world outside, snatching fugitives as they burst from the paper.

Afterward, the scoured corridor blooms in fast succession: molds, springtails, web-runners, then the paperers resealing. The fronts prune the Choir, take its sick halls, strip its accumulations. Call them the white cells of the body. Call them the fever with mandibles. Nothing argues with weather.

**The old tenants**

At the Ghost Margin the abandoned shells stand hollow, comb gone to honeycomb stone, and even stone is inhabited. Mineral-snails rasp the fossil galleries for salts. A mason-wasp re-chews the petrified paper of the dead into pulp for the living — the ancestors, literally re-chewed, house the descendants. In the copal rooms, spiders anchor webs across resin-sealed doorways and fish the halls of the embalmed.

And in the deepest galleries, tunnels through the petrified nests of animals a hundred thousand years dead, the true troglodytes walk. The long-ear is a bat that gave up flight: pale, huge-eared, hunting afoot through the dark. It does not call. It listens to the Choir's own note bending around feet and bodies and walls — it sees by borrowed voice, a parasite of the hum, reading the dark the way you read ripples on water. The glass roach is translucent and tide-locked, its whole year bent to a single night. Eyes here have gone huge or gone; there is no middle price for sight.

**Alate night**

Once a year, at dusk, the chimneys smoke with the winged. The exhale carries them up and out — wasp, bee, ant, termite, the year's entire harvest of queens and males — and the cold crust receives them, and most of them die there. By morning the wings lie in drifts in the lee of the domes, ankle-deep, and the crust-fauna eats the tithe for months: ice-crickets, the glass roach in its one season of plenty, a gleaning bird working the domes at first light. A moth times its caterpillars to the fall; they eat nothing but shed wings. A year of the crust is made in a single night.

And the few — the mated queens — crawl back down into the flues at the margins, each carrying her inheritance. The bee foundresses carry retinues and wax. The wasp foundresses carry the stance and the spit. And the leafcutter queens carry, in their jaws, a pellet of the luminous garden — so that every terrace, every pale sponge, every glowing edge in forty kilometers of Undermold descends from mouthfuls carried across the cold crust, lip to lip, down a hundred thousand years. The dark's agriculture is an oral tradition.

**The census**

So there is no count, and there cannot be. A body cannot be censused from inside; it can only be summed. Put your ear to any wall, any floor, anywhere in the basin. The note is the roll being called, every life in the Choir answering at once, always.

Listen. That is everyone.
