Four hundred people, near enough — a hundred hearths, and the Sough roster knows the number to the soul, because the roster forgets no one. Not one of the adults was born here. That is the first thing about them: everyone in the Dig came from somewhere else, and everyone came the same way. Ask where they're from and they'll name a fen, a coast, a forest edge, a debt, a brother already here — and then glance upward, because that is the town's word for the world: *up*. Every adult carries the same memory beneath all the others. The day they came down. The long stair from the Stance, the wind shutting off behind them like a door closing, and the white town rising around them with its small sounds — the ticking from the doorways, the knocking through the walls. Nobody was born to this place, so everyone immigrated through the same silence. It is the Dig's one shared biography.

Newcomers still arrive, a few each season, and the town teaches them quietly. A new household spends its first week being shushed by six-year-olds.

The Dig-born are another matter entirely. There are perhaps sixty of them, none older than the town itself, and they are the first natives of anything here. They knock without an accent — adults knock with one; you can hear a fen childhood in a man's knuckles — and they have never heard an echo, because chalk eats sound and gives nothing back. They have never seen a tree except the sallow-man's fenced thicket at the outfall, which they visit by ones and twos to watch the leaves move. Most of them find the plain above frightening. Taken up to the rim, a Dig-born child will hold the turf edge and look at the size of the sky and cry, while the wind — the loudest thing they know, louder than the Loud — hauls at their clothes. Down is safe. Walls are weather. They can read a face the way other children read weather: they can date a wall by the overlap of its pick-scallops, tell a fresh cut from a five-year one at a glance, and name the household that made it. To them the world has always been white, terraced, and descending. When the adults walk out to marvel at the first rill — the first watercourse the valley has ever made — the children come along and cannot see the fuss. The world makes streams, doesn't it? And they touch the sheen on the third tread of the old stair because the grown people touch it, learning a reverence whose object they haven't yet lived long enough to understand.

Quiet has made their bodies. Everyone is some shade of the town: chalk meal ground into hair and cuffs, the gallery-men coming up at dusk with it in their eyebrows like early age. You can read a trade in a handshake — the knapper's horned thumb and flint-sliced knuckles, the digger's shoulders, the lime-burner's split and chalked-white palms. Flint cuts clean and heals clean; the bone-setter, who also draws teeth, will tell you it is not the cuts that keep her in cores but the chalk cough, the slow dust that the old diggers carry in their chests and bring up on cold mornings. But it is the hearing that marks them most. They hear the way hawks see. The commonest gesture in the Dig is a flat hand held up, meaning *wait — the wall is saying something*, and whole conversations stop on it, defer to it. They know by tapping; it is the town's sense organ. A knapper flicks a core with an antler tine and hears the flaw asleep inside it. The old lime-master slaps a kiln's flank and judges the burn by the ring. A digger knuckles the roof of his own chamber before he sleeps, the way you'd thump a child's chest. The whole town is a thing they are forever knocking on, gently, to ask it how it is.

There are no dogs. A dog cannot learn the Quiet; one came down with a newcomer in the seventh year and lasted a week before being traded back up to the herders, who like loudness in animals. The two goats wear no bells — the children call them Goat and Other, and the goat-child holds the job for a year and is envied. The cats are silent by profession and the fox by nature. Even the livestock here is soft-voiced.

What the walls carry is the knocking, and the knocking is a true language, with grammar and manners. A household is known by its rhythm the way a bird is known by its song — three taps, a pause, two is Marn's at the pool mouth, meaning *water, all's well*, and it goes through the floors at dawn ahead of the queue. There is a knock for *come*, a knock for *hurt*, a long low roll that means *out, now*, which nobody has used in earnest since the fire. No knocking after lamps-out except emergency; an unanswered knock is an insult known to everyone on the wall. Lovers tap through two feet of chalk at night while their parents pretend deafness on both sides. Quarrels are conducted in the same code, which every household along the wall understands, so the town's arguments are silent and universally known. News travels through the stone faster than feet: a birth in the New Cut is known in the Old Dig before the father has washed his hands. Three winters back, when a lamp caught in a gallery chamber, the alarm went wall to wall ahead of the smoke, and the whole quarter emptied into the dark without one voice raised — the loudest thing that night was the fire itself. They call it still the night of the knocking, and the chamber, soot-black, was left as it was: the burned wall. In a town with no stain and no patina it is the only dark thing there is, the first ruin, and children are dared to touch it, and grown people touch it too, and say nothing.

Work sorts them loosely, and nothing is a caste yet because nothing has had time. The face-owners dig; the knappers sit in the doorways for the light, and their ticking is the town's rain. The tally-men are the memory-keepers, since the walls are the account books — when a wall must be re-whitewashed, or begins to weep in the damp quarters, a tally-man is called to *read it down*, transferring every owed and re-owed basket to a fresh wall with witnesses, and the neighbors come to a reading-down the way you'd visit a sickbed, because damp is an enemy and a blurred scratch is a debt dying. When a tally-man dies, his district has to be read down out of his head: three days of gathering, everyone whose credit lived in him, and what cannot be proven is forgiven. They say a dead tally-man learns everyone's truth. The lime-burners live white on white at Kiln Row and winter becomes theirs; the chest-sick and the old drift to them and sleep near the draw-holes, and pots crowd the kiln flanks. The gallery-miners keep their daylight down the cut shafts. A family tends the reedbeds at the Well-Pit for thatch and basketry, and the sallow-man tends his fenced green at the outfall and is rich in a way that has nothing to do with flint — the only man in the Dig whose wealth has leaves. He owes Sough days like everyone and pays some of them in hurdles and rods. The granary shafts are guarded by two half-feral cats who belong to no one and are fed by roster, and a kitten is the one gift in the town that outbids a dug chamber.

Every household owes days to the Sough, and the warden — Dace, who drove its last fathom — knows the adit the way shepherds know a path. After a storm he walks it with a lamp and comes up at the outfall and reports a single word, *running*, and that word goes through the walls like the knocking itself.

The town's strangest office is held by a child. Chalk ticks before it falls, and young ears are best, and so before a household undercuts toward a neighbor's floor, someone goes for the Listener. She is eleven. Her name is Sough — the Dig-born are being named for the place now; there is a Sough, a Core, a Rill born last year after the storm — and she sits with her ear flat to the party wall for an hour, sometimes two, while the household stands behind her not breathing, and then she says *cut* or *wait*. She has never said *wait* twice for the same wall and been argued with. In hard frost she walks the Old Dig at night beside her father, counting ticks, and the town lies awake in the dark counting with her.

The deepest room belongs to Marn, who found the water. Twelve years back she was chasing the seam down the face of the Well-Pit when her pick went through, and the green-black water rose after it, and she came up out of the pit with the town's life in her cupped hands. The pool got its name half in jest and kept it long past joking. She keeps it now: no digging near it, no voices raised, and now and then a flawless core, too beautiful to sell, turned over once in the lamplight and dropped in. She is first in the queue every dawn, not by rank but by right of discovery, and on wet autumn nights she keeps the eel-watch alone. Nobody has ever seen an eel. The eels are known entirely by touch — a baited line drawn up bitten and slimed, once a year at dusk a back that breaks the water, or seems to — and their presence is the town's proof that the pool goes somewhere, that the Dig is rooted in the world and not merely missing from it. Marn says she can hear them through her floor on still nights, water moving against water in the dark. Or thinks she can. She is not pressed on the distinction.

Down-seam, where the flint pinched out and the cuts went shallow, lie the Pinches — the weeping cul-de-sacs everyone can name and nobody wants. The latecomers live there, and the unlucky, and poverty compounds in damp: the wettest walls belong to those with least to spare, and a family whose tally-wall weeps lives one bad winter from losing its credit to a smear. They eat from the tips, nettles from the nightsoil ground and fat hen from the kiln ash — the waste feeds the poor, an irony everyone sees and nobody mentions. But you can dig your way up in the Dig. It is the one ladder. A Pinch lad courts a Kiln Row girl by cutting her a room, and the room is his argument. The Tippet boy came from there; his basket-ledge fell when he was nine, hurting no one, and now he is a landmark — *past where the Tippet boy's basket-ledge fell* — a boy growing up inside his own cautionary tale. He knaps beautifully these days, and carefully, the way the once-burned handle fire.

There is no headman. There is the queue, the roster, and the wall. Grievances are spoken low at the pool mouth while the line stands and listens, and you cannot boycott your own trial when the trial is the water. Punishments are logistical: you go last in the queue, you draw double days in the Sough. Two winters ago the undercut law was proven in blood when a face cut against custom took a chamber down in the night and six people with it. The face is sealed and left raw, and on the storm-day each year the children are brought to its mouth to recite the law and the six names. It is the town's only scripture, and the six are its first ancestors.

For the first years nobody died, and so the town had no customs for it, having invented none. The first funeral was a digging accident, and they stood around the body with the terrible freedom of people making a rite. In the end they did the obvious thing, the thing the place suggests: they gave him back to the chalk. Now the dead go into niches cut at the foot of worked-out faces, sealed with a chalk block, whitewashed, the name scratched above — so the walls that keep the accounts have begun to keep the people too, and the directions, already biographical, are turning ancestral. *Below Alder's face* — Alder is hale, and digs on, and says he likes being a landmark, it is the nearest thing to living twice; but he knows, and everyone knows, that someday the direction will find his niche. The Dig is filling with its history at the rate of its dead.

The year sorts them as much as the work. Spring brings the martins, the only residents who pay nothing and are loud, and by custom no face they colonize may be cut until the young have flown, so face-owners stand under their own rich seams watching their fortunes incubate. A man whose best wall is colonized is called martin-rich, and is pitied and envied in equal measure, and the children keep the count, and the day the last young fly, the face is given back. Nobody begrudges the birds the lease. They eat the midges off the sumps, and the town loves its one season of licensed noise. Storms are the other pole of the year: everyone to their stations, bailing chains at the sumps, turf blankets on the tip-slopes, the children gripping the sod and everyone listening between bursts of rain, because rain on the roof-turfs is loud and loud means deaf. Afterward they go down to the outfall and stand in the sound of the Sough running — the loudest water in the town, and the sound of survival — and nobody says anything there either, but they stay a while. Fog mornings there are ropes on the stair-cuts, and the queue forms in white silence, and you know your place by the shoulder ahead of you. Winter drifts everyone toward Kiln Row; summer sends them down into the cool of the deep rooms, and work follows the light, because light here is scheduled by geometry.

And once a year, on the day the Sough broke through, the Quiet is lifted and the Loud comes down like a weather of their own making. Drums on the granary-shaft mouths. Shouting. Every song from every elsewhere sung at once, badly, at full throat — it is the only day the town hears all its pasts together, the fen songs tangled with the coast songs, and the children find it terrifying and then glorious, and by dusk the quiet comes back like snowfall, a rested quiet, the quiet of a thing that has stretched.

Above them, always, the rim people: the herders, loud as the wind they live in, with dogs, which the Dig considers a kind of bell on an animal. The herders call the townspeople the whisper-men and mean it half in fear. Each spring the tally-men go up for the sod-meet and pace the turf strip and negotiate every sod, and each side comes away baffled by the other. Sometimes a herder marries down, and the town says of such a one, kindly, *she laughs like the rim*, and waits. Sometimes a Dig-born goes up and does not come back, and the town says *gone loud*, the way other towns say gone to sea. The carters come with the warm months — Hobb the head carter has come all twelve years and keeps a notch-stick of the Well-Pit's depth, the outside world's only archive of the Dig — and they lower down grain, hide, timber, salt, and news, news being cargo here, arriving on the same ropes. Everything that enters this town enters by descent, brides included: a bride comes down the stair like everything precious, from above, with everyone watching the rope.

When two households join, a door is knocked through the party wall — the only door-making in the Dig that has ceremony — and the neighbors come with lime and give the new wall its first coat, and the first scratch on it is the record of who came. Every wall begins its life as an account with a debt of welcome. A birth is announced through the walls and is known by breakfast. Before the first stroke on a fresh face, a digger stands a moment and tells the chalk where he means to cut. Nobody calls this prayer. It is manners. They live between the ribs of the thing they are making, and they treat it as alive because it behaves as alive, and because a town where every floor is a ceiling learns early that it is one body or it is nothing.

At dawn the fog fills the bowl and the queue moves at the pool without a word, Marn first, and the knocking goes wall to wall — three taps, a pause, two — *water, all's well*. At night, when the frost works and the fresh faces tick and flake, the whole town lies awake in the dark, counting sounds, a hundred hearths listening to their own ground. And in the deepest room Marn lies with her ear to the floor seam, above the still green water she found twelve years ago, and sometimes — not often, and never when anyone is there to ask — she answers it: three soft knuckles on the stone, a pause, two. Goodnight. *All's well.* Knocked down to the eels, the only residents of the Dig who have never once been seen, and are believed in anyway.
