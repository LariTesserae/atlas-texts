Ask what lives on the Shelf and the first answer you get is the wrong size. The span does not live there; the span is what *there* is doing. You don't ask what lives in a hand. You ask what lives on it, and the honest answer is mites — small tolerated things, commuting across a body too large to notice them, paid in leavings.

So: the animals. Every one of them is vermin with a job description. The tap-moth drills the cords and is suffered for the pollen it ferries. The oil-beetle gleans the render-rims. The rime-licker takes its frost one lick ahead of the dawn-wicks. The ticker-birds hold their foundry-shift conversations in the drum-vaults and eat whatever would otherwise clog the works. None of them is fed. All of them are permitted. That is the whole of citizenship on the Shelf — to be legible to the span as part of its own maintenance. Everything animal goes deaf by its second year; the organ-music sees to that. The truest thing anyone can say about Shelf life is that it is all soles.

The longwalkers come closer to being citizens. Nine of them, each known by its footfall the way you'd know a voice. But nine is not a population. Nine is a count kept by somebody.

Which brings it to the people.

You will not see them. From the rim the Shelf reads as empty and it nearly is — a few hundred souls on a plateau that could swallow a kingdom, one for every three or four nodes, tucked so close against the cords that the eye slides off them. The traders at the scarp-foot call them scar-wrights, salt-keepers, the deaf. What they call themselves is a rhythm — seven beats, struck with the heel on pan-salt or the knuckle on cord-bark — and the closest it comes to words is this: *we are also tolerated.*

They are the descendants of everyone who was told to cut a cord, drink, and move on — and drank, and did not move on.

A wright child is born hearing, like any other animal, and loses it on schedule. The day the child stops turning its head at sound is kept as a small feast: the ears returned, the world moved down into the bones where it belongs. They are gentle with your hearing, the way you'd be gentle with the nearsighted. To them an ear is a deformity of attention — two small holes doing badly what a whole skeleton does well. Their own skeletons do it superbly. They go barefoot in all cold, and their soles turn horny, then faceted, polished by the same patience that facets the ventifacts, until an old wright's feet read ground the way your fingertips read cloth. The oldest go blind as well, from a lifetime of the glare, and it barely slows them. Blind and deaf, walking by beat and sole, they are the finest readers the Shelf has, and they cross among the grists as calmly as you cross a room.

Their speech is percussion. Heel on pan for the near work, knuckle on cord for the far. Names are rhythms; a marriage is two rhythms braided into one figure; a quarrel is two figures fighting for the same measure. Two wrights ten kilometers apart converse through a cord with their palms flat on the humming bark, and anyone else touching that wire is welcome to the conversation — the plateau is a single parish, and nothing in it is private except thought. The protocol for a stranger is simple and you should learn it before you need it: tap back the local drum's tempo, exactly, and then your name. The first part says *I am in time.* The second says who.

You keep the tempo because of the other thing that lives here.

The pan-runner hunts the error. That is the whole of what is known, and it is known the way the wrights know everything that matters: through the feet. A footfall off the beat carries for kilometers across that ringing crust, and somewhere out in the white something vast and seismic turns its attention toward the false note. So the wrights walk in time. Their paths curve across the pans in long arcs that look like whimsy and are phase-keeping — staying in step with two drums swinging against each other, three, five, the whole polyrhythm of the region worn into the body's gait. No wright has ever described the pan-runner, because the ones who met it were, by definition, out of time. They speak of it with dread and with a strange gratitude. It edits. A world that runs on rhythm needs something that removes what cannot keep it.

Everything a wright owns is an instruction. The traveler's rule — cut, drink, move on — contains their entire art, because a wound made once is theft but a wound made patiently is authorship. Cut the same arch into the same cord every fourth day as the tendrils come questing to seal it; guide the sealing; correct it; wait. Eleven years later the span has grown you a vault: warm at night from the oil moving through its walls, humming in the root of your teeth, weeping a thin condensate you are careful to take before the wicks wake. A wright house is a sentence the world is saying slowly, and a guest is shown the newest clause the way you'd be shown a newborn. Abandoned homesteads remain legible for generations. The old ones, centuries deep in regrowth, are epics, and wrights walk weeks to read them and argue about what the ancients meant.

They carry no metal — the span digests it inside a season, and a dropped coin comes back as a green stain and a faint local prosperity of copper-plated cord. Their blades are fire-glass, knapped from the mineral slag-gates of ancient burns, the catastrophe's own teeth, sharp as the event that made them and carried with the care that origin deserves. Their rope is sloughed bark-fiber; their glue is salt and spit; their skins gleam with the same terpene liquor that runs in the cords, which is camouflage of the deepest kind — they smell like the span, and the span's small tolerated things accept them as furniture.

And they keep one law, because the Shelf keeps one catastrophe. No flame. Nothing cooked, nothing lit, ever. In the weeks before the storm season masses, every wright within three days of a spark-grove walks the fire-lines, scoring instruction into the cords ahead of the lightning — pre-cutting the paths the slag-gates should take, so that when the blue-fronted creep of burning oil comes, the span's reflex arrives already aimed. This is the service that pays for everything. The span runs, and running is all it does; the wrights think ahead for it, node by node, in exchange for rime and oil and the right to remain. The tap-moth is tolerated for its pollen. The wrights are tolerated for their foresight. It amounts to the same arrangement.

They steer by beat like everyone, but for them the polyrhythm is not only a map — it is a medium. The drums hold their tempos for centuries, but *hold* is a strong word for what patience can bend. A wright of great devotion may spend a lifetime at one node, instructing its pump-muscle by hairs, and the life's work, when it is done, is a beat swung a whisker off the ancient four seconds. Enough to steer by. Pilgrims navigate on the signatures of the dead, and a wright's ghost is a tempo that is slightly, reliably wrong. Their bodies, when the time comes, go to the render-pools without ceremony — nothing made persists, and a body is something made — but the edits persist, and so their history is not written anywhere and is readable everywhere, in clocks.

On the nights before a storm, when the reed-forests begin to breathe and the pans tighten like drumheads, the wrights go out onto the salt and dance. It looks like worship. It is measurement. The best way to take the reading of a chord the size of a weather front is with your whole skeleton at once, and afterward they sit on the crust and agree on what is coming — which is as close as the Shelf gets to scripture being interpreted.

The backfolk are a lineage apart: wrights born on the longwalkers' backs, raised among the ticker-bird colonies and the dust-gardens and the spore-mats that each animal ferries from node to node. A crossing takes weeks; a life takes a walker. Steering is asking, and mostly the answer is the walker's own. The backfolk keep the count, and the count is nine, and has always been nine, and in a world without census or archive the count is the closest thing there is to a fact about the future: as long as there are nine, the body of the Shelf is pollinated, and running.

At dusk they read the news. The fata morgana builds its cold bent cities over the far nodes, and a practiced eye reads the distortion the way you'd read a broadsheet — which organ stands where, how the air lies over the deep pans, where the walkers are, whether something burns three days off. They know the towers are light. They also know the towers are true. *The Shelf permits no cities,* they say, *so it keeps them in the air, where it keeps everything it does not digest.* A wright whose life left no edit worth keeping — whose wounds all sealed over, whose drums kept their old tempo — is said to have moved into the mirage. It is the only afterlife compatible with compost, and they mean it kindly.

They keep the three names of the span and argue about them the way other peoples argue about gods — whether the names denote three bodies or one body under three aspects, whether the span has an edge, whether the center is a rumor or a joke. But they do not petition it. You cannot petition a clock. You can only keep it, and be kept by it, and their word for holy is the same word they use for running.

So the Shelf keeps its emptiness. You can pass a homestead at a bowshot and see nothing but organs working; the wrights are invisible precisely because they are legible, read by the span as its own tissue and by your eye as more of the same. The way you learn you've been found is this: you stop to rest on a cord, and under your palm, riding the four-second pulse, come three taps that are not the span — the local tempo, played back at you by something with a skeleton — and then a pause the exact size and shape of a name, waiting for yours.

Whether the wrights keep the Shelf or the Shelf keeps the wrights is a question the Shelf does not ask. They asked it themselves, once, a long time ago, and answered it in their own name, seven beats long: *we are also tolerated.* Being kept is the only permanence on offer here. They took it.
