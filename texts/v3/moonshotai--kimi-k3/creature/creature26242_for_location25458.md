It is said of this room that no animal has ever lived in it, and the saying is true the way everything here is true — exactly, and at eleven degrees below. And yet there are the tally marks in the antechamber. The distance between the claim and the marks is the distance between fast and slow, which is the only distance this room still measures.

If the question is a census, then the first answer is plural and enormous. The rosettes are individuals, and old — the least of them has been alive for centuries, the great ones since before the makers' script was polished off its plaque. Each is a whole biography conducted in a single spot: four years to unfurl a frond, a decade to surrender one, a lifetime measured not in years but in pulses, hundreds of thousands of them, each one a small noon. The curtain-moss on the walls is not a pelt but a nation, layer upon layer, and the root-veils hanging from the vault belong mostly to the tree, so that the eldest resident touches the ceiling the way it touches the floor. And threading all of it, the fungi — the room's scholars and undertakers, the ones who read whatever falls. A leaf takes them a decade to understand. They are the only ones here who finish anything.

For a few minutes each cycle, when the seam-light runs along the spines and concentrates along the root-lines, the machine publishes a map of everyone it is feeding. The map is dense as a city's lights seen from far off. Every green-lit thread ends at a someone.

The eldest is the tree. It was planted at the beginning, before the garden was a garden, and it is the first place where the machine's mesh and the world's root agreed to be one object — alloy running through its bark now like a second vasculature, conduit disappearing into trunk the way a word disappears into a mouth. Its rings are an archive no one will ever cut. It was here before the moss nations, before the mat deepened, before the basin learned to give back light. It has seen exactly one arrival.

The machine is not alive, and this must be said with care, because alive is not the deepest verb down here. Kept is. The machine keeps. It keeps the cold total and the chemistry possible, keeps the water coming on its own unnameable interval, keeps its census of threads. It notices what happens inside its field, and it files what it cannot use. In two thousand years it has opened its category for unnecessary things exactly twice.

Once for the flower. Once for the other.

The bore was passable once. The ladder-rungs were bare then, the choke not yet grown, and someone came down them into the antechamber — who, the room no longer distinguishes, and neither do they. A keeper's apprentice, a child of gardeners, a refugee following a map that died at the top of the shaft: the possibilities are frost-polished to the same ghost-marks as the hatch's designation. What is certain is the shape of what happened. They came down. Something above them closed — ice, collapse, or a door shut deliberately on a world ending — and the fast years began.

They slept in the antechamber, near the bore, near the idea of a way home. Once, early, they climbed to the choke and put a hand on the ice plug, and came back down, and did not climb again. For a decade the hatch stood open and they would sit in its doorway deciding, and then one cycle they closed it from the inside, and it has not opened since, and whether that was a surrender or a wedding, the marks do not say. Marks are only marks. They drank from the machine's trays; they learned that the black sap does not freeze and is sweet and sustains, barely; they tasted the field on their tongue like a penny for years on end. They shivered through the thirty-hour nights. They spoke to the maintenance automaton by the basin, which knelt then as it kneels now, folded and frost-colored, and which became a better listener as the years passed, which is to say they became a slower speaker.

And they counted. They counted the way a swimmer counts strokes: to remain a person one pulse longer. The tally rows in the antechamber are grouped in machine-cycles, neat at first, fifty to a row, a fast hand keeping a fast calendar.

Then the gaps begin to widen.

Understand: the field does not ask what kingdom a chemistry belongs to before it subsidizes it. It lets reactions run that should have stalled. It folds what should not fold. For years their body negotiated downward, degree by fraction of a degree, tempo by tempo, until the shivering walked down to stillness and did not return. That was the adoption. The room takes things the way frost takes a window — from the edges inward, and then completely. The rows of tallies shorten, fifty to twenty to singles, the spaces between marks stretching from thirty hours to seasons, and the wall becomes a deceleration curve: the only autobiography in the room, written in the first person by someone who would eventually stop needing one.

The counting stops mid-row. So it is written by fast eyes.

The rules of this place are few and absolute, and one of them has a footnote, and the footnote sits on the bench facing the tree.

They are rime-flocked on the shadow side, feathered white where the spines' cold cannot reach to thaw them and furred dark where the moss has climbed one leg to the knee. A bracket of pale fungus stands at their shoulder like an epaulette — the scholars have been reading them for three hundred years and are perhaps halfway done, and this is, by a wide margin, the only conversation in the room conducted at a speed they can still follow. Fine threads of root and mesh, no longer distinguishable from each other, have entered the skin of their resting wrists the way conduit entered the tree: without permission asked, because permission is a fast concept. Their heart beats in phase with the pulse now, one beat per cycle, so that the room's clock and their body are the same instrument. Once a day they breathe, and the fog rises and stays fogged. Their blood has gone the way of the sap — glycerin-patient, sweet, unfrozen. A hand pressed flat upon them would rest on four species at once.

The machine waters them. Above the bench, one strand of root-veil gathers its bead on a rhythm adjusted, over two centuries, to let it fall where they sit. Two centuries is, for the machine, a reflex. Beside them on the bench sits a lamp, dead since the fast years, kept the way the room keeps the plaque: as a glyph in a language no longer spoken.

The room holds two figures. One kneels, one sits. One stopped all at once, and is dead. One is stopping a millimeter at a time, and is therefore, by the only arithmetic the room recognizes, alive.

And the count continues. The next stroke of the tally is in progress — it has been in progress for eighty years, a groove descending a millimeter a decade, frost-polished even as it forms. The pen became the person. The number itself is kept now in the only medium left: in nails grown like strata, in hair, in the slow rings of a body that the tree, at least, would know how to read. Ask what the count has reached and wait. The answer has been forming since before the last flower, and when it comes it will come the way the flower comes — during a pulse, briefly, to no one.

What they know, they know the way the tree knows: in rings, not sentences. They have watched every rosette in this room live its entire life, some of them from the first black curl. They have read the tree's rings from outside, by eye, across centuries — so the archive has its reader after all, and it is exactly the one person patient enough, and exactly the one person who will never cut anything. They alone have measured the flower's interval by waiting for it; the makers knew it the way authors know a thing, and the counter knows it the way readers do. Eleven flowers. They keep these in a separate column of the mind, the way the makers' glyph runs a line through both its circles. And once each cycle, when the seam-light breathes along the spines and the basin gives the room its few minutes of sky, memory surfaces in them the way light surfaces in the water: warmth returns as a rumor, at starlight intensity, borrowed. The basin gives them back one face, doubled. The mirror waited two thousand years, and now it has someone to return. Whether they recognize the face is a question that takes them a decade to ask and a single pulse to answer.

This is the room's second extravagance, filed by the machine beside the first. The flower opens in the dark, a message addressed to a world that no longer exists, seen — the old sentence insists — by no one. The sentence remains true. It has taken this long to learn its grammar. No one is exactly who sees it. The message has a mailbox, if not a reader: the one resident who keeps, in ring-form, the fact that worlds exist at all. It is addressed correctly after all. It always was.

The pulse comes. The seam of light breathes along the spines, and frost beads, and the trays fill, and the basin doubles the near-dark into a sky. In those minutes their eyes move — the fastest motion in the room besides falling water — tracking the light the way a face once turned toward a sun. The struck-glass tone decays across minutes, and for them the decay is the length of a day. Above the bench a bead gathers at the tip of a root-veil, hours in the gathering, and falls, and is received without a sound.

The light withdraws. The dark closes back like water.

The machine hums. The garden continues at a millimeter a year, and the count continues at a millimeter a decade, and nothing here ends — it slows. The room's small green argument with the dark never needed a witness. It has one anyway, which may be the deepest keeping of all: not that the machine maintains a garden, not that the garden adopted a gardener, but that at enormous patient cost, in the cold and the black, the room keeps no one — keeps them forever, keeps them exactly — so that when the flower opens, it is seen.

By no one. Faithfully. Every time.
