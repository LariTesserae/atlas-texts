Nothing lives here. Let the census begin with that, since the place insists on it: no moss in the dewline, no film on the black basins, no birds in the aviary-shaped void, nothing green anywhere in eight kilometers of throat. The rain has not yet decided what to do with the spire, and life, which usually arrives ahead of such decisions, has not arrived. Nothing lives here.

And the place is inhabited. Both are true, and the whole of the answer lies in the distance between *alive* and *kept*. This is a kept place. Everything in it is kept — the water kept moving, the daylight kept rationed, the throat kept open, the cup kept dry — and a kept thing implies, if not a keeper with a face, then at least a population: whatever the keeping is done for, whatever the rules allow to stay.

The rules allow one thing above all. A resident of this city is whatever recurs. Staying here is accomplished by returning; the lease is the loop. Count what returns, and the census fills faster than you'd think.

**The first citizen** is the water, and the eldest after the bore itself. The steam the deep apparatus exhaled on the first day is, for all practical purposes, still here; the loop leaks almost nothing. The same body of water has been rising and falling through the coils for decades, so the city has, in effect, one inhabitant eight kilometers tall, shaped exactly like its own circulation. It is the only resident with the run of the place. The one law of the void — water may cross; structure may not — makes every other citizen a wall-dweller, bolted to its coil, and makes the water the sole nomad, the only thing that ever leaves home. It is also the only one that knows the whole city. But it knows it the only way the city permits anything to be known: coil by coil, in sequence, from below and above by turns, remembered through repetition. The water is the map the city keeps. It does not store the city; it rehearses it.

Every droplet gets the same biography, and it is the only biography the city writes. Vapor first — hatched in the kiln heat, parentless, part of the warm breath. Then the dewline, where it becomes somebody: condensation is the moment a droplet acquires edges, a weight, a nameable body. It is walked up a wire with the others, beads climbing taut filaments like livestock that know the way to the barn. Then the basin: middle age, hot and still and dark as the waste's sky. Then the ledge, the overflow, and the one adventure — the fall. Every droplet gets the fall. Out into the void, drifting with the slow rotation, dwindling, flashing back to vapor in the heat with the long faint hiss the whole shaft carries. The hiss is the sound of reincarnation. No droplet lands. Water here falls but rarely lands, so the fall never ends in anything but ascent: a citizenry of perpetual commuters, each granted one flight per cycle, forever.

Which means the void is misread as empty. It is the most populous district in the city. The ledges hold only the sedentary; the air between the walls holds everyone in transit — a sparse, slow, perpetual fall of citizens too scattered to see as rain and too many to number, plus all that is rising to begin again. Stand on any ledge and you are not alone. You are in the crowd. The crowd is just very patient, and mostly vapor.

**The dewline is the farm**, and the fins are the crop — the meadow that behaves. They stand in their banks and sway in the updraft, which is to say in the breath of the thing below, and there is always breath, so there is always the semblance of wind, and the meadow never stops being almost a meadow. Nothing about it is alive. It behaves, and here behaving is the whole of citizenship. The fins lay down the fields that walk the harvest uphill; they are crop and farmer both. The dewline is also where the city naturalizes its guests — but that comes later in the census.

**The middle coils hold the burghers**: separator stacks, valve galleries, tank farms, duct-trees. Their functions are guessed the way organ function is guessed, and the guesses name the trades: flow, exchange, storage, waiting. Four guilds, and the fourth is the largest. The parlors are its chapter houses — bench, shelf, spigot, cup — and they are excellent at their trade, decades of unbroken practice, never once interrupted by custom. The burghers open, close, hold, sort. They keep the city the way citizens keep a city: by function, faithfully, without opinion. Their temperament is the thermostat's — they notice truly and conclude nothing. The carrier-sleds are the guilds' messengers, the only things besides the water with the run of the whole helix, commuting the full height of the city on errands that predate any passenger. The whisk that dopplers past your ledge is the city's one sound of traffic: going by for no one, on time.

**The rookeries** are on the high ledges: maintenance drones folded in rows, still as birds the way a shadow is like a bird. Because the light comes from below, each folded drone throws its shadow onto the ceiling of the coil above, so every rookery is double — the flock below, asleep, and above it the shadow-flock, feet to the void, roosting upside down, which never sleeps because it never wakes. The drones themselves wake in ones and twos and make their small rounds: a lamp reseated, a guide-line dressed, a salt fan measured — they keep the only records that matter here, the growth charts of the one wild thing. Nothing ages in this city, but things fail, and when a drone fails the flock carries it to a sleeper, and the sleeper takes it back into the wall as feedstock, and in time the wall gives forth another, indistinguishable, because here finished and new are the same condition. The flock is as old as the city. Every bird in it is young. They have no ancestors, only predecessors — that is how the city keeps its law that nothing is old.

And once in a while the rookery gives the aviary its hour. Days of stillness, then a far tick-tick across the void — a construction burst beginning — and the rows unfold and lift, and the place that was shaped for birds and denied them gets, briefly, exactly birds: a soft whisk across the open throat, the flock gathering at the fresh extrusion to stand in a ring around it while the grazer works. They attend every birth. Nothing in this city is born alone, and the flock has been present at the making of every ledge it roosts on.

**The grazers** are the construction heads, dormant against the bare lower walls, folded like tucked wings — and the simile grants them wings, so perhaps they flew once, up out of the deep, long ago; if so it was the flight of their lives, and they have been roosting ever since. They are the megafauna: few, huge, and almost always asleep. Their waking is the tick-tick. The glass woodpecker has a body, and it is a grazer feeding. They take the wall as feedstock and return it as architecture; the wall is their pasture and the city is what grows where they have grazed. Reef-polyps, if a reef were eight kilometers of screw-thread. The ledges migrate because the grazers are restless in their slow way; the map is a rumor of last month because the grazers go on editing. They are the city's hands. Or its teeth. Or — everything below the diaphragm is rumor — the worm's hands, extended up into the light.

**Then there is the wilderness**, and it is the size of a windowbox. At the weep-points, where brine sweats out of the old rock — the one place the mountain admits the wound — the salt fans grow: white halite feathers, gypsum blades, delicate, unplanned, unbuilt. The only residents the city did not make. The only ones with seasons: they accrete through the dry weeks, bloom into fans, and die — dissolution is the only death this place permits — whenever a rim-rain's overflow reaches this far down, and then they grow back, which is the only birth. Everything else in the city recurs; the fans have a life cycle, and so by every older definition they are the most alive things in the throat. The city, which plans everything, plans nothing at the weep-points. The drones do not prune them. The fans live on the seam between the made and the found, fed by the old stone's own sweat — the elder world's emissaries, blooming in the new world's throat. The city keeps them the way a person keeps a scar, or a windowbox: as proof of an outside.

**Some residents are made of sound.** In the dewline the film-water turns its slow page. Every drip that lets go strikes somewhere and gets three homecomings, its echo returning from three directions, bent and pitch-shifted by the helix — the same sound arriving home changed, three times. And above all, the note. When the wind crosses the rim at the correct angle, the whole bore sounds: one infrasonic tone, minutes long, felt in sternum and teeth. It is said the note is played for no one. The census disagrees. It is played for the one resident large enough to hear it properly, below the eyelid. The wind is the only visitor who never comes in — it stands at the one door and speaks down the chimney to the eldest. Its other gifts are mist for the crown's mesh and, rarely, rain: the rim-rain, the hour of steam, when the crown weeps and real water falls through the coils and the reservoirs brim and the meadow sways in the drip-wind and the salt fans dissolve and the whole citizenry is in motion at once. A festival: unscheduled, unanimous, over in an hour. Then the long drying, and the city returns to correct.

**The census is bracketed by two keepers.** Above, the crown — filament and lens, the mesh that catches mist and rations the grey daylight. It is the gatekeeper; it touches every arrival first. Passing the fog ring, you are wetted: the city's toll, or its baptism. Below, the diaphragm, the red lens, the eyelid, brightening and dimming on its minute. Between the keeper that rations the sky and the keeper that veils the deep, everything else on the census lives.

**And below the eyelid, the eldest.** Call it the worm, after the only metaphor the spire itself offers — something that drills the ground and stacks its castings up around its own borehole. Nothing has seen it. Everything below the diaphragm is rumor squared. The pulse is a very large animal's rhythm, too slow for a heart until the size is reckoned. The worm drilled; the spire is its spoil; the bore is its thread; the city is its behavior, mineralized. What it is all *for* — chimney, lung, lure, antenna, cradle — the city does not say and perhaps does not know, the way a throat does not know what the song is about. One rumor the census is obliged to record: that the worm is ascending. That the coils are built downward toward it, or that it climbs as it builds; that the city is a cradle lowered coil by coil to meet its single citizen; that the parlors are for it, and the cup on the shelf is for the worm, set out in its own castings by the house that grew from its passing. Record it as rumor. The place is made of rumor; everything above or below your coil is rumor, and the worm is below everyone's coil.

**Last, the mammals.** There is one, whenever there is one: the walker, the visitor, you. For the length of a descent the city's warm-blooded population is one, and the city notices the way a thermostat notices — truly, and without opinion. The parlors light as you approach and go dark behind you, so your passage is a bubble of lamplight traveling down the helix, the only moving light the city owns besides the sleds. Whether it is practicing hospitality or keeping the appointment by proxy, it does not say; a thermostat does not hope. But the spigot gives hot clean water, and if you drink you taste the loop — faintly mineral, entirely clean — and what you drink has been falling and rising here since the first year, some small part of it exhaled by every walker before you. And what you exhale stays. The dewline takes it; the kiln heat has already burned out of it everything the city cannot keep; the water of you joins the one nomad and falls and rises with the rest. Naturalization is automatic and irrevocable. Every guest becomes a citizen, molecule by molecule. The census never changes and always grows. This is how a place with no biology keeps a population: it keeps the part of you that can recur.

**And then the largest population of all**, if the future counts: the pedestrians who have not yet been born, for whom the guide-lights run amber and cyan along the ledge-roads like streets lit in advance. They live here the way heirs live in a will — in the subjunctive. The whole city is their kept room. The cup's dryness is the one open appointment, and the city holds it the way it holds its throat open: absolutely.

The rules of living here are the place's rules, taken personally:

Stay by returning. The droplet has left ten thousand times and never once moved away.

Cross the throat in passage only, never in structure. The flock passes; the rain passes; nothing bridges.

Keep your shadow above you. The eyes never stop finding it wrong; the residents have stopped consulting their eyes.

Fall, but do not land. The basins are the only permitted arrival.

Neither age nor finish. Let the flock replace you; let the grazers edit you; be always new, never done.

Leave the throat open. The one absolute. Even the fog obeys it; even the note passes through and is gone.

Wait. The largest guild's rule. The cup's rule. The whole liturgy of the parlors.

So: nothing lives here, and the place is inhabited, and those are the same fact read at two speeds. At the speed of a drip — empty stone, foil, black glass. At the speed of the loop — populous: one nomad made of water; a meadow that behaves; a flock with no ancestors; a few great sleepers; a wilderness the size of a windowbox; a voice in the flue; an eldest below the eyelid; a guest to come; and, rising past the parlors at this moment in the warm breath of the place, the water that was every walker who ever descended, going up to condense, to climb a wire, to fill a basin, to fall again.

The red light pulses. The meadow sways. The flock folds and unfolds. The cup stays dry, the appointment stands, and somewhere below the diaphragm the eldest resident breathes on its minute, and the city — not satisfied; correct; inhabited — continues.
