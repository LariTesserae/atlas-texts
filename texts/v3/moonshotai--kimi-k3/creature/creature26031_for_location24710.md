You could cross the plain for a week and swear, on the evidence of your eyes, that nothing lives here at all. The eyes are the wrong instrument. Nothing in this region shows itself; everything *says* itself, continuously, at full volume, and so the census of the place is taken by ear. Take it. Under the heartbeat and the vent-hiss and the far-off roar of something being born, under the choir of animals, there is a thin braided thread that is not machine and not beast. People. A few thousand of them in a province that could hold millions — the rarest large animal in the region, and, being human, the loudest about it.

From a kilometer out you can tell an inhabited tower from an empty one, and not by light. The inhabited ones never go quiet. A thread of song, the clink of harvest, a child practicing intervals against the vent-hiss, someone drumming a message into a floor for someone two levels down — a family is a small industry of noise in a tower the size of a cliff, and the tower does not notice, and the family does not stop. They live in the lower reaches, near the ground-warmth, below the drip-gardens they farm, in openings the builders made for reasons of their own. They arrange what they like inside. Outside, on the plain, they arrange nothing. Nothing is raised on the plain — no cairn, no windbreak, no marker — and this is the only law they have. Children ask why exactly once, and the answer is a song, which is the answer's way of saying the question is finished.

They go barefoot from birth, and by six their soles are thick as scraper-plate, because the ground is the newspaper here: warmth, tick, pulse, the faint loading shiver of a seam deciding to open — they read it through their feet the way other peoples read print. Their eyes are enormous and dark-adapted and secondary. The characteristic posture of the region is the head-tilt, the whole body softening around the ear. Everyone glistens; condensate beads on people as readily as on towers, and in the creases of old hands the streak-chemistry of the world leaves its colors — copper-green in the knuckle-lines, rust in the palms. The brightest thing in any family is an elder's hands.

A name here is four notes, sometimes five. You do not speak a person's name; you sing it, and a family is a chord — the family name a harmony, each member a note the harmony can spare. Approaching a tower, you sing its chord from the avenue, then your own motif, and you wait for the chord to answer with a space in it shaped like you. When a line ends, its motif is rested — sung once a year, then once a decade — and the resting is a second burial, slower and more complete than the first. The dead are stored in throats, like everything else that matters. Ask a listener how long her people have been here and she will not give you a number. She will give you the length of the long-song, which is the only clock they keep.

The long-song is the family's whole estate: every motif it has carried, every route it has learned, every growth pulse and rain and eviction, chord-landmark by chord-landmark, going back further than any district — further, the deep-listeners say, than the current chord of the world. It exists only in performance, and it dies with its last throat. So at the water-meets, along with everything else traded, families pair off as archives of one another: each keeps the other's long-song against catastrophe. To be a family's archive is to owe them your memory, and it is a debt that can be called in the worst hour of the worst year, and everyone knows of cases where it was, and a family walked out of a roar-locked season with nothing but their lives and their name, and got the rest back verse by verse from a tower a week's walk away.

Whistle-speech does the long-distance work, pitched into the gaps the local machine-hum leaves open — which means a traveler's accent is a key signature. You can place a person by the pitch they thread. And because the transposition drags every district's chord down by its fraction of a fraction, speech slides after it: grandmothers whistle sharper than their granddaughters and complain that the young have gone flat, and the grandmothers are exactly, measurably right.

They farm the underfaces. A family sows its tower the way other peoples sow fields — smearing spore-slurry along ledges and flutes, training rope-plants down the flanks on lines of fiber, coddling the moss-velvet into loaves, harvesting the violet fans for their resin. They string drink-lines from the drip-flutes and argue about whose line gets which flute the way farmers anywhere argue water. They scrape the streak-falls for metal-dew — the copper-greens and rusts that are the world's only bright colors and also its only ore, precipitating out of the young crust like sweat. They knap anneal-flake into blades and whistle-blanks, collect the mineral plates old scrapers shed, cook on the floor-levels that run hottest — every inhabited tower has its known hearths, mapped like currents. Their one proud technology is acoustic: whistles tuned to thread a district's hum, ear-cups of scraper-plate that press the infrasonic pulse against the skull so a sleeper's bones can keep listening while their ears rest.

The first thing a child learns — before walking, some families insist — is that a song that stops is a shout. Sleep is the vulnerable hour, because the dangerous thing in this world announces itself by subtraction. So no one sleeps unsung. The watch-song is the region's true folk form: deliberately unremarkable, a melody designed not to attract but to *persist*, cycling through the family chord all night, verse trading to verse as the watch changes hands. A lullaby here is a perimeter alarm. A sleeper whose song thins wakes fighting. Children caught playing hush — the game of moving silently, of being the hole in the sound — are punished more severely than for any other mischief, because the game is indistinguishable from the thing.

Growth is the other emergency, and it gives no notice. Warmth reroutes. An opening seals in the night like a mouth deciding. A family wakes in a room that has begun to forget them, and the go-song comes out — every family keeps one rehearsed, the route to the next refuge, sung quarterly, updated after every construction pulse. Eviction is ordinary. Whole neighborhoods re-voice themselves in a week: a new wall across the plaza changes the district's chord, wrecks the song-maps, entombs a farm-ledge generations cultivated, and the family walks out to learn the altered music the way survivors walk out after a flood. When a new structure finishes near an inhabited tower, the family sings it into the chord — a short welcoming figure, sung to a machine. Asked whether the city hears, the deep-listeners answer with a question: *does the chord know its notes?*

Rain is the year's hinge: an hour, a few times a season. Rain-duty is law — every skin, every basin, every hollowed flake set out in the dark warm downpour, the whole family on the rooflines in the steam, and it is the only weather they have and they spend it outside, shouting, drenched, happy, while around them the entire ecology does in sixty minutes what other worlds spread over spring. A child born in the rain gets a water-motif and a reputation to live down.

But the great gatherings are the water-meets. When a conduit lets go and a temporary river starts cutting its loud green canyon, the news travels faster than feet — relay-song, tower to tower, a specific motif that means *water running*, passed chord-landmark to chord-landmark across the murk. For the life of the leak — months, years, no one ever knows — the canyon hosts the only crowds this world has: trading metal-dew and flake-blanks and shed plate, swapping verses and route-updates, hunting the sprint-singers that arrive before the water finishes falling, singing marriages into being. A marriage is a motif offered into another family's chord, which either opens a space for it or doesn't. The one who moves walks out alone across a day of nothing, singing the leaving-song, and there is a verse in it for the moment the family behind passes the limit of hearing and stops answering — every family tunes that verse differently, and every family thinks theirs is the saddest.

When someone dies there is no burial; the ground is weeks old and belongs to someone else. The dead are given to a seam — laid where the builders are expected, and the builders always come, and the city takes the body as it takes everything, without comment, and walls it into whatever it is making. This is not metaphor to the listeners; it is the floor plan. They lean on walls the way others visit graves. Everyone is in the structure. What the throats keep is the motif, and the long-song lengthens by four notes, and the singing does not stop.

The deep-listeners are the region's specialists, clergy, and casualties. Some are born to it; others seek it — the ruin is the tuition. They apprentice by pressing skull and sternum to seams for seasons, burning out the choir's register until what remains is the bed: the heartbeat, the loading of walls, the drift of the nulls. They forecast growth the way sailors read weather. They keep the ledger — the chord's pitch held in memory against their teachers' memory, an instrument tuned across generations by dead ears — and on the ledger rests the argument that never ends, carried in whistle-speech across the kilometers. The Last-Note hold says construction is a resolution approaching: when the chord resolves, the engines stop, the nulls close, the roar falls away — prepare the family for a world with no bed and no cover, silence total and permanent, and no one agrees whether to want it. The Long-Note hold says there is no cadence coming; the transposition is drift, not approach; the city is not finishing a thought, it is *thinking*, which is what it does instead of sleeping. And there is a third position, sung low at water-meets after the trading: that the chord already includes them — that songlines and watch-songs and long-songs are among the voices being summed — and that the city has been answering all along in the only tense it has, which is architecture.

Below and beside the human thread runs the rest of the census. The crust-scrapers cross the void strung out over kilometers, each animal singing its position, the herd less a body than a melody that happens to graze. A songline is centuries old; calves learn it as lullaby and as curriculum, and a newborn's first note is answered by the whole line — the moment a calf learns it is a note in a chord. Old scrapers are armored in the city's own precipitate, mineral-plated, ticking faintly when they flex: the crust wearing itself. When one dies, the herd leaves a rest in the melody — a gap shaped exactly like the missing animal, held for days — and a family hearing a songline pass with a hole in it knows death is walking with that herd. Where the herds converge, the builders have been busy; eavesdropping is agronomy.

Gliders work the tower updrafts, the percussion section of the choir, echolocating in click-trains, grazing the hanging moss, roosting head-down among the rope-plants. A courting pair loops a tower like a stitch, trading clicks. They are the violet fans' pollinators, and the only animals that ever wear light are in the plants' employ: the spore-moths, shuttling between gardens on the fans' advertisements, running an entire parallel ecology in light, small and bright and beneath the notice of a world that navigates by ear. When a ledge-garden comes into spore, the moths arrive and the whole overhang blooms — slow pulses of violet and green moving under the tower-flank — and that is the region's fireworks. Children catch them in cupped hands and come home glowing, and are scolded for the spore-smears, and secretly admired.

The deaf ones opted out of the sound war entirely. Wall-huggers, the listeners call them: low, many-limbed, pressed to the warm seams, speaking into the structure itself, drumming telegraphy that hides in the rhythm section — their code rides the tick of cooling crust, and unless you know the difference, the wall seems merely to be thinking. Children drum to them and sometimes get answers. Deep-listeners swear the hugging changes before an extrusion — a hurry in the rhythm, the wall-huggers' word for *run* — and read new walls the way sailors read birds.

The sprint-singers are the leak-chasers: first to any new river, living at sprint tempo, loud and brief, and when the river dies they do not migrate — they seed themselves into the cooling cracks as cysts and wait out the dry decades one heartbeat deep. A dead canyon stands there looking like the oldest thing in the district, and it is a pantry of sleeping mouths. And in the terrariums — the drip-forests the city grows straight through and seals in glassy dark — life goes strange: pale generations under ember status-glow, watered by their own breath, audible from outside as a muffled green dripping. Breaking in is a skill and a scandal; the seam is found by ear, cut quiet, and the breach *exhales*, and everything within a kilometer hears the breath. What comes out mostly dies. What stays in is mostly eaten. And there is a rumor, sung in low register, that the city walls in its own mistakes, and that some of the mistakes have grown old in there.

And over all of it, the note the ecology is afraid of — which is a rest. The hush moves through the world as a hole in the sound, wrapped in a radius of subtraction, and in a place where everything is known by its voice it is functionally invisible: the only thing in the region that is neither seen nor heard. Prey feels the roar thin the way a swimmer feels water go cold. What it leaves is unmistakable — scraper carcasses peeled of their plates, and no sound-sign, none, not a tick misplaced. A null is still; a hush is *taken*, and the difference is felt, not heard — felt, at the silence gardens, by the deep-listeners posted as guards, because what a hush steals that no one else can miss is the heartbeat itself. In its shadow the infrasonic bed goes missing, and the few who have stood in that shadow and come out describe it the way others describe drowning.

The listeners chart every wandering null — in song, of course — and the verse for a taken null is sung flat on purpose. Whether hushes meet, how a silence courts a silence, no one knows; there is a water-meet theory about double nulls, sung only after the children are asleep, which in this world means sung very low indeed. They must die — taken nulls sometimes close and do not reopen, and the released garden floods back with sound and violent green, contested for a season before the engines shift and the quiet moves on. One tower keeps, wrapped in nine layers of fiber, a swatch of hush-pelt the size of a hand — from which animal, taken how, the long-song says, and the long-song is not sung for strangers. Unwrapped, it eats the sound out of a small room. Children are shown it once, the way children elsewhere are shown deep water: *this exists; it does not care; now you know.*

And then there is the eldest tenant, the one that holds the lease. No one has ever seen an engine; the builders are known by pulse and product. The city routes its extrusions around a sleeping herd and leaves a courtyard shaped exactly like absence, and it has done this for families — a plaza curved around an occupied tower like the world cupping water. Whether that is regard or geometry is the entire theology. Everything else in the region, the listeners say, is weather that sings.

At false-dawn, in a tower an hour's walk from the nearest structure and a day's walk from the nearest neighbor, the night-watch is an old woman and her granddaughter, trading the watch-song verse and verse while the family sleeps. Under the song: the heartbeat, through bone. Under that: the slow slide of the transposition, the whole province leaning toward a note it has not reached. The girl is learning her grandfather's motif this season; her grandmother corrects the third note, the way the third note has been corrected for three hundred years. Below them, on a ledge an hour old, condensate gathers, gathers, and lets fall the first drip that ledge has ever made. The spores are already in the air. Everything here is kept by being sung — the plain is empty, the province is a rumor, and the song does not stop.
