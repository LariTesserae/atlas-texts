**The Tenants**

The census is short enough to memorize. The floor of the basin holds its few objects the way a mind holds a few clear thoughts, and the life is the same: one frog, one midge, one fern, one heron — or two; a scum, a film, a ring of moss; a handful of commuters; and something that counts as a cairn until the afternoon it doesn't. Emptiness is the dominant material among the stones, and among the species too. Nothing crowds anything. But what is here is here entirely, each species doing the work of ten, and all of them keeping the basin's hours rather than the sun's.

The first tenants were the pools themselves — or what clouds them. Before moss, before frog, the colored water was alive. Color is the census: the ochre, the milk-blue, the rust, the sulfurous yellow — each tint a different consortium, iron-eaters here, sulfur-workers there, mineral-drunk and multiplying in water so saturated it cannot take anything more from you. That is the whole secret of the rule. The clear pools are clear because nothing can afford them. Water that pure is hungry; it leaches, it pulls salts out through the skin of anything that lingers, and nothing lives in it because it has not finished taking. The colored pools are full. They have eaten already. Drink where the water has a color — the rule is chemistry, but it is also courtesy: you drink from the crowded pools, not the hungry ones. Whether these consortia arrived on the wind or came up with the deep water, no one can say. If they came up, then the colored seeps are where the inside of the world is still leaking its own biology, and the Egg's oldest tenant is the yolk.

There are fish, and no one stocked them. They come in as eggs glued to a heron's toes and feathers, and they are small, translucent, the length of a fingernail, found in no other water on earth. Each pool has begun to keep its own strain — the ochre pool's fry run faintly amber, the milk-blue's opalescent — and the overflow at pulse carries fry downhill to seed the lower basins, so the rills are constantly dealing the population down the terraces like a slow hand of cards. You are watching speciation with the paint still wet. Nothing here has had time to finish being anything, and the fish least of all. The heron that carried them reaps them. Vector and reaper both: the bird stocks its own larder and has never once been thanked.

The bellfrogs are the voice, and the voice is a clock. Thumb-sized, grey as wet new stone, with paired vocal sacs that inflate like glass bubbles, each frog sings one note and sings it for life. The note is set at metamorphosis: the tadpole's ear-bones form in humming water, and the froglet climbs out pre-tuned to the column nearest its birth pool. But the columns deepen as they lengthen, week by week, and the frog does not. So every frog drifts sharp of its column as it ages, and you can age a frog by its dissonance — the gap between its note and the stone's current tone is a birthday, exactly. The choir is the basin's whole growth history held in microtones, and the herders listen to its spread the way you would read a moss ring: a tight choir means slow years; a wide one means the building has been fast. At the pulse, every frog re-locks to the climbed pitch, hundreds of them, one species, a choir snapping onto a tuning fork. Between pulses they tick over quietly, keeping the note warm, murmuring like strings before the downbeat. They spawn on the flush, into the rising colored water; eggs laid in a clear pool stay glassy and never cloud, and the frogs know the difference by sight, which means the rule about the water was theirs before it was yours. And the hush is theirs too: an instant before the whomp, every frog in the bowl stops at once, as one. When the choir cuts out, sit down.

The midges live their entire adult lives inside the loudness. The warm flush hatches them; they mate in the shrieking air; the eggs go down as the rills fall; and by the time the basin winds back to its ticking, there is not one adult midge left alive in it. Born in thunder, dead in the dripping quiet. Between pulses the sky of the bowl is empty of them entirely — the basin holds its breath between generations — and to a midge the world has a single weather, and it is always the flood.

The gorge swifts are the only birds that truly live here, because nothing perches. There is nothing to perch on; the columns are too warm, too wet, too slick, and so there is no birdsong in the basin — only the swifts' screaming, which is not music, it is traffic. They glue their cup nests behind the columns, in the lee of the drip, and their saliva cures on the warm stone fast as mortar, and nothing can climb the new rock to reach them, so the nests hang safe as bells. They sleep on the wing, up inside the fog cap, and at night you hear their soft contact calls filtering down out of the cloud — the lid of the Kettle talking in its sleep. When the pulse comes they are off the columns in a wheel before the first midge has cleared the water. And when the fog tears and the light-shaft stands in the bowl, they pour into it and spiral up it as if it were a chimney, and a fledgling's first flight may be straight up the light.

There is a heron. There may be two. No one has seen two at once, and no one has ever seen the one asleep. A slate-grey heron works the colored pools by day with unbearable patience, stirring the edge-broth with one foot to cloud it, to panic the fry; and after dark a heron stands ankle-deep in glowing water, striking at the phosphorescent churn of fry and frog-spawn; and whether the night shift is the same bird working late or a second bird keeping the first bird's hours is a question the dark declines to answer. Either way, it is the oldest tenant. Herons live long, and this one — these two — may be older than the floor they fish: older than the bridge, older than the hall-buds, old enough to have watched the gateposts move. At night a swallowed fry goes down glowing; you can watch the small light descend the throat and go out. It is the only bird in the world whose dinner is visible. It tolerates you at the pool's edge exactly as it tolerates the wardens: as furniture.

The ibex are not residents. There is nothing to graze, so there are no residents with hooves; every one of them is a pilgrim. They come in file down off the old mountains, days of trail, and cross at the low notch, and stand for hours at the licks, tongues polishing the crust into hollows deepened within living memory. They arrive, uncannily, in the warm hours after a pulse, when the seeps weep fresh and the crust is soft — how they time it, from days away, no one has clocked. A sick one sometimes stays, and dies, and that is the lammergeier's business. The rest leave nothing but the hollows and their dung, which the afternoon storm takes down into the rills, and for an hour afterward the glow runs brighter below the licks: the schematic drinking the pilgrims' tithe.

The lammergeier comes over the rim on a wingspan out of scale to the little bowl, and because nothing under the fog cap casts a shadow, its shadow is an event — the only moving darkness most days, crossing the floor like a hand over a page, and the fry scatter under it though it has never once stooped at anything living here. It eats bones. It has learned that the paved paths, the neat flat stones that end in mid-air, are the one trustworthy anvil in the bowl, and it drops its bones onto the paths that lead nowhere, so the roads to absent terraces serve meanwhile as its ossuary. It circles, it checks the carrion situation, and if nothing has died it leaves on the next thermal. The basin's auditor: it arrives, it verifies, it signs nothing, it goes.

The green things keep to their strict small holdings, and every one of them is a clock. The moss rings age the columns — count a ring's width and you read the drip-years, the wet fogs and the lean ones. The one fern keeps its spray zones and asks nothing. And the lichen grows only on the wardens, which tells you more about the wardens than about the lichen: lichen needs a stone that will hold still for decades, and in this basin everything else grows, floods, drips, or hums. The only still stones here are alive. The oldest vegetable life in the bowl rides around on the backs of the animals no one can classify, and the wardens permit it — they could shiver, tilt, stand in a rill and scrub themselves clean, and they do not. They carry their lichen the way the columns carry moss: as a clock.

As for the wardens themselves: a stack of flat dark stones, five to nine courses, knee-high, no mortar, the same grained warm stone as everything grown here. Indistinguishable from a herder's cairn until the hour it isn't. It unfolds a single leg — jointed like columnar basalt, hexagonal segments rotating on one another — reaches down with the delicacy of a man threading a needle by touch, re-seats a marker stone the rain has nudged, and folds again, course by course, into stillness. An hour to unfold, an hour to fold; the correction takes an afternoon and no part of it is hurried. The counts never agree — eleven, fourteen, nine — because some hold still for seasons, and because small stacks of three appear where no stacks were, and if those are young wardens, no one has ever seen one assemble itself. Perhaps they accrete, a course every few years, a warden's age readable in its courses like the moss rings. Old ones slump a little; lichen on lichen. They are warm, wrist-warm, like the rim, and warmest — like the markers — in the hour before a pulse. Lay a hand flat on the top stone then and it is blood-warm, and it does not mind, or it minds too slowly to matter. During the afternoon storms they stand in the rill-splits, one leg deep, nudging breaches in the fresh rimstone so the terraces flood evenly — the basin's fingers in its own plumbing. At night some stand in the glowing water, harvesting nothing visible; the word *charging* is one you will not say aloud, but you will think it. A traveler once built a perfect decoy cairn beside a true warden, to see. No warden ever touched it. It stands there still — a pile of stones that fooled a person and nothing else. How they know the difference between their own and a heap of rocks is the whole question, stacked knee-high and lichened. Machine, animal, the basin's tortoise, the makers' maintenance crew, the part of the work that checks the work — the valley names multiply, and the wardens clarify nothing. They are in no hurry to be understood.

Of the makers there is nothing to describe, and that is the description. One hut, grown, dry, warm-walled; a bench; a bowl fused to the floor — the furniture of a body that needed only to sit and hold something. No tools. No ash. If they live here still, they live the way a schedule lives: as what happens next.

And so the census sorts itself, in the end, into three professions. Drink the color: microbe, fry, tadpole, heron, ibex, you. Sing the note: the frogs, and after their fashion the midges, whose whole sky is one held chord. Hold still: the wardens, the moss, the patient scum. Everything in the bowl practices at least one of the three. And the largest tenant practices all of them — it drinks the fog, it sings its two notes, it holds still at the pace of stone. By its own rules the Kettle qualifies as the dominant animal of the Kettle, and it may be that this is not a place with inhabitants but an inhabitant with a place — that everything on this list, frog and fry and film and swift and warden, is its wildlife in both senses: the small lives it carries, the way a body carries its microbes, mostly harmless, mostly helpful, mostly unaware.

For a night or three the census includes you: the warmest-blooded thing in the bowl, and the only one that will leave before it dies. You came to the licks of the place like everything else — call the deficiency what you like. Sleep anywhere; the ground is hearth-warm and the night never truly chills, and you will dream with your ear against a second heartbeat. Weeks afterward you will hear the two notes in kettles, in wind around a door, in the space inside a fridge's hum. Every kettle you ever meet will sound like this place, and not the other way around.

And near dawn, if you are still there, lying ear-down on the warm stone with the glowing rills drawing their circuit in the dark, you will feel it gather — the tightening in the teeth, the heron standing to its full height, the wardens blood-warm at their markers. An instant before the pulse, the whole choir stops at once. Hundreds of frogs, as one. And in that held breath you learn the last thing about everything that lives here: they are all listening to the same thing.
