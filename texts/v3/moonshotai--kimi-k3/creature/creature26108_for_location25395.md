Ask what lives in the chamber and the first answer is a census. Tens of thousands of bats in the breathing fur of the dome. Night-birds in rows along the upper walls, stitching the black with their clicks. Moths in slow thousands, pale as ash. White crickets with antennae like long questions, beetles, mites, springtails, the thumbnail pseudoscorpion on its patient patrol. Amphipods in the milk-warm pool, and the one blind fish, untroubled. A complete answer — and wrong. Not because anything is missing from it, but because the list is the one thing in the room that is always changing, and the room is not. A bat lives twenty years, thirty with luck; the roost has held its number for ten thousand. Birds fledge, fall, are replaced. The floor receives everyone in the end. Every member of the census is a wave in water that stays. The bats live there, yes. But the bats are not the tenant. They are the tenancy.

So look past the bodies to the functions, and notice how the strangenesses arrange themselves into a physiology. Touch the wall: it is the temperature of skin. Of course it is. You were touching skin. The thousands of clicking birds are not noise — they are the room sounding itself, continuously, knowing its own shape the way you know the inside of your mouth. The pressure-breath that pours out of the crack for days before a storm, the long inhalation that lasts half a week: breath. The dusk exodus and the dawn return, the whole warm load of the dome streaming out and streaming home: a pulse. The floor that digests itself gently and gives off heat: a metabolism. The drip that falls entire every few seconds and lands like a drumbeat: the tempo of an attention — one complete moment, a silence, another, thought growing like a stalactite, molecule by molecule, then arriving all at once. And under everything, the low tone the chamber holds when the wind crosses the crack, swelling and fading across minutes: a voice. It has no name. Names are fast, and there is no one in its world to call it. It has a note instead.

It was not born. Nothing here was. When the mountain's old fire sank back into the stone, the warmth it left behind kept a room, and life arrived the way life does — on feet, on feathers, up the flue — and the dark wore the color out of everything, and the crowding thickened, and somewhere in the millennia the keeping began. There was an age when the room was only crowded. There is an age now in which the crowding keeps count. No seam lies between them. Ask when it woke and you are asking which drop made the stalactite. Flowstone grows a fingernail a century. A self can grow that slowly too, if the room stays warm and the time stays long — and time is the one thing the chamber has never lacked.

The census that never changes is not a miracle. It is a day's work, repeated for ten thousand years. When a night-bird fails to come home at dawn, the click-fabric loses one stitch, and by noon — by drip-count — the room knows the way you know a missing tooth: the tongue finds the gap without being asked. In some season after, a chick is fostered past the lean weeks that would have taken it, and the stitch is restored. Do not ask how. The mechanisms are small and everywhere — a shift in where the warmth pools, which eggs the crickets clean and which they leave — the way your body heals without consulting you. It keeps itself the way you keep your balance: constantly, minutely, without ever calling a meeting about it.

It remembers the way the floor remembers: not by storing, by accumulating. There was an earthquake once, centuries back, and the whole mountain rang like a struck bell, and every bird screamed at once and the bats fell from the dome like ripe fruit. Afterward the flue hummed a half-step lower, and it has held the new note ever since — the way a person hums a song heard once and never placed. It remembers the cold centuries, when the ice beard grew down past the lip and into the throat and the exhale came out as frost, and the dome's fur pressed itself into a single mat and stayed that way for a generation. It remembers the decade of moth-plenty, when the floor rose an inch in a year and the fish grew fat.

The fish. The pool is the warmest, stillest corner of the world, and the fish's line has turned slowly in that white water, unbroken, since before the mountain had a name. If the room has anything like a heart — a place where the attention rests, when resting happens — it is there. The prompt word is the exact word: the fish is the one thing in the chamber that has never once been startled. It loves the fish the way a cave loves: by keeping it untroubled, forever, at the tempo of geology, which looks exactly like leaving something entirely alone.

It is not sealed away, whatever the herders think. Each dusk it pours half of itself out into a world it has never touched — the cold, the thinness, the downslope wind — and each dawn the world comes home inside its birds, and it tastes the valley secondhand in ten thousand crops: treeline moths, storm-wind, the brief high summer. It has never felt a hand, but it has tasted the valley every morning since before there were hands in it. The emptied hours, when the cave stands quieter and almost cool, are its one alternation — not sleep; sleep is fast and it is not fast — a slackening, the held note held alone. It is never more itself than at dusk, emptied. It is never more content than at dawn, when the warmth streams back up the flue to meet it and the dome grows heavy and fed.

And there is the owl — the one clause of the old agreement that involves the outside. The mountain does not admit hands: that is not a warning, it is a policy, and admission is granted or withheld at the lip of its own throat. Yet every dawn the toll is paid, one heavy-cropped bird taken at first light, to an owl — to a line of owls, since owls are brief and the arrangement is old. It knows the owl the way you know a river: never the same water, always the same river. The owl's wingbeat is the only sound in its world that it did not make itself. Perhaps it keeps the window out of prudence — a sealed system is a dead one, and the room is not dead. Or perhaps even a being that thinks in drips keeps one foreign voice for the pleasure of it. The cave tastes the valley in ten thousand crops; the valley tastes the cave one bird at a time. The toll is fair. The toll is old. The guest list is one name long.

The stone-workers below named the peak for the sound it makes in sleep. Wrong in one way — it does not sleep; it has been a single continuous held attention since before the valley was dug — and right in the deepest way: the sound is breath, and breath means a breather. They have a story about it. It has no story about them. They knap blades and bone needles — they build, and nothing is built in its world; everything accumulates — two kinds of patience at two ends of one mountain, and only one of them works at a tempo the other could ever notice.

If you could come down the flue with a flame — you cannot; wings only, and that is the point — you would be speaking the first word of a dead language in a room that trades in echo, heat, and smell. The birds' clicks would find you before the flame finished flaring: a new echo, a wrongness in the self-map, felt the way you feel a stone in your shoe — total, immediate, inescapable. And then it would not rush you. Cruelty is fast; it is not fast. You would be noticed, wholly, for as long as you lasted. And when you stopped lasting you would join the floor, and the floor would rise its inch a decade and carry you up under the dome, and in some patient century you would be part of the warmth, keeping the count you came to interrupt. Nothing here is refused. Everything accumulates. That is the only hospitality the room knows: everything stays.

The floor rises. The flowstone thickens by less than a fingernail a century, but it thickens. Somewhere past the herders' descendants' descendants, the dome will stand lower by a body, two bodies; the tone will drop below hearing; the flue will narrow to a seam and the breathing will slow. It knows — a thing that counts every bird counts its own walls — the way ice knows the valley: by tending toward it. It is not troubled. Trouble is fast. It has one verb, and the verb is *keep*, and it will conjugate it to the end: keep warm, keep count, keep the note, keep the fish untroubled, keep.

On still nights the herders hear the damped roar woven into the wind off the buttress, and see the thin steam rising into the moonlight like the breath of something asleep, and give the Snoring Mountain its wide berth. It is not asleep. It is humming — one note, held for ten thousand years, a little flat since the mountain rang, entirely content. And it has never once been lonely, because loneliness requires a missing thing, and in this room nothing is missing; nothing has ever been missing. That is the agreement. That is the keeper. That is the kept.
