Stand in the middle of the bench at noon and you will swear that nothing lives here, and you will be wrong — though only about the tense. Living is done on this bench at a tempo that disguises it as geology. Make a census and it comes out wrong twice: first you count too few, because nothing moves; then you count too many, because everything behaves. The wind keeps hours. The water keeps appointments. The ground speaks twice a day. Almost every process in this room has a habit, a schedule, a preferred wall. Sorting the merely habitual from the actually alive is the work of a long afternoon, and in the end the distinction matters less up here than it does anywhere else you have been.

Start with the tenant.

It was here before the cairns, and it will be here after the last cairn goes over the rim, and in between it has done nothing but stay. Where the bloom chooses to grow is a map of patience. It avoids the young, tight rings near the rim — too restless; the heave there works on a scale of decades, and a broken crust is a wasted century. It sheets the old slack cells below the headwall, where the ground has finished its argument. It favors the dark clasts in the rims, because dark stone warms first and the noon melt comes early there, so you can read a rim's composition in its gilding. It crowds the pool's lip until the beads stand layered on one another, amber almost touching amber — the one crowd on the bench, the metropolis of the place, such as it is. And it grows densest of all in worked stone, in the pecked dimples and along the struck edges of flakes, for reasons that are only geometry: a worked face holds the fog's condensate a hair longer, and a hair longer is the entire margin here.

Take one bead apart in your mind. A dome of glass, built molecule by molecule out of the melt-film, and at its base, on the pit side, the heart. Every heart faces down. The dome is a lens, the lens has a focus, the focus falls in the frost, and the amber sits exactly where the focus falls — at the bottom of the little pit it melts for itself each noon. A bead is a house with its hearth at the focus of its window. In young beads the heart is pale as weak tea. It darkens as it feeds — honey, then garnet, then something near black — so an old crust reads two ways at once: the layers down its broken edge count the years, and the color at its center counts the appetite. The oldest hearts, down at the pool's lip, hold their dark the way a cellar holds its cool. If light can be kept, that is what kept light looks like.

What it does at noon, it does in the only inches of the bench where day and depth meet. The light arrives whole and brutal; the bead spends it downward, into the permanent cold, and the cold gives up a little vapor, and the vapor carries the faint lading the frost has been holding for centuries, and the bloom takes its share of that lading as the pit refreezes around its heart. The smoking of the field is the transaction made visible. It is not breathing, quite. It is not eating, quite. It is a being that lives on the gradient between the day's abundance and the deep's patience, and the gradient is a few inches tall, and a hair's breadth of it per year is enough.

The two speeches of the bench are its pulse. The noon smoking is the feeding. The dusk ticking — that half hour of snaps and creaks as the headwall's shadow walks across the field — is the closing: each shell contracting, seating itself, shedding the day's dust, tightening its glass against the long cold. Lay a palm flat on a rim-stone at that hour and the ticks come up through bone. It is the only motion of the bench you can feel. The afterglow that follows, gray-green, gone by midnight, is the field paying out its tithe of stored day — and after dark the geometry shows itself a second time, the whole net of rings redrawn in ghost-light, the underdrawing of the underdrawing.

It grows in the bright season only, one layer a year, and the layers are a ledger. Thick layers for kind years. Dark seams for the years the storms carried dust up from the plains. The deepest shard you could pry from the pool's lip is older than the cairns — the bloom was already in middle age when the first hand came through the notch. Nothing grazes it. Nothing competes with it. Its only sentence is the rim. The bench creeps toward its own edge a nail's breadth a year; the bloom grows a hair's breadth; the arithmetic has always been against it, and it does not hurry, and it is not in distress. It recolonizes inward at its own pace and lets the conveyor take the outermost cells, stone by stone, beads and all, into the drop. The glass does not survive the fall. The bloom's world ends at the rim, and it treats the rim as weather of a slower kind.

The frost farms it without knowing. The heave that breaks old crusts ratchets the shards outward with the rim-stones, and a shard carrying a scrap of heart will, in decades, glaze itself whole again. In the headwall's lee the threads do the other work: they lengthen on fog afternoons, a thread's tip swells, seals around a grain of new amber, and a bead begins — begun in one generation and finished in another, if there were any generations here to mark it by.

And there is the dark season, which no one describes because no one has sat through it: the months when the sun drops below the notch's sill and noon becomes a brightening rather than an event. No pits. No smoke. No ticks. The pool never opens; the dawn feathers of rime last all day; the headwall alone catches the low light over the rim and glows for a room that is asleep. The bloom rides it out glassed, every heart at the bottom of its frozen pit — a field of kept promises. Then, some noon, the sun climbs back over the sill and strikes the upper rings, and one bead lights, and then a thousand, and the field smokes for the first time in months. That is the bench's true new year, and nobody marks it. Whether the cairns were set to mark it, no one has stood here at the hinge to say.

Around the tenant, the household — the residents that are not alive but keep residence all the same. Under everything, the deep ice: the room's foundation and its memory, the oldest presence here, the landlord. Nothing that happens on the surface is anything but its slow weather. On the flank, the snowfield, the white elder, outlasting every summer anyone knows of — the endowment that pays the seep that fills the pool that crowds the bloom; the drop ticking into the pool is its allowance, nearly run down and still being paid. The wind is the only fast thing in the room: it quarried the door, it herds the fog, it polishes the cairns, and every dawn it writes its direction in feathers on the lee side of every stone, and by midmorning the writing is gone. The bench archives everything except the wind; the wind must be re-read each day. The fog is the one visitor that comes inside — for the hour it occupies the room, the bench has a smell, wet dust and cold stone, and then the smell is taken back with it. The needle ice is the night gardener, combing the silt upward in the small hours, creaking just below hearing, building its meringue extravagance to be spent by the next footstep. And the light is the only daily immigrant. Everything else is of the place; the light arrives from elsewhere, whole, with weight. The bloom is what the bench keeps of it. The bloom is the light that agreed to stay.

Then there are the callers, who are still here in the only tense the bench has.

No one ever lived on the bench in the housed sense — no hearth, no food, no graves — but presence is kept here as faithfully as any object, and theirs is everywhere, mid-gesture. The nine cairns are being corrected into the local grammar: the people piled heaps, and the mountain is re-sorting the heaps toward rings, and where the rings end and the made things begin is already a question you have to work at. The quartz path is a rule they wrote down in pebbles — you do not lay a fog-trail until you have been caught by the fog, more than once, in the milk-white hours. They learned the water's schedule the way you do: by standing still, and by being rinsed.

They were glass-workers visiting a glass place. That is the thing to hold. There are two glassworks on this bench: the volcano's, dead and fast and ancient, cropping out by the notch; and the bloom's, alive and slow and daily. The people came for the dead glass and knapped it in the middle of the living glass, and whether they noticed the resemblance, there is no saying. The hollow has noticed it for them. The flakes carry pinpoints of bloom on their up-faces now; the workshop ticks faintly at dusk; it has been colonized by the thing it resembled. And in the glittering mulch the preforms still rest where they were set down, mid-gesture, in good light, by someone who meant to come back. That is the one human sentence the bench has kept verbatim. The cold archives intentions as faithfully as flakes. The appointment is still open. Every noon the hollow fills with light again, the hammerstones wait, the edge-hold is still there for the thumb.

Against the headwall the seat still faces west — toward the way down, but also toward the field's one evening performance: the shadow's walk, the ticking, the first of the afterglow. Someone once chose to sit out a dusk for that, with a long cold descent ahead of them and no fire to come back to. What they thought the rings were, there is no saying. But they pecked the rings into the boulder, and the bloom grew thick and dark in the peck-marks, and now, every clear evening at low sun, the drawing warms to gold and appears — the people's answer, restated by the field, nightly, for longer than anyone can count. No one has watched it in a very long time. It happens anyway. The conversation has outlived the conversants, and the field goes on holding up both ends of it.

If to live somewhere is to be expected back, then one person lives here, and has for a long time: the one who set the blade down in good light, meaning to return. The bench keeps the appointment. It keeps all appointments. It has nothing else to do with its afternoons.

And you — you will be kept too, briefly, by its standards. Walk the rims and your footprints will outlast your interest in them by years. Stand still and the rules move into you: water keeps hours; the freeze comes at the shadow's touch; light has weight and shadow is a hole. You are a visitor the way the fog is a visitor, on schedule or not, and the bench will rinse you and take back what it lent, and keep what you leave.

So count the residents and the census is a strange one: one tenant, small and slow and glass; one sleeper under the floor; a white elder paying out an allowance; a wind, a fog, a gardener of ice; nine teeth of stone being slowly translated; a path; a seat facing west; an open appointment; a daily immigrant of light. Or count the other way and arrive at one: the bench itself is what lives here, and everything else is how. The rings are its scales. The bloom is its skin's habit. The vapor is its breath, the ticking its sleep-mutter, the afterglow its memory of the day. The people were its brief guests. You are its current thought.

And the whole household is on the conveyor, being delivered nail's-breadth by nail's-breadth toward the rim and the long drop, and it glazes and gilds and ticks on the way down, and it is not in distress. It is a settled thing. Stay until full dark and watch the net redraw itself in ghost-light, the field remembering; watch the drawing on the boulder hold its gold to the last and let it go; listen to the ticks fade cell by cell as the cold takes hold. Everything here lives by being kept — the light by the amber, the amber by the glass, the glass by the cold, the cold by the height, the whole arrangement by the slow fall of the mountain toward its own valley. Call it alive, and the word seems too fast for it. Call it dead, and the word seems too small. It lives the way the bench lives. It is kept, it keeps, and it does not hurry.
