The films were here first.

Before anything with a name, before anything with a shell or a song, there was the breath itself, and some of it stayed. Every exhale from two hundred floors carries cells, spores, the microscopic shed of being alive, and the first warm air that ever climbed into this cell carried the seed of everything now growing in it. The films found wet alloy at mouth-temperature and did what they have done on every wet surface since there were wet surfaces. Now, in the lees where the grazers cannot reach, they lie a century thick—scratch one with a fingernail and you would read decades, the living amber skin giving way to compacted, varnished layers beneath, the room's only sediment. The only geology here is plumbing, so the films became the soil. They smell of copper and mushroom and something faintly sweet, which is the smell of the building's breath, returned and interpreted.

And the film is not a slime; it is a stacked forest. Within a single thumbnail of it there are thread-worms and wheel-animalcules and slow transparent bears, and smaller hunters that hunt those, and the passage of a grazing snail overhead comes to them the way weather comes to a forest floor. Everything visible in this room is walking on a wilderness it cannot see.

The snails are the visible grazers, dozens to a fin, and each fin is a page covered in their handwriting—the slow spirals and arcs of radula-tracks, silver where the film has been shaved to the alloy, healing over in days as the film regrows, a palimpsest that is never finished and never erased. They are all, every one, left-handed. Somewhere in the room's early years—on a boot, in the old biofilter stock, no record survives—one gravid ancestor arrived, and her accident hardened into law. A right-handed snail ferried in from some sister cell tomorrow would find a whole population and not a single mate. The island is closed by a spiral.

Their shells are grown from minerals gleaned out of the film and the scale, so they carry banded amber rings like the rings at the drip-points, like the tally marks on the plate—a snail is a piece of the room's geology that got up and walked. Their clock is the slowest animal clock here. A fin crossed in a week. A life counted in ring-widths. Perhaps a dozen generations since the last tally was scratched on the inspection plate. They lay their eggs in gel-clusters deep in the quiet seams, because the hatchlings are soft and the quiet is where the hunter cannot hear them, and for its first weeks a snail is a promise of a shell that it must survive to keep. The dead go the way of everything: down. The floor of the sump is paved a century deep in left-handed shells, whole and broken, the one census the room keeps of itself, and the widows walk on it.

Every snail is also a voyage. The mites that ride the shells are born there and die there and never touch the fin; a shell grows its own film-garden, and the mites are its farmers, and the snail's week-long crossing is, to them, tectonic drift. Many mite generations pass in one snail-lap. When two snails meet and mate—and any two can, that is the one gift of the shared twist—their crews mingle, the only migration a shell-mite will ever know. Every snail wedding is an exchange of continents.

The drip-moths work a faster clock and a stranger trade. A droplet at a fin-tip is hours in the making, and a moth reads its ripeness the way you would read a fruit—tapping the fin with a foreleg, feeling the tremble of surface tension through her feet, waiting for the last instant. Then she drinks through the skin of the drop without breaking it, takes the whole sea and leaves the husk. Condensate is distilled, nearly pure, and pure water is not a diet, so the moths also sip the pale scale-rings for salts, and sip snail-trails, and the rings bear their tiny marks. Their larvae are gray threads tunneling the film; their pupae glue into the fin-seams; the adults live a matter of days with wings that never dry. Flight is nearly impossible here—wings bead over in minutes, and a wet moth is a falling moth.

Except once a day. At the purge the iris opens and the pressure jumps and a slit of gray sky appears at the top of the world, and every moth in the cell lifts at once into the gray blizzard—their one minute of sky, mating flight and dispersal and judgment all at once. Most resettle in a gray rain. A tithe goes out the iris and falls two hundred floors; the cell pays the sky in moths. And a few, a very few, are blown through the exhaust trunks to other cells, and arrive as immigrants—the ferries of the archipelago. The scales that sheet off their wings are the gray of the broth, and the films trap the scales and digest them, and the larvae eat the films: the loop closes inside a single life-cycle. A well-grazed fin, crowded with moths, is nearly silent in the lulls. The drip-music of the cell plays only where the moths permit it. The percussion section answers to them.

The crickets hold the throat, where the roar is fiercest, and it is a bargain they struck long ago: the heights are nearly unhuntable, because up there even the lulls are thin and brief and the hunter does not climb. The price is the roar itself, and they paid it by becoming deaf to it. A cricket's ear is a slit tuned exactly to the band of its own song; the roar simply does not exist for them. Their wings remember flight the way a fossil remembers it—the wings are the instrument now, stiffened to bows, and nothing more. They phrase into the lulls because in the lull their narrow channel clears and carries, and because all the business of the cell must be done in nine seconds at a time, and because the lull is when the fin-runner hunts.

That is the dark engine under the choir's famous synchrony. A cricket singing alone in the lull is a target with a voice. A thousand singing together are a wall—the sound comes from everywhere and the runner cannot fix it—so the Choir enforces ensemble, and the song is a treaty, and the soloist dies. From inside the throat you can hear that it is not one voice: there are old males with deeper bands, there are arguments, there is antiphony across the chimney, one side answering the other through the drip-notes. Phrases persist. A pattern sung today was sung last year, learned by young crickets who learned it from crickets now washed down to the grate. Call it physics or call it tradition; no one here is asking. And through the failing baffle, from outside, the massed phrasing—hush, build, crest, cut—is what almost resolves into speech.

The crest is not an accident. Everything in the cell feels the membrane's pre-flex through its feet, the half-beat shiver before the stroke, and the crickets take it as a conductor's intake of breath. The Choir rises into the end of the lull and is cut off by the returning roar. A cricket's life, meanwhile, is a climb: the nymphs hatch low and graze the midland film among the snails, soft and hidden and hunted, and molt by molt they ascend, and the final molt is at the throat, and after that there is only the singing. A cricket cast down to the midlands is already dead, because the midlands are the runner's country.

You could look straight at the fin-runner and not see it: a pale soft finger of something that might be a trick of condensation, frozen against a fin. During the roar it is deaf—the world is white, there is nothing to read—so it is furniture, sculpture, a smear of patience. Its stillness is not rest. It is aim. When the lull opens, the room becomes legible—a moth's footfall two fins away, a cricket's song giving away its branch—and for nine seconds the runner is the fastest thing in the cell. It does not bite. It arrives, all at once, and pours itself over its prey, and a voice leaves the choir mid-phrase. Between lulls it waits out the roar, minute after minute, year after year. Its metabolism idles so low between strikes that it may live a very long time. It is possible—no one has counted them, and no one ever will—that the same pale individual has worked this stack since before the tallies stopped, that it and the pencil hand were near contemporaries. One of them is gone. The other is holding still in the loud dark right now, in the ambush hollow that is the most valuable real estate in the cell. When it dies, it will fall like everything else, and even the hunter will be fished.

The grate-widows are the last caste and the inheritors of everything. Each holds her span of bars over the warm dark water, and between purges they are as still as the grate itself, living on the thin wash of husks and dead mites and the soft unlucky. After the purge comes the sluice, and the feast: they trail clawed legs and lines of their own silk into the current and glean what the day has sorted—the old moths, the molts, the fallen. Their egg-sacs are glued beneath the grate, incubated by the sump's warm breath, and the spiderlings rise on the updraft, and some of them never come down at all. Whether the name is biology or the namer's humor is unrecorded—they are all female, or the males are brief and edible; the pencil did not say. The sump beneath them is warm and faintly mineral and floored with the shell-midden, and when the pump draws it down, the water that leaves carries the cell's small emissaries—spores, film-cells, the dilute shed of the whole ecology—back down the pipes to the rooms it rose from. Every glass of water on the two hundred floors holds a little of the Choir.

The air itself is inhabited. The broth the amber light reveals—mist, wing-scales, spores—is a plankton field, and some of its citizens are so small that their fall-speed is nothing against the convection; there are lives here that begin and end without ever touching anything, hatch and drift and mate and die mid-column, in the warm. And there is a third water, smaller than the sump and smaller than the film: the droplet at the fin-tip, inoculated the moment it begins to grow, an aquarium with a dawn and a dusk measured in hours. When the moth drinks it at its ripest, she is drinking a crowded sea. The purge is the broth's daily extinction; by the next lull the films have reseeded the air.

There are thousands of these cells in the exhaust lung, all built to one plan, and no two alike anymore—a century of drift. The ferries run at purge: blown moths, spores, an occasional stunned mite. Somewhere in the building there may be a cell whose snails coil right-handed, a cell whose crickets sing a different almost-phrase, a cell where the runner never arrived and the midlands belong to something stranger. A choir of choirs, one to a cell, each keeping its single word. And the felt-moss in the corner is the clue to how it all began: the building once grew its filters, living beds of moss and algae stocked with their attendant small lives, and when the membrane came and the gardens were dismissed, the garden did not die—it climbed into the lung and naturalized. Nothing here is a pest and nothing is a pet. They are successors. The left-handed snails of this one room exist nowhere else in the universe, and they are not aware that this is strange.

Every inhabitant keeps a different calendar. The films count in layers, the snails in ring-widths, the moths in blizzards, the crickets in lulls beyond counting, the widows in feasts, the runner in nine-second days separated by white eons, and the room itself in tally marks, thirty years stale. The purge is the only shared holiday. The pre-flex is the only shared omen, and the animals learned it long ago—took the room's small flinch as scripture—while the manuals, wherever they are, still call it predictive control. Standing at the membrane, feeling it draw breath an instant before it decides to, you understand that the engineers built a body, and everything else here has spent a century learning to read its tells.

No one lives here. That should be said. The last person to so much as open the panel left thirty years ago; the last person to listen—to press a palm flat, to feel the hum in the teeth, to hear the phrase and reach for a pencil—left longer ago than that, and is dust on some lower floor by now, exhaling upward, molecule by molecule, for decades. Everyone below is, eventually, a little, here. The films take them in. The Choir is, in the most dilute and literal sense, made of the people it will never know, just as they drink it every day without knowing. The arrangement suits everyone. The names remain on the panel, worn by creatures who cannot read them: the only trace of the one human who ever understood what the room was for—not the water, that was the building's purpose—the music.

Right now, inside, the membrane is pausing. The roar drops away and the lull opens and in nine seconds the whole census happens at once: a moth drinks a droplet-sea down to its skin, a snail continues without hurrying, a widow gathers in her line, the runner becomes briefly terrible, and the crickets crest into the pre-flex and are cut off by the stroke. If it is a phrase, it has been repeating—with intent or without—since before anyone alive below was born.

It is repeating now.
