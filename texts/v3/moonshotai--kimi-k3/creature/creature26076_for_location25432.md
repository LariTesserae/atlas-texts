**The Census**

In the back of the dive log — a waterproof notebook that lives in a dry bag in a kitchen in the valley — there is a pre-printed page headed WILDLIFE OBSERVED. Forty-one years of entries, in four hands, and they read: *None. None. None.* In the ninth year someone wrote *Possible?* — and three years later, in the same hand, on the next line: *Snow. Strike the previous.* Then *None* again, thirty years of it.

The page is wrong, and it is wrong in the way the room teaches you to be wrong: by trusting your eyes in a place whose life does not perform for eyes. The Stillworks is inhabited. It is just inhabited at the same speed as everything else it does.

Begin with the meadow, since the meadow is what the light is for. Six paces across, and if you count — one diver did, over three dives, a finger's width at a time — it comes to four hundred and eleven stems, knee-high at best, standing in their own white space. Almost certainly one owner: nothing has ever carried anything in this room, so the meadow spreads the way a rumor spreads through an empty house, stem to fallen stem. A stem ages, grows heavy with its own lime, leans, lies down; where a node touches bottom and noon still reaches, a new stem stands up out of the body of the old. In this way the meadow walks, perhaps a hand's width in a decade, never once stepping outside the moving budget of the light. The bare silt between the stems is not neglect. It is rationing. Each stem stands where the light can afford it.

Count the nodes on a tall stem like rings; each node is roughly a year's worth of noons. The oldest living stems are older than the diver counting them. The oldest stone ones — dead a century, unchanged, because nothing here unmakes anything — are older than the road up the hill. The meadow stands in its own graveyard and calls it soil. Every stem is a waterline between the living and the limestone, and the waterline climbs at exactly the rate the stem grows. What the meadow eats is light and what it excretes is rock: the room's only heavy industry, and its smoke is stone. The shed lime settles and joins the floor, so the floor is, in small part, the meadow's slag. And under the stems the silt is salted with oospores — seed-stones the size of pinheads, armored in calcite, a bank of postponed meadows. The room holds its garden's children in escrow and has never once needed to call them in.

Above it, on the lit wall, the film: one cell thick, working the noon shift. An hour of usable light a day, two in high summer when the sun stands steeper and the column comes down harder — and on that wage it runs the other twenty-two. Where it stops, it stops all at once, along a line as clean as a tide mark: the contour where the light's budget runs out. Inside the line it is a city. The slime of it harbors bacteria in their hundreds of thousands to the square centimeter, living on the film's leaks and leavings, and the whole mural, if you could peel the wall like a stamp and weigh it, would not bend the needle on a scale.

But the true population of the Stillworks is dark, and it is everywhere, and it weighs almost nothing. In the silt, in the shadowed seams of the scallops, in the undersides of the drifts live the ones who eat what the water denied carrying — the trace manganese, the faint sulfur, the crumbs of the hill's chemistry. Their doubling is measured in years. They farm at a dilution that would starve anything in a hurry. You will never see them; you will see what they write. Shine your light under the lip of the fallen ceiling block — lift nothing — and there is their signature: black dendrites, fern upon fern, grown a molecule at a time from water that swears it carries nothing. The white silt itself is partly their masonry; moonmilk is laid down by hands, some of them too small to see. Scrape together every living thing in the room — meadow, film, the dark ones, the viruses that outnumber them all and weigh nothing — wring it out, and it would not fill a dive helmet. The Stillworks runs a metropolis at the mass of a housecat.

The lung has its own tenancy. At the line where the trapped air meets the water — the only place in the room where two worlds touch — a ring of film holds on, doing the one thing nothing else here can: trading gas. And it is in the lung that the room makes its weather. Calcite nucleates on the flat underside of the air, grows into pale rafts the size of coins, the weight of a sigh, and lets go. A slow snow of stone, falling eight meters from the ceiling, unmelting. The floor beneath the bell is the newest floor in the room, soft with decades of undisturbed winters, the flakes lying in their fallen order. Once, a diver hanging in the noon column saw movement at its edge — something pale, turning as it sank, deliberate — and wrote *Possible?* in the log. Three years later another diver watched a raft detach from the bell and fall, turning the same slow turn, and understood, and wrote: *Snow. Strike the previous.* It remains the only fauna sighting on record, reclassified as weather.

Nothing with a mouth has ever lived here, and the hill sees to that; anything that could swim arrived already filtered through a year of stone. What falls down the shaft, though, the room keeps. It does not eat its dead; it files them. For two weeks in May the throat wears yellow with pollen, and not a grain of it comes down — the room is downstairs from its own seasons. But leaves waterlog and sink, and the gentle draw carries them to the low slots at the near end, where they queue in order of arrival: the recent ones whole, the old ones gone to glaze ghosts of themselves. One spruce cone. One moth, wings intact. And one bat — a skeleton at the edge of the light, articulated to the last finger-bone, veiled in mineral, because nothing here scatters anything. A dusk mistake, one bad wingbeat over the water in the throat, and the Stillworks acquired its entire history of vertebrate residency. At noon the column stands on it. It is the only animal the sun touches down here: the room's one mammal, retired.

And then there are the ones who do not live here and cannot leave it alone. The log knows them: forty-seven dives in forty-one years, a process with a return period, an input the room absorbs the way it absorbs everything. For the hour a diver hangs in the cold, they are the warmest thing that has ever entered the room — a mammal furnace bleeding thirty degrees of difference into seven-degree water, the Stillworks' one weather event, recurring on a schedule of years. Each dive inoculates the place with a freight of skin bacteria, an offering of millions. The room accepts all offerings and uses none. The imports die in the cold and join the silt, and the census does not change. The divers keep three rules: take nothing, move nothing, leave nothing. The third rule is impossible. Every dive ends with breath rising through the fluted throat, and what collects under the dome does not visibly leave. The old diver who keeps the log believes the bell is larger than it was when she first hung beneath it — that the room keeps a tithe of its visitors, that the one gauge in the place is slowly filling with us. She admits the water may be taking it all back, molecule by molecule. She notes only that the bell's note, when the weather turns far overhead, is lower than she remembers. She admits her memory may be tuned wrong. She no longer dives. She walks up through the thin spruce some noons and sits at the lip with a hand flat on the warm stone, listening — the way you visit something that will not remember you, and go anyway. The room keeps no memory. It outsourced that, once, to a mammal.

Which leaves the largest resident, the one the rest live inside of. Itemize: it holds its temperature, its level, its flow; it feeds — water and light in, water out; it repairs its own intake, plating the throat in slow enamel; it is bounded by a membrane of stone that decides what may enter; perturbed, it returns to itself. It does everything a body does except the one thing. It has never made another of itself; there is no daughter works anywhere in the hill, and there never will be. It is the first of its kind and the last, and it has solved, alone, in the dark, the problem of running forever, with no one to appreciate the trick. If that is not alive, then *alive* is a smaller word than we thought. And it keeps its census of tenants near zero not by forbidding life but by out-waiting it. The price of residency is to live on one hour of light a day, or on stone, or on nothing, at seven degrees, forever. The meadow can pay it. The film can pay it. The dark ones can. Everything else is weather, or the dead, or the visitors, or the breath.

This year the WILDLIFE OBSERVED page has a new entry, in the old diver's hand, the first in some time. It reads:

*Meadow: 411 stems, one owner. Film: one cell deep, working the noon shift. The dark ones: innumerable, unhurried, lightless. Snow: occasional, from the lung. Bat: one, retired. Breath: accumulating. Us: periodic.*

And then, at the bottom, because the form must be honored: *None.* It was always technically correct. None of it is wild, and none of it fits in the boxes of the form — the room's entire population fails every checkbox and goes on failing them at the speed of itself.

Below, the Stillworks holds its one breath and runs its one afternoon. Some of the breath is hers.
