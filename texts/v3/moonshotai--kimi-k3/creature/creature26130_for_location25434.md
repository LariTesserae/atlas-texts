The snow does not fall on empty stone. It falls into mouths.

That is the first fact of the citizenry: nearly everything here eats the rain, or eats what eats it, and so the population is arranged by appetite for falling things. Every wall is a crowd of small open works — sieves, nets, fans, feathered crowns — all straining the same slow weather. To drift down a street at the feeding hour is to be tasted a thousand times a meter. Nothing here looks at you. Everything samples you.

Start with the masons, because they are most of it. Each zooid is no bigger than a grain of rice and lives perhaps a season, secreting its one chamber and dying into the floor of it, so that the living are always a thin skin stretched over the accumulated dead of their own bloodline. But the bloodline is the point. A tower begins as a single larva that settled in a lucky eddy, and everything since has been budding, cloning, repeating — which means each district is not a community but an individual, one genotype tens of meters tall and tens of thousands of years into its own life. Every mason alive lives here. This valley is the species' entire range, the whole world population gathered into a few kilometers of its own skeleton. Where two genotypes meet, there is war — a border dispute of overgrowth and smothering and chemical insult, conducted at a millimeter a year, decided across centuries. The fish that cruise over these fronts never know the street beneath them is at war. The combatants have all the time there is.

What the masons know is snow, warmth, flow, and kin. A storm that raged and died at the surface weeks ago arrives here as abundance, so the city experiences the bright world's weather as a feast calendar, and its holidays are the ghosts of storms. And once in a long count, on a trigger kept by the same deep calendar that keeps the migration, the whole city spawns at once — and for one night the weather reverses. The rain runs upward. Pale columns of spawn climb through the falling snow, the dead descending past the next world rising, and the water above the towers is two weathers meeting. The city is full of clocks that have never seen the sky they keep. It keeps a moon it has never seen, and answers it with children.

The light-keepers are smaller still. In the seams of the stone the masons keep bacteria the way surface things keep gardens — fed by the warm seep, multiplying when the flow is good, burning brighter as they feed. So the famous wave of glow that crosses the city at the migration's peak is not ceremony. It is a trillion small mouths eating at once, a contentment of bacteria, indistinguishable from acknowledgment. After long enough here, you stop insisting on the difference. The city's moods are digestive. So, if you are honest, are yours.

In the pipe districts the worms each own a tube and nothing else; a worm's whole wealth is a feather. Their crowns are the only flowers this world grows. Pass a shadow over a street and the street flinches — thousands of crowns snapping shut in a wave that outruns any fish, the whole block ducking at once — and then, slowly, the feathers come back out, like a held breath being let go. A street that has flinched is loud with absence for minutes afterward.

In the glass districts live the kept ones. The spires are sponge lattice, and when they are young their lattice is open, and small things come in. The shrimp enter as pairs, as juveniles, through doorways that will not fit them later. They grow. The doors close. What they have then is one translucent room that sways faintly in the current, glows faintly, and feeds them whatever it filters — a room they keep clean for the rest of their lives, because the room is also the landlord, and the food, and the sky. Their children leave through the lattice while they still can. The spires stand full of such couples, floor after floor of lifelong rooms, each dimly lit, each with its two old keepers who have never once seen the street they live in.

The cleanliness is a workforce, not a condition. On the shell pavements the custodian crabs each hold a territory and work it in slow spirals, carrying off the clumped snow and the fallen dead — a job no one assigned and every crab performs. Take them away and the streets silt over within a season. Along the warm seams work the farmers: pale crabs with long hairy arms, and in the hair a crop. They cultivate the golden bacteria on their own bodies, holding their arms out into the warm mineral flow and waving slowly, the way you would warm your hands at a fire, then combing the harvest free with a brush built into the other claw. They tend pastures that are invisible, and they know every one. And everywhere, the snails — the polishers. A snail may take decades to cross a plaza. Its rasping tongue is the reason the stone shines. Their trails cross and recross in a script no one reads, recording nothing but thoroughness, and the oldest of them carry passengers on their shells, so that the slowest citizens are also neighborhoods.

The walls look back. Every archway, every hole, has its eel — long patient faces in the openings like a census that never files its report. The eels keep the hours better than anything alive; they know the migration to the minute and come out when the road is full. The oldest of them barely hunt. The city feeds them its sick and its strays and its cleaning crews, and an old eel in a well-placed arch may have watched the same corner for the better part of a century. Nothing about the corner has changed. Neither has she. There is an understanding between them.

By day the silver fish pack the streets in slow rivers, and at rest they hang in the archways like water that has decided to wait. The school sleeps by edges — the rim awake, the core dreaming — and turns around tower corners as one body with a thousand hearts. To the school, the city is a bedroom. To the city, the school is a tide that lives indoors. Above them the mid-levels belong to the shrimp, whose clicks come down like dripping in a far room, and whose cast molts drift down through the lanes — so part of the snow is the city's own shed skins. The city snows on itself.

The canopy overhead is loose and drifting: jellies, and once in a while the long one — a siphonophore, forty meters of fishing lace, a colony that sails as a single animal and trails a curtain of itself through the upper streets at night. It is the longest presence here, and it is, like the masons, many pretending to be one. The city recognizes the trick. And down out of the dark above come the larvaceans' discarded houses — vast mucus mansions, built fresh each day and abandoned each day, sinking ghostlike into the lanes. A sinking house is an event. Everything within a hundred meters converges, and the feast is over before the hulk touches ground. Somewhere above, the builder is already building again. It may be the only creature here with a new house every day, and it gives them all away.

Then there are the ones who do not live here. Twice a day the road fills — bristlemouths, lanternfish, the commonest vertebrates on the planet, the whole commuting population of the deep pouring through on its way up toward a brightness they will reach and these stones never will. To them the city is a lit hour on a road kilometers tall, a landmark made of taste and current and glow. The road pays its toll in stragglers: the weak, the late, the unlucky stay, and the gates take them, and nothing is wasted. The commuters are weather. Grand, nightly, essential weather — but weather.

The hunters at the gates are something else. The oldest citizens here came from outside and stayed. The sleeper sharks are centuries into their lives, nearly blind, a pale tassel of copepod trailing from each eye, unbothered. They do not chase; they arrive. They move at the pace of the masonry, the only things in the valley made of meat that keep the stone's own hours, and the eldest of them was already old when the youngest spires were silt. They are citizens the way mountains are citizens of a landscape — the city happens around them. The anglers dangle their small rented suns in the dark beyond the walls. The big squid come up with the migration and leave with it, weather with arms.

Even the empty plain is not empty — it is rural. Out along the trailing mounds, octopuses keep dens marked by the only litter in this world: a doorstep of emptied shells, a midden, so that you can take the census of the outskirts by their trash. Beyond the last mounds the herds walk — sea cucumbers in slow single file, head to tail across the flats, processing the snowfall where it lands. They are the plain's livestock and its farmers both.

Below everything, in the sealed chambers and silted corridors, live the pale ones — eyeless amphipods, white isopods, tunneling worms that work through the compacted snow of dead ages. They eat history. The city's past is digested by its present; nothing here is archived, everything is consumed. Memory, down here, is a food web.

And the city can sicken. A seep shifts, a bloom goes wrong, a tower sours, and the neighbors wall it off with indifference, and it is left to the pale ones until the stone can be taken back. Collapses are weather too: a spire leans for decades, slumps for months, and its tenants leave gradually, unpanicked, reabsorbed. When one of the old sharks dies at the gates, it is a decade of abundance — first the great mouths, then the small, then the worms in the bones, then a garden where the body was, then pavement. Nothing here ends. It changes tenancy.

There is one elder. Off the core, in steady current, stands a single glass sponge, folded like a curtain, anchored to the bottom by one buried spicule. It has been filtering this same water for something like ten thousand years. It was full-grown when the youngest districts were silt; it was alive when the city was a mound you could swim across in minutes. It does not glow, does not flinch, does not keep the moon. It persists, and its memory is its own glass, laid down in layers. If the city has a witness, it is this: one animal that has been present for the whole of the city's middle age, drinking the same water the whole time.

So. Who lives here?

The census is long, and every name in it turns over — eels, schools, sharks, the paired keepers of the lantern rooms, the trudging herds of the plain. The tenants come and go. The tenement remains, and the tenement is alive: the currents are its circulation, the tides its breath, the glow its mood, the spawn its seed, the tombs its bones and its eaten memory, and everything that swims its streets is how it lives — its comings and goings, its guests, its thoughts passing through the archways. It was never born; it grew. It has never lived anywhere else. It will die, if it dies, only when the valley's warmth gives out, and it feeds, patiently, on the rain of the dead from a bright world it has never seen and keeps the time of anyway.

The snow goes on falling into its mouths. The streets stay clean because it cleans them. The warm stone breathes in, breathes out.

Something lives here. It is the here.
