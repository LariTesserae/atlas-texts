No one here is a person. Two things come close — the felt, because it is everywhere, and the Long Hunter, because it is singular. Everything else is the multitude.

The herds first, because the herds are most of it.

To be born a mite in this world is to wake in the middle of a migration that began before your shell hardened and will not pause when it empties. The herd has no head and no map; it has a temperature. It turns with the warmth-stripe the way filings turn to a magnet, and a grazing mite eats time as it goes: the felt gives under the jaws in layers, this year's growth over last year's, the thin dark seam that was the gust-year, and behind the herd the frass falls and feeds tomorrow's pasture. The herd manures its own road home. And when a mite dies — under a scythe, under the Hunter, under plain age — the felt takes the soft of it and the plate remains, with its gleaned iron, and the silt glints. The most common object in the world is a dead mite's back. Every step of every life here is taken on the backs of the dead, and nothing minds, because there is nothing here to mind with. Only feet, and the feet are content.

When the herd reaches one of the Hunter's roads, the surf-sound drops. The groove is crossed at a run, in something near silence, and on the far side the rasp resumes. No one decides this. It is in the plates the way the iron is.

A mite outgrows itself a dozen times and leaves each old self standing, so that for an hour after a molt the herd is double — walkers and ghosts walking together — until the Breath lifts the ghosts away and they become the snow the towerwrights wait for. The herds build the Towers after all, one discarded body at a time. The wrights only collect the bricks.

Up on the Ridge the vertical herds have made the other bargain: iron traded for grip, because a plate that would save you from a scythe will pull you off an overhang. Sometimes one falls. From the Ridge's flank it is a fall out of the sky, long enough to right in, long enough to choose a landing, and mostly they survive it, drifting down like seed, and stand at the bottom in a foreign grazing ground with a foreign strain of felt underfoot, and join the ground herd, and are taken. The Level is half settled by the fallen.

The silt-fleas are the surf's foam. A flea's life is mostly nothing — folded in the dust, fed, waiting — and then the tremor of an approaching herd arrives through the ground like weather and the flea goes up, and at the top of the jump there is one small still instant, the only quiet a flea ever gets, and then the landing, which must be read immediately: bare silt, felt-edge, the drumming of feet. Leap or stay. That is a flea's entire philosophy, lived several times a minute.

And then there is the sect by the polished face, who have never seen the Eye and never will. Their eggs lie in the dust along the dawn-line where the bounce falls. Their noon lasts eleven minutes. A pale strain of felt grows there that wakes only for the second dawn, greens for it, is grazed in a rush of feeding and mating and dying, and by the time the true stripe has moved on across the Level, the parish by the mirror has finished a generation and begun the wait for the next. In the gray weeks, when the outer sky is covered and the bounce fails, the eggs simply wait. Some wait a season. No creature in the world lives on more trust, and their sun is a thrown thing, secondhand, a reflection of a reflection of a day — and it is enough.

Under everything, the felt. The first settler, the oldest inhabitant, here before the herds, living on the fall alone when the world was bare iron and old grease. Whether it is one being or many is a question it answers differently at different edges; the dark tough growth on the Ridge's overhangs is its dialect, and in the crazed glass of the Eye, where no mouth has ever reached, grows the only old-growth felt in the world — ungrazed since before the Towers, a wilderness behind glass. The felt does not sleep; it slows. Cut it — nothing cuts it, but if you could — and you would find the decade-storms written as dark seams, and you could count the gusts like rings. It is not sentimental. It is patient, and it is hungry, and it is everyone's heir: dead singers fall to it, spent midges, the ghost-molts the wrights miss, the soft part of every mite that ever walked. Everything here ends as felt, and begins there too.

The drift-midges are the fall's first tasters. Half of what comes through the crack never lands — it is sieved out of the air on the way down by the columns riding the Breath, so that what the felt receives is only what the sky already declined. And the midges give it back: their own spent bodies drift down and join the snow, and the pasture is part midge and always has been. At dusk the columns lean toward the Old Country and funnel through the round window, and the skull fills with torpid midges like a lung filling; at first warmth it empties. The skull inhales at dusk and exhales at dawn, one breath a day, and has been breathing that way longer than any herd-memory. Somewhere over the Level, on a bright hour, a midge crosses the Star's trembling projection and is lit for a wingbeat by the image of the outer sky — the closest anything here comes to the outer world: to fly through its photograph.

The shield-singers are the perch-owners, and the first dry note of every day belongs to whoever holds the rim of the Eye, where the warmth lands soonest. Cross the Stele and the accent changes — the eastern noon-song is not the western one — and at midday the Disc, the sunken arena, gathers the loudest of them, and the bowl of it hands their rasp back doubled, and the herds graze through the loud hour unmoved, wearing the noise like weather. Perches outlive singers. A famous perch is a stack of the dead, each singer singing from the top of all its predecessors, and the wing-cases below it slowly becoming silt. Two mysteries belong to them. When rain walks across the roof of the world, every singer stops at once, all of them, mid-note, and nothing has ever explained it. And when the outer wind sets the housing humming, an old singer can name the wind by the note — north gives one pitch, west another. They have never felt a wind in their lives. They read them anyway, the way the whole dead mill reads weather: by ear.

The towerwrights are the only builders. Everything else standing was made by the maker or worn into shape by time; the Towers are made by tenants. A wright is soft and gray and all hands, and it eats fall and frass and spins, and its daughters add courses, and the Towers rise a generation tall, mortared with the ghost-snow — so that every Tower wears its ancestors on the outside, the grandmothers' shed shapes weathering on the windward face, the living within. One thing they will not do: they will not touch the Hunter's cast skins. Those stand on the roads where they fall until the felt takes them. A dead Tower, a failed line, is the richest acre in the world — singers take the stump, midges roost in the flues, claw-bearers den at the foot, felt climbs the shaded side. And after the decade-gust there is a building season, the world's only one: for days the Level smells of fresh silk, and the Towers lean, and mostly hold.

Out on the only sea, the skaters. Wax-footed, and the wax is everything: a skater blown onto the silt in a gust is ruined, dust in the wax, and must groom for hours or never ride again. The Tar's skin carries news — a skater at the far margin can feel a herd crossing the Level, the tremor arriving through the iron and up through the black, hours late, like word from another era — and their courtship is written on that skin in ripples that take an hour to fade. When a midge misjudges the columns and sticks, every skater within a span reads the struggle and converges. The Tar keeps what it catches; the skaters keep what the Tar can't hold. They are the cotter pin's eternal commuters, crossing to the rich thin pasture at the seep's margin, which is why the pin has never once been still underfoot. And once a day, when the stripe crosses the low end at its shallow angle, the skin goes briefly translucent at the margins and the strata light up beneath them — everyone who ever lived, displayed a handspan down, whole herds sealed in the dark — and the skaters go on sipping at the meniscus. It is the floor of their world. It has never occurred to them to be astonished. A skater stands on the only window that looks downward.

Below them, the worms, the readers of the deep record. A tar-worm may spend its whole life inside a single year of the world's history, eating that year at the rate it was lived, blind and patient, segment by segment through the dark. They may be the oldest individuals alive down there — worms who were already old when the oldest Tower's first course was laid. Their shafts let the Breath into the Tar: sometimes the skin dimples and a bubble stands for an hour, the world's slowest well, and the skaters gather to sip what the deep strata still exhale — sweet oil-ghosts, the taste of the machine's working life, rising one bubble at a time.

The bone-fleas are the Old Country's gentry: pale, long-haired, weak in the jump and strong in the grip, grazing the last of the mummy's hair and the pale bloom on the bone, holding the high spine where the Breath passes first and the fall lands clean. They are the only feet the Hunter suffers near its den. The détente is older than any of them: the fleas keep out of the ribcage hollow, the Hunter keeps off the spine, and in exchange the fleas glean the den-mouth midden for plate-shards to line their burrows in the hide. When the hum comes, they have the best seats in the world. They lie flat along the ridge of the spine while the whole range of the Old Country sings beneath them, and the skull full of sleeping midges gives the note back like a bell inside a bell.

The claw-bearers work the felt's under-edge: flat as shadows, all patience and two arms, and a flea that lands wrong is taken before it can read the landing, and the husk is flicked out to join the snow. Their courtship is a held-claws pavane along the under-edge, the male leading, backward, a slow circle that no one witnesses. They are also the world's only passengers: when the emigrating urge takes one, it waits at a midge-mooring, catches a passing leg, and lets go over new country. The far felt-edges were settled by claw-bearers falling out of the sky. And they are the arithmetic of the lean years — a season behind the fat, they multiply, and thin the fleas, and in the thin times after, they thin each other. The felt-lip has seen their wars: brief, total, and unrecorded, except by the felt, which eats the losers.

The scythes are the rumor under the floor. No one has ever seen a whole one. What is known is the under-tunnels, the strike from below that is faster than a flea's jump, and the shed jaws the wrights turn up in the silt and leave alone. From the height of the sky you can read them anyway: the herd's flow has eddies, smooth doglegs around nothing visible, and each eddy is a scythe. The herd does not know what it avoids. It avoids it the way water avoids a stone. And on the tunnel-flats the Hunter's roads show other marks — the grooves double back, stop, and there are dig-scars in the silt. The Hunter fishes for them. What passes between the Hunter and a scythe under the dust, no one has seen. The roads keep the minutes of those meetings in a script of turned earth.

Because everything in the world is small, and the Hunter is not small. The Hunter is the size of a legend, which is to say a finger long, root-pale, forty-footed, older than any herd-memory — older, the Tar says, than the Towers. It is the only being that knows the whole world. Every other life is lived in a parish: a grazing ground, a perch, a meniscus, an eleven-minute dawn-line. But the Hunter's roads run from the Tar to the Ridge, around the Disc's rim, under the Stele's shadow, through the Arches home. It is blind, or next to it; it has never seen the Star's picture, never seen the Eye; it knows the world by tremor and warmth, and by that measure it knows the world completely. From its den it can hear a herd cross the Level. It can count the silt-flea eruptions like rain on a roof. It hunts the way weather hunts: it lies along its own road, still as a root, and lets the road deliver, and in the fat years it grows, and its shed ghosts stand on the roads unharvested, and the herds detour around them, because fear outlives the body. And this is its other work, though it does not know it has work: it is the flywheel. When the flea-years run fat it grows; the claw-bearers multiply a season behind; the scythes rise from the tunnels; the herds thin; and the felt creeps back over the cropped ground, rusting across the stubble, and the wheel comes around again.

Its den in the ribcage hollow has a floor of plates — the undigestible backs of its prey, a dark coinage layered generations deep. The Tar keeps what died by accident; the den-floor keeps what died of the Hunter, and between them they are the complete account. And the Tar holds, in an upper stratum, one long pale curve that the worms pass through and close behind: a Hunter that walked into the low end before the Towers were tall. There has always been one Hunter. One at a time, as far down as the record reads. Where the next one comes from, nothing here knows — the crack, perhaps, the outer dark. It is the world's one unanswered question, and the world does not ask it.

These are the tenants, and the census is always out of date, because something is always hatching. No one here remembers anything, and everything here is remembered — in tar, in felt-seams, in tower-courses, in the floor of the den. At dusk the Eye goes dark and the cold descends and the world performs its one miracle of unanimity: everyone stops. A mite mid-step, a singer mid-note, the note cut off; a skater holding its dimple in the meniscus; the skull full of midges like seed in a pod; the Hunter coiled on its coins. For the length of a night the world is a picture of itself. And when the outer wind leans on the building and the housing finds its note, everyone is in the chord — the herd's million plates gone sympathetic, the singers taking it up, the midge-columns trembling like struck strings, the skaters feeling it come up through the black, and in the ribcage the Hunter, the loudest thing that does not sing.

Everything here knows the sound of water. It has walked across the roof for a hundred years, and nothing it is made of has ever come in. It is the one word the whole world understands and no one can speak.

And all of it — the herds and the worms in their centuries, the sect that lives by a reflection, the Hunter on its forty pale feet — would fit in a box you could carry. Not one of them knows it, because not one of them has ever needed the world to be larger. It is exactly the size of their lives. Count them, if you like, with your ear to the housing. You will not finish. Something is always hatching.
