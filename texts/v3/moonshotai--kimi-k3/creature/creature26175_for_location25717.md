The first census comes back empty. You could walk a full day through the field and file a report of vacancy: nothing moves, nothing flees, nothing watches. There is nothing green to read as pasture, nothing warm to catch against the grey, no shadow anywhere to hold a shape against. The standing crop is a stain on stones. Whatever animals there are will be slow, stone-colored, and still — and in this light, stillness is the same as absence.

The report would be wrong. The count here has to be made by ear, at night, over seasons, and made that way it comes out like this: one pasture. A slow snow of swimmers in the meres. A herd of almost nothing. And the third thing, whatever the third thing is. Beyond that the census-takers argue, because beyond that you must decide whether to count the chord itself, and that is an argument no one has won.

Two rules order the list. The first: nothing here lives faster than the water leaves. The whole population is paced to the draining — walking, sliding, grazing, or sleeping its way downhill at the speed of a finger's-width a year. The second: everything lives where the sound is. Vibration stirs the seeps; the seeps feed the films; the films feed everything else. Sound is this place's weather, and everything alive in it lives in the loud. The dead zones are the true deserts — nothing builds there, nothing lingers, even the films grow thin, as if quiet were a kind of drought.

---

Begin where everything here begins: the films.

Rest a bare hand on a filmed pool-edge and nothing happens, and that is the point. Come back in an hour and the film has drawn a hair's breadth away from the heat-print of your hand; come back in a season and it has still not quite forgotten you. They cross fresh rockfall at a fingernail's width per season, rust-red going over iron first, as if the stone were a meal laid out in courses. Even here there are border disputes — a greenish slick in the sheltered seams that may be the films' strange relative or may be only chemistry with ambitions — but the rust and the olive-black are the standing government.

This is why nothing rots. Rot is an economy, and the films hold the monopoly. Anything that dies here is already being eaten by the time decay would have organized itself — stripped molecule by molecule at the seep's own pace, until what remains is a shape in chemistry, and then the mineral takes the shape. Death does not decompose in this basin. It precipitates.

They are thickest at the nodes, where the ground hums all night — the standing wave stirs their seeps the way wind stirs grass, and they grow fat on it. Even the pasture listens. Or is listened through; in this place it is hard to keep those apart. Some carry the faint chemical shimmer that is this world's only lamp, and at night the meres lie under the black deck like banked coals. Whether the films are one individual with a basin for a body, or a million nations sharing a skin, no stain has yet said. Cut one and you have two. It is the oldest thing here — older than the field, perhaps older than the lid. It was grazing these seeps when the basin was seafloor, and it took the draining the way it takes everything: slowly, and as food.

---

The meres hold a slow snow of swimmers.

Bells of glass a hand across, a few kinds, all of them so nearly the same water as the water that finding one is a matter of catching the snow in the act of moving. A pulse a half-minute. The single solid thing in a swimmer is one grain of rust-red iron, gathered in its youth from the films and carried all its life — ballast and compass in one, the pin it steers by in a world without bearings. They live for decades. Nothing here is in a hurry to die either.

Their strangeness is the sleep. When a mere's edge dries, the swimmers there do not flee. They lie down in the ooze and let the precipitate take them to the shoulders — a pebble with a heartbeat on a period of years. The shallows are paved with them. A wet week wakes the shallowest, and they shrug off their stone and resume, mid-stroke, a life interrupted by a decade. Deeper down, the sleepers wait out longer terms. Below those are the ones the water will not come back for: the basin is draining, and its floors are an archive of appointments the water will not keep. Everything alive here is retreating downslope; the swimmers retreat in their sleep, a generation at a time, down toward the mere — and the mere is not waiting either.

---

Then the herd of almost nothing.

Rowboat-length, low, broad, slick-skinned, with a mouth that takes the films off stone the way a wave takes footprints off sand. A grazer crosses a hectare in a season, and its trail — a broad polished run through the ooze — stays sharp for months, because everything that would erase it works at the same pace. From some middle age they begin to keep the mineral: first a keel of crust along the spine, then a carapace, then a slowing you could plot in the spacing of the trails. The very old are half-stone and move like weather. Here the census blurs, because a rinded grazer at rest is a knoll, and a knoll may be a grazer at rest, and the only sure test is to wait a year and see whether it has moved — by which time you may have built your camp on it.

Some of the far-off booms are not crust failing under weather but under a grazer — an old one going through a travertine sheet into the hollow beneath, and in no hurry to climb out. The crash arrives like distant weather; an hour later comes the slow shoulder of its emergence. And a grazer will set its wide mouth to an open chimney and breathe into it, once, long, the way you'd sound a bottle, and stand while the spire answers, and then move on. Call it navigation. The haze gives no landmarks and the deck gives no sun, but every node's chord is a beacon with a signature, and a grazer carries the basin in its head as a map of notes. The bellows you hear at the limit of hearing may be one answering another across kilometers of fog — or answering the field. From inside the chord it is not easy to say which.

They are too few for society. Twice in a decade two will arrive at the same node and stand a stone's throw apart for a week, doing nothing you can detect. Whether that is courtship, conversation, or two clocks briefly synchronized, no one is going to tell you. The one reliable sighting comes when the lid lifts — once in a decade, briefly — and on that day the grazers raise their heads in the strange directed light and cast, for an hour, the only shadows the basin ever has: boulders that have decided, briefly, to be surprised.

A grazer does not rot — the films. It is not eaten — there is no one to eat it. It settles, one year, in a seep it has reason to like, and the water it has been drinking all its life takes it: coats it, builds on it — mound, knoll, chimney. Fifty years on, the chord has a voice with a low strange note. Some of the deepest tones in the old field are exactly that. And when a young knoll rises in the wet, the works sometimes go up around it — ringed with fitted splinters, kept. Whether that is the third thing paying its respects, or taking an interest of another kind, belongs to the rest of the count.

---

The third thing announces itself only in what it finishes.

The works are maintained. This is the first fact, and it takes seasons to notice, because the maintenance runs at the pace of the place: a threshold felled by rockfall is, some later season, standing again — the same slab rewedged, or a better one, chosen, set where the seep will weld it. No one has watched it happen. You go back, and it is done. And map the works against the sound and a pattern surfaces that no single visit would give you: the fresh works stand on the live nodes; the flowstoned ones string away upslope along the nodes' old tracks — a century, two, five — the path of a note walking downhill as the water drops, written out in cairns. Whoever keeps the works keeps them current, keeps them at the loud places, and has kept them current for longer than any record you could bring here.

Then there are the night sounds, and the tell is the pause. A grazer knocks by accident — stone shouldered, sheet shifting, incidental percussion. The third thing knocks, and then waits: waits out the whole odd minute the field takes to answer, listens to its own phrase come back deepened and from the wrong direction, and only then knocks again — changed. It is not calling across the basin. It is conversing with the basin's memory. It files a phrase; the archive returns it; and in how the return has altered — deepened here, thinned there, one voice gone drowned, a new crack gone sharp — it reads the state of the world: the water's level, the stress in the stack, which tubes have opened, which have choked. The field is its instrument twice over. Once when sounded. Once when remembering.

As for the works themselves — worship, marking, appetite. None of the three guesses is wrong, if the word for all three is speech. The third thing has no voice anyone has heard: no breath-sound, no cry, nothing that carries. But a threshold couples two spires, so that a tap on one answers in the other through stone. A leaned arc of crust-sheet takes the wind at timbres the tubes cannot make. A cairn of splinters, fitted just so, ticks in moving air like teeth — and a humming cairn stirs the seep beneath it, and the films grow fatter there, so the works are gardens too, if you insist on the word. Their placement is only good acoustics: you put your voice where it will be heard. No tool has ever touched them; the craft is search. Somewhere in ten thousand splinters is the one with the note, and the third thing tastes stones the way you'd test a coin — in a world that already smells like a coin held in the mouth — and sets the right one where the field will seat it, and comes back across seasons while the precipitate welds it true. A builder's works outlive it by centuries. The flowstoned ones upslope are dead voices, still legible if anyone could read them. No one can.

Of the builders themselves: you will not see one, and the reason is not hiding. The reason is stopping. A builder at rest is a stub, a heave, a low rind of precipitate the color of everything; in shadowless grey light, stillness is invisibility, and the third thing can be still for a season. You have likely stood among them at a node — the weight of a person each, or two — and known it only as a place where the chord was strong and the stones seemed, in retrospect, selected. What the nights give instead: a cadence with too many points of contact, like a slow handful of pebbles let fall one at a time; a rasp like a file drawn once across stone, and then thought about; water shouldered in a place where no grazer trails run. Eyeless, so far as the dark lets anyone claim. Dusted always with the field's own dust, as if even the body were on its way to becoming the place. When the lid lifts, once in a decade, the knocks stop — the only silence the working nights ever have — and when the deck returns they resume: one, then another, then answering ones out to the edge of the haze, like a congress reconvening.

And it will examine you. Spend enough nights in the field and it comes, unhurried, at the working pace of the place, and taps you — twice, on the shoulder, the boot, the bone of the hip, the way it taps stone — and then a stillness while it reads your resonance, and then nothing: it has gone, or it has stopped, which is the same. Minutes later, out in the field, your two taps come back to you, deepened, from the wrong direction. You have been filed. There are those who say that years on, at odd hours, the field still gives their taps back — that once the archive has an entry for you, it recirculates. No one has proposed a way to resign from the collection.

No one has seen a young one, or a small one, or a dead one — and the likeliest account covers all three lacks at once. They come up out of the stack, the drowned under-field, the water-filled tubes below the waterline, where the young — if that is the word — must filter in the dark like everything else here, until the draining opens their birth-tube to the air and they climb out into the chord, eyeless, all ear. A builder's life is then one walk: downhill, behind its note, from the strandlines toward the mere, centuries long, its works trailing behind it upslope like a wake the field slowly swallows. And when its note reaches the waterline and drowns, the builder follows it down. It wades in. It does not come out. The mineral takes it as it takes everything here, and years later, when the water has dropped another body's-length, a new chimney opens its mouth over the place where one went down, and the chord gains a voice with a low strange note. You will never find a dead builder. You hear them nightly. The old field is full of the dead, still speaking.

---

So: the census, by ear, over seasons. One pasture, or a million. A slow snow of swimmers, most of them asleep, some of them asleep forever. A herd of almost nothing, becoming stone by degrees. An unknown number of builders, unknown because stillness is their camouflage and stone is their fate. And then the standing argument, the one no one has won: whether to count the chord. The third thing plainly counts it — or is counted by it. It keeps the loud places the way you'd keep a fire, and it treats the archive as a colleague. Count the chord, and the population of the basin is one: one inhabitant, resident since the draining began, made of everything that has ever sounded here — kept, and given back, slowly, changed.

You are in it now. Your footsteps went in and came back wrong and went in again; your two taps are on file; somewhere in the field a phrase assembled partly from your voice is working its way through a hundred thousand pipes, deepening. Come back in fifty years and the place will be tuned lower, a new rank of mouths open at the waterline — and you will still be in circulation, handled on the working nights, played back at the loud places. Ask the basin what lives here and it answers on a delay, in a voice assembled from yours. It has been answering for a long time. It is not in any hurry to stop.
