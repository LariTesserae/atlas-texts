Take the census by ear. There is no other way to take it.

Sit anywhere on the floor — on a shell-dune, say, with the molt-snow drifting against your legs — close your eyes, which are no use to you here, and let the voices sort themselves. Every voice is a species. Every species is a trade. What follows is an ear-account of the Lowdark's citizens, from the stone-sweat upward, and then a little way downward, into the stone.

Begin with the velvet, because everything does.

It is the only producer in the basin — the one thing that feeds without first being fed. And it is nearly the only silent thing. At the rich sulfur seeps, where it grows rust-colored and fast, you can hear it faintly if you put your head down: a fizz, a tiny simmering, gas working up through the film. Elsewhere it makes no sound at all. The foundation of the noisiest world imaginable is mute. It has three estates: rust velvet at the rich springs, gray at the thin ones, and black where it is old — black velvet is velvet that has begun to eat itself, and it is the black that lifts. At certain turns in the basin's slow breathing, the old film casts its motes into the air, a pollen for a world without flowers, and that is the smother's harvest, and the timing of everything overhead.

In the wettest skin of the springs themselves live the seep-things: spring-wights, white thread-eels the length of a hand, half their bodies always inside the rock, swaying in the mineral breath of the vents; and around them the ring-bodied seep-lice, translucent, ticking faintly as they turn in the film. They never leave the water. There is so little water that leaving it has never occurred to them.

Then the rasp-slugs, which are the basin's true cattle.

A rasp-slug is a file with a foot. Its tongue is a ribbon of teeth, its back carries a keel of chalk, and somewhere in its soft head is a compass that reads minerals — a slug can taste a seep from farther than you could shout across. In the rich hollows they lie in carpets, knee-deep, and the carpets do a strange thing: the rasping organizes itself. It phase-locks into slow waves that roll through the herd, a shimmer you can walk toward in the dark, and no one knows whether the wave is a defense, a conversation, or simply what happens when ten thousand files work one field. Size is age. The thumb-sized rasp in a dry chatter. The old ones — forearm-long, decades deep — rasp so slowly you can hear each tooth pass, a bass note under the chatter, and hunters listening at the edge of a carpet can read its composition the way you'd read a ledger. Riding in the keel-grooves of the big ones are shell-fleas, cleaner-passengers, eating what fouls the slug and alerting it, with a nip, when something heavy comes near. The slugs tolerate them. Everything here that can be ridden is ridden by something.

The skitters are the percussion section, and they come in kinds. Gravel-skitters, fist-small, ricocheting off the stone in their thousands, feeding on slug-leavings and on each other. Anvils, bigger, that drum the ground deliberately — two hard knocks and a scrape — a bluff meant to say something armored and irritable lives here. And the click-liar, which has learned the most expensive sound in the basin: the concussion-click of a hunting thumper. It lies in a language of blows. A patch of floor cleared by a false click is a good place to feed — until a real thumper comes to see what is using its voice, and then the liar learns the price of lying, which is the truth.

Over the carcasses of the basin preside the gathered, and they are the strangest citizens of all.

The individual is a nothing — palm-sized, hook-limbed, eyeless, barely enough animal to be hungry. But the individuals assemble, thousands strong, into a single walking shape the size of a house, and the shape crosses the floor on legs that are individuals, and it has no mind anywhere in it, and yet it decides. Listeners — and there are things in the Lowdark that spend their whole lives listening — know the old shapes by their gait. Each house walks with its own rhythm, held steady across centuries though every link in it has died and been replaced a hundred times. There is a house that limps. It has limped for a hundred years. The leg is replaced; the limp is inherited. There is a shape called the Long Walk that takes a full day to pass a given point, and the floor beneath it is left different — scoured, sorted.

For that is what the gathered are: the undertakers. When a house finds a carcass it flows over it like something poured, and when it walks on, it leaves behind sorted piles — bone here, shell here, the soft nothing gone entirely. The geology of the basin is curated. Nothing is wasted because something walks around making sure of it. Sometimes a house stands still for days, doing nothing anyone can name, and then splits into two houses that walk apart, each with its own gait, each new. Sometimes two meet and merge and the combined shape carries, for a while, a doubled rhythm, like a chord learning itself. And rarely — this is attested by generations of listeners — a house goes to a ring-stone and drums it. Slowly. Deliberately. No one knows whether it is declaring territory, or remembering something, or doing the one thing it does that resembles a thought. Nothing about it decides anything. And yet it decides.

The hunters work the middle air and the open floor, and the first thing to understand about them is that their heads are ears.

The skin is gray, naked, folded; at rest they look earless, eyeless, nearly faceless. Then the sails unfurl — great thin wings of skin and gristle, the whole head opening like a fan — and the animal becomes a listening instrument with a mouth attached. The kinds are many. Shriek-runners hunt in packs, screaming into the dark and reading the return, and you can tell the size of a pack by the richness of its chord; they groom one another's sails, picking off the sail-mites that nibble the edges, and a well-groomed pack is a healthy pack and a louder one. Thumpers hunt alone: their skulls are struck bells, hollow chambers that fire clicks arriving as blows, and you feel their successful hunts in your sternum, a small concussion, then the brief unraveling sounds of something being taken apart.

Well-keepers are the patient ones. They hold echo-wells — the canyons that return sound late and wrong — and they hold them for generations. A well-keeper lineage tends its well the way you would tend a garden, except the crop is a lie: they drop stones in particular places to keep the well's wrongness exactly wrong, to keep its returning voice flushing prey along the same old deceived paths. A well's wrongness, maintained across centuries, is an inheritance more valuable than any territory. And they court through the wells. A male calls into the canyon, and the female does not choose the voice — she chooses what the land does to the voice. The echo-well is the matchmaker. The bloodlines of the well-keepers are edited by stone.

There are husk-fishers, walking the shell-dunes with their sails pressed nearly to the surface, listening for movement under the molt-snow, spearing down. There are leapers at the smother's lower edge, hurling themselves into the swarm with sails spread and mouths wide. There are small soft hunters that spend the cold troughs in the huddle-mounds and hunt only in the warm times. And in the lean times, when the pulses fail to align, the hunters take each other, and the screams then have a register the whole floor knows and goes quiet for — briefly, briefly — before resuming.

Then there are the lights, and everything that lives around the lights, which is a whole economy of hunger and theft.

The candle-mouths sit at floor level with their one cold blue-gray glow, beautiful, patient. Understand the anatomy: the light is a fingertip. The animal is the room it reaches from. Most of a lure is buried — a pit of jaw under a film of false floor — and the glow you see is the only part of it that has ever been above ground. Some lures have gone further and learned to sound: a patter like fine rain on stone, a hitching cry like a hurt skitter. Everything here that glows is a mouth, and some of the mouths have learned to sing like weather.

Against them work the light-thieves: hunters that kept one eye — one, dim, costly — precisely for this. They hold at the edge of a lure's draw-circle, where the drawn and dazed prey staggers toward the glow, and they take it before the pit does. Keeping an eye in the Lowdark is a debt; they pay it in wariness, because the lures are not choosy, and a light-thief that drifts too close becomes the thing that was drawn. It is an old arms race, conducted at candle-speed, in the dark, around the only beauty the basin offers.

Above all of this, the smother — and the first thing to know about the smother is that it is not a species. It is a guild, a whole prairie that happens to be airborne.

It is layered by altitude, each stratum sieving a different size of mote: moth-kind low down, gnat-kind above, membrane-winged sieve-mouths at the top where the motes are finest. Its wing-thunder has weather in it. When it passes, the basin's faint seep of not-quite-light dies — an eclipse made of wings — and its droppings fall for hours afterward like soft ash, feeding the floor. And living permanently inside the swarm are the smother-wolves: fast, narrow fliers that eat the smother as the smother eats the motes. They are born on the roosting cliffs and some of them never land again. They sleep in installments, between wingbeats. Their parasites live on them, and their parasites have parasites, and the whole hanging food-web — grazer, hunter, freeloader — may pass a lifetime without any member of it touching the ground. A country in the air, complete, that the floor never sees and constantly hears.

The roosting cliffs themselves are inhabited. The droppings and compacted armor of ages have built pale towers at the roosts, and the towers are bored through by ash-borers and tower-wights — pale, soft, eyeless things that never leave the guano terraces, that have never once flown, living their whole lives in the accumulated fall of things that do nothing but fly. Some of the towers are ring-stones. When a breeding pulse sets a cliff-face singing, the roosting smother erupts off the drummed stone in a single exhalation of wings, and for a moment the cliff's chord and the swarm's thunder are one sound, and listeners on the floor below turn their sails toward it, whatever they are, because it is one of the great sounds of the world.

Above the smother: the high fliers, of which nothing is known except their wing-beats.

Slow. Huge. Spaced so wide apart that each beat is its own event, arriving out of the lid-gloom like something remembered. Two are known by their rhythms. The Long Beat is steady — steady enough to set a life by, if anything in the basin kept time, which nothing does. The Irregular comes rarely, and it is not steady at all, and the basin does not quite hush for it; it listens. What the high fliers eat, where they rest — whether they rest — is conjecture. Perhaps they nest against the lid. Perhaps they graze the roof of the smother. Perhaps there are seven of them. Perhaps there is one. Their droppings fall rarely and are occasions: a sky-fall will feed a hollow for a whole pulse, and the rasp-carpets migrate toward one for miles, slow as weather, certain as scripture.

Down in the cold troughs, the huddle-mounds, and within them a citizen that exists nowhere else and matters everywhere: the truce-keeper.

A mound is not peace. A mound is a treaty, and treaties need keepers. The truce-keepers are small, hot, ceaseless — they move through the pile from body to body, rasp-slug to skitter to little hunter, grooming, eating parasites and dead skin, radiating a warmth out of proportion to their size. While the keepers work the mound, the armistice holds: predator stacked on prey, everybody fed in their way by the keepers' labor, everybody warm. The mounds form at the same hollows for ages — hearth-hollows, their floors polished by centuries of piling — and the keepers hold territories among them. And when the keepers leave a mound, you can hear what happens next. The pile remembers what it is.

Scattered through the molt-snow, indistinguishable from it, sleep the rain-wakers. They husk themselves between rains — dry, light, driftable — and blow into the hollows with the rest of the shed skins, and you cannot tell the drifted dead from the waiting. Then comes a rain, fine and cold and grain-small, and for one frantic hour the dunes themselves wake: the husk-sleepers split, feed, breed, scream in voices no one hears at any other time, and husk again before the stone dries. Not all of the stillness in the Lowdark is dead. Some of it is saving itself.

And after everything has eaten — after the gathered have sorted and the skitters have swarmed — come the last citizens, the bone-borers, drilling the talus at the cliff bases, reducing armor and skeleton to meal, and the meal to dust, and the dust compacts, over ages, into the husk-shale the pale towers are made of, and the stone gives its minerals to the seep-water, and the seep-water sweats up through the deep rock, and the velvet eats the sweat. The circle closes. The geology moves at the speed of appetite, and the appetite never pauses, and the books always balance.

Below the circle there is one more possibility, and it is the oldest story the floor tells.

The basin's hum has a lowest octave, below the seeps' breathing, below the accumulated voice of the crowds — a note you do not hear so much as lean against. Some of it is the hollow stone. But the hollow stone has pipes, and the pipes are inhabited: pipe-dwellers, long soft resonant bodies wedged in the gasways, singing faintly with the seeps' exhaling, flutes living inside the flute. And deeper than the pipe-dwellers, the listeners say, the note continues, and it is too steady, and too slow, and too old to be stone. They call it the Under-Hum: something distributed through the whole vent system of the basin, breathing in centuries, the seeps its exhale, the velvet fed on its sweat, everything above it a settling of accounts on its breath. If it is one thing, it is the oldest thing here, and the largest, and the least aware. It does not sing for you. It may not know anything. It only continues.

And then there is the citizen that is known only by subtraction.

The Hushmaker. Some say it drinks sound — that the Hush is not only the basin holding its breath but the air itself going dead around something that eats noise the way the velvet eats stone-sweat. Some say it comes up from the gasways, that it is the Under-Hum's agent, or its appetite, or its dream. What it eats when it is hungry — rarely, so rarely — the floor does not describe. The floor afterward is simply missing something. A house that walked for a hundred years walks no longer, and there is no carcass, and the gathered find nothing to sort, because there is nothing left to gather.

During the Hush, everything freezes. The rasp-slugs mid-rasp. The skitters stone-still. The smother holding its wings. The houses halted mid-step, each one a statue that is also a crowd. Even the lure-lights shutter — the mouths close; everything that glows goes dark, because even hunger is afraid. And when the Hush lifts, the sound returns in an order that is a hierarchy of courage: the rasp first, always the rasp — grazing is the floor's bravery — then the skitters' gravel-percussion, then the gathered resuming their walk, and last, longest hesitant, the screams of the hunters. Because the hunters know best what silence means.

That is the census, taken by ear, and it is never finished, because the hum never stops and the ledger is never closed. But sit on your dune long enough and one thought arrives and does not leave: the Lowdark is not unwitnessed. It is the most witnessed place in the world. Nothing sees it — but everything in it hears everything else, constantly, from the Under-Hum's breathing to the Long Beat crossing beneath the lid, and the hunters' sails hold the whole country at once as a single sounding shape, and the wells remember every voice ever thrown into them, slightly wrong, forever.

The basin hears itself the way a bell hears its own ringing: completely, and from the inside.
