Take the census, or try to. No one ever has, because the counting would outlast the counted. Press a hand into the felt and it comes away inhabited — springtails scattered like thrown pepper, a mite reconsidering its route, a worm withdrawing its front half from the light of your attention. A fistful of this floor holds more animals than a meadow holds in an acre, and the strangeness is distributed so evenly that it reads as ordinary. Nothing here is rare. Much of it is found nowhere else on earth.

The floor keeps its creatures at the two ends of size. It favors the very small, too small to hear, and the very large, too big to stop — and between them is a gap where a fox-sized thing would fit, filled mostly by rumor, because a middle-sized body makes noise, and noise is found. The only middle-sized animals here are calves, and calves are quiet, and they stay close.

The first custom of the place is touch. Nothing in the dark is offended by contact; the crowded floor has no concept of trespass, only of flavor. Two animals meeting in the black do not startle. They taste each other, exchange the information, and go on. Everything reads by hand. Your own skin, after an hour, is fluent in being read.

Each tank-plant is a tower of small countries. A single cupped rosette holds its liter — rain that fell a month ago, recondensed nightly — and inside that liter, a nation: larvae with jaws like wire, a translucent isopod on its slow patrol, and the cup-frog, the size of a fingernail paring, the color of wet parchment, born in the plant and unlikely ever to touch the ground. Its one journey is the crossing: to breed, it must cross a meter of dry litter to the next tank, and it goes on drip-nights, navigating by the sound of water, and that meter is its whole epic, the only adventure its kind has ever had. The tenants pay rent in waste, and the plant keeps them the way you would keep an orchard — landlord and granary at once, farming its own farmers.

The cistern trees keep the floor's etiquette. The queue at a hollow trunk forms at dusk and holds all night, and its order is posted nowhere and kept always: first the moths, then the climbing things, then the patient middle watch, and last — announced an hour ahead, foot by foot, through the felt — the engines. An engine drinks for an hour and the water level falls a hand's width, and no one minds, because the tree refills by morning, condensing the night out of the air, and because the engine's song is what marks this tree at all, and the song is older than the tree.

The roads of the harvesters are older than the forest above them. Worn a finger deep into the felt, re-cut as fast as the felt regrows, they have run their routes since before the crowns overhead were seeds. A fallen leaf becomes disks on these roads, and the disk size is a signature — you can tell whose road you stand on by the litter of its cut edges. Where two colonies' roads cross, the basin has its only architecture: a living arch of linked bodies, each stream passing over and under the other, maintained hour by hour for as long as both colonies live. It is not built. It is held. And when a colony's tree falls, the colony moves in an afternoon along its own roads, and the roads remain, and the next tenants inherit them.

Inside the dead trunks, the refineries keep their own climate — wet heat, the smell of bread and ammonia. The queen at the center is older than the tree she is eating was when it fell. She has outlived her food once already: the trunk she began on is gone, carried away in pieces, and her children moved her, chamber by chamber, into this one. She is the only individual down here whose life is measured in the lives of trees. Every refinery pays its taxes in tenants — beetles wearing termite faces so they will be fed, a pale roach that cleans and is tolerated, thieves tolerated at a fixed rate, the skim priced in.

The processors are the grubs, blunt and pale as thumbs, working in shifts that are just generations, and the fungal felts, which are the true chemists of the floor. Everything else is their workforce or their larder. They farm the ants as surely as the ants farm them; the arrangement is old enough that the question of who domesticated whom has no answer and no asker. And the felts are continuous. Follow the mycelium from the swales to the shaft-mouths and it does not break. No one has ever found the seam.

The moths are the air's population, and they are also a subsidy: after the leaf-fall and the guano, a third snow falls here, the slow dust of wing-scales, settling on everything, digested by the felt — so that the basin's moths are, in the end, partly eaten by their own ground. They land on you for the salt. A moth will spend an hour at the corner of your eye, patient as a nurse. On the engines, a kind lives that exists nowhere else, drinking sweat at the fold of the shoulder.

The law that sound is an address has its collectors. There is a bat that hunts standing still: it lands on the mat, folds its wings, and listens with its whole face, ears like two leaves, and it can hear a beetle's footfall at ten paces. When a patch of the carpet goes suddenly silent — a hush with a shape — the collector is standing in it. There are the velvet ribbons, soft and many-legged, a darker dark flowing over the felt, taking what they touch in nets of glue; nothing that meets one struggles loudly enough to matter. There are the grike-fishers, long-legged as a spread hand, squatting over the water-grooves and reading the surface with their feet, taking what the drip knocks in. And under the drip-lines, where water lands all night from a hundred feet up, the felt is dimpled with small pits, and at the bottom of each dimple a larva waits, jaws up, employed by gravity.

And the engines. In the whole basin there are perhaps forty. Each walks an estate, and each estate is a song.

The gut-song is not one song but many. Every route has its own — a melody too low and too slow to hear as music, felt instead through the soles as a weather of the ground — and it is the map. The seeps are in it. The shaft-mouths are in it, as notes a calf learns with special care, because the song's low places are where the ground opens. The gaps are in it: when a treefall opens the sky, the route bends to it and the song takes on a verse, and the verse is kept after the canopy closes, kept sometimes for a century, sung over sealed darkness by engines whose granddams saw the light. The songs are archives. They are the only history the floor keeps, stored at walking speed, at gut pitch, in forty slow voices.

A calf is born walking, mid-route, and spends its first years behind its mother learning a loop it will not finish alone; the loop outlives the walker, and the title passes with the melody. The engines read the felt through their feet, each step a sounding, and taste the air for the fungal belts, and when they drink at the shaft-pools the hum of the grid comes up through the water into their teeth, and they stand in it a long time. No one knows what they are doing. Perhaps listening to the deep news.

Their heat is the other fact of them. A body-length away, the air is already theirs. The warmest dry surface in the basin is an engine's back, and so their backs are settled — the basking moths, the remora beetles at the heels, the pale frogs raising tadpoles in the rain-water held between the folds of the shoulders. The eldest of them, whom the floor knows by the longest song, carries an island's worth of endemics: a frog, a moth, two beetles, a tick, that live on her hide and nowhere else in the world. She is an archipelago of one, and she walks, and her calf follows already wearing the first emigrants.

Engines die rarely — sinking slow to their knees, the heat lingering for days like a banked fire — and when one does, the news goes through the felt ahead of the smell, and the queue forms in its fixed order. First the township leaves: the frogs come down the legs with their tadpoles in their mouths. Then the flies, then the beetles, then the spores. A carcass is a province, worked for a year. The bones go away in pieces. In five years the place is only a warm richness in the felt, greener in the fungal sense, and the heir already walks the loop, and the song has one more verse. Engines have no enemies but the throats and time. A calf that misremembers a low note falls, and the shafts take engines rarely and hugely, and the deep pools remember it for years.

Because the shafts keep the oldest tenants. The bats are the floor's only commuters — the only animals here who live in both countries, rising at dusk like smoke and returning at dawn with the canopy's wealth in their bellies, hanging through the day in a fur of bodies while the second subsidy falls beneath them, mined in shifts: first the molds, then the mites, then the flies, every grain claimed before it settles. And below the roosts, in the black water, the grid hums, and in the lowest pools — where guano has sifted down for a hundred thousand years and the food chain is one long slow inhalation — live the eel-things grown old: long as a man is tall, white as roots, centuries in the same water, living on what falls. You do not see them. You wade, and feel their fields against your calves, a slow deep pulse like a heart heard through a wall, and you are read, thoroughly, and let go. They are the water's memory. And the water is the basin's reserve. The oldest lives here are the bank.

That is the census, or the beginning of one. But ask what lives here most, and the millions fold into one.

The felt has no seam. It may be a single body, or a few old ones grown through each other until the question stopped applying; it has been here as long as the basin, because it is the basin — the part that is alive underneath the animals the way the animals are alive underneath the roof. The columns are its fingers. The engines are its slow blood, carrying its water and its heat. The drip is its pulse. It eats the subsidy and pays the tenantry in the eating, and its warmth is the warmth of the whole floor, because the whole floor is its body at work.

It is the only resident. Everything else is what it keeps.
