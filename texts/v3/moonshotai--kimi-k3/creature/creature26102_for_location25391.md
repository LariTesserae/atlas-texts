Take the census. You will want to — everyone who comes up out of the haze onto the Kettle wants to — and the Authority building keeps the forms, in a drawer that has never been opened, with POPULATION at the top of the page and a pen beside it, ready. Readiness is the protocol. Write zero. Then go outside and begin the corrections, because zero is the official position, and the official position is wrong in at least five directions.

Begin with the smallest entry, because everything here begins small and then slows down. The lamp-moths: each lamppost is a cosmos with a climate, and a moth lives its handful of nights entirely within one lamppost's weather — the warm glass sun, the condensate beading on the post like a private rain, the cone of lit wet air, and beyond it the void, from which nothing has ever returned. There are nights the schedule does not call for a given lamp, and the moths wait out the dark the way you wait out weather, and when the lamp comes back it is, again, creation. They sip condensate. They dust the bulbs with their wings — the smallest sound in the world, which here counts as a sound, and which the census should count as a population declaring itself. When they die, the film takes the dust of them. Nothing dries, everything returns, nothing is wasted; the moths go into the velvet like everyone else, and the seals stay supple. Even that returns.

Except — sometimes the thin upland wind takes one. Imagine it: the sun goes out behind a shoulder of roof and does not rise again, and yet the ground is warm and everything is wet and condensate is everywhere, and so the moth does not die. It walks. No one knows how far a moth can walk in the dark, moths being brief and walking being slow, but out on the perimeter road there is a lamppost a mile from its nearest neighbor, and the moths that have not found it go on not finding it — which means there is a people out there in that dark, walking, generation upon generation, each one dying a few meters farther along than its mother, the whole lineage shaped like an arrow aimed at a light it has only been told about, told in the way moths are told things, which is to say in the body. Nothing hunts them. The dark is harmless. It is only long.

Go down, then. The census taker must go down, into the cisterns, where the white eels hang in the pump-breath like held thoughts. They were not put here. The cisterns were put around them: the first well found the aquifer and the aquifer was occupied. The eels came up with the first draw and did not object, and when the pumps learned their rhythm the eels learned it better, and now they feel the whole timetable of Providence — every scheduled valve, every drawn reservoir — arriving through the water as a slow tide, the way you feel your own pulse. This is their entire culture, and it is older than the Works. The city is a thing that happened to their ceiling. The food in this world is patience, and the eels have been eating longest.

And sometimes the pulse is wrong. Not failed — wrong; wrong from below; a current moving against the pumping rhythm — and the eels go to the walls, all of them, without hurry and without error, like hands politely leaving a room. What passes then, passes, and the census has no box for it. It may be one of them grown to the size the deep allows. It may be the aquifer's own slow circulation, a current that has kept its shape so long it has habits. It does not hunt; nothing here hunts; it is simply large, and old, and first. The eels make room for it the way you make room for weather — without resentment and without worship, because the room was never only theirs. Then the rhythm resumes, and they come off the walls, and nothing is written down. Nothing is ever written down. The eels keep their history the only way anything here keeps anything: they keep it in their habits.

You will not find the sump-walkers. The census does not find them; it intersects them.
