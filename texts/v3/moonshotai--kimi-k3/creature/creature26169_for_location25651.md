They are of the world's own chemistry, and it marks them the way it marks everything the Anvil makes: they run warm, they run slow, and there is more stone in their temperament than they would ever admit.

Start with the body, since the world started with the sky. A person of the String is warm to the touch in any weather — blood held at the temperature of the ground, which is the temperature of everything else, so that taking someone's hand here carries none of the small shock it does elsewhere. You touch a person and they feel the way the world feels: like something that has agreed to carry you. Their hearts keep a deep, unhurried time, a beat you can wait for. Their eyes are built for the enormous nights — wide, dark, catching lamplight the way mica catches it — so that a walked causeway at midnight is a road and not a rumor. They do not thirst. There is nothing to drink, and so they do not drink; what moisture they need comes folded into food, the way the felt takes what it needs from stone. The mug of something warm at dusk is for the hands. It has always been for the hands.

Their skin they dress with beast-tallow every morning, against the dry and against the charge; an unoiled person crackles when they remove a coat, and by dusk trails light like a dune-walker. Their hair they wear long and weighted, braided with metal thread, cuffed with carcass-bronze, because loose hair stands with the static — and a person whose hair is standing is either furious, careless, or about to be storm-struck, and in all three cases is approached with courtesy.

Then there is speech. They keep two languages: one for the air, one for the hands. Air-speech is low, close, and slow, a thing of doorways and small rooms, of mouths near ears. Hand-speech crosses streets, platforms, quarry floors — precise, unhurried, fully capable of contracts, insults, and verse. Beneath both is the third register, the intimate one: palm-speech, signed into a held hand, used by mothers to infants, by lovers, by the dying. A child learns air-speech first and is forgiven its noise. The rest of life is the slow unlearning. To raise your voice is to spend what belongs to everyone, and the String does not shame its spendthrifts with shouting; it looks at its hands. Shouting is for emergencies and for grief, and the two are given the same look. After a death, a household is granted one loud day — doors open, voices up, the unsayable said at full volume into the thin air that mercifully will not carry it — and then the hush returns, and is kept.

A life runs three centuries, four for the stubborn. The first decades are the fast years, indulged and watched with amusement: children run, which adults do not; children play at stillness, holding a hand-sign until someone laughs; children are taken once to the Rim to look at the Undersky and learn the world's one fast thing, and they come back quieter, and it lasts a decade or two. Then the learning, then the long middle — a century and a half of work and love and debts — then the slowing, which nobody calls decline. The old are said to be approaching repose, and the very old do almost nothing, and do it perfectly, and are treasured for it. They are the proof that it can be done.

They do not say *died*. The mountains are finished; people come to repose. And the people do not bury their dead in the ground, because the ground has other arrangements. The dead are carried out along the causeway at walking pace — everything at walking pace — on a low sled, and laid in a charted herd-path with the name spoken once into the warm stone. Then the family goes home. The beast may be years away. It does not matter. The path is charted; the beast will come; no one is buried quickly and no one is eaten quickly and everyone arrives. A millbeast spends millennia trading its tissues for stone and storing the rare metals of the range in its bones, and what it has chewed goes with it. So every fall is heavy with ancestors, and every founding melts them down into keel-beams and wire and door-handles. Every door-handle in Harrow has somebody in it. The people of the String do not find this morbid. They find it to be the entire point of the exercise. You are born a short word and you die a paragraph, and in time you are a street.

History is kept in falls the way other people keep it in dynasties: born in the long middle of the Fifth, wed early in the Sixth. A birth is recorded as a small founding, in the same ledger, with a lighter hand. Bells are for the true foundings and the exhaustions, and people travel for them — the young are taken to a founding if one occurs within reach, the old are helped to an exhaustion, to stand in a district whose carcass is finally empty and hear it rung quiet. Two bells in a life, if the world is generous. A life with no bells is a life spent in a district that was prospering, and nobody pities that.

Work is where the slowness becomes a craft. The fall-wards watch the ancients, each ward assigned a beast, each beast charted across a working life. The report is always the same: *it walked.* A ward files it ten thousand times and means it every time, because the day it stops being true is the day of the horn. A ward loves their beast a little. It is difficult not to love something you have watched for ninety years. And when the stagger comes and the horn sounds, the ward becomes briefly famous and permanently unemployed, and is given a seat by the bakestones and asked to tell about the fall for the rest of their life, which they do, slowly, as a courtesy.

In the quarries the ribs arch like scaffolding, and the press-keepers practice a discipline that is mostly refusal — the great press cycles once an hour, and the whole art of it is declining to make it faster. A casting cools at the rate the stone prefers; to hurry it is to ruin it; ruined castings have names, and the names are remembered. In Harrow the Great Lathe has been turning six hundred years, and its crews are generational — apprentices who know to the decade when they will die and to the century when the work will be done, and who sign their passes on the inside of the metal, where the name will hide for four hundred years. Everyone who has ever worked the Lathe is still working it.

The line-keepers ride the tram wires, brushing fines off insulators, reading the galvans that cluster there fist-sized and silver, drinking stray current. They insist the readings are half a joke, and they file them. A galvan rookery that shifts districts is news. A galvan on your table is *news*. The dust-pilots take the barges across the silt-lakes at walking pace, consulting the kettle-mouth charts, which are updated when there is something to update — which is not often, and is always taken seriously — and a barge arrives at the far shore before its own wake does, so that news from across a lake comes in two kinds, and the pilots trust the slow kind. And when the dry storms gather and the ridges flicker in silence, the storm-wardens dim their district deliberately and open the accumulators to drink, and the masts crown themselves in cold fire, and the children are kept up past everything to watch. The storm pays the year's power, and is thanked with a festival it cannot hear.

Aboard Patience, where the refinery-district rides its two-hundred-year grazing loop, live the calmest people on the Anvil, which is a competitive category. Their address is a matter of astronomy; their horizon changes on a schedule of decades; a child there grows up in a town that is somewhere else every few years and learns early that home is a thing you carry bolted to something older than your street. The engineers swear the machines run steadier for the step, and go quiet and pleased at each day's footfall, like people whose choir has reached the good chord.

They eat the felt, one way or another, as everything does: felt-curd, gray and sour and warm and beloved — the four food groups of the String — flatcake baked on the griddle-warm slabs the streets keep for public use, the quick seep-things crisped in tallow, ray-flesh on feast days. At dusk the horn sounds its one note, tuned to the resonance of the district's own skeleton, so that every district sings to itself in the key of its beast, and means to. Then the promenade: the young walk the dune-edges trailing cold glow on purpose, and everyone pretends it is exercise.

Between districts lies the dark, and the dark is kept as carefully as the quiet. The causeways are textured underfoot — ribbed for straight, dimpled for junctions, one long ridge that means *edge* — and a day's walk apart stand the way-stones, warm refuges stocked by custom: take tallow, leave tallow, sign the book. Hospitality on the Anvil is extended mostly to people you will never meet, which is the purest kind. A person is considered educated who has walked to three districts. And beyond the gulfs no one crosses, the sibling strings burn on their far mesas, and from certain high rims you can signal them — lamp-code at the speed of nights, letters at the speed of years. Some of the great love stories of the String have been carried on between people who have never shared air. Every district keeps someone whose job is the count of kindred, the nightly tally of far lights, and when one goes out the whole String knows by morning and is quieter than usual, which a visitor would not think possible. There are no visitors.

Their pleasures match their clocks. The choirs hold a single chord for an afternoon by relay, one voice spelling the next; the percussion is the press; a fashionable piece changes one note a season, and the connoisseurs argue about whether the change was earned. On mesa stages the sign-epics are performed over whole nights, and everyone knows the stories, and the pleasure is in the slowness of the telling. They lend at century terms; debts are inherited as matter-of-factly as claims; every contract and most letters close the same way: *plan accordingly*. Their feuds are conducted one move a decade — the grandchildren are briefed, the original insult is etched somewhere and no longer legible, which settles nothing. Their betrothals run ten or fifteen years, because you do not marry a person here, you marry a work, and the person comes with it. And everyone knows Pellion — the district that built across a herd-path, long ago, certain the beasts would go around. The beasts went through. Pellion is a verb now; it means to ruin something excellent through optimism, and the telling takes an evening and is done slowly, as a courtesy.

The other persons of the place are accommodated. House-pickers — the same meticulous many-legged creatures that tend the felt-gardens on the ancients' backs — live in every district as an unlicensed sanitation guild, grooming charge off cables and crumbs off floors, and people lean down and sign to them against their carapaces, palm to plate. Whether the pickers read it is a settled question, settled differently in every household. They are never eaten; neither are the galvans. The ancients are effectively persons in law: named, horoscoped, tracked like currents, and owned by no one — what dies on your land is yours, but nothing about a living millbeast is yours except the view. The fall-pools take wagers on which ancient goes down next; the odds shift over years; a well-placed bet can retire your grandchildren. As for the kettle-mouths waiting under the silt-lakes, older than most districts: the chart-keepers do not say the kettles are aware. They say the charts are scripture and leave it there, which is the same thing, said carefully.

And beneath all of it the people carry one understanding, held lightly, without doctrine, the way the world holds its heat: that the Anvil is listening, as slowly as it does everything else, and that they are how it listens. The mountains are done. The presses, the bells, the ledgers, the loud day and the kept hush, the name spoken into warm stone at the herd-path's edge — the people are the part of the world still being made, its one remaining work, carried out at walking pace, in the warm dark, by hand.

Plan accordingly.
