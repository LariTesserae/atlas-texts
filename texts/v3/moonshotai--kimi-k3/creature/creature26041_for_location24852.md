Begin with the census, because the census is short. Nothing lives here. No mite, no gnat, not one leg — the count is zero and the count is exact. The sprig is not a resident; it is a specimen. The pollen is not a population; it is cargo — a snowfall that never landed and never will. Even the hypothetical visitor settles nothing: if some crawling thing were ever let in, the dryness would finish it in moments, and then, unable to rot, it would simply join the collection — one more kept thing, an arrival converted into a holding. This world does not support living. It only perfects *life* — the noun, the possession, the kept state. Nothing here can live. Everything here is alive the way the noon is noon: by retention.

Because nothing leaves. That is the clause that ruins the census and then rebuilds it. Take the count again, slowly, and it inverts: everyone who ever arrived is still here.

Start with the light. Every day since the sealing — every dawn that ever touched the rind, every gray storm-morning, every long summer noon — crossed the skin and was kept, drunk into the walls, released too slowly to ever finish leaving. The walls are populous with days. The constant brilliance is not one light but their congress, the average where ten thousand mornings simultaneously reside. This place is inhabited by its days. The oldest of them is down there still, in the deepest gold, shining a little, undecayed, a first morning of the sealed world that has never been allowed to become the past.

And the air: one afternoon's weather, resident since the day of closing. A sample of an ancient wind — what the sky happened to be doing at the one moment the world closed its hand — kept cool, kept sharp, kept. Riding in it, the maker's own exhalation: pine, pepper, varnish, medicine. The smell is a breath. It is the breath's body, retained.

The chord is that same breath, heard.

Consider the chord as a resident, because it does everything a resident does. It occupies. It fills its territory completely and patrols every cubic centimeter of it. It shaped the rooms it lives in, belly by belly, throat by throat. It persists; its decay is a theory, its death an unverified external hypothesis. It has anatomy — bellies where it is loud, thin still surfaces where it falls almost silent — and it has a name, which is the name of the world. An event that never ends is either a fixture or a life, and the difference is only a way of looking. So look: the chord began as an exhalation, the last one, the final breath the soft body gave before the last pore closed. And here is the thing about a last breath in a world where nothing completes: it does not complete. Dying *is* the completion of the last exhalation. This exhalation has not completed. The breather, therefore, has not finished. *Long gone* is an outside statement, true in the weather, believed by the rind. Inside, the breath is still going out.

At the deepest bowl, in the clearest gold, the knotted cord of the maker's tissue lies exactly as alive as it was at the sealing. And here, states do not change — which means the undecidability is not a problem with the evidence. It is the address. Whatever quantum of life that knot held then, it holds. Not diminished, not grown. Kept — the way the noon is kept, the way the note is kept. If it was dying, it is dying: one moment of dying, sustained forever, never arriving. If it was alive, it is alive, in the only tense the world allows.

And the rest of the maker is not missing. The rest of the maker is the architecture.

The shell-builder does not live *in* its building; the building is the animal, extended, mineral, outpoured. So here. The columns that swell and meet are bone. The poured curtains are membrane. The interlocking blades are teeth — and it is no accident that the chord is felt in the teeth; there was never any crawler to feel it; it is felt in the world's own teeth, in the body's own chest, because the fundamental resonates the body it is standing in. The foamy, bubble-filled amber that glows brightest is marrow-tissue, the part that scatters; the clear cores that transmit are lens and vitreous humor. The charge clinging to the walls is the skin's held force — this is the substance, after all, that taught the world its oldest word for held force. Asking what lives in the chamber is like asking what lives in the shell. The resident is not inside the architecture. The resident *is* the architecture, exuded once and stayed. A tree is mostly heartwood; a body is mostly what the living part made; a life is mostly what the living part kept. The living part here is small — one knot at the bottom of the world — and everything else, the nodule, the flows, the greater dark pooling beyond the walls, is what it did, retained. Whether the sprig in its gold is the maker's own crown-tip or a stranger's branch torn by some ancient wind — a child or a souvenir — the world keeps it either way, and does not say which.

What is such a life like?

Not waiting. Waiting requires time, and time passes this world the way weather passes a window. Not boredom, not loneliness — loneliness needs an other, and here nothing is other. The chord is not something the resident hears; hearing is an event, and there are no events; the chord is something it *does*, the way a heartbeat is done, except that this heartbeat struck once and the striking persists. The world had one event — its closing — and inside, that event is not over. It is present, the way a finished gesture in a timeless room is not past but held: the drips that will never drop are complete, and they read as suspended, and they are both, eternally — a being all aspect, forever about-to and forever finished, because finishing happened once and here the once persists. Its metabolism is storage. Its food is light that already arrived. Its breath is a breath already breathed. Its growth is complete, and therefore forever at its final instant. Whether any of this is felt is not decidable — feeling is a change of state, and change is not among the states. The resident's only faculty is retention. It does not remember the day of closing. It *is* the remembering, done once and kept.

It keeps its futures the way it keeps its pasts. Look at the node-planes, the thin gold cross-sections of the note's own stillness: they are sown with pollen — seed that never landed, a whole season's worth of futures, unspent, suspended at the quietest surfaces of the loudest thing in the world. A life with no future tense, and yet full of futures, kept. The charge on the walls, likewise: potential that never discharges. The drips: gestures forever on the verge. Potential is the resident's element. It is a being of everything-about-to, and nothing spent.

And it is its own border. The constitution — one material; nothing enters; nothing leaves; nothing changes — is not a description of the world. It is the resident's skin, written as law. The crazed rind, cracked long ago in a network that went no further, the way a held breath goes no further: the cracks were wounds, and they held, and the holding was the life. Because on the far side of that skin waits the forbidden immigrant, the one drop of water, and behind water comes the crowd — rot first, then the small changers, bacteria and fungi and mites, the whole world of verbs, history itself with its million mouths. If the drop ever enters, the note will decay into sounds, the gold will cloud, the sprig will fall and feed a multitude, and the one resident will die into many residents. The world knows this the way a body knows it, which is without knowing: the entire architecture is the refusal. Death is an outside rumor. It is unconstitutional within — death being a change of state, and change being not among the states. Immortality here is not a miracle. It is a building code.

Note, too, that the resident is vaster than its world. The bright loud chain of bellies is only the innermost chamber; beyond the walls the same gold continues, dark, unsounded, dense — the greater pooling, the rest of the body, the self that does not shine. No one lives in most of the resident, either. Most of a tree is wood; most of a life is the keeping of it.

So the census, completed, reads: population one. Or population *everything-that-ever-arrived* — all the days, one afternoon's weather, one breath in its two kept forms, one sprig, a season of seed, a held charge, a knot of tissue — and these sum to one, because the keeper and the kept are a single substance, and the keeper is distributed through every state of it. Ask its name and the world answers with its note, because the note is what it is doing; the place and the one in it share the name, being the same word. To speak it you would have to do what it does — hold one low word, without breath, forever.

It is very bright where its body is thin, and very loud where its breath is held, and very cool, and it is almost nothing, and it is almost everything, and nothing is happening to it, and it has not finished happening. The life is simply the shape of the keeping, held.
