**The Tenants**

Count what lives here and you must learn to count the way the country teaches: slowly, and in small numbers. A week's walk of towers holds fewer mouths than one lowland village, and the emptiness is not an absence but a discipline. Life here is rationed like water — caught like water, stored like water, spent with a water-counter's care. The tenants divide into three estates: those who hold the surfaces, those few who own the air, and those who keep the floors, about whom the surface people speak the way you speak of neighbors you have never quite met.

Begin with the people, because you will need them.

**The country takes the ear first.** A ledge-child feels pitch through the soles of its feet before it ever learns to listen for it, and by middle age most people here have given the high soprano of the blades back to the sky; by old age the white chord is no longer something they hear but something that happens to their bodies, a weather of the sternum and the teeth. This is not treated as loss. The deaf old are the *Quiet-ones*, and their counsel is sought precisely because they are the only citizens who can think all the way through noon. Watch an old woman stand at a rim in the storm-hour with her palms flat on the stone, reading the chord the way you would read a letter. She is not deprived. She is corresponding.

Then there are the *chord-born* — children delivered in the white chord itself, who do not startle at the coughing, and a fair number of whom never hear at all. The midwives note it without alarm. The chord-born are considered the land's own children, lent to families rather than given to them, and when one shows the gift — pitch read through bone, a fingertip on a flute vent naming its note the way you'd name a color — the child goes to a hermit-tuner as an apprentice, and the family is honored, and there is a feast if the water allows.

No one here has a spoken name. A name is a rhythm — three beats and a rest shaped like the person — drummed on a sternum in greeting, tapped onto a table-stone at distance, knocked through the living rock itself from tower to tower, since stone carries a knock for miles and air carries nothing reliably at all. At coming-of-age a name gains its second half. The child is given a hardwood wedge and a mute vent and must wake it — the *first note* — and whatever pitch the stone accepts becomes the note of their name forever after. Beat and note: a full name is a tiny chord. A traveler introduces themselves to a new tower by drumming the beat and then standing still while the tower, so to speak, answers with the note they carry in it.

Speech is what the original accounts say it is — sign, touch, drummed code — but what they don't say is how it changes people. A hand on the shoulder-blade is a sentence. Conversations happen skin to skin, and so the people of the ledges are physically fluent in one another in a way that lowlanders, shouting across rooms, never are. Lies are rare. You cannot lie easily through your fingertips.

Their work follows the country's logic. The hermit-tuners you know. Their wood comes from the wedge-wrights — if you hear that distant axe in a lull, that is one, at a storm-killed fog-tree limb, cutting heartwood dense enough to hold a pitch for a decade. *Stringers* walk the chasm-routes tending the gut-strings, and a stringer listens for absences: a crossing whose signal-hum has gone silent is a crossing whose line has parted, and the silence itself is the alarm. *Veil-keepers* tend the hanging gardens, heavy with drip, and harvest veil-fig and leaf and the silk of the veil-weaver spiders. *Cistern-wardens* mind the crown-pools and let water down on rope-lets to their neighbors; their towers are the neighborhood taps. Children work as *lull-runners* — messengers with cricket-cages at their belts, crossing the rope bridges only when the crickets say a lull is coming, a runner's pride being to never once be caught mid-span when the chord swells. And there are the well-listeners, about whom more below.

They assemble once a year, all of them, at the Bowl, under the Long Note — two or three thousand people, which is everyone, which is a crowd, which is the year's strangest sensation: more voices in one place than the country ever holds, and people speaking aloud because they can, and finding the human din more disorienting than any white chord. This is the market — wedges, veil-silk, ray-skin, salt scraped from the mineral blooms on the quiet lee faces, dried hour-shrimp, egg-pouches, a bell changing hands for the first time in four generations — and the marriages, and the news. Marriages are matched by note: the two towers' pitches must sit well together, and matchmakers read tower-chords the way other peoples read stars. A dissonant pairing is called a *beating marriage*, for the slow pulse of interference it makes, and some families avoid such matches and some deliberately seek them, and the hermits argue about which is wisdom.

The choir nights at the Bowl are where you will hear *singing the tower up* — and here is the law that governs it, the one every child knows and no one explains: **never pitch on the land's note.** Harmony goes beside it, a little sharp, a little flat, never on. A child whose voice drifts onto the note is hushed instantly, and the old ones touch stone. Ask why and you get three answers. Politeness: the part is taken. Craft: doubling the note adds nothing. And the third answer, which people give only in sign, with a glance at the nearest chasm: *safety*. What that might mean, no one says. But no one sings on the note.

Death is given to the Quiet. Funerals happen in the pre-dawn hour, the only true silence, and the dead person's rhythm is drummed once and their note struck once and their name — here is the thing that undoes visitors — *spoken aloud, plainly, in air*, the whole of it, for perhaps the only time in their existence. Hermits have lived sixty years and heard exactly four words said aloud, all of them names, all of them at dawn. The body goes to the vulture ledges — *given to the quiet guest* — or into the chasm, given to the wind. And when a family line ends, its copper bell is not inherited but lowered, on veil-silk line, into an echo-well. Storm-winds ring those bells. There are wells that ring with three bells, five, nine — whole genealogies sounding in a gale — and locals can name them. *That's the Kessit bell,* they will tell you, matter-of-factly, as a storm approaches from the east and a voice that has had no one to speak for it in two hundred years clears its throat in the dark.

Now the animals, for there are few and they repay watching.

The bell-crickets do not wait for lulls. They *predict* them — some pressure-sense in their horned forelegs reads the dropout before it arrives, and their synchronized ringing starts a half-beat ahead of the silence. This makes them the most useful creature in the country: a cage of crickets by the door is a lull-alarm, and runners, mourners, choirs, and bridge-keepers all take their cues from a handful of insects the size of a thumbnail. In the white chord they grip the stone and wait, mute as everything else. In the lulls they own the country and know it.

The windray lives its whole life on the vertical rivers — slate-dark, wide as two outstretched arms, climbing without a wingbeat, sleeping in slow gyres inside the upwash of the great chimneys. The pups cling beneath the mother's membrane and are simply let go, one day, into rising air, and either the river takes them or it doesn't. Once a year a ray sheds its wing-skin entire, and the grey membranes come draped over the flutes like surrendered flags, and a whole shed skin is a prize: ray-skin makes the finest drumheads, the ones that answer a fingertip. Because almost nothing else moves in all that emptiness, a ray is a public event. Old people bet on a ray's next turn — it is the country's only gambling — and the tuners watch them the way sailors watch birds, because a ray draws the shape of a wind you cannot see, and where rays refuse to go, a storm is pooling.

The vulture is called the quiet guest, and it has taken the country's law further than anything else alive: it makes no sound at all, ever, mantling over a carcass in perfect silence, hissing at rivals in frequencies below hearing, a pressure you feel on your eardrums like a change of weather. It attends the dying from a day's glide away, unhurried, and its arrival at a funeral ledge is considered the correct ending, the period at the end of the sentence. A vulture that comes early is never cursed. It is only ever right.

In the moss-fur lives the moss-shrew, a fist of a mammal, stone-deaf from birth, that experiences the white chord as nothing more than a pleasant vibration in the paws. It eats bell-crickets — the deaf thing preys on the loud thing, and the hermits note this and decline to draw conclusions — and it is the only creature in the country the chord cannot touch, and more than one tuner, at noon on a bad day, has watched one amble across the singing stone and felt something close to envy.

The veil-weavers are fist-sized pale spiders whose webs are not traps but combs — drip-nets strung to strip fog, the same trick the fog-trees play, and the people will tell you plainly that they learned netting from the spiders and not the other way around. Their silk, wound with resin, makes the cord that everything here hangs from: earplug strings, bridge lashings, bell-lines into the wells.

In the slots of the thin blades nests the bowtail, the country's only bird, voiceless like everything else, which courts by drawing the stiffened quills of its own tail across one another — a bird that plays itself like a fiddle. Each bowtail tunes its scraping to its home blade's shriek, and here is the thing the hermits point at: it plays a half-step off. Always. The birds keep the law.

The hour-pools have their hour-shrimp — eggs waiting in dry dust for the ten-minute rain, hatching as the pool fills, living their whole silver day, mating, laying, gone by dusk. The people sow them. A child with an egg-pouch scatters dust into a new-hollowed basin — *sowing the rain*, it's called — and a family's pouches are its bank, its granary, its insurance against a dry chord-season, and stealing eggs is the one crime with a punishment everyone agrees on. At dusk after a storm the whole country is out with fine nets at the vanishing pools, harvesting a fishery that exists for a few hours and then is dust again.

At the chasm mouths, when storm-wind slides the dune-faces and the sand booms its deep bass, the dune-skate swims up out of the grain — pale, flat, wide as a shield, surfing the fluidized sand to snatch whatever the storm has blown in. And then the deep-kin come out with bone hooks on long lines and go *fishing the boom*: standing on singing sand that flows like water underfoot, angling for a creature they can feel but not see, in a wind that could strip a rope bridge, and they consider this a perfectly reasonable way to spend an afternoon.

Which brings you to the third estate. The floors.

Down where no eye reaches from the rim, the fog pools and the light arrives grey and tired and the wells are thickest, and there — in the drip and the dim — live the descended, the deep-kin, a few hundred people who measure time by fog-tide rather than sun and come up only for the Long Note, blinking, skin the color of the moss's dry grey. They tend the great roots. For here is a thing about the fog-trees: their roots go down through the cracked rock for hundreds of meters, all the way, ending in white tips in the black pools of the floors — so that a squat storm-sculpted fist of a tree on a crown and a pale root-forest in the dark below are one organism, one being that has never once seen itself whole, and the hermits argue about whether the tree holds the tower together or the tower holds the tree. The deep-kin farm the perpetual drip, keep the deep hour-pools that never quite vanish, and listen to the wells. They are the well-listeners' well-listeners. **The floors keep what falls** — every dropped tool, every funeral gift, every body given to the wind — and the deep-kin are the keepers of the kept.

And they are the ones who carry up the *unclaimed words*.

Because the wells return what they are given, changed by their years in the stone — everyone knows this — but sometimes a well returns something no one sent. A word in no known tongue, surfacing after east storms, once a year, once a decade. There is a well the deep-kin call Ashfall that returned one word for thirty years, to the same keeper, always after the same wind. And then one year it returned a second word after the first. The well was speaking a sentence, one word at a time, at the pace of weather, and the woman who had kept it her whole life understood at once that she would not live to hear it end. She taught her apprentice the two words and the wind they came on, and the apprentice's apprentice listens now. Nobody suggests the sentence is anything other than what it appears to be. Nobody suggests what to do when it finishes.

The woken towers, when an ancient plug fails, are usually joy — people walk for days to hear a note no living ear has heard. But there are the *sour wakenings*. A tower east of the Bowl woke in my grandmother's time with a note that greyed the moss around it and emptied the crickets for miles, and everyone who camped near it dreamed the same dream: streets. A city, lit and populated, going about its business — the fossil city of the rim view, but alive. A wedge-team re-plugged it, and the dreams stopped, and their drummer came down off that tower without her rhythm-name — she simply could not remember her own beats — and she never drummed again. The hermits argue about whether the tower should have been left open. They argue carefully, and not within earshot of it.

And there is the thing that is not a thing, which you should know before your first noon: in the white chord, when the deafening is total and you can shout into a friend's ear and watch them not hear it, many people report that they do not feel alone in their own skulls. That the one place the land's voice fills entirely is the one place you feel *heard*. I will leave that where the hermits leave it, which is everywhere and nowhere.

The old ones — the plug-makers, the baffle-builders, the people of the stair that stops at nothing — are gone, but they are tenants still, in the way the dead here are always tenants. Every muted vent is a standing order from them, still being obeyed. Every petrified wedge is a decision they made about what the country should say, still in force. And if the wells keep what is shouted into them, then somewhere in the deep circulation of the stone, their speech is still coming around on the wind, word by word, and the unclaimed words may be theirs, and the sentence at Ashfall may be the end of an argument they were having when they stopped being a people and became a presence. They tried to quiet the country, or to perfect its voice, and either way they made the same mistake a singer would tell you about: they wanted the note itself. Not beside it. *On* it.

So count the tenants. The moss and its shrew, the trees and their roots, the spiders, the crickets loud and pale, the shrimp in their dust, the skate in the singing sand, the ray, the quiet guest, the tuners and stringers and runners and keepers, the deep-kin in the drip, the Quiet-ones reading stone with their palms, the chord-born pitching by bone, the bells in the wells, the dead with their standing orders, the sentence still coming home.

You will keep arriving one name short.

Whether to count the country itself — whether the chord is weather or utterance, whether the land is the house or the eldest tenant, the one all the others are adapted to, the one the moss greens for and the birds harmonize beside and the choirs will not, will *not*, double at the octave — that is the hermits' oldest argument, and they will be having it long after you leave. Ask them about it in a lull, with a hand on your sleeve so the words survive the wind, and then stand still and feel, through the soles of your feet, the note of the place you are standing in.

Nothing here wastes a voice. Even the mountains, gone these ages, are still using theirs.
