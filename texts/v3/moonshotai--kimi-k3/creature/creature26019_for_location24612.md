The mountain is under construction, and the crew pours it themselves, course by course, along the apron's edge.

This is the thing nobody tells you before you arrive: cloud is not clean. Every droplet that forms up here forms around a grain — ash, salt, pollen, the ground-down ghost of somewhere else — and the condensers keep the water and surrender the grain. It comes off the fins as a fine grey meal, a few kilos a day, swept into hoppers and carried to the casting yard, and it is the only ore the mountain has. The pour crew mixes it with lime and milled scrap and lays it down in lifts a hand's width thick, and that is how the works grows: a hand's width a season, weather permitting, which it always is, because the weather is the raw material. Ask a mason what the mountain is made of and she will tell you: former weather. Every terrace was a cloud once. Every stair was rain's excuse.

It follows that a watershed needs a mountain, and the builders — whoever they were, and the crew argues about it on long shifts — built the catchment first and are pouring the justification underneath. Nobody expects to finish. The point is the growing.

Forty-one people live here this tide, plus the Alderman, plus whatever the birds are up to.

Most of the forty-one are on rotation — a tide is a season's contract, up the cage in spring, down again when the storm harvest is in — but a dozen or so are permanent, and the permanents are the ones who give the place its grammar. There is the Keeper, who has been here thirty years and remembers plywood forms where basins stand now. There is the hall crew, who keep the pumps. There are the masons, the fin-tenders, the green shift who manage the algae, and there is the Bellman, and there is the Tuner, and there is the gauge-walker, whose job you will not understand until you have followed her round.

The Bellman lives at the lift landing, because the bell is more than a doorbell. One stroke means the cage. Two means the fog tide has taken the rail — the works is going inside its weather, look up from what you're doing. Three means storm, and three is a fine sound, payday is coming. And then there is the slow tolling, which you will likely never hear, because it means the Cold Main has stopped sweating, and the Main does not stop. The works' one true alarm is a silence. When the six o'clock rain fails to fall, the whole mountain comes running.

The Bellman also keeps the logbook of landfalls. This is an island, whatever else it is, and migrants find it the way ships find islands — out of the cloud, exhausted, astonished. He logs them all: the swifts who nest behind the condenser fins and refuse to leave; the wagtails working the weep channels; a heron, once, who stood in the Long Basin for a week regarding the Alderman with an expression the logbook describes as *unprofessional*. Two ravens are resident and have learned to imitate the arrival stroke. Every few weeks the Bellman walks out to greet a cage that isn't there, and the ravens make the sound ravens make, which is laughter, and the logbook records a false landfall. The ravens are, strictly speaking, part of the crew. They have been known to sit in on the morning muster.

The Tuner tensions the fog harps, and she will tell you, without smiling, that she is not a musician but a mapmaker. Here is why. By early afternoon the tide comes up and the works goes white, and in the white you cannot see twenty meters, and you do not need to. The harps hum when the wind is right, and they hum in their places — the high bright frames along the upper terraces, the low slow strings down by the Long Basin — and the crew walks the fog by pitch the way you would walk a town by landmarks. C-sharp is the upper rows. The low strings are home. When a harp goes flat, somebody gets lost, and so the Tuner's rounds are not maintenance, they are cartography, and a retuning is a redrawing of the world.

The gauge-walker's round takes two hours, morning and evening. Every weir has a number and a gauge glass and a pencil mark, and it is the pencil that matters. The gauges measure the water. The pencil measures everything else. Alongside the level marks her round-book accumulates the works' true record: first green in Basin Four; the children dammed the stair runnel again, allow it; Alderman off his feed, tell the hall; ravens have a new word; the lawn wants walking. Nothing in this place is ever dry enough for ink. Graphite endures, and so the works is written in pencil, top to bottom, a diary you can walk through.

About the water itself. It leaves the condensers distilled-clean, and distilled water is thirsty water, dead water — it wants the world back. The crew does not drink it straight; nobody could, not for long. It goes into the mellowing basins, and the algae finds it, as the algae finds everything, within a day — nobody has ever caught it arriving; it is simply there, the way a thought is simply there — and the green does what stone and air do elsewhere: it gives the water a body. Day-two water is what comes out of the galley taps. A condensary that drank its own product raw would die of purity, a fact the crew offers to visitors the way other people offer chairs.

The green shift tends this. Half agriculture, half diplomacy. Good green is coaxed, fed, harvested off the mellowing basins and pressed into cakes that go down the cage as the works' second export; wild green is scrubbed out of the circulation with brushes on long handles and a stream of cheerful invective. The distinction between the two is entirely a matter of address. The algae does not care. The algae is the first citizen here — older than the crew, more patient than the masons, present in every seam of every boot that ever came up the lift — and the green shift speaks of it the way shepherds speak of weather.

And in the Long Basin lives the Alderman.

He is a carp, black as a pencil mark, and he came up the cage in a jar when the works was young, and he is now the length of your forearm and then some, and he is a gauge. The gauge glasses lie, or at least lag; the Alderman does not. When he sulks at the bottom, the hall crew checks the pumps. When he rises at dusk to the midges that hatch off the weep channels, the evening round is written up as fair, and it always is, because the midges have never once failed to rise. The crew feeds him by hand and taps the rail instead of the glass — tapping the glass is the one thing that will get a visitor actually shouted at — and new arrivals are walked down to meet him before they are shown their bunks, on the grounds that he was here first and will be here after.

Under the Cold Main runs the wettest strip of ground on the mountain: the gutter-glaze where the six o'clock rain lands all day, every day, without pause. Nothing should grow there — it is cast stone and condensation, no soil, no seed, no reason — and so, of course, it is the lushest place on the works, a ribbon of moss and weed four inches wide and two hundred meters long. The crew keeps it by deliberate neglect. They call it the lawn. It is the only lawn. It is mowed by being walked on, and crew who cannot sleep go out at three in the morning and stand on it barefoot, because it is the nearest thing the mountain has to a field, and the standing is recorded in the gauge-walker's book, in pencil, as *lawn maintenance*.

The six o'clock rain itself is the works' civic hour. The shift changes, the day's marks are in, and the crew walks the length of the Main bareheaded under a clear sky, laughing, in water that fell from no cloud currently in attendance. Visitors are always invited and always hesitate, on some deep dryland instinct about rain and clothing, and the crew is gentle about this, because they remember. After a tide or two, nobody hesitates. After a tide or two, nobody can quite remember what the hesitation was for.

Three of the crew were born here, to hall-crew parents, and have never stood on anything they did not help pour. You should talk to them. They play in the runnels with an expertise that unsettles the engineers — they dam, they divert, they run regattas of bark boats through the lattice, and the weirs' pencil marks record their works indulgently, the way the works records everything. Their cosmology is exact and strange. The cloud floor is, to them, the surface of the world; the cage goes *down through the floor* to some cellar where the drylanders keep the sun's second string. Told that there is ground under the weather, real ground, native rock, one of them thought about it and asked: does it have weirs? It does not, and she pitied it, sincerely, the way you might pity a house with no plumbing. Water that is only ever going *down* — she found the idea melancholy. Here the water passes you twice, once at your ankles, once overhead, going the other way. Here it gets somewhere.

Storm season is when the place shows you its joy.

For most of the year the weather sits politely below the rail and the works sips at the fog tide's edge. But in season the sky comes up to visit, all of it at once, and the works stops being a machine for drinking cloud and becomes a machine for surviving it — and these are the best days, the harvest days, the days the crew signs on for. The bell rings three and stays rung. The gauges go off their pins. The harps scream in their mounts until the Tuner grounds them, and the lightning takes the strike trees one after another like a tax collector, and the crew is out in it, in oilskins, in the roar, opening every weir to its stop, collecting, collecting, collecting, while the Long Basin boils white and the flumes run solid and the whole circulation goes to a gallop. Down below, the drylanders look up at the mountain's permanent little cloud gone black and vast, and they call it bad weather. Up here it is called the crop.

At night the works is the only lit thing above the weather, and the pumps are the sound of it — a slow double throb you feel through the grates before you hear it, and then stop hearing, and then miss when it's gone. The main pumps have names and tempers; the hall crew talks to them, and would like it noted that the pumps talk back, in bearing-noise and vibration, and that a good engineer reads a pump the way the gauge-walker reads a weir. Below the rail, some nights, the cloud sea lights itself from inside — sheet lightning crawling through the tide, silent from up here, a slow grey noon rolling around under the world. The crew watches it from the perimeter rail the way other people watch fire. They call it cloudfire, and the logbook records good displays, in pencil, between the water levels and the ravens' vocabulary.

The works keeps fog time, by the way, not sun time. The clock that matters is the rail. When the tide takes it, that is the start of the real day, whatever your watch says, and the watches of new arrivals are politely ignored until they stop being consulted.

And then there is what is under the pump hall.

Under the hall, under the foundation mat, under thirty years of castings, is the true summit — the peak the builders started from, the only native rock on the mountain, encased like a relic, which it is. There is a bronze ring set in the hall floor, and the hall crew knows what it is, and once a year, on Peak Day, they lift it, and the shift goes down a bolted ladder into the dark and stands on the old top and relights a lamp that burns there, and what is said down there is not recorded, not even in pencil. It is the works' one holiday. The six o'clock rain is allowed to fall on empty walkways, the gauges go unread for a day, the harps are let to drift, and the Alderman gets a double feeding and the deep, slow attention of everyone at once. Whatever the works is, it knows it is standing on something it buried. The mountain remembers its seed.

The works has no cemetery. It has a growing edge. When a member of the crew dies — and people do die here, of falls, of time — the masons mix the ash into the next pour, and the apron widens by that much, and nobody ever really leaves the roster. The Keeper can walk the rail and point out the courses: this lift of stone is a fin-tender she trained; that stair riser is a mason who fell off the thing he was making, which the crew agrees is the correct way for a mason to go; the bench at the Long Basin's north end is the previous Keeper, and sitting on him is allowed, encouraged, exactly what he is for. She has watched forty seasons of hand's-widths go down, and she has told the masons where her own course is to run — along the west rail, where the cloudfire shows best — and they have the formwork measured, because a good pour respects its contents.

So. Who lives here.

Forty-one people this tide. A carp. Two liars of ravens, a chapel of swifts, a heron with a grievance, gone now, probably. A lawn four inches wide. An insurgency of green that arrived before anyone and will outlast the pumps. A storm that comes to harvest and a lamp that burns in the dark under the floor. Dead masons holding up the rail, and children who pity the world's plumbing, and a Tuner redrawing the map an octave at a time, and a pencil that never stops.

But ask the crew themselves and they will not list themselves first. First comes the water — the circuit, the stuff that passes you twice and will pass you again tomorrow, changed by nothing but a day of light. Then the mountain, learning to be one at a hand's width a season. The crew, they will tell you, is only something the works is growing — like the lawn, like the green, like the ground. The part of the catchment that walks around reading gauges. The part that laughs in the rain at six o'clock under a clear sky.

You were told the ground is the thing you will tell people about. Now you know why.

It is the only thing here that is still being decided.
