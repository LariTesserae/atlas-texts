The people are counted the same way everything else in the basin is: in stairs. They have no word for themselves as a people. There is the town, and there is up, and there is down, and a folk with one neighbor to the east, one to the west, and a roof of mist over all does not need a name. A child is born four up, or six, and that is the first stair of her name. Each time the water takes her floor and she carries the coals up, another course is added to how she is spoken of. An old woman's name is a staircase. When two elders meet they recite their courses to each other like introductions, like genealogies, like addresses — which is what they are, or were, the addresses now drowned and the names still walking around above them.

The water gets into them the way it gets into everything. Their chests are deep and their hearts are slow, and even so, diving is work: the body floats too well, rides too high, and so children are taught the emptying — the full exhale, the last silver thread of breath let go facedown in the shallows, until the body begins, grudgingly, to sink. Until a child can empty, she may not touch a guide-rope. The day she first hangs from a rope with hollow lungs and looks down into the green at the courses below is the day she is given her own glow-jar, and she sleeps with it that night like a pet.

The rind does not spare them; it only spares what moves. People move more than houses, so the water finishes them slowly. Divers scrub with ash and reed-fiber, but the chalk never entirely leaves, and old divers carry stone in their knuckles and the hinges of their hands. Ask a grandmother whether it hurts and she will close her creaking fingers and say no — it holds. Their teeth come in flecked white. Their hair dries stiff and pale after swimming and is combed out with pod-wood. They speak low, always, because the water carries and the mist returns, and a voice raised at breakfast is heard at supper by strangers. Lovers can speak across a lane as if across a pillow. Arguments are conducted at the pace of the rind, over days, and it is not unusual to pick up a quarrel with a neighbor exactly where it was set down three years ago, both parties having grown, like walls, in the meantime.

Hurry is the nearest thing they have to an obscenity. The basin notices anything that moves quickly, and so do they.

The builders call themselves gardeners, and mean it. A gardener knows a wall's thirst the way a shepherd elsewhere knows weather — how fast a given lattice will take stone from the spring-runnels, when to rub the face of it, when to leave it alone. Their adzes are grown, not made, and inherited, and a gardener whose blade snaps will turn the broken edge to the light and show you the ghost of the wood grain inside the stone, and grieve less than you'd expect, because the tool has only finished early. Gardeners die white to the elbow and are buried so, and the family is proud. A person is measured by the courses they helped raise, and a gardener's measure is the town itself.

The readers go down. Since all archives are underwater, scholarship is diving: the lower courses are the library, and nothing can be written there, so the readers memorize. They keep the recitation — the lanes of the drowned town, house by house, whose loom, whose shelf of pots, which sill holds the wax-white doll — and they teach it to children who will never see any of it, because history here is an address list and someone must remember where everyone lived. Depth is counted in bells, the air-bells still shining under the old vaults, and the count keeps changing, because the bells shrink; when the last bell in a room is gone, the room is finished, and the readers strike it from the recitation with a small ceremony that is, in every way that matters, a funeral. Their units of measure are drowning along with everything else.

There are two recitations. The one they teach the children, and the one they keep among themselves. The second one is shorter, and it is not spoken where the water can carry it.

The listeners sit with copper rods and their ears against certain drowned lintels known to carry sound well, and they keep the tally. The knocks that come up from below the deepest known course — three slow blows, or five; twice in all the counted years, seven — are logged by course and season in a record that goes back further than the rope has ever reached. The listeners are chosen for patience and trained in it: an apprentice spends her first year in silence, striking nothing, learning to hear by first learning not to speak. And sometimes — this is the part the tally does not settle — the knocks answer. A question goes down in code and an answer comes back correct and fifty years out of date, in the code their grandparents used, as if a letter had been replied to by the house you grew up in. Once, in the oldest listener's telling, the reply came in a code no one living could read, until a reader matched its rhythm to the chisel-pattern of the deepest masonry anyone has reached, the style no living person recognizes. No one knocks down on the days the knocks come up. That is the rule, and no one remembers making it.

The surveyors are the only people whose work goes up. Rope-and-plumb people, climbing to the cairns on the rim, keeping their rival counts of rim against water, and the town regards them the way you regard a beloved relative who talks of leaving. They come down smelling of wind. Some of them have stood near the Gap when the cap thinned and felt the thread of genuinely cold air slide past, and a few of those are never right again — cold-mad, the town says, gently, because cold is the one thing nobody here is built for, and to love it is a derangement. The heresy of the Gap lives mostly in them. They build the next stair anyway. Everyone builds the next stair.

The face-keepers dive to the eleventh, to the dead. A new dead is set seated in the niche with a lit jar of glow-moss beside them, and the family renews the moss for as long as the face is still a face — ten years, thirty, fifty, a grandmother blunted and patient and still herself. You can tell how a death is progressing by the light. When the features have softened past recognition and the visits thin to festival days, the family lets the jar lapse, and the dark receives that niche, and this is not grief ending. It is simply that the dead no longer need to be found. When a family line ends, their dead pass to everyone; it is ordinary courtesy, passing any niche, to wipe the algae from a cheek with one thumb, so no face is wholly neglected between tendings. The town calls them the common dead, and the builders say — half belief, half engineering — that a course with many finished dead in it never settles, that the lower terraces stand because the dead are diligent. Load-bearing ancestors. No one in the basin fears collapse.

The fish are blind and white and hand-long and slow, and they drift the lanes of the drowned town like its last inhabitants, and some say exactly that, and mean it precisely: that when the water receives a house whole, the house gives up its people slowly, and what stays, stays as fish. People claim to recognize things. The set of a fin like a grandmother's way of turning in a doorway. Nothing here eats the fish; there is no law about it because none has ever been needed. They are kept in sunken cisterns and read for omens — a sick fish means a taking soon, a room or a person — and they gather thickest on the eleventh, among the niches, where they seem to read the faces with their blank skins. When a face is finally smoothed past all suggestion, the fish leave that niche. They are the ones who announce finished. And below, in the courses where the light has gone entirely, the readers sometimes find, at the very edge of the glow-jar's reach, a slow white shape keeping pace with them, person-sized, unhurried. This is in the second recitation.

The bird lives somewhere in the cloud cap and is almost never seen, but its call comes down out of the white a few times a season and the whole town stops — adzes, mallets, conversations — because it is the only wild voice the basin has. No nest has ever been found. No one can say whether there is one bird or many or whether it has always been the same one. What is found, rarely, are its feathers on the open water: pure white, and wrong in a way no one can explain, because they will not take the rind and they will not sink. Push one under and it works back up like held breath. It is the only thing in the basin the water cannot keep, and a diver who carries one is carrying a promise that she will come back up. A surveyor at the rim once saw the bird itself cross the Gap, white on white, and people who see it talk less afterward, and look up more.

There is one more inhabitant, and it has no name, because a name would give it an address in the recitation. On still nights a wake crosses the open water with no boat at its head. Pad-meadows are found folded, like closed books. A poler came home once at first light with his pole undipped and his face wet and did not say why, and never poled again, and the town did not ask, because the town has no gods — only neighbors — and this one keeps to the deep water and minds the fast things, and is why paddling at night writes your green fire on the surface for anyone, or anything, to see.

And below the deepest known course, whatever knocks. The elders say the mountain is growing, which is true in general and explains nothing in particular. The orthodoxy says the springs have throats and the throats shift. The readers, who do not speculate where the water can carry, say something else among themselves: that you do not dive past a certain course for the same reason you do not walk uninvited into a house whose occupants may be home. The ropes have never been long enough, and the lungs have never been deep enough, but those are not the reasons. The reasons are that below the last air-bell the glow-moss in the jar goes out — the light refuses first — and then, a little deeper, the diver's certainty refuses, and what turns a diver around at the bottom of the longest rope is not fear, exactly. It is courtesy.

The heresy holds that one day the knock will come up in the current code, and that someone should be ready to answer in it. The listeners have argued for a generation about what the answer should say. What they have settled on, if it ever comes, is the count of the courses, and that we are well.

The other settlements keep their own kinds. The Rust Stair runs orange; its people are stained rust at the teeth and fingertips, their dead become red columns, and you can tell their knocking from the town's knocking the way you tell an accent — a slightly harder strike, copper on ironstone. The Far Steps are small and grow the best moss and produced, everyone agrees, the finest listeners; it was the Far Steps that heard the seven-blow knock first, and knocked the news around the whole dark bowl in an afternoon. Brides carry their hearth-coals across the water in sealed clay pots, fire traveling over the face of the deep, because the fire is the oldest inhabitant of any house — older than the walls, older than the terraces, carried up out of courses no reader has reached, burning continuously in clay for longer than the town has memory. A house that lets its fire die has forgotten something essential, and the neighbors come with coals, and no one speaks of it.

At night, from the water, you can take the census of the place in light. Above the waterline, the dim strung amber of the living town, the jars along the lanes, the tick of a late adze. Below, the inverted constellation of the drowned courses, where the moss has gone feral down the old stairwells and the fish drift their slow rounds among the niches and the finished dead hold everything up. Somewhere above, in the white roof, the bird keeps whatever count it keeps. Somewhere below, the tally that the listeners log and cannot explain grows by three blows, or five, a few times in a life.

A diver comes up a rope in the dark, slowly, because everything here is slow, chalk-white to the shoulders, a feather worked into her belt. She breaks the surface with a small soft sound and rests her hand on the gunwale and listens: drips, a low voice, the tick of stone. The living climb. The dead settle. The fish keep the lanes, the fire keeps the coals, the readers keep the addresses, the listeners keep the tally, and the water keeps everything it is given, whole, forever.

Up, down, or finished. In the basin, those are three ways of staying.
