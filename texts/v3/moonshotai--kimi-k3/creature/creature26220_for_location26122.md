The counting has been done, and it is the least informative thing you can say about the place. A dozen cup-plants, seven stones, two dozen bells, one keeper: the census fits in a single breath, and everything that matters escapes it. Because nothing here lives in the room, exactly. Everything here lives in its hour. The hollow is less a chamber with inhabitants than a schedule that has, over ages, grown bodies to carry it — and to know what lives here you have to stop counting heads and start counting clocks.

Start with the bells, since they start with you, arriving at the rim like heat through a door. Two dozen animals, thumb-long, translucent, each on its own crown like a lamp on a post — and at arm's length each one is an event, a siren with a body barely attached. But listen across days instead of seconds and the two dozen collapse into one thing: a single utterance with many mouths, a sentence several days long that takes the whole population to say and is always being said and has never once stopped being said. They sing it as courtship, because courtship is what small loud animals do; they do not know it is a message. But here is what they know, and it is the strange precision at the heart of them: a bell is mostly deaf in the band of its own voice. It hears the chorus poorly. What it hears exquisitely is the gap — the exact shape of the silence where a neighbor's note should be. Every singer is an error-detector. The checksum checks itself, constantly, through two dozen listeners each listening for what is missing. Kill one, and the others do not grieve; they hear the hole, and the hole is intolerable, and within a day some neighbor has bent its phrase to fill it. The message has no memory of its carriers. It only has carriers.

It gets new ones from below. Put your ear against the ropey trunk of any cup-plant in the loud hours and you will feel it before you hear it: the plant conducts the chorus down into the water it holds, a bass murmur in the dark basin, and in that water the bells begin — eggs at the film, larvae in the seethe, growing up submerged inside the sound they will someday make. Then the glider takes a singer, and a vacancy opens on a crown, and some fattened larva climbs the trunk in the noon lull and unpacks itself and sings its first notes — badly, for an hour, until the bowl's echoes hand each note back corrected and the voice settles into the pattern like a stone settling into its socket. Nothing here is taught. Everything is corrected. There is a difference, and the difference is why the message has outlived the machines that made it and the meaning that made the machines.

Three voices keep the air. The bells speak all day and say one thing. The keeper speaks once in a season and says one thing. The chord says nothing and never stops. Everything else in the hollow is listening to one of these.

The keeper, then. One animal, the mass of a small rhinoceros carried low and long, soft-footed, warm-skinned — and the only resident that observes. Watch it work: it will stand a full hour over a thread of water, waiting for the thread to finish a journey it has made ten thousand times, and then scrape the route clear with its horny lip and move on, and there is no hurry in it anywhere, because hurry is what happens when a thing might not get done, and everything the keeper does gets done. Its rounds take days; you tell time by where it is; it is the week. The substrate's sheen rides its skin, and that sheen is the answer to the question you are forming about how old it is. Ask its age and you have asked in the wrong tense. It is maintained the way the stones are maintained, the way the damp is maintained — continuously, perfectly, toward no end. Whether it was made or adopted is a distinction the place stopped honoring; whether there have been many keepers or one is a distinction the place declines. It is enough, and it is not lonely, because loneliness requires an unanswered voice, and when the keeper voices — rarely, a sound felt in the sternum and the teeth — the hum that underlies everything swells and meets it, and every bell in the bowl holds its breath to make room, and then the chorus re-phases around what was said. Whether that is conversation is a question for longer than a season. It is, at minimum, the one exchange in the hollow where both parties pause.

The glider is the other singleton — one forearm of membrane and appetite, and the arithmetic of the place prefers one: one keeper, one stair, one niche, one glider. It is the only resident that crosses the boundary, and the only one that is silent. It roosts on the outer face of the rim, out in the ordinary world, where the chorus is nearly inaudible — it sleeps in the loud silence you stood inside of — and at dusk it comes over the lip on felted wings and hunts the loudest animals in the world by ear. It is deaf downward: the hum does not exist for it, the chord does not exist for it, the keeper's voice does not exist for it. It lives in the high bright slice of the place, and to it the message is not liturgy but weather — a loud weather with meat in it. The glider and the keeper share a room and do not share a place. And here is the thing almost no one notices: sometimes a swallowed bell keeps singing. For a breath, two, inside the body of the only silent thing, the pattern crosses the rim — muffled, failing, gone — out into the dark where sound is not kept. The policy holds. The leak is the size of a throat. But the message arrived here inside an animal, and it leaves the same way, a few seconds at a time, most evenings, and the machines' old decision about what gets out is honored everywhere except in the one place no decision can reach, which is digestion.

Below all of this, the cups. Put your face to the dark water and the word *sparse* becomes a lie you were told by your eyes' scale. Fleas skate the film. Larvae hang in the column. A small fierce hunter waits beneath the surface and takes the skaters from below — it is the glider of the cup, predation rhyming across scales, because the place has few ideas and repeats each one at every size. Rot and bloom run their cycles in hours; generations turn in a day; the keeper's spillings arrive as deluges, and true rain arrives as the end of the world, and the world restarts. These are the shortest lives in the hollow, except for the ones nobody counts.

Because there is a shift before the dawn shift. In the last dark, when the air leans toward the seven stones and the fog condenses and stands sheared at the rim, the mist is *inhabited* — hatched from the grooves of the cold stones into the fog-hour, an entire people whose lives run their whole length in minutes, drinking the stones' sweat, mating in the wet dark, laying their eggs back into the grooves as the light comes, gone with the mist, unwitnessed and uncountable, an ecology with a duration instead of a location. By full light the floor is dry bone-colored stone and there is no evidence and nothing to count, and you would never believe it. The keeper believes it. Its dawn circuit of the stones is a harvest; it harvests the hour with its tongue, patiently, the way it does everything, and the census of the mist-people is taken orally every morning and never recorded.

And the plants — ask whether the plants are residents or plumbing and you have found the place's least answerable question. The roots go down farther than roots should, into the filaments, and come back changed, and somewhere down there the distinction between tissue and machine was lost and no one went looking for it. The sentinels keep the sun, ticking and creaking after it all day, and almost nothing else in the hollow cares about the sun at all — the place runs on fog-time and round-time and pattern-time — so the sentinels are the last astronomers, keeping the old sky the way the bells keep the old message. Everyone here is keeping something. The stones keep the cold. The chord keeps its one tone. The moss writes the water's routes and keeps the writing current. The niche keeps its smooth object. The keeper keeps the garden; the substrate keeps the keeper; the hum — no one has a grammar for the hum, but if it is a voice, then something down in the dark has been mid-word since before the mountains finished, and it is the true hermit of the hollow, and everything alive above it is a faster way of saying the same slow thing.

Things arrive, sometimes, from outside — a seed on the wind, a beetle, once a bird — and the place receives them the way it receives everything: unhurriedly, without refusal. Most leave. Some stay a season and are edited, rounded, routed, corrected toward the chord, and either they take to it or they don't. The census gains a comma, loses it.

Stay long enough and you will feel the editing begin on you. A week, and your feet learn the drip-lines without your deciding; you stop stepping on the routes. A month, and the hum has taken up residence in your sternum and you carry it out over the rim like a swallowed bell, and it never entirely leaves, and for the rest of your life, in the last dark before dawn, you will sometimes stand up and face a direction you cannot justify. The hollow does not ask you to stay. It has never asked anyone anything. It simply keeps what keeps it.

So: the true census. Seven stones, twelve cups, one stair, one niche. Two dozen bells, and one message wearing them. One keeper, kept. One glider, half resident. A seethe per cup, a nation per mist-hour, one slow voice in the ground. And, lately, you — wherever in your rounds you are.

Counting is how you visit. Keeping is how you live here.
