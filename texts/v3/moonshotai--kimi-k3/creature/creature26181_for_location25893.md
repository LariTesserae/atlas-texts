Nine hundred is not a population. It is the size at which recognition becomes absolute.

Everyone in the Winding knows everyone — not by face, mostly, since faces are the one thing the town economizes hardest on, but by everything else. By gait on the grating. By the pitch of a breath. By the sound a particular boot-heel makes lifting, which is more identifying than its landing, since everyone lands soft. A Winder can stand at a junction tree in full dark and name the six people within earshot without one of them speaking. Visitors find this uncanny. The town finds visitors deaf.

The body gives the trade away, here, because there are two lights in the Winding and no one gets both.

The Pin-marked work the outside: felters on their sunward plots, vane-tenders, flyers, wardens on their rounds. They are burnt dark in a land with no heat, squint-lined, their eyelashes gone pale from ultraviolet, and they work in felt eye-shades slit like a hawk's nictitating membrane. Pin-eye is the beginner's injury; every outside worker has wept through it once and only once.

The Bore-pale work the inside: glow-gardeners, Still-crew, cell-vault clerks, the old. They go the color of candle wax, and their pupils open past what plainsfolk would believe, and they can read a deck-weld by Crown-light the way you'd read this page by a window. *Dark-sight* is the word, and it is partly trainable and partly born, and the born version is the town's quiet pride: children whose eyes were made here, for here, and would be wasted anywhere with weather.

Both kinds are barrel-chested — a Winder child's ribs widen visibly by seven, the town says the chest *finds its shape* — and both kinds move with the same economy, the deep-water slowness of people who learned early that air is a ration. Nobody runs in the Winding except children, and children run out of breath in four strides and consider it worth it.

Their hands are their biographies, which is why a handshake here is a reading. You take a Winder's hand and you get the whole resume: the felter's grey to the wrist and grey half-moons in the nails that no scrubbing lifts; the wright's constellation of solder-burns on the thumb-pad; the hauler's winch-callus across the palm like a second life-line; the splicer's rope-sliced thumbs; the yarder's hands, scarred and dust-paled, that speak better than their voice does. And the handshake serves a second purpose, because you also take each other's ground. A person carrying a tingle is teased — *you're holding weather* — and sent to touch the shaft before they're allowed near a junction.

Hair lifts, up here, near eleven kilometers of charged glass. So the town braids it tight and threads the braid with a strand of conductor bonded at the clasp, and that is the Winding's jewelry, its one vanity: hair-wire. A clasp given is a serious gift. During a strike, in the Bore, everyone's braid-ends float free of their shoulders at once, nine hundred people in the warm dark with their hair rising, and nobody laughs, because it means the town is drinking.

Everyone wears the grey. Felt, layered, hooded against the dust that sometimes climbs. The wealthy show it the only way power allows — a second lamp, lit, hooded, amber — and anyone who wore light on their person would be considered obscene. The small change, clinker-shell beads, is carried in felt purses deliberately muffled, so that money makes no sound. The only people whose beads clink are plains merchants. You can hear a plains merchant from two turns away, and the town can, and does.

A Winder carries three names, and uses, on average, half of one.

The milk-name is spoken, and speech is breath, and breath is rationed, so the milk-name gets used at births, marriages, sentencings, and funerals — four times, essentially, a life's worth of occasions on two hands. The sign-name is the working name, a flicker of the fingers, and most are descriptive and slightly insulting in the way of names given by friends: *burns-soup*, *afraid-of-kites*, *owes-me*. The bell-name is the formal address, a short pattern struck on the junction bells — two short, one long, one short, like a heart with a limp — and the bell-code is sized for the town, nine hundred patterns and change, every child fluent by five.

Here is the thing about the bell-code. When someone dies, their pattern is struck once, at the lee rail, and then retired. Never assigned again. The code has holes in it, and the holes are the cemetery, and every Winder carries the map of the holes by heart. Ask a warden where the dead are buried and she'll strike two notes and stop. The silence after is the answer.

The sign-language comes in registers. Deck-sign is big, whole-arm, made to be read in wind and underlight-flicker at twenty meters. Close-sign is small, lamplight work, fingers only, for the Exchange and the dinner ledge. Touch-sign is signed on the palm, in total dark, and it is the bone-yarders' gift to the town: they came back up out of the Murk year after year with voices the dust had taken, and they made a literature out of the loss. The town's poems are palm-poems. They cannot be written down, by design — a poem that exists only while one hand touches another is a poem that obeys the fourth clause — and lovers speak touch-sign, and during storms, in the Bore, whole conversations run palm to palm while the thump arrives through the floor.

Writing exists, but the Winding's true script is cord. The town is half rope by mass and fully rope by culture, and the four clauses are four knots before they are four ideas. The ground-knot. The muffle-wrap. The lashing-hitch. And the release-knot, which comes undone at one pull, which is tied in every gift and every grave-good, which is the hardest knot to teach a child, because tying it means practicing the moment you stop holding. Every worker splices a signature into their lashings — a personal knot-grammar — so that responsibility in the Winding is not a matter of records. It is a matter of handwriting you can hang from.

Even addresses obey the ethic. A turn is born at the top as the First, ages down through the work-names, spends its middle years in the Exchange ring, and ends as the Waterline, and then the dust takes it. Nobody's home stays young. Your address ages around you at a fingernail's pace, and everyone knows it, and this is considered good for people. When a turn is condemned to the Murk there is a rite — the Shedding — where the whole town walks the doomed deck once, touching nothing, and then the lashings are cut while everyone watches. Children are brought to the front. *This,* the wardens say, with no particular sadness, *is the sermon.*

The Winding has no pets. It has colleagues.

The rule is that the kept must earn, and everything that lives here earns. The mite-trays earn twice — once in meal, since mite-meal bakes into the flat grey bread that is the town's staple, provided you bake it the hour you mill it, because the meal walks — and once as instruments, because mites migrate toward potential, and a tray that empties itself uphill is a storm warning the wardens dismiss and every grandmother trusts. The wire-worms earn in castings and in light, and old glow-gardeners swear the trays breathe with the Spindle's hum, brightening and dimming on the mountain's own slow meter, though the wrights have measured this and decline to publish.

The clinkers earn in the junction cabinets, and are half tool, half flock. Every cabinet has its resident population and every junction-keeper has their favorites, and a cabinet's startle-clink is a chord with a signature — a warden can diagnose a cabinet with her eyes shut, can tell you which shell is cracked, which beetle is old. Children race them in felt-lined lanes, for shell-bead stakes, in whispers. Once a year, at the founding-feast, clinkers are roasted in the shell, and the shells pop as they open, and a feast is the one time the Winding clinks on purpose. It is the town's idea of noise. It is, by any other world's standard, a library clearing its throat.

The felt itself is a standing argument. The felters hold that all the grey on the shaft is one organism — a single skin, kilometers tall, drinking the mountain's potential — and that a plot is not a field but an appointment with one animal. The wrights hold that this is unfalsifiable and that the felters should reseed their plates. Once in a decade the felt blooms, on the sunward side, and sheds a spore-dust that glows faintly and drifts down the turns like slow snow, and children catch it on their tongues, and it is savory, and the harvest of it — *snow-salt*, hoarded in thimbles, the town's only native seasoning — is worth more per gram than kite quill. On bloom days nobody works. Even the wardens don't pretend it's about the salt.

And the kites are not colleagues, and not livestock, and not gods, though the town talks about them in a tone reserved for all three. No one keeps kites; the kites keep the fins. The town leaves bright wire out on the lee ledges — a tithe disguised as carelessness — and the kites steal it and weave it into nests, and both sides pretend this is theft and victory. Kite-readers, a guild of maybe eleven, have decoded part of the wing-flicker: patterns for storm grades, for strangers, for *person-on-the-fin*, and patterns that appear to be individual. A kite that passes you will often flicker your pattern — your pattern, the one it has assigned you, which you will never know, since reading your own kite-name is considered the height of vanity — and a person whose escort-flicker goes missing notices, and says nothing, and grieves, off-schedule, in the correct manner, which is alone.

Kites die the way the town believes a person should. When the time comes, an old kite flies out of the lift column, folds, and drops into the Murk, letting go of the sky all at once and forever. Kites never need burying. The town watches them do this its whole life, generation after generation, and it would be wrong to say the Winders learned the fourth clause from the birds — but when a child asks how to tie the release-knot, someone always points at the sky.

---

Work marks everyone, and some of the marks are worth setting down.

The haulers are the only human beings who have ever seen the Winding. Everyone else lives in it, close as a hand on a rope; the haulers ride the car down through the Murk and come out under it, and look up, and see home entire — a thread wound on a needle, furred with decks, vanishing into a charcoal sea — and they carry that sight around like a second spine. It makes them strange to their neighbors. They speak plains-tongues. They know what rain smells like and will describe it, badly, for a thimble of anything. The dust takes their voices in the end — Murk-lung, the yarders get it too — so the old haulers sign, and their sign is full of plains loan-gestures, wide wet motions the town finds faintly obscene and can't stop watching.

The bone-yarders come up from the drowned turns with salvage and with one other thing: a hand-sign that has no spoken word. They use it rarely, and only among themselves, and if a child asks what it means the yarder will say, quite honestly, *charged dust does strange things*, and then change the subject. The wardens file it under hallucination, see also: hauler's fever. The yarders keep working certain turns and not others, and they will not say why, and the not-saying has been going on long enough to have become a kind of title-deed: the yarders are the town's keepers of the thing the town does not discuss.

The wardens' law is quiet like everything else. Sanctions, ascending: forfeiture of lamp-hours, so that your evenings go dark while your neighbors' glow — a fine paid in light. Bonded service, where you work clipped to a crew's strap, grounded to other people until the wardens are satisfied you remember why. Suspension of your bell-name — the town cannot call you; the junctions will not carry your pattern; to be uncallable in a town of nine hundred is to be a ghost with a job. And finally, for the unforgivable offense, which is endangering the ground during storm season: sent down. Not killed. Put on the car, lowered eleven kilometers, given to the wet loud world below. Most Winders would take a blade first. The wardens know this. It has been used four times.

The most trusted person in town has no enforcement power at all. She is called the Thimble, after the office, and the office is the Still and the cistern and the daily measure, and she wears an actual thimble on a cord, and it is the only badge of office in the Winding, because it is also the only one that is a tool. Betrothals use thimbles too — lovers exchange them, and *drink mine* is the closest thing the town has to the unsayable sentence — and the joke writes itself and no one makes it, because water is the one subject on which the Winding has no humor.

And then there are the flyers, and the splicers who keep them alive. A kite-harness is a human thing: webbing and splice-work worn by the ones who lean out off the fins on lines, into the permanent updraft, riding the lift column like the birds to service the outer vanes and arrays. It is the closest a person comes to kite-hood, and children are passed their first harness the way other children are passed a first boat, and the sensation — eleven kilometers of nothing under your heels, the column holding you like a hand — is called *lift-drunk*, and it is the town's second intoxicant. The first is free to everyone: stand on the fins after a strike, breathe the doubled ozone, feel the cleaned world scour your head. *Storm-drunk.* The Winding has no liquor worth the water, and has never felt the lack.

Every flyer leans out over forever on a splice, and every splice is signed, and so the splicers are the load-bearing souls of the town's trust — the people whose handwriting other people's children hang from.

The rites are few, and they all point at the same four ideas.

Birth happens in the Bore, in the warm, and within the day the newborn is carried up — up the ladders, up the turns, in a sling lined with cast-seeded felt so the infant glows faintly, its own nightlight — to a viewing deck, and held up to the Crown. The Upholding. First sight, given before the eyes can focus, on the theory that the mountain should be the first thing even if it can't be the first thing seen. Then childhood: the clauses before letters, the bells by five, the glow-braid every child wears so parents can find them in the dark, so that the Winding's children are the only lights in town that move where they please.

Marriage is done by a warden, in the dark, in about forty words, most of them knot-names. Dark-married, it's called. And then the couple waits — a season, two, five — for a Bloom, for that one minute when the strike out-gives the vaults and every lamp in town blazes at once, and in that minute they finally see each other. Bloom-confirmed, it's called, and the town considers the second marriage the real one. You can live with a voice and a gait and a pair of hands for years, the Winders say, and then the mountain shows you their face, lit, for sixty seconds, and that is the wedding portrait. It is also the only portrait. Nobody draws faces in the Winding. It would feel like hoarding.

Death is the Upholding backward: the dying ask, and are carried up, and look at the Crown last. Then the body is wrapped in its own bedding-felt, tied with a release-knot, given to the lee side on a wind day, and the bell-name is struck once and retired, and that is the end of the ceremony and there are no anniversaries. Grieving on schedule is the only error. Grief itself is weather — it comes when it comes, and you stand in it, and it passes, and you do not build it a house.

What the town cannot let go of, it gives to the Loadbearer.

There is one sanctioned hoard in the Winding, and the rule is that it must be carryable — one chest, one person, up the ladders, at a dead run if the bells say so. When something new is admitted, something old must go out, dropped from the Waterline into the Murk with no ceremony whatsoever. The current contents, as the Loadbearer will recite if you ask at the right hour: a sealed bottle of rain, taken off a caravan a century back and never opened, the town's entire supply of the lowland rumor. A wooden cup, wood being stranger here than gold. A bronze bell from a drowned turn so deep its pattern is retired past all memory — the bell of someone the town no longer knows it lost. A kite egg, mineralized, unhatched. A child's glow-braid. And a plain cord of release-knots, one knot for every Winder who ever chose the car over the sky, the down-hearted, the ones who went below and did not come back. No names. The knots are names.

---

The town's theology, such as it is, fits in a palm.

Storms are never named. Naming a storm is asking it to know you. The kites are read, the mite-trays are read, the nap of the felt is read, and before the miners cut a new gallery into the glass they ring a small peal at the face — *telling the glass first* — because the Spindle carries the memory of its own making in its magnetization, and a mountain with a memory deserves notice before you take a bite out of it. The miners also report, and will not elaborate, that the hum is not the same in all galleries. The wrights have measured this too. They decline to publish that as well.

On the clearest days, from the top turns, you can count the Brothers — the other spires of the Brood, standing off at the horizon with their dead windings on them. Every child learns their names as geography. And one elder, old Sef, who is all touch-sign now, remembers being a small girl at the rail the night the nearest Brother went dark: a town's lights, visible through a gap for her whole life, and then a storm, and then no lights. Her town did the one extravagant thing in its history. They lit every lamp anyway, deliberately, a blaze paid for out of a season's surplus, the most expensive funeral in the world, for neighbors they had never met and never heard and never would.

The wardens will tell you the Bloom is an overflow custom, a matter of excess charge. The older hands will tell you the overflow is an excuse, that the town blazes once a season because a town should be seen burning once in a while, even if only by itself.

And everyone in the Winding, once a year or so, dreams of rain. Nobody mentions it. It is the one thing the whole town keeps, off the books, in common.

---

The someone in the lamp-room has a milk-name, though you'd wait years to hear it.

It's Merra. Sign-name: two fingers drawn across the palm, the splice-check gesture, given by her crew and accurate. Bell-name: two short, one long. She is the splicer of record for the northeast fins, which means the flyers who lean out over eleven kilometers of nothing do so on her handwriting, and tonight the handwriting on her bench is for her own kid, whose first solo lean-out is set for calm season, and she has checked the splice nine times and will check it three more before she lets herself drink the thimble.

The water is going warm on the shelf above the relay, the way she likes it, waste heat being the one heat the Winding gives away. In the wall cabinet, Hinge — the cabinet's eldest clinker, named by the kid years ago, in the years when the kid named things — ticks in his sleep, once, settling his shell. The glow of the worm-trays comes through the felt door in a green seam. Below her feet, below the Bore, below the Waterline and the bone-yards and the drowned turns of strangers, the Murk breathes, and through the soles of her feet the mountain hums its one remembered note, the note it has been holding since the sky touched it once, impossibly, and made it.

She holds the harness up by Crown-light and reads it the way she'd read a face. It reads back: *held.*

Somewhere above her the kites are wheeling, voiceless, hundreds of them, flickering their small patient patterns at one another — *person below, storm in four days, person below, person below.* Nine hundred people, wound around the glass in the dark, lashed and grounded and muffled and practicing, every day of their lives, the one knot that matters.

The Winding keeps. Ask Merra what the word means and she'd show you her hands: *to hold, and to be held, and to not let go before the storm says so.*
