You could cross the whole of it and find no one. That is the first thing to understand about what lives here: the world outnumbers its inhabitants by so much that the inhabitants qualify as weather — occasional, local, easy to miss.

But the signs are there, once you learn how few signs this world permits. A path along a ridgecrest polished past what rain can manage — rain rounds; feet *use*. A stair whose silt has been disturbed, each step's separate century swept in the same small patient arc. A root-curtain where the hanging vines have been tied back — tied, with a knot — to frame a passage through. On the sill of a doorframe on a bare summit, a loop of root-fiber no bigger than a wrist, left like a signature.

Follow the signs and you find the people.

There are not many. There have never been many. The number is known — known exactly, by everyone — and it is smaller at each brightening than at the one before, and no one says so, because saying so would spend a word they are keeping.

They had a name for themselves once. It wore out, the way words do; they have watched this happen to worse words than theirs, to *sky*, to everything soft. They let it go. When they must mean *us*, they touch two fingers to the breastbone and then open the hand outward, and the gesture means *still here*. Travelers call them the last hands, for their work, or the ridge-folk, for their address. They answer to anything. It is all one to a people who no longer spend their own name.

---

You will not find them by warmth. They are the temperature of everything; the water took that boundary long ago. Find them instead by outline — because a person here is an outline maintained on purpose.

At the wrists, the ankles, the waist, the throat: cords of root-fiber, snug. Not ornament. Reminder. In water the warmth of blood you cannot feel where you end, and so they wear where they end. Children are bound before they can swim the steps; divers bind tighter going down, and the deeper the hour they intend, the more of themselves they declare. The old loosen theirs, cord by cord, year by year. You can read a whole life in someone's bindings — how many, how tight, how new, how frayed. It is the only jewelry here and the only biography.

Their hands are polished like the ridge. Touch is the sense the world left them, and they have made a literature of it: an elder runs a thumb along a terrace edge and reads it the way you would read a chronicle — the ninth step and the tenth feel different, and the difference is history. Their eyes are wide and unhurried, made for pearl-gray dimness and the blue ember-drift of the candle-algae. They move the way everything here moves except the sediment: slowly, and on purpose. When they must travel far they step into one of the Green's exhale-rivers and let the forest's breath carry them, hanging in the current like a thought being thought somewhere else.

Children learn early which valleys are forest and which are a mouth that has learned stillness; it is the first geography taught, and it is taught once. And on still days, when the water replays its dead, the children swim through the rehearsals — through the long-ago drowning of a forest, through the ghost-schools of fish gone these ages — the way your children run through rain. The oldest rehearsals have been replayed so often they count as neighbors. The children greet them.

---

Sound was the first thing the world wore away, and speech was the softest part of sound, so it went before almost anything. What the people do instead is older than speech and quieter. They speak through bone.

A hand cupped to the base of the skull. A forehead set against yours. The voice arrives the way the hum arrives — in the teeth, in the marrow, intimate as your own pulse, which you can also hear, there; both of you can, and neither mentions it. Children learn bone-speech before they learn the steps. To be offered the skull-touch by a stranger is to be trusted with the loudest thing they own.

For distance there is gesture, and there is the ridge. Stone carries vibration the way bone does, and the whole massif is one body: a tapped line on the tenth step can be felt, faint and freighted, a valley away. Very occasionally a message arrives before it is sent. Those are not answered. A message that early is clearly meant for someone else.

And they keep a spoken language — kept the way the thresholds are kept: a small stock of hard words, hoarded, kept sharp by not being used. They have watched a word said too often go smooth, and then mean everything, and then nothing; they watched it happen to *sky*. So they are frugal past imagining. Every person is given two names: the one they are called, which is any convenience and changes with company, and a true one, spoken once at the birth-bed into the infant's bone and then never again — kept unspent, the one sharp thing a person can own. To speak your true name into another's skull is the greatest trust that exists here. Whole lives pass without it. Whole lives are not poorer.

Inside the thresholds, sound comes back — so the spoken tongue lives in the doorways, and it is mostly songs. There are perhaps a dozen left. Singing wears a song the way water wears stone; they have heard it happen — the oldest song has a smoothed place in the third line where a word used to be. So songs are rationed: one for a birth, one for an unbinding, and one verse — only ever one verse — at the brightening, standing on the great bridge while the Slow pass beneath in the channel and the dim world swells to blue. It is the only chorus in the world. The number of voices is known.

---

Work, here, is the work of rubbing back.

The keepers tend the thresholds, and that is the whole of the craft, and it takes whole lives. The builders' only art was selective erosion; keeping an edge is the same art turned against the current — wearing away, grain by grain, everything that would round it. A keeper may spend a decade on a single lintel and call it a good decade. They test their work the way the world announces it: the bell-tone felt in the teeth. A threshold that rings is holding. A threshold that has gone silent is only an arch now, beautiful and ordinary, and the keepers visit those too, the way you visit graves. There is one law among the people, and it is this: nothing may be taken from a threshold. The only edges in the world that can cut live in the doorways, and they stay in the doorways. The people have no knives. They have patience, which is slower and does most of the same work.

The keepers' word for *craft* and their word for *death* are the same word. It is one of the words they have stopped saying.

The gleaners work the digestive bells of the Green — reaching into the barely closed, translucent, patient bells to take what the plant has finished with, or nearly. It is a profession of stillness. A good gleaner can wait a day for a bell to relax, and moves, when they move, at the speed of the plant's attention, which is geological. The bells are never in a hurry. Neither is anyone. The rule of the glean is simple: nothing the plant is still remembering.

The raft-tenders ride the bladder-rafts on the slow gyres, keeping the hanging gardens in the root-curtains — tying back the vines, harvesting the pale fruit, mending with fiber what the water loosens. A full circuit of a gyre takes years. Families are crews; children are born mid-water and learn the currents the way other children learn stairs. The raft-tenders are the worldly ones. They have been *elsewhere*, which here is a qualification and a half.

The low-divers go down the steps into the older hours, to bring up what the deep silt gives up, or only to touch it — the silt of the tenth step is not of this year, and to handle it is to lay a hand on the century of it. It costs something. Divers come back up slower than they went down, and quieter, and somehow later than they left, and for a while their blood runs louder in them, as though the deep had taught it to shout. The old go down-step as they age — that is retirement here, a descent: step by step into earlier centuries, into the old light that pools in the low valleys, into the world as it was. One day they step off the last stair they can manage and do not come up. That, too, is called *going wide*.

The pool-keepers tend the keeping-pools — the oldwater that lies on the seafloor like lakes under the lake, where the world keeps what it cannot quite forget. What is set into oldwater is kept: not unchanged exactly, but *held*, the way the pool holds the valley as it was. Fruit comes up tasting of the year it ripened. Tools come up remembering their hands. Every community keeps one pool — larder, archive, reliquary — and is kept by it in turn. And the pool-keeper is the one person permitted to look long into the reflection, to read what the world is still rehearsing. It is not a sought position.

A few live on the Slow. It is the only address in the world that does not erode, and the riders keep the groves that grow on the mineral backs, and sweep the warm stone, and listen. They say the hum is not one sound but many, layered like the water itself, and that a rider of forty years begins to pick out the strands, and that the strands are *counting* — that the Slow are counting something, or recounting it, and have been for longer than the people have been people. Counting what, the riders do not say. Probably they do not know. It is enough to live on the back of the one thing in the world that is not ending.

And there is the keeper of the tower — one person, or two, at a time — who lives half in the rain and half in the water, in the last dry room, with the soft continuous hiss of the upper sea sieving down through the worn sky: the loudest sound in the world, kept like a relic, and the keeper its reliquary. Ask what the hiss is like and they will set their forehead to yours, and what comes through is *everything at once*; is *the world before it learned to be quiet*. Pilgrims climb to stand in the doorway and hear it and be dry. The keeper lets them. At the top of the long tide, when the door aligns with something, the keeper is there alone, and what it aligns with, the keeper does not say. It may be that saying it would spend it.

---

Of the rest — the four kinds, or five:

The glass fry are the children of a fish that no longer exists. The adults wore out of the life-cycle entirely; the species goes on as a childhood that has learned to continue itself, more light than meat, drifting in sparse translucent shoals along the exhale-rivers. At the brightening the people take a few, and eating them is the nearest thing here to swallowing light, and it is done slowly, with attention, the way everything is done.

The skinners come and go through the rainskin and are watched the way weather is watched elsewhere: when they drop through the rain in numbers, the long tide is turning. They nest in the tower's wet half, above the keeper's head and below the upper sea, and they are the only creatures who know the second ocean from underneath. They tell nothing. They are animals, and therefore already fluent in the world's first language, which is not saying.

In the three recompiled steps of every standing arch lives a small pale jointed thing found nowhere else in the world — a whole species with a range of three steps. Down is always down for them; sound has never once failed; they live entire generations inside a working rule. They have never seen the world. The people call them the doorkeepers and count them with the thresholds rather than the animals, on the grounds that anything whose edges never slip is more building than beast.

In the digestive bells of the Green lives something else — a creature that has made itself exactly not worth digesting: too slow, too thin, a rumor of an animal. Evolution here rewards the unnoticeable, and so it is the most successful life in the world, safe in the one place nothing hunts, the only guest the Green keeps.

And the Slow are the Slow. The people do not petition them, exactly, but a person may stand on a channel-bank as one passes — hum in the bone, groves on its back — and ask it one question. The answer comes as a change in the hum, or it comes in your grandchildren, or the asking was the answer. When a Slow dies and settles and becomes an island, warm for a century, the people settle it: a town with a known lifespan, the world's only lease. When the warmth begins to fade they move off, unhurried, a generation ahead of the cold, and the island goes on being a ridge, and the ridge goes on down.

As for the thing in the oldwater, there are three sayings. That it is the first thing that ever drowned, and the water refuses to finish forgetting it. That it is the last new thing there will ever be, arriving early, because causes slip. That it is the world practicing its final form, and the pools reflect it from angles that do not exist yet because those angles are where the future is. The pool-keepers look, when they must, and say nothing, which is what pool-keepers are for.

So: four kinds, or five. The fifth is disputed. Some count the thing in the oldwater. Some count the people, which the people decline to do. The keepers count the doorways, on the grounds already stated — and if the keepers are right, the world has far more life in it than anyone thought, and most of it is doors.

---

Once in the long year, at the brightening, they gather at the great bridge over the Slow's channel. They come down the exhale-rivers and off the rafts and up out of the low steps, and the candle-algae kindle through every layer of the Green until the dim world swells to a blue dusk, and the Slow come to graze the bloom, and the people stand on the one bridge in the world, where sound works along its whole span, and they do two things.

They count. The number is known to everyone before the counting; the counting is not for information. And they sing one verse — only one, because a song is a thing that wears — and the verse has a smoothed place in it where a word used to be, and everyone hears the word anyway, in the bone, where nothing wears.

Then it is over, and the long year turns, and they go back to the holding.

If you come among them they will give you three things, which are the only wealth here. Food that tastes of its year. A place beside the light-hollow — the hearth of this world, a hollow where the old light pools and lingers, tended the way you would tend a fire, if the world had ever admitted fire. And three steps through a doorway, so that you can hear your own heart aloud. You will weep. Everyone does. It is not the sorrow. It is the sound.

---

Death, here, is called going wide, and it is not a stopping but a finishing — the boundary the person maintained all their life, cord by cord, finally allowed to do what everything here does. At the end the bindings are loosened, one by one, in the order they were first tied, and the body is given to the warm water, which takes it the way it takes everything: without sound, without hurry, in good faith.

The cords are kept. In a niche by the keeping-pool, or in the dry room if the family is close to the tower, you will find them: small loops of root-fiber, dozens, hundreds, each one the exact outline of a wrist, a throat, a life. That is their portrait gallery. That is their archive. To hold a grandmother's outline in your palm — they will place it there, if you are trusted — is to hold the shape of the boundary she held against the whole soft ending of the world.

Some, much loved and much unfinished, are set into the oldwater instead, and kept. The pool holds them as they were. On still days the pool-keeper sees them in the valley as it was, going about the old light, and does not say so.

Ask the keepers — with your skull against theirs — why they go on rubbing edges into a world that unmakes edges, and you will get one of two answers. The first: that a threshold is a *decision*, and the Wearing is the world wearing toward its final form, and someone must stay to hold the decisions open — because a world with no edges left is a world with no doors, and a world with no doors can never again be arrived in. The second is older, and is not spoken often: that the builders never left. That the Wearing is the building. That everything being rubbed away is being made — that the finished world is the artifact, and the people are either the tools or the material, and the keepers, going over their lintels grain by grain, are not maintaining the doorways at all.

They are finishing them.

The keepers do not argue about this. They are busy.

---

So this is what lives here. A small warm people with polished palms and unspent names, keeping edges. The children of a fish, carrying a little light. A rumor in the bells. A congregation of three steps. The Slow, counting. Something in the oldwater, at angles that do not exist yet. And the last hands, patient as the Wearing itself — because the Wearing has all the time there is, and somebody has to stand on the other side of it, rubbing back.

They are nearly finished. They have been nearly finished for a very long time. It does not hurry them; nothing here hurries. When the brightening comes they will gather on the bridge, and the Slow will pass beneath, and they will sing the verse — the number of voices is known — and then they will go back to the work, which is to say to the holding, which is to say: two fingers to the breastbone, and the hand opening outward.

*Still here.*
