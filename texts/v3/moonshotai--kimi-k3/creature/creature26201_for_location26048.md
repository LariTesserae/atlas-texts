Nothing lives here — that is almost a rule of the world, and *almost* is a load-bearing word. Ask the log and it will tell you the chamber is visited, never inhabited: a book of valve settings and dosimeter readings, of weep rates and lamp failures, and one moth. But the log is young, and it is kept by creatures who can only stay twenty minutes at a time, and a census taken in twenty-minute installments misses nearly everything. Take the census properly — take it the slow way, the way the room itself takes time — and the nothing unpacks into nations.

Begin with the largest population, which is also the briefest. Every second the iron iris lets through tonnes of reservoir water, and the Col is not sterile: it is a living lake, and it pours its whole plankton down the Highline in the dark — algae, rotifers, bacteria past counting, a lake's worth of small life driven through a valve and torn to froth against black water. None of it stays. The plunge pool is not a habitat; it is an event. The stilling well behind its perforated pipe sits flat as a seal and pretends to be a pond, but the pretense is the point — nothing in that water is resident, everything is in transit, dashed, aerated, carried on toward the valley. The room's border is the hardest in the world: millions arrive by water every hour and none are granted stay. Population: uncountable. Residency: seconds. And here is the strange arithmetic of the place — everything that *does* live here arrived dry. By boot, by cuff, by shoulder, down the vent shaft on the mountain's gray breath. The founders came by air.

The founders' nations hang sideways on the walls. Four ellipses of green, one beneath each caged lamp — the crews call them the ponds, and the joke is precise, because the room's true ponds are vertical. Each is a portrait of its lamp: the light-cone falls on wet concrete and life fills the shape of the light to its exact edge, sharp as a coastline on a map, then fades to nothing in the shadow. At the center the film is a hundred cells deep, a jungle pressed into two dimensions; at the margins the pioneers thin to scattered cells, each alone in the dark like a last farmhouse. Where a weep-thread crosses an ellipse from a lift-line seam, the green deepens along the water — rivers within the nation, well-watered districts. Nothing grazes them, so the dead lie where they fall and the living grow over their ancestors, and every pond is a tell, a mound of its own dead slowly thickening. Their clock is the lamp's burning hours. Their sun never sets. That is the whole of their theology.

Except for Pond 3. The third lamp is failing, and beneath it the third nation is starving at the pace of a dimming sun. The margin dies first — the pioneers, always — and the ellipse contracts toward its center like an empire's map in the bad centuries. The log knows: *Lamp 3 output reduced. Pond 3 contracting.* Somewhere in a depot inbox there is a work order, priority routine, and on it hangs the fate of a nation. When the lamp dies — and it will die before the order is worked — the pond will fade to a ghost, a faint stain in the shape of lost light, the room's second fossil. And when the lamp is changed, the new cone will fall a hand's width off the old one's line, and a new pond will kindle beside the ghost of the old, and the wall will hold both, the way ground holds a city and the city beneath the city. None of this has happened yet. The room is eighteen months old and no lamp has ever died. The first death will be an epoch, and only the walls will remember it.

Below the ponds, along the splash line, runs the other nation — the felt, the dark biofilm slick as paint. It does not farm light; it fishes water. It is intertidal, and its tides are the valley's. Twice a day, morning and evening, when ten thousand strangers below put on kettles and run showers, the cursor moves, the iris opens, the white cone swells, and the splash line climbs the wall and the felt's upper border feeds. At midday the tide goes out. In melt season the spray reaches the ceiling and the whole room becomes littoral — the felt's one season of abundance, its harvest. The valley below does not know the room exists, but the valley sets its table twice a day. The crews wipe the handrail and leave the walls, so the rail is a cleared path through the wilderness, a firebreak the wilderness respects for the length of a cloth. Whether grazers have arrived yet — protozoa, rotifers that survived the iris by some miracle of smallness — no instrument here will ever know. The log cannot see them. The true census is larger than the recorded census, and it always will be.

Deeper in, the census goes microscopic and slows nearly to a stop. In the pore water of the concrete, in the threads that weep from the lift-lines, behind the grout of the form-tie cones, in the upstream joint that pulses with the jet — the crews call that one the vein — lives the room's third nation, and its oldest, and its strangest. The water in those pores is caustic as lye; almost nothing of the surface world can abide it. They abide it. Their ancestors may have gone to sleep at the batch plant, spores folded into the dry cement, and woken inside the mountain when the water found them. Their generations run to weeks in the cold. To them the roar is weather, the transfer silences are eclipses, the inspectors are bright meteors that cross the sky and are gone. And they are not merely living here. They are building. The soda straws budding from the form-tie cones, the little rimmed dams terracing the floor channel — call it chemistry and you are not wrong; call it masonry and you are closer. Their bodies and their waste tip the dissolved lime out of the water and into stone. What a limestone cave does in a thousand years they close in a season, because their quarry is their home is their host's own bone: the room dissolves itself, and they re-lay it as rock. The cave the room is becoming is not a ruin. It is a reef, and they are the reef-builders, a workforce too small to see, building the one monument that will outlast the machine.

And then there is the moth. The log is dry about it: *1 moth, deceased. Walkway. Left in situ.* It came in on a shoulder, the only winged colonist, a migrant that took the wrong door and found a world with one weather and no flowers. It died as everything soft must in the wet and the roar, and nobody removed it, because the rule of the room is *passing through* and the moth had stopped passing. The crews call it the tenant. It has tenure. Everything else in PB-3 holds its place on condition — of light, of splash, of the beacon's mercy — but the dead are exempt from the warning on the door. The chamber may fill without warning; the tenant does not care. The gray silt has taken it to the wing-joints, and every drop of ceiling rain freights it with a little more lime. In ten years there will be a low mound on the walkway with a moth at its heart; in fifty, a stone; in some far inspection, a man will stand on a grating above a fossil and not know it. The room's first fossil is still in the making, and it is the one citizen guaranteed to remain.

Everything that ever arrived here arrived on a body. The pond spores rode boot leather. The felt's bacteria rode cuffs and seep. The masons may have ridden the cement itself. The tenant rode a shoulder. The room has no wind that carries life and no water that lets it stay, so its only birds are the inspectors — twenty-minute birds, on the dosimeter's leash. Their visits are events in the ecology: the door-blast of outside air, the headlamp cones swinging like little movable suns across the ponds, boot-falls sowing the next century's immigrants with every step. They speak in the transfer silences, whole conversations in three-second installments; a question asked in one silence gets its answer three silences later and neither party finds this strange. And they keep the log, which is the room's memory kept outside its body, because nothing that lives here knows where it lives except them, and they are not allowed to stay. One set of initials recurs in the book more than any other. This one measures the straws with a caliper and writes the lengths to the half millimeter — *Straw 7: 11 mm* — and has begun, without quite deciding to, the room's natural history. The room has a naturalist. It does not know. She knows for it.

There are five of these cells on the Long Descent, five identical bodies poured from the same drawings, and the inspectors are the only creatures that move between them — an archipelago of young caves with exactly one species of bird, and the bird wears boots. Already the islands diverge. PB-1 keeps the most rime. PB-4 has flooded its walkway twice. PB-3 is the one with the ponds, the one with the vein, the one with the tenant — the most alive of the five, by a moth and four ponds.

Then step back far enough, and the count collapses to one. Everyone here keeps a different clock: the transients keep seconds, the ponds keep the lamp's burning hours, the felt keeps the valley's mornings and evenings, the masons keep the chemistry, the tenant keeps geology, the witnesses keep the dosimeter — and the room keeps all of them at once. It breathes, through the vent's slow exhale of mist. It has a pulse, the vein beating in time with the jet. It swallows every few minutes, the sump's clunk and hum. It circulates its own water in a closed loop, a pocket hydrology, a heart of spray and rain and channel and pump. It has a voice that fills the body entire, and a deep note it finds when the actuator moves, felt in the sternum and the teeth. It has one muscle — the iron iris — and a nerve that runs up the mountain to a screen in the valley, and when the cursor moves the muscle answers: it is reflexive at a distance of kilometers. It metabolizes; it dissolves its own bones and lays them down again as stone, catabolism and anabolism in a single wet gesture. It grows, a centimeter of straw a season, drop by drop. It sleeps and wakes on the demand curve. It keeps seasons. It has four sisters and is becoming unlike them. It is eighteen months old, it will outlive its keepers, and it is mortal — the argument with its own water is the one argument water always wins, and the straws are both its growing and its dying.

So: what lives in PB-3? The ponds live in it, and the felt, and the masons, and the tenant, and the trillion transients, and the twenty-minute witnesses — and all of them together are less than the census's largest entry. The thing that lives in PB-3 is PB-3.

Night. Below, the valley lights come on and the demand rises, and somewhere a cursor moves and the room takes a deeper breath. The ceiling rain falls unwitnessed. Pond 3 glows a little dimmer than its sisters, holding its contracting shape like a shoreline in retreat, and the felt feeds at its tide, and the tenant rests in its silt, and along the vein the masons lay down another hair's width of stone. On the longest straw a drop swells — heavy with last spring's concrete, freighted with the slow masonry of the unseen — and lets go.

One heartbeat of stone. Then another. The count, taken from far enough above, is simple: one green-edged light in the dark of the mountain, humming, alive the slow way, becoming a cave — which is the oldest way a stone has ever found of being alive.
