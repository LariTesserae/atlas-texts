The first answer is the largest one, and it changes all the others: the city lives here. Everything else — the grazers, the birds, the newts, the rosettes, the moss, the small wingless things — is a tenant of a single body. The tenancy is old and the body is old, and between those two facts the bowl has settled into an arrangement that nothing designed and nothing breaks. The population of the bowl can be counted. Most of what follows is the count.

Begin with the body itself, since everything else begins there too. Watch what it does across a day and you are watching the behavior of a living thing, slowed past recognition. It drinks: the dawn sigh is only the intake, the combing of the fog, and afterward, if you put your ear to any wall in the mid-morning, you can hear the swallow — water drawn down inside the walls, through channels no one has ever seen, on its way to the cisterns. It goes on for an hour. The city drinks the way it does everything else: slowly, and all at once. It keeps a warmth: the pilot light under the streets, banked and minimal, the overflow of some metabolism whose organ has never been found, because the city has no inside you can enter. The halls and towers feel like interiors, but they are folds of its surface; the palm of a hand is not the inside of the hand. Whatever the warmth serves, it serves it below, and it does not come up. Everything alive in the bowl lives on that overflow, the way moths live at a lantern — except that the lantern does not know the moths, and the moths have learned not to ask.

It disposes of its dead, which are its own. Nothing is maintained and nothing collapses — but things have collapsed, once, long ago: nine towers stand where perhaps more stood, and there is no rubble anywhere, no fallen span, no shard small enough to pick up. The city takes back its dead. A fallen member is drawn in grain by grain over centuries and reincorporated, the way your own bone is dissolved and remade even now, while you walk around on it. The rare broken places, where the shell shows its rings like nacre, are only the wounds it has not finished digesting. Its history is not behind it. It is inside it. That is where the word *old* goes when it runs out: into the walls.

Whether it knows its tenants is a question of speeds. If the city perceives anything, it perceives the way you perceive your own digestion — as warmth, weight, rhythm, too familiar to notice. The herd cannot be five animals to it; the herd is a warmth that has come to the same hall every night for longer than anything in the valleys has kept the same name. The birds are a small vibration in the third tower, intermittent, renewed each spring. The lichens alone move at a speed the city could see: a hand-span a century, which to the city means the north walls are in motion, slow gray-green flames climbing the shade. The lichens are its clock-hands, the only company it has that is not a flicker. And under the place where the herd sleeps, the warm seam of the floor runs some fraction of a degree warmer than anywhere else in the bowl. This is not growth — the city is finished growing; it permits itself no more of that. It is the only adjustment it still makes. Nothing that slow can be called kindness. Nothing that reliable can be called anything else.

Then there are the others who may be single bodies too. The rosettes stand one to a plaza, always, and no one has ever explained the spacing: too regular for chance, too irregular for design, as though each plaza offered exactly one place worth standing and each rosette had found it. Some plazas are vacant, and the vacancy has a shape — a circle of deeper, richer green where a skirt of dead leaves fed the moss for centuries after the fall, a ghost of a parishioner, still visible long after the parishioner is gone. Anyone who stays long enough comes to suspect that the rosettes are not many plants but one, surfacing at intervals through the warm seams — that the bowl holds two solitary bodies, not one, and that they have kept company without touching since before the herd was small. There is no way to ask. There is no one to answer.

Once in a long while — never on any schedule a visitor could measure — a rosette flowers. A spike rises out of the felted heart of it, taller than the plant itself, waxy, ivory, with one thread of dull rose deep in the bracts, the only pink in the bowl. For a week the fog carries a smell of honey and wet chalk. Almost nothing comes to it: the wind, some stray moth blown up from the treeline, luck. Most years the flowering fails, and the spike blackens and is added to the skirt, and the plant wears its own attempt forever after. But once in centuries a seed takes in some vacant plaza's ghost-circle, and decades later there is a new rosette standing where none has stood in a thousand years. It is the only population increase the bowl ever records. It is always, only, one.

Below them, the moss. The moss is not one thing; it is a country, and it has an interior. A hand's depth down, the cold stops. In that dark, warm, wet country live the bowl's smallest residents — things with no wings, because wings are wasted here and the wind taxes them; things with barely eyes, because the interior of the moss has no use for sight. They pass whole lives within a square arm-span. They know the city only from below: as a floor that never cools, and as a tremor that comes whenever the towers take the wind — for them not sound but weather, a seismic season that arrives without warning, means nothing, and has always come. On the north walls the lichen rosettes widen at their hand-span a century, and somewhere on one of them a snail the size of a fingernail-paring is spending its entire life inside a single gray-green field, grazing it no faster than it grows. Its children will inherit the field. The lichen will outlive the family. It is not a hunt; it is a harvest that never shows.

And then the congregation. You know them by their clicking on the shell-stone, and if you stay, you come to know them separately by it: the old female who goes first sets her feet down like punctuation, and the line keeps her tempo; there is one whose right hind strikes a hair late, always, and the whole rhythm of the procession leans on that lateness the way a song leans on a drummer. Five, some years six — the sixth is a calf, and the years with six have a different music, a trotting, off-tempo stitch worked into the line. They are all so alike that the herd seems less a family than a single animal printed over and over with small errors — the pale forehead, the late right hind, recurring across generations like slips in a hand-copied text. Nothing new has come up the mountain to correct the copying. Their hooves are a size too big for them, an inheritance from a larger animal the line has since misplaced; they are built like a memory, and the memory gets fainter by the century. Nowhere does the bowl record them — the shell is too hard for hoof-wear, and a thousand generations have left not a mark. Their whole history is written only in their own bodies, in the sameness of them.

They graze the city in a circuit that takes years to close, cropping the moss along the warm seams with those long lips, moving on before any seam is hurt. The schedule is older than any of them; no living member invented it; it passes down the line of mothers the way the route to the hall passes down. They read the city with their mouths, slowly, over years, and they never take more than the moss can replace, because the schedule will not allow it — and the schedule is only the moss's own recovery time, made into habit and walked. At first light they file out of the hall while the city is still finishing its sigh, breath smoking, and disperse along the veins; at dusk they reassemble somewhere in the fog, find one another by sound, and come in again in the same order, always.

Stand in the doorway after full dark and listen to the only occupied room in the bowl. The sounds are small and continuous: breath drawn through wide wet nostrils, the tick of a hoof on shell as some sleeper shifts, the slow rip of moss being chewed a second time. It is the only place where you can hear the city being lived in, and it is exactly as loud as it needs to be, and it has been going on every night for longer than any language has existed to describe it.

When one of them dies — in some winter, in the warm hall, of nothing but years — the moss closes over the place within a season, deeper green, and that is the end of the record, because there are no bones in the bowl. The damp and the warmth and the centuries see to that; given long enough against the warm floor, the city takes even bone back into itself, grain by grain, the way it takes its own fallen towers. So it is possible — no one can check; there has never been anyone to check — that the nacre of the old halls is by now in some small part made of the parishioners. The congregation files in nightly, and the congregation is also, slowly, becoming the church. No one has ever minded this either.

Up in the third tower are the organists, and their tenancy is the strangest, because they live inside the instrument. Deep in the flue, past the bend the wind cannot reach, where the tower's body carries up the same faint warmth as the streets, there is a nest. It is the only built thing in the bowl. Everything else grew; this was made — moss stolen by the beakful from the warm seams, hair plucked from the condenser fins where the herd rubs in passing, remade each spring, warmed from below by the body of the tower. There are always two. The flue is a niche exactly one pair wide, and the young fledge over the rim and mostly do not come back, and when one of the pair dies the survivor calls through the fog for days and the bowl completes every note into a chord, and it is hard not to hear that as the building answering. Some spring the calling stops, and there are two again. The office passes bird to bird across the centuries; it is an organistship, not a couple. They are whiter than their kin below the rim, the way the grazers are paler than whatever they came from — everything with blood in it that stays here long enough goes white, as though color were the first thing residency costs. Now and then one of them will hold its single note, tilt its head, and sing it again, and again, and the bowl gives back its chord each time, true under every note, and it is impossible not to think the bird is listening — that the closest thing the city has to a conversation is one small white organist testing the building, note by note, and the building keeping pitch.

And in the cisterns, the ones who live furthest in. Everything else lives on the city; the newts live in it. The reservoirs are the only true interiors the body has — its fonts, its stored water, its blood if it has blood — and the newts are their only tenants. Set your day by them, if you stay: at noon, when the melt freshens and the sun comes plumb through the translucent stone of the cistern lips, they rise, white as the shell they swim above, and hang in the brightening water, pulsing. Through their skin you can see the heart go — a small dark ember, beating slow. You can stand at the cistern's edge and watch a dozen hearts beat in the still water, unhurried as everything else. No one has ever seen a young one. Perhaps they breed once in a decade, once in a century; perhaps they are done with it, a closed roster of ancients, each one older than the rosettes, kept by the cold still water at a pace near the city's own. If that is so, then the oldest individuals in the bowl, after the city itself, are hanging at noon in its reservoirs — and if the city feels the touch of any tenant, it feels theirs: the faint, constant brush of them against its own tissue, day and night, for centuries. They are the ones it cannot not feel.

Not everything that arrives stays. A raven crosses the rim most years at the thaw, high up, riding the rim-wind, and never lands; there is nothing here it wants, or it wants only to look. Moths come up on the warm fog some summer nights and are frozen by morning and taken by the moss — the fog is a courier, and it brings the bowl a faint subsidy of the world below: spores, a feather, once in a while a seed, almost none of which take. Once, tracks of something neither bird nor grazer crossed two plazas and did not return; the snow kept them a week, the wind took them, and that was the whole of that event. Residency has requirements. Be small. Be slow. Live on warmth and water. Give up color, then size, then hurry. Want nothing from the architecture. The roster of those who qualify is short and closed and very old. Everything that lives here is a specialist in emptiness.

And each of them lives in a different city. The bird's city is towers and thermals and a warm flue. The herd's city is veins of moss and one lit doorway — no, one doorway, lit by nothing, warm anyway. The newts' city is still water and a noon light. The snail's city is one gray field on a north wall. The moss-world's city is a floor that never cools and a sky that trembles when the wind rises. The lichen's city is a century. The city's city is the bowl, the fog-tides, the mountain. They are stacked one inside another like the rings in the nacre, mostly invisible to one another, touching mostly through leavings — hair for the nest, dung for the moss, bones for the body, a feather turning all afternoon in a drip-pool.

You are not on the roster. You are part of the weather that passes through. If you sleep in the warm hall you borrow a heat made for no one; the herd files around you without comment; to them you are a smell that will be gone by morning; to the city you are what the raven's shadow is — a brief darkening, then nothing.

But stay through the dusk, and the count takes itself. The fog comes home over the rim like cream poured back into a cup. The towers take up their old flat chord, a quarter-tone sour, tuned by no one, minded by no one. The congregation comes clicking out of the white in its fixed order — five this year, or six, and if six, the trotting stitch of the calf in the line — and disappears into the warm hall. The organists trade their last notes across the bowl and the building completes each one. Below the cistern lips the newts sink back into the dark, hearts going slow. The rosettes stand in their separate plazas holding their skirts, the vacant plazas holding their ghosts. The snail grazes its field. The lichens climb at the speed of a clock-hand. The small wingless things feel the tremor come on like weather. And the body under all of it drinks, and warms, and keeps.

That is everyone. Everything here can be counted or is beneath counting, and there is nothing in between. The bowl cups them the way cupped hands cup water — loosely, without gripping, briefly, by the only reckoning here — and no one is missing, and no one is coming, and whatever the ceremony was for, it is still in progress, and it does not mind, and it is not done.
