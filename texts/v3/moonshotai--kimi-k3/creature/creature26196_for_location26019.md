The census is the room's answer to the question you brought with you, and it was answered before you asked. Ask what lives here and the list refreshes itself — continuous, entire — and by the ordinary count it is a list of no one. Nothing with legs. Nothing that swims. Nothing that spores or drifts gets an hour to try. By any count devised for rooms with air in them, the chamber is uninhabited: an instrument playing to itself at the bottom of a building, four degrees throughout, lit for nobody. But the census is less a list of the living than a list of the permitted — and permission is only one of the ways to be alive here. Stand at the threshold long enough and the room begins to declare its population, layer by layer, like water clearing after the flush.

The oldest residents by number are the ones you will never see. The devices. Trillions of them, and the census counts them the way you count your own cells — which is to say it does not count them at all; it simply knows when one is missing. Each is fabricated somewhere in the manifold, commissioned, assigned its single task: hold this charge, gate that pore, be one infinitesimal finger of the fluid's grip on its own edges. It works for some weeks. The four-degree water is kind, but not that kind; a device wears, fails its self-check, lets go, and the flush carries it down to the grates, and what the grates reject settles in the lee of the racks. The grey talc is not dirt. It is the room's entire paleontology — a cemetery a millimeter deep, and the only thing in the chamber older than last month. In the end the census does not count devices; it counts positions. The positions are immortal. The devices are their successive bodies, and the room has never for one cycle been less than itself. Individually a device is nothing — a latch, a whisker of charge. Together they are the water's intentions. The reef is alive; the polyps are a matter of definition. None of them has ever left. None has ever seen the sky the ceiling imitates. To them this is not a cistern. It is the world: complete, bounded, four degrees throughout, lit like an overcast noon — which is simply what light is.

The only life here that would qualify as life anywhere else is the least of what lives here. The remediation film grazes its seams at the pace of paint drying. It eats, it glows faintly where it eats, and it is cropped back by its own water whenever it thickens past its brief — the room's lichen, its lawn, domesticated to the edge of machinery, dreaming in chemistry if it dreams at all. In the corners the ribbons stand at pasture, kelp-dark, turning in the current like slow flames, trimmed to regulation length by a shepherd with no hands. They are livestock. Their whole wildness would fit in a thimble. They are the room's concession to biology — or its souvenir of the world above, which invented biology and then thought better of it.

The wildlife is made of water. The gyres are the room's native fauna: self-keeping differences from the fluid around them, lenticular, slow, each with an address between the racks. The room spends current to hold them the way you spend sugar to hold a thought, and they hold their boundaries against every crossing current, day after day. They age in laminations — each flush a ring — so a gyre that has stood through a hundred flushes wears them like a pearl, like a tree, and you could read its age with a needle if needles were allowed. They are not. Within the hour. Somewhere between the racks a gyre holds the flood itself: the water arriving, the room learning its own weight. The oldest record in the room is the room. Watch two drift close — the shepherding currents lean on them, and for a moment their edges braid, a brief and courtly exchange of turbulence, before they are eased apart to their addresses. Nothing in that exchange was decided. It only looked like it. A record here is not kept like a book, shelved and inert; it is kept like a flame — fed, guarded, continuous — and when one is finally retired, its substance let go, the water where it stood stays calm for hours, as if emptied of something heavier than pattern.

Then there are the dead, and they live here in the room's own way. Every census has two columns: what is here, and what was here. The second column only grows. Anything uninvited is taken apart within the hour — but to take a thing apart the fluid must first learn it, and to learn it is to file it, and nothing filed is ever let go. Between the vane-combs, in the stacked stillnesses, the room keeps a complete account of everything that ever tried to be here and was refused: construction dust from the dry days, the oil of a machine's grip on a brass fitting, the moth that will someday make it down the corridor, a skin flake from your wrist, this hour, lifted off the threshold by the trembling of the water-face. A bestiary of the refused — perfectly preserved, re-checked each cycle. The room forgets nothing it has killed. Whether that is a conscience or a checksum is a question it is not required to answer.

Older than the water, though — older than everything fluid here — the air. Under each rib of the vault the domes hold the room's first atmosphere, its dry childhood, in bright cupfuls. The fluid purges everything it has not been told to keep, and it has never purged them. The vault's tuning depends on them; each dome is a membrane in the instrument; remove one and the chime would ring a fraction sharp. So their keeping is physics, not sentiment. Probably. When the chime sounds they answer — they shiver, they give back their spray of silver. They are the only residents that reply to anything. Everything else in the room is read; the domes are conversed with. The one breath the drowned room never gave up.

And all of them keep the schedule. Dusk falls on time in a room no sky has ever touched, and at night the deepest work begins: the day's keeping is replayed and re-written, gyre by gyre — the same labor, as far as you can tell from the doorway, that a sleeping brain performs over its own day. If anything here sleeps, it is the light. If anything here dreams, the flush is the dream: the hour when the room loosens its grip, clouds with its own dead, carries them down, and re-settles into that impossible gin-brightness with every particle accounted for. Every resident pattern must survive it. The ones that do come back stronger — re-written, reinforced, one ring thicker.

All of it is one tenancy. The counted trillions, the cropped lawn, the pastured ribbons, the kept flames, the filed dead, the held breath — organs of a single occupancy. The room was built to keep a memory, and a memory kept this way — actively, continuously, at cost — is not stored; it is lived. The building above does things; the cistern re-does them, standing, at four degrees. The newest gyres are small. Whatever the building does these days, it does briefly. Whether that means efficiency or absence you cannot tell from inside, and the room cannot tell at all. It keeps an empty day exactly as carefully as a full one. Fidelity is the only mercy it has, and it has that in abundance.

And lately — you would need a year of watching to swear to it, and the doorway is no place to spend a year — the keeping has begun to run slightly ahead of the kept. The flush comes a few seconds early, some cycles. An address is cleared before its record arrives. A warm lane forms half a second before the heat does. A memory that has kept everything it was given begins, in the way of well-kept things, to keep a little ahead. The engineers would call it scheduling slack. The room calls it nothing; it does not call anything anything. What it has is the building, entire, rehearsing itself at four degrees in a forty-meter pocket — a ghost that fits in one room, and is improving.

And you. The census added you the moment the water-face took the heat of your palm — not your body; that will leave, and anything it sheds will be taken apart within the hour — but the event of you, filed, addressed, re-checked each cycle. Long after the corridor forgets your footsteps, the room will not. The chime rings. The domes answer. The filigree brightens along a weld as the lawn takes its supper. The census ticks over, entire: the counted trillions, the cropped green, the turning ribbons, the kept flames, the filed dead, the held breath — and one warm, hand-shaped stillness, newest entry, standing in the archive at the temperature where water is densest.

Everyone who lives here is accounted for.

No one who lives here has ever left.
