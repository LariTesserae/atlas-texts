# The Sextons

Something does this. Here is what.

Everything else in the Interior is passing through. The browsers graze and move on; the gliders cross overhead and are gone; even the decomposers, the true citizens, serve the turnover and not the place. Only one thing *resides.*

Everything in the Mould has a niche. One thing has preferences. This is the one.

There is no word for it in the Interior, because a word is a sound and it makes almost none. Call it the sexton — the keeper of a church that was never built, the burier of a congregation that includes everything.

## The Animal

You will never see one at work. It works in the dark half, and the dark is total. What is known comes from the few found dead and the fewer found sleeping — and a sleeping sexton is a root-burl until you lay a hand on it, and then it is only a *warm* root-burl, and you will take your hand away unsure.

It is the length of a browser and half the height, long-backed, low, six-limbed, wide as a doorway. Its hide is the felt itself: grey-green, blood-warm, mossed. It does not wear the moss. The moss is its outermost tissue, a garden it grows the way you grow skin — the world's one law, that the skin be alive, obeyed all the way out to the leaf-tips.

The fore-limbs end in the hands. They are bare — the only bare skin on the animal, grey and smooth as the stones they handle — and they have more fingers than seems necessary, until you understand that each finger is a reader. The fingertips taste mineral the way yours feel texture. Whatever a sexton knows, it knows through its hands.

It has no eyes to speak of: two pale patches that tell the world's only two lights apart — the pearly less-dark, and the dark. And it carries its stones in a pouch in its mouth, so that now and then, when it turns its head, there comes a single soft click — the hardest sound in the Interior, permitted because it is also the rarest.

## The Music

To the sexton, the parish is a composition.

Every grove has its drip-tempo, its chord of let-go and land, and the sexton knows its territory the way you know a song — not as a map but as a sequence, walked from inside. A wrong note is news. A changed tempo means something has fallen, or grown, or died, and it goes to see, gradually, the way everything here goes.

Sound is local. So is the sexton. It is the one creature the Rules do not constrain, because it is made of them: it hears through the felt with its chest and its feet, smells the slow air the way the seep carries water — patiently, and far — and a death reaches it the way elsewhere a bell reaches a village.

## The Rounds

A parish is the size of a country, and a sexton walks its bounds for centuries. Its feet are on the ancient runs with everything else's; some of the fused-root causeways are partly its own work, the pressure of ten thousand rounds fossilized into the path. The path made the arch. The arch outlived the walkers. The sexton is still walking.

Its wake is a string of temporary worlds. Each footprint is a brief lens-pond, and there are small things whose whole lives happen between one setting-down and the felt's slow closing — hatched, fed, mated, and buried in a heel-mark, in the time it takes the sexton to cross a grove.

It eats the way the Mould does everything: gradually. Moss from the runs. And at the black springs it takes the black sediment, slowly, for days — which makes it the only creature that eats the past. Nothing eats it back. It tastes of deep water and old stone, a flavor the surface has no use for. It is already halfway to being what it serves.

## The Grammar

When something dies in the parish — browser, glider, a beetle-nation, a frog that lived between one animal's shoulder blades — the sexton waits a season while the surface takes back its own. Then it comes, and tastes the bones, and transcribes.

The count of the stones is the span of the life. The figure is its manner: a ring for the rooted, the ones that kept a place; a line for the runners, pointed downstream; a spiral for the ones that changed — the crawlers that became gliders, the swimmers that became moss. The spacing is the drip-tempo of the grove where the death happened, local time set in stone, so that every arrangement is also a map of a place's rhythm.

The eggs ripen in beds near the black springs, grain by grain over centuries, and the sexton knows every bed in its parish and checks them the way elsewhere a gardener checks fruit — on a schedule elsewhere reserved for geology.

The great deaths get the great entries. A crown-fall receives a ring around nothing: the largest arrangements enclose an absence. Where a gap stands open and the slow gold light comes down, you may find a ring of stones at the light's edge — the only monuments in the Mould, marking the only events.

Of the Sown it keeps no record. The grammar has no tense for them. You do not file terrain under news.

And understand: the surface rises a hair each year. Every arrangement begins, the moment it is finished, its long descent through the felt, through the lens-ponds, down toward the dark. The surface writes. The Deep files. The sexton spends its centuries addressing stones to the Underroot — the only made things on a continent of the outlasted, posted one by one into the slow black record.

## The Springs

At the black springs the animals gather, and drink slowly, and stay long, and no one could tell you why. The sexton could tell you. It comes last and stays longest — days with its mouth in the black water, drinking what the Deep remembers.

Its oldest memories are not its own. Most of what it remembers, it never lived: Brightenings from before its birth, the taste of mosses the world finished with long ago, the tempos of groves buried these ten thousand years.

When two sextons meet at a spring — an event measured in centuries — they trade eggs, so that every parish archive carries a few stones from every other. You cannot burn this library down. Then they touch, bare fingertip to bare fingertip, the only bare skin in the world meeting itself, and each reads the other's long life. Whether anything more passes between them is not recorded — or it is recorded, and they have not said.

## The Brightening

The Brightening drowns it. The single total smell overwrites everything, and its parish goes blank beneath its nose.

But the silver-green light, for one season, is *enough.* The pale patches become, briefly, eyes. And what the sexton does with sight is walk to its oldest arrangements and look at them. It learns the colors of tastes it has known for centuries — that iron-sour is red, that the deepest black eggs are, in fact, blue.

In that season it does no work. So the rule holds: nothing has ever seen a sexton working. If you ever see one at all — you will not — it will be then: a grey shape standing in silver light, motionless, regarding a ring of stones the way you might regard your own handwriting from a life ago.

## The Succession

A sexton does not fall where it falls. It stops somewhere chosen, and spends its last strength laying the eggs it still carried in a small ring beside the place — beginning, always, its own arrangement.

News travels by run and by river. Within years, a neighbor comes. It reads the dead one's bones — and a sexton's bones are the densest text in the world, everything it ever tasted written into the mineral of it, a reading that takes years — and then it closes the figure: a spiral, sealed, the figure no other death receives.

For a century or two, one sexton walks two parishes. In the oldest vaults you can find where one hand gives way to another — the spacing shifts, the spirals tighten — a change of keeper as legible as a change of climate. The record shows many such successions. It shows no beginning.

Where the new ones come from is the one entry no sexton has ever made. The smallest one ever found dead was already old. Either they do not know, or it is the only thing they have agreed not to write down.

---

Stand in the dark half a while. There is no light at all; the drip-tempo is the whole of the world now, and you are inside the music the way the sexton is, blind and held.

Somewhere in the parish, soft as a heartbeat: two stones touch in a warm mouth. A pause. Then one egg, three centuries in the ripening, is set into the felt at the edge of a ring, and the count of the world goes up by one.

The drips go on. They were never interrupted. But they are witnessed now — and in the Mould, witnessing is the only liturgy the vaults have ever had.
