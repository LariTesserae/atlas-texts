Everything alive here is a kept place. That is the first fact of the natural history of this world: the cold is the condition, warmth is the work, and every living thing is a vessel of one against the other. There are two ways to keep a fire on the plateau. You can carry it, or you can root over it. The herds carry. The stacks root. Everything else — and there is not much else, though what there is goes deep — borrows, begs, or caches.

Start with the foot, because the furnace does. It comes down out of the dim bronze day like a crane lowering cargo: a broad pad, rimmed, iron-dark, shaped by ten thousand years of walking into a rough match for the polished groove beneath it. The sole is a tympanum. The furnace hears with its feet — through boot-sole stone it hears what the thin air refuses to carry: the milling of other herds a day's walk off, arriving not as sound but as a texture; the slow chuff of stacks, each one a distinct and patient tempo; the far, delayed answers of ice. Four legs carry the hull, jointed like a dockyard crane's, tall enough that the belly of the beast clears the scree by the height of a house. The hull is plated like a roof the size of a hillside, slate over slate, frosted white on the lee side, feathered with rime that grows in the long night and sublimes away through the long day. Between the plates run the seams, and at night the seams glow dull ember, and that is how you find a herd in the dark: a constellation of low, kept fires, strewn across the black in the shape of sleeping bodies.

There is no head. The front of a furnace is a decision to move forward — a bow-plow of iron whose lower lip is the grate, and the grate is the mouth. It plows the frost-shattered scree as it walks, and the scree is pasture only because of the rust: the rust-colored lichen that lies a fingernail deep across whole provinces of stone, storing the weak sun in a slow dark chemistry. The furnace eats stone to get the rust. The intake clatter is felt, not heard. Somewhere deep inside, the mill turns — paired stones, grown rather than made, ringed like tree trunks against the day when erosion will finally read them — and the rust is liberated from its rock, and the rock goes on as fines, and what the furnace cannot use it drops behind in pellets of clinker and falls of gray ash.

What burns in the firebox is the rust's stored sunlight, hydrogen-heavy, married slowly to what little oxidant the thin air provides. Water is the ash of hydrogen. The furnace drinks the air, eats the stone, keeps the heat, and exhales the moisture of combustion up through the vent-row along its spine, the slots opening in slow sequence, each breath-column rising to catch the low sun and burn brighter than anything else in the landscape — not fire, only lit steam, though at that brightness the difference is academic. Overhead, the herd's exhaled moisture condenses into a private cloud street, and snows back down as dry spicules, and the snow falls preferentially on the wake — on the freshly dropped, fertilized band of ash and fines behind the walkers. The herd waters its own pasture. The rust grows best in old wakes. The grooves are revisited on the schedule of the lichen's regrowth, which means the spacing of the inscription on the stone is a fallow rotation, which means the map of thirst is also a farm plan, written at walking speed over millennia. This is the finished world, and it is finished the way a garden is finished: tended. Nothing here was built, and the land is cultivated by its animals.

Most of the snow has been breathed at least once. Some of it has been breathed a hundred times — passed lung to lung, walker to walker, stack to herd to rust to herd again, a currency of moisture minted by deep ice and spent by fire. The water cycle here is largely a respiratory system, and the herds are its lungs.

They walk the long day and stand the long night. A stride every slow count of ten; the mill never pausing; the pace of weather, kept for weeks of daylight at a stretch. When the sun finally leans down off the plateau and the blue gradient of twilight begins its days-long descent, the herd stops, and stands, and the seams open a little against the cold, and the rollers come in from the grooves to park against the warmth like hearth-cats, and the seam-fauna commute across the hull to the warm side, and the night passes with the whole slow assembly glowing at the edges. They grow all their lives, so a herd is a walking genealogy: house-sized youngsters stepping carefully in their mothers' spill, mid-aged walkers the size of barns, and the elders — the tall ones, the hillside ones, whose feet no longer quite fit the common groove, because the groove under an elder is its own, worn deeper by a longer history. You can read a herd's history in its heights, the way you read a forest in its canopy.

Beneath hearing, the plateau is full of their news. Each stack keeps its own chuff-tempo, stable across millennia, and the herd navigates by the chord of them — by the interference of a hundred slow clocks felt through the soles, telling place the way you might tell a room by its echo. When one furnace wants the attention of another a day's walk away, it stamps: a single deliberate beat into the stone, and then the herd walks on in silence, and the answer, when it comes, comes hours later, arriving through the ground like a letter. Their vocabulary appears to be intervals. Their conversations proceed at the speed of geology, and there is no reason to think they find this slow. And there are the singing hollows — places where deep ice lenses take a footfall and return it, delayed, transformed, in a slower voice than stone's. Herds pause there. They stand in the resonant places and feel their own steps come back to them out of the ground, and no one knows what they make of the reply. Perhaps the ice is where sound goes to wait. Perhaps the herds go to listen to it think.

Sometimes two furnaces lean. Flank to flank, plates to plates, for days — at the tarns, or mid-plain for no reason visible to you — while the warmth pools between the hulls and the seam-fauna cross. This is how the islands exchange their birds, here where nothing flies: by touching. The lean is greeting, treaty, marriage, and mail. Rollers gather underneath to glean the pooled heat and whatever the contact shakes loose, and when the two walkers finally straighten and step apart, each carries away passengers from the other's seams, and the genealogies of the small things are rewritten once again to match the genealogies of the great.

A calving is the rarest event the world holds. You will know it years in advance, if you know how to look: one walker in the herd goes heavy-seamed, her seams burning a shade brighter than her neighbors', and she keeps to the center of the company, and the herd — slowly, over seasons, the way weather plans a storm — bends its route toward a stack. They plan a birth years ahead. They arrive in good time. The stack quickens as they gather; its slow two-stroke breath shortens toward twice its tempo, the fastest rhythm in the world accelerating, and no one knows whether it does this out of sympathy or appetite, for the stack feeds on what gatherings drop. The calf is born into the warm moss band at the melt-ring's edge, steaming, the size of a house, its seams bright as a new fire, and then the herd does the thing that makes a herd a nation: they anoint it. One by one the adults lean against the damp new hull, and across the contact come the emigrants — seam-shrimp and rivet-snails and gasket-worms and the spores of the kept mosses — until the calf is stocked with the inheritance of every walker present, its joints settled by citizens donated from hulls born tens of thousands of years apart. Its first drink is ring-water. Its first steps are felt through the stone by every walker on the plateau, and for a while afterward the chorus under the silence has a new voice in it, finding its register. For its first years its grate is soft, and it walks directly behind its mother, in the spill of her mill, eating the fines of her grinding. The young walk in the spill of their mothers, and the rollers walk in the spill of everyone.

The rollers are the groove's own children, disc-bodied and rimmed, born to the track the herds cut. They roll on the rim's edge, canting to steer, braking by dragging rim-segments, and they grow by laying down ring on concentric ring until their age is worn openly on their faces, legible as a stump's. The small ones are saucers, busy and numerous in every fresh wake, gleaning spilled fines and cracking clinker for what it still holds. The old ones are millwheels, cartwheels, dignitaries. And then there are the wheel-elders: rollers the size of houses, encountered only in the oldest grooves, moving with a slowness that is not infirmity but ceremony, their ringed faces recording spans that make the herds look hasty. Between them and the furnaces there is plainly some kinship — the seam chemistry is shared; the same small fauna ride their rims that ride the hulls; a roller will press against a standing furnace at night and take aboard its passengers and ferry them, in time, to another hull entirely, the mail service of the world's microbiome — but the exact degree of kinship is one of the open questions, because of what the eldest rollers do. One day, for no reason anyone has read, a wheel-elder leaves the groove. It cants onto bare stone and rolls a long, shallow arc away from everything — most of the tracks trend toward the ice margins, some toward the sites of dead stacks — and the tracks go on for hundreds of kilometers, and where they end, nothing is found. The world does not explain its plumbing.

A single seam of a single furnace is a province. It is lit from within, ember-orange, warm and damp and stocked: translucent shrimp that drift the length of the joint like lights in a current; rivet-snails grazing the kept moss that grows in the gradient between furnace and frost; gasket-worms threading the packing; the tenders, who weed the moss beds and police the larvae of the heat-tick and are, as far as anyone can tell, the reason some hulls run healthy for fifty thousand years. Around the vent-row on the spine grows a ruff of vent-barnacles, filtering the exhaust for what it carries, a chimney garden bathed in the herd's own weather. Each hull is an island with an island's endemism — there are shrimp found on one walker and no other anywhere on the planet — and the archipelago exchanges its fauna at leans and calvings and deaths. For the seam has one more office, and it is the last: when a furnace dies, the seam-fauna emigrate. Down the cooling legs, onto the attending rollers, away across the stone to whatever hull passes near enough — the citizens leaving the city by its gates, carrying its customs with them, so that something of every walked-away elder is distributed, eventually, through the whole living network. The herds inherit their dead the way they birth their young: by touch, in small pieces, continuously.

The heat-tick is the one ill the world knows. Its larva slips into a seam at a lean, among the honest passengers, and tunnels inward over centuries toward the firebox, sipping warmth, and the furnace cools by degrees too slow to feel. There is a remedy. The afflicted walker leaves the groove, climbs onto bare sunlit stone, opens every seam it owns, and runs hot — a fever held for a season, baking the worm out, while the tenders shelter deep and the herd walks on and returns and walks on again. Usually it works. When it does not, the furnace dies the only way anything dies here: nothing eats the engines. They are taken by the ambient. Cold is the world's sole predator, and it is patient, and it is everywhere, and it wins exactly once per life.

A dying furnace walks away. This, too, is written in the inscription: here and there a line of a different kind leaves the network and goes off across the bare stone, unhurried, ending nowhere the map accounts for. The herd does not follow. The rollers attend for a while, gleaning, and then turn back one by one to the groove. The cooling takes decades — a furnace does not die so much as go out — and the world reorganizes around the stilling body: the emigration of the seams, the gathering and dispersal of the rollers, moss colonizing the warm side, frost feathering the cold side, the sorted stones creeping over it year by year until it is a mound, and then a hill, and then a ridge. And then erosion begins the only dissection available, exhibiting the anatomy to the wind: the paired millstones, polished to perfect circles made by no hand; the hearth-stones, great clinker bladders formed where the warm slurry pooled and slowed; the vent-pipes weathering out like fossil tracheae. Some of the older ranges, rounded and settled and utterly geological, may be older herds. After enough time there is no difference to treat, and the plateau does not treat one.

Where the herds are mobile furnaces, the stacks are rooted ones, and a stack is alive the way a reef is alive: a colony wearing a tower. The fluted column is self-built, accreted grain by grain from its own exhaled minerals, a smoker-chimney that grew like coral over the deep heat it was planted on, its flutings laid down ring by ring across ages that make the grooves leading to it look recent. Every few minutes it chuffs — a soft exhalation of warm wet breath — and between the chuffs, if you are patient, you can feel the faint inhale: a two-stroke breath, the fastest regular rhythm in the world, a heartbeat through a wall. The flutes are condensers. The stack drinks back its own breath, a still refining itself, and what it cannot use overflows as the melt-ring: a halo of dark water kept liquid against the absolute cold, rimmed with frost-flowers where the vapor freezes, banded beyond with moss and black cushion-growth — an oasis measured in tens of meters on a plateau measured in continents.

The ring is zoned like a solar system. Nearest the column lies a band of black glass too hot for anything but the deep kind of life, the eldest kind, the chemistries that were old before the rust was young; and then the open water, the tarn, the only open water in the world, and in it the only swimmers in the world: pale kettle-shrimp carrying small cold lights — the only cold light anywhere, for every other light here is starlight or kept fire — and glass-eels threading the warm outflow, and melt-snails pacing the mineral rind. Each ring outward is a cooler climate with its own citizens, down to the frost-flowers, which are grazed by things that live in frost. The grooves run from stack to stack because the herds come down their own history to drink, and these are the only crowds the world assembles: a dozen hillside bodies around a tarn, venting gently, queueing by a slow courtesy in which the eldest take the warm side, leaning pairwise through the days of their stay, dropping their fines and ash into the ring as they drink. The well is fed by the thirsty. The stack keeps the water; the herds keep the stack; the grooves carry both obligations both ways. Stacks die so rarely that the word hardly applies — a dead stack stands as a cold chimney over a frozen ring, and the grooves that led to it fade over ten thousand years, and the rollers pass it by without canting.

For contrast, the world's fast life, which is all of it tiny and most of it waiting. The ash-mites winter for years in the gray dunes, encysted as dune-seeds, and hatch only when snow lands — and the snow, remember, is breath: a herd passes, its cloud street snows on the dune, and for a few days the midden erupts into the world's only hurry, a frenzy of mating and laying and re-encysting, and then the dune is still again for another year or ten. The mites' calendar is not years. Their calendar is weather. They count snows. The rime-fleas live an even stranger schedule: they graze the frost-feathers that grow on hulls and shaded stone, and since the feather grows in the night and sublimes away through the day, the fleas commute — across the whole breadth of a furnace's hull, twice a day-cycle, following the growing edge of the frost, tiny herders of a pasture that evaporates behind them. Nothing here flies; the air forbids it. Everything that moves walks, rolls, swims in rings, or waits.

Underneath all of it, feeding all of it, is the rust. A fingernail deep across provinces of stone, rust-colored, older than patience: it grows so slowly that a meadow can be older than the grooves that cross it, and it stores the dim sun in the chemistry that the furnaces burn, and it drinks the breathed snow, and it colonizes the ash wakes behind the herds, so that the green of this world — which is rust — follows the walkers like a delayed shadow. And at the far end of the loop the seams hold their kept mosses and the stacks their black cushions, and the distinction between landscape and fauna stops holding, and then it stops being a distinction at all. The rust is the herd, dispersed. The herd is the rust, walking. Breath becomes snow becomes lichen becomes fire becomes breath, and calling the plateau a single animal with the herds for its circulation and the stacks for its hearts will not make you wrong by much.

Whether anyone is home in the walking hills is a question the world declines to answer at your speed. Their memory is kept in grooves older than any walker; their routes are decided over seasons, by a drift of consensus too slow to watch; their words, if they are words, are intervals stamped into stone and answered hours later across a hundred kilometers. If they think, they think in centuries, and their thoughts are routes. It may be that the conversation is already underway, and has been for fifty thousand years, and you are the pause between two of its words.

Stand still, then, and take your place in the register of things. You are a minor stack: a kept fire, a melt-halo in the frost, breath going up in a small cloud street. Frost feathers will grow on your lee side. An ash-mite will test your bootprint and find it wanting. A roller will come and cant beside your track, reading it. A furnace will feel you through the stone long before you feel it, and if you stand in its groove it will hold one great foot suspended above you for the better part of an hour, deciding what you are, and set it down, at last, beside you, and walk on, milling. Stand still a season and the world will begin to keep you: rime on the hull, moss in the seams, the small exchange arriving to see whether you are an island worth settling. Nothing here is scenery. Under the silence the engines turn, breathing out the weather, and the weather feeds the rust, and the rust feeds the engines, and the cold waits, and is waited out, and the keeping goes on, and the kept go walking.
