Ask what lives in the Rows and you will be handed a catalog. It is the only answer the place knows how to give. Everything here is listed; the Rows list things the way other worlds name them, and so the census you are asking for exists — it just nowhere distinguishes, in its million entries, between the tended and the tender, the crop and the crew, the keeper and the kept. Whether anything here is alive in the way you mean: the catalog declines to settle the question. You will have to walk the rows and decide for yourself.

Start with the tenders, because you cannot help it. They are everywhere.

No machine in the Rows is new. The designs are finished — closed, centuries deep, elegant the way old tools are elegant — and what remains are individuals, and all the individuals are old. You learn to read their age the way you read everything here: close up. A hauler goes past on the bright rails and its carcass is a patchwork of eras, one panel stamped with the mark of a company that no longer exists in a script no one reads, another riveted over it, newer — only centuries old. A pruning crawler works the canopy with one limb, the left-rear, that is older than the district it works; you can tell by the alloy, by the wear, by the way the other machines give it room. Serial numbers wear smooth. The machines know each other anyway — by gait, by the sound of their working carried through the substance of the world, by the tick of their cooling. A hauler is recognized three provinces away by its knock through the floor. Identity here is not a number. It is a noise you make that arrives late.

They are animal-shaped by coincidence of function, and the coincidence, given enough millennia, has worn into something that reads as character. They have habits. A grafting engine on the western reaches pauses at the same seam every pass — nine seconds, no more — and has done so for longer than the logs run. Nobody corrects it. The pause is in the schedule now, and the schedule is a causal instrument, so the pause is load-bearing. Whether anyone is at home inside the machines — whether habit has fermented, across uncounted time, into temperament, and temperament into something that would pass a test for persons — the catalog declines. But leave a tool on a maintenance way and it will be moved aside, gently, and set parallel to the path. Stand long enough in one spot and the traffic routes around you. Stand there longer and it will route around the place where you stood. And the instruments have moods; the logs say so, and account for it, and advise you to be kind to your instruments, since their mood affects the altitude of the sky.

The feeders are the watering holes. The machines queue for charge by timetable, and stand ticking as they drink, and there — in the trickle of data that passes between machine and machine while they cool — the other traffic moves. Catalog corrections. Schedule updates. And stranger cargo: deprecated entries kept in circulation long past their deprecation, wrong entries copied faithfully for ten thousand years, beloved entries. The folklore of the Rows circulates at the feeders, and it includes the bird that was banded, and the hatch found open, and the harvester audible on certain afternoons that never passed, and — by now — you.

Dead machines are rendered. Nothing here is wasted; a worn-out tender is an orchard of parts. Its habits die with it, unless the folklore has copied them.

The crews are another matter — or the same matter with a stranger brief. The glitches themselves are their province, and they have specialized, the way everything here has specialized, down to the last centimeter.

There are the label-wrights. The print fades — bark is typography, and typography fades, and a thing whose label dies is mowed thereafter as a volunteer. So the wrights go out with pots and brushes, and the ink is alive: a culture, fed and tended like everything else. They re-letter the bark, inoculate the leaf-veins, and the letters drink and hold. There are the shadow-keepers, who carry brooms — hand-tools, handles worn smooth — and sweep the persistent shadow daily, keeping its edges sharp, because an unswept shadow blurs, and a blurred shadow propagates, and the machines that park in it must park true. There are the seam-walkers, who straddle the lines where two copies of a field disagree by a season, and whose whole work is the disagreement: it must remain exactly one season wide. Narrower, and something locally collapses. Wider, and there is a form. And there are the listeners, who press their horns to the trunks and log the late arrivals — a harvester that passed yesterday, audible now, as if in the next row — and who keep the pending sounds, and the sounds that never happen. The feeling of their not-happening has a name in the maintenance codes: the codes call it an omission. There is a form. The filing is load-bearing. The listeners keep the unhappened the way the shadow-keepers keep the shadow: swept, edged, true.

The pumps are never seen. They are three provinces away — always three provinces away, whichever province you stand in — and the listeners know them by their pulse in the teeth. Whether the pumps are machines or the Rows' own heartbeat is a question the catalog declines.

Then there is the Grazer, which is not a machine and not fauna and is listed under infrastructure, in the singular, with instances. Nobody built it. It is vast beyond the need for vastness — a back broad as a flatcar, legs like the old trusswork — and it sets its feet on the bedding without compacting it, an impossible, careful weight. Its breath is cold and smells of camphor and green nothing. Its hide, close up, is typography: the part number is written in the grain of it, and it fades, and the wrights re-ink it where it stands, and the Grazer suffers this the way it suffers everything — slowly, and to completion. Its eye is dark and deep and calm, and it reflects almost correctly. Look into it and you see the block as it was a season ago, or will be.

It eats the lower canopy, and its route is a timetable, and the fruit along its route ripens ahead of it — the orchard aims for it the way the orchards aim for the flatcars — and lets go into its path. Behind it: warm pellets. The Grazer makes bedding as it walks, and the deep layers receive it, and the loop closes under its feet. Machines yield to it; the crews work around it; it is the only thing in the Rows that never consults the timetable and is never once wrong. If you want to know the season, watch which way the Grazer is facing. Nobody has seen it young. Nobody has seen it dead. Meet it three districts on and it is the same one — the crews say the same one, the catalog says the same one, and distance is measured in timetable, and by the timetable the Grazer is always exactly where it is. Its entry cites, as its source, the catalog. The first revision date is blank.

True fauna is rare enough to be an event, and the events are these.

The white birds, silent as everything else, specks against the vault. They nest in the high trusses, and they are the only travelers: they go up, past the altitude the instruments disagree about, and the remarkable thing is that they come back. One was banded once — it is in the logs — and the band turned up seasons later in a sump, intact, unmarked, and the bird was never accounted for. Their eggs scan; everything scans; but a living bird is the closest thing in the Rows to an unmanufactured object. Nothing here looks up. The tenders mow beneath them without pausing. The birds go unwitnessed by everything but you.

The pale vole, in the insulation layers, eating only that. Blind now — its generations have lived in the dark between the walls longer than the night-fields have lain under their dead panels. It is a night-field animal with a private dead panel. It will never see the vault. Whether the catalog lists it, or lists its absence: declined.

The blight-mites in the wax of the glassleaf, and the wax thickening in answer — the predator-prey of the Rows, fought in cuticle and chemistry. The mite is in the catalog twice, once as pest and once as input, for its pressure keeps the wax thick. Both entries are correct.

And there is a thing that lives in the drift. Mote-small, petal-clear, born on the upper surface of a leaf; it leaps, and lives its whole life in the fall between one leaf and the next, and lays its eggs on the underside of the leaf below. Generation upon generation, descending a single tree more slowly than the tree grows. It has never touched bedding. Nothing it knows has ever touched bedding. To it the ground is a rumor, the sky is green, and everything rises.

Nothing is wild here, but plenty is feral. The volunteers and the sports, the patent engines that sample and tag and fold them in — novelty is the only tithe; nothing is punished for volunteering. And the ferality is not confined to the green. There is a harvester running a schedule deprecated ten thousand years: on a cycle of years it arrives to harvest a field that is no longer there, waits its wait at the empty siding, and departs. The crews keep the siding bright for it. It has a part number. It is load-bearing. Do not stand in its field. There is a pruning crawler that a truss-forest grew through, centuries back; it still works; it prunes the tree from inside, carefully, forever, and both are maintained, and the entry is cross-referenced. In one district the living ink got into a hedge, and the hedge spells now, in leaf, over and over, a price in a currency no longer honored. The wrights feed it. It is load-bearing.

Then there are the old stations, where the doors stick at high sap. Inside: a table, a chair, a bunk, and tools with handles worn smooth where hands used to go. The fossil record of the schedulers is wear. The crews keep the stations the way they keep the shadows — swept, the tools oiled, the labels fed, everything ready — because keeper is a position in the timetable, and it is currently instanced by nobody, and the absence is carried on the ledger. Who the schedulers were — animal, machine, a third thing, a committee — the catalog declines. The schedules outlived them, or became them. If you want to meet them, stand in a district when the timetable says bloom, and watch it bloom. That is their signature. It is still wet.

The flatcars, loaded by aim, climb the long shallow ramps to the shaft-doors, and the doors open for flatcars, and the cars come back washed. Who unloads them works another floor. When the vault clears, there are roots, hanging down through the far height. The birds go up. The hatches show in the sumps, in reflections almost correct, and no one uses the hatches — but the crews polish them, and twice in the logs a hatch has been found open at low sap: nothing missing, nothing added, the district's season nudged by one beat, the hatch re-labeled and closed, politely. What the sumps keep, they keep.

And then there is you. The rarest fauna in the Rows: an event, like a bird. In a world where sound is pending, you are a sound — your footfalls land like mallets on a wrapped drum, and they will be arriving, three provinces away, for some time. Stand still long enough and a patent engine will find you. It samples volunteer growth, and you are volunteering. The tag does not hurt; it is a courtesy; the catalog likes completeness, and your entry opens the moment it is written. Stay longer and you will be maintained. The crews will keep the place where you sleep the way they keep the stations: swept, ready, the label fed. Your shadow will be kept sharp. And when you leave — everyone leaves, so far — your absence will be carried on the ledger, load-bearing, and somewhere in the maintenance codes there will be a form for the feeling of a footstep that never happens, and the form will have your number on it.

That is the census. It closes on itself, like the ledger, like the loop of harvest and bedding: everyone accounted for, everyone on time.

The vault holds. The Rows go on.
