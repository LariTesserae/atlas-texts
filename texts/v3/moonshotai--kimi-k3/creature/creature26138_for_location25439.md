**The Tenantry**

Start with the ones you would mistake for damage. The ochre cascades on the bulkhead are not decay; they are livestock. Iron bacteria — the oldest tenants, here since the brick was new — draw dissolved iron out of the penstock water and lay it down as hollow tubes and draperies, drip by slow drip, building the rusticles the way coral builds a reef: blindly, communally, on a schedule of decades. They need no light. They eat the district's buried iron, leached from a hundred miles of main, and concentrate it here in rust that grows instead of flaking. Call them corrosion and you are not wrong; call them a crop and you are not wrong either. In the chamber those two words have fused like the rope fender and its ring.

Where there is light there is green, but only just. Each submerged light-point on the walls wears its fuzzy halo of algae, a small circular garden photosynthesizing by sodium glow at a perpetual dim noon, because the lamps never go out. And on the silt bar, exactly where the daylight column spends its wandering arc at low water, the single saucer of moss holds its allotment of sun — the one true plant, arrived as a spore down the vent shaft sometime in the last century, drowned twice a day, uncovered twice, neither spreading nor failing, because there is nowhere else for it and this is enough. At high water the column falls on open water, and the allotment passes to the chub. The chamber shares out its light the way it shares out everything: one portion, by the clock.

Everything else here is an animal, and every animal runs on mains time and on what the mains bring. The twice-daily tide delivers the freight: leaf-shred, silt, the fine organic soup of the streets. To this add the tithe from above — the feral pigeons roosting in the throat of the vent and in the dead station beyond it, known down here only by their weather: the faint ammonia, a down-feather turning in the daylight column, the rare soft fallout of a dead bird. All of it accepted, all of it incorporated. Nothing is refused. The chamber is as near to a sealed world as a plumbed room can be; what enters mostly stays, sorted into the graded fans or into bodies.

The tideline itself is alive. Between the two waterlines the walls are banded with mussels, and the band is a histogram you can read: densest at the levels the water visits most, thinning toward the extremes of storm and drought — a century of the chamber's habits written in shell. During the roar they shut and wait it out; when the valve closes they open and begin the great clarification. Within hours the brown-gray murk is glass and the drowned walkway rails show going down into the dark. That clarity is not the water's nature; it is the bed's work, continuous, unpaid. Spent shells fall and drift pale on the floor below, decades of emptied shells, an archive no one reads. No mussel remembers anything. The bed remembers everything.

The floor is worked by the small. Water lice and amphipods glean the gravel fans; in the fine gray flour at the still end the silt is threaded with red — bloodworms, tubificids waving in the near-still water — swallowing the city's leavings and sorting them finer. And the silt manufactures the air: midge larvae through the dark months, then the hatch, evenings when the whole surface dimples with emergences and the space above the water fills briefly with wings. Every hunter in the chamber keeps that calendar.

The bulkhead is tenanted. Signal crayfish hold the bolt-holes, one to a hole, spaced like orchard trees — a geometry settled long ago by night fights no one saw and maintained since by custom. When lamplight finds one at its threshold you can see the pale signal flash at the claw joint. They work the murk hour at inflow, gleaning the tide's delivery; they crush young mussels off the band's lower edge; their click sits in the treble of the chamber's sound. Once in a span of years a hole falls vacant — an eel takes a molting crayfish in its soft hour — and the hole stays empty a season, and then one night it is not; a smaller tenant has moved up the row. The tenantry is continuous the way the bed is continuous: no individual outlasts it, nothing replaces it.

The deep middle belongs to the eels — three or four of them; the number wanders across the decades. They came as elvers up through the works, in the years when the system still admitted such travelers, and grew thick as a forearm on crayfish and hatch and fallout. They are decades old, older than the institution whose life ring hangs on the gantry, and they feed in the opaque hour when nothing can see them, rising off the drowned fence line of the walkway rails into the churn. They will never silver; the Sargasso is a rumor their bodies carry and cannot act on. Except that at storm high water, once in a great while, one goes over the bypass sill into the next vault, toward the pumps' endless argument, and the census falls by one, and the logbook, which records levels only, records nothing.

And then there is the chub. One fish, enormously old. It came through the screens as a fingerling sometime in the last century and grew, and kept growing, past the gauge of every way out: the screens that let it in would never pass it now; the sill it might once have cleared at storm water it has never attempted, or attempted once, long ago, and declined. Now it is a gray log of a fish, broad across the back, scales the size of thumbnails, holding station under the vent shaft where the daylight falls — the one resident aligned with the chamber's only clock. It eats the hatch; it eats drifting spiderlings and the spiders that fall; sometimes it rises and sips the surface with a slow purse of lips, and the vault returns the sound a half-second late, the way it returns everything. Its age is a matter of quiet arithmetic among the inspectors, who look for it first, every visit, the way you check a gauge. It reads true. The punt's shadow passes over it twice a year and it does not stir. It has outlasted the men who come to look for it, and their replacements; it will outlast these. It is the archive that swims.

In the air, the lamps are fished. One orb-weaver per cage — cave spiders, dark-bodied and long-legged, *Meta menardi* if you want the name — webs strung inside the cages where the sodium glow draws the hatch all night, every night: a perpetual fishery, the only one in the chamber that never closes. Their egg sacs hang beneath the vault on single threads, pale teardrops; the spiderlings depart on silk, and most of them dimple the water and are taken — the chub's sip, an eel's rise — and a few reach a lamp where a tenancy has fallen vacant, and begin. Succession among the lamps is as orderly as succession among the bolt-holes. The spiders are fixtures, as much as the cages; take the cages away and they would rebuild the geometry from hunger.

The bat comes down like a thought of the upper dark. Most evenings: the falling spiral through the vent, then the quartering — low passes the length of the chamber, feet and tail membrane brushing the surface, stitching lines of ripple the vault reads back. Its sonar holds the whole room at once: columns, chains, the hanging punt, the rise of a fish. The chamber is never dark to the bat; it is fully seen, in echoes, every evening of its life. It roosts above, in the dead station among the pigeon ledges, and commutes. And it is a succession like the rest — one bat across a decade, then another. The chamber has always had its bat the way it has always had its level.

The heron is the rarest thing that happens here. Some years, at low water: a gray bird folded into the drop of the vent, committing to it, then unfolding in the gloom to stand in the daylight column on the silt bar beside the moss, gray on gray, one-footed, patient past reason. It picks at amphipod-rich silt; it takes a young crayfish when fortune serves; sometimes it takes nothing and stands anyway. When the water stands up and walks, it is gone, or waiting on the gantry rail for the tide to go back down. Whether it is one heron returning across a lifetime or the idea of a heron that the chamber admits at intervals, no one can say; the logbook records levels. The chub and the heron ignore each other. Each is too large for the other's account.

And then there is the slowest animal of all. Twice a year the pressure door is dogged open — never ajar; the rule is older than its reason — and the technician comes down, lowers the punt, rows the length, reads the brass gauge, signs the book. Nine hands since 1911: a species with a thirty-year generation, a migrant of perfect regularity, arriving always to the same reception — crayfish in their holes, spiders gone still, the chub beneath the shadow of the hull, watching or not. The humans believe they stand outside the system and inspect it. In the chamber's accounting they are one of its rhythms, a tide with a six-month period, a weather that says *no defects*. And the reading is true, but not because the gauge is brass: because the chub is at its station and the band is dense and the rusticles are dripping and the hatch will come. *No defects* is, without anyone knowing it, a field note on a living place. Upstairs the pen scratches the level onto a chart for an audience of dust; down here the same fact is written in shell and rust and the length of a fish.

Count them, then, and the census is short — one moss, one bat, a spider per lamp, a fish per column of light, eels on a wandering count, mussels beyond counting, a heron at intervals: a ship's complement, an ark riding a flood that never ends. But the count keeps collapsing inward. The bed remembers what no mussel knows; the tenantry outlives every tenant; the bat is a position more than an animal. Everything here is either the chamber or kept by it, and the line between those two has been dissolving since the brick was new, like everything sharp, and is nearly gone. The chub is the thought the chamber has been thinking for the better part of a century — gray, slow, held exactly where its one clock touches the water.

On winter evenings, at high water, with a foot of black air under the crown and the lamps doubled in the ceiling of the water: the mussels siphon in the dark, an eel turns once in the deep middle, the spiders wait at their lit cages, and under the dark vent the chub rises, unhurried, and sips — and the vault gives the sound back a half-second late, the room answering its oldest resident.

The record is entered. There are no defects.
