## The Census of the Basin

Count what lives in the Singing Basin and you will get the number wrong, because the basin does not count the way you do. It counts in voices. By that reckoning the city built for twenty thousand is full past its building; by yours it holds almost no one. Both countings are true, and the distance between them is the whole of what lives here.

Begin with the easy ones. Begin with the people.

---

Twenty keepers, give or take the year, and a handful of children — four, at the last counting. The number holds steady the way the pitch holds steady: not by accident, but by repair.

They are deaf, all the adults, by degrees and then entirely. They call the losing of it *the deepening*, and it is treated the way other peoples treat the loss of milk teeth — as proof of growth, not of damage. The wax-and-fiber plugs are children's gear. A child working near the rim wears them; a young keeper who sets them aside and goes down the stair to the terraces has begun, deliberately, the long graduation. No one is rushed. Everyone, in the end, goes down. *Hearing is a childhood thing*, they sign, and they say it the way you would say that crawling is.

Which makes the children the only ears in the loudest place in the world — and the city uses them, gently, knowingly, the way you would use any instrument you are borrowing. Each dusk, before the moths rise, a child stands at the rim and gives the report: not how the chord feels — the elders feel it better than the child ever will — but how it *sounds*. The high material. The Shimmer's hiss. The thin overtones that bone and foot and fork cannot reach. *Today there is a splinter in it. Tower row nine is thick. The garden is slow on the west side.* The elders drum questions through the floor; the child answers in sign; discrepancies are argued in struck stone. Then the child is sent to bed in the felt, having spent one more evening of a faculty that will not last, and nobody mourns, because everybody remembers.

Ask any keeper for their last sound. They will sign it without pausing — it is the local genre of reminiscence, the way other peoples tell first meetings or dreams. An old woman on the fourth terrace: her sister saying her name, in a felt room, under the roar, the year the moths came early. She has carried it forty years. It has not faded; if anything it has grown more precise, the way a song improves in the keeping.

And hearing returns to them in sleep. For years after the deepening, a keeper's dreams are full of sound — so the mornings have their second custom, the signing of dreams, and the whole archive of the basin's music lives in the skulls of people who cannot reach it awake. The city is deaf in its twenty waking bodies. It hears itself only in its dreams, and lends itself the memories at breakfast, in sign, like weather reports from a country everyone emigrated from.

---

Their bodies have been re-made by the place: small, dry, hard, barefoot always — below the rim no one wears soles, because the floor is the medium and a boot is a stopped ear. Each carries the bone tuning-fork on a cord, and taps it to the teeth a hundred times a day, and hums. Every keeper hums — not song, nothing so organized — a pilot light, a private note held against the chord so its holder can feel where they stand inside it. A keeper who stops humming is watched the way you would watch a friend who has stopped eating.

Their language has three registers: sign for daylight and line-of-sight, touch-sign for the dark and the dust-blow and the gloved hand, and floor-drum for distance — the whole city is a telegraph, any wall or stair carries a struck pattern, and a keeper alone on a far terrace is never out of reach, because reach here is measured through stone. Names are rhythms. Two long, three short, a pause. To say a person, you drum them. The sign for the city itself is two cupped hands, trembling: a bowl that shakes.

The work rotates: field-keepers with their wooden caps, irrigating by sluiced sound; cistern-wardens metering the stored afternoon out through the night, keeping ledgers in knotted cord; mirror-keepers firing the kilns with focused glare. One keeper walks out. The far-listener carries a drum and a bone-headed staff a full day into the Hush, strikes the salt, and reads what comes back — the fence's reach, the hunters' shallows. It is the loneliest work in the world, and out there she sleeps with a rattling seed-pod strapped to her sternum, because no keeper can sleep in true silence; the body keeps waiting for the tremor the way a rocked child waits for the rocking. She comes home with a hunger for the chord like a diver's hunger for air, and drums her report at the rim for an hour.

No one here drinks water. There is none to drink. The keepers drink the garden: stalk-milk pressed from the bronze blades, thin and faintly sweet, the same oil the moths cross a hundred miles of salt to reach, cut with the little moisture the plant hoards in its heart. Seed-cake from the rattling pods. Moth-oil in season. Salt without end. A keeper's body runs on the chord at two removes — the towers feed the leaves, the leaves feed the keepers — and they say it with the flat pride of farmers anywhere: *The garden keeps us.* Sometimes one will sign the heresy, only half in jest: that it kept them first, that the city built itself a staff.

At the bottom of the Throat, where the lake made its last stand, one seep still weeps — a cup of brine a day, no more. The mirrors take the salt out of it over a slow fire, and it is rationed to the sick, the pregnant, and the very young, measured in mouthfuls. The keepers handle it with a visible awkwardness, and that awkwardness tells you the jar in the shrine is not the only relic. Just the only sealed one.

When a keeper dies, the mirrors do one more job. The ash is folded into the clay of a repair, and the dead go up into the towers, and are tuned. When a keeper re-cuts a worn flue she signs the name-rhythms of the ones whose ash is in the clay into the wet stone with her fork, so that repair is also remembrance, and a tower row is a genealogy. Ask how the chord stays so even with only twenty pairs of living hands, and you will get the flattest answer in the basin: *The dead do the loudest work.*

---

The pilgrim moths are the garden's emigrant leaves — hand-sized, gold-dusted, deaf as their keepers, reading the Drone as texture against their bodies across a hundred miles of featureless white. Somewhere out in the pans are lesser notes: failed basins, single vents, one-tone flutes of bare stone. The moths island-hop from note to note the way sailors hop harbors, and the basin is their capital — the loudest island in the world.

In season they arrive at dusk, and for those nights the Shimmer has wings: the whole garden seems to lift two feet off the terraces and hover. They work the horn-flowers for the oil, and they pay for it. Their wing-scale is the wax of the children's earplugs — the basin's youngest literally wear the moths in their ears — and their dead are gathered at dawn and rendered, the wing-parchment peeled away for drumheads and brushes. The wing-hiss sits at the very top of hearing, and for many children it is among the last sounds ever filed away before the deepening. The keepers' signs for *moth* and for *remember* differ by a single motion of the hand. This is not a coincidence, and no one will explain it to you. You are expected to understand.

---

Exactly one animal in the basin makes no sound at all.

The salt-mole is eyeless, white-furred, long as a forearm, and it surfs the chord beneath the dust of the terraces the way a fish rides current — moving in the music's groove, silent, eating what the moths leave. The keepers feel it as a comma moving through the floor's long sentence, and they know them individually, by gait: the mole under the third terrace digs in threes; the one below the granary works in fives. Nine, at the last counting, some of them decades at their rows.

If a keeper finds a mole lying *on top* of the dust, stunned, like a fish thrown from water, she does not stop to bury it. She goes and finds the fault in the chord, because there is one, nearby, always. *A beached mole is a cracked note.* When one finally beaches for good it is given to the garden — the tower takes the keeper, the soil takes the mole — and the keepers tell the children that the mole is the hunters' small cousin: the same organ, the opposite doctrine. It is what the pale ones would be, if anything out there had ever agreed to be fed instead of to feed.

---

But the true census of the basin is botanical. Ten thousand listening leaves, every one a citizen — the city built for multitudes is full, if you count the way the basin counts. Walk the terraces at noon and you can see the lean: whole rows tilted toward the towers like crops toward a sun, fields bending toward the Throat. At dusk, when the chord hands itself down the scale, the garden settles — audibly, a long soft applause sitting down. A field-keeper reads her rows the way a shepherd reads a flock, by tension and by gait: a leaf gone slack is hunger, a leaf gone sharp is sickness, and every plant has a voice a shade off its neighbor's, which the keeper of that row knows the way you know faces. Even the dead keep working. The sound-canals are the garden's elders, retired into plumbing, and they still sing faintly when the chord is poured through them — the sound of the field remembering itself.

---

Then there are the residents who are not alive, and who are loudest of all.

Everyone who enters owes the city a voice — but the toll-keeper at the intake horn does not treat it as a fee. She sets her fork to her teeth, reads your note through her skull, and signs what the chord needs: *a third lower, hold it, again.* You are not paying admission. You are donating a part the instrument is missing. Erosion tunes most of the city, but some partials only a human voice can lay in, and the towers keep yours a week after you've gone — or, for the rare voice that lands exactly on a gap, a year, felt by the keepers the way you feel a fire in the next room, and welcomed.

At any given hour the towers hold perhaps a hundred voices of the gone, thinning by the day. The keepers say that when the builders left, they left by districts, and each district sang itself out at the horn — twenty thousand voices paid in a single generation, the Great Payment — and that the chord the wind has tuned ever since is tuned toward the memory of that massed one. This may be true. Erosion keeps the pitch. Something keeps the direction.

And the Standing Chord itself: it eats wind, it grows with the heat, it has moods the keepers read like weather, like a face, and it dies if it is not fed. Whether it is alive is a question they decline to sign. But it is the eldest thing in the basin, and everything else — leaf, moth, mole, keeper — lives in its weather the way fish lived in the lake's.

---

And then there are the ones who do not live here, and keep arriving.

Out in the deep salt, the hunters read the world through the same organ as the mole and the moth, and silence is how they know a place has died. The keepers know the story of the Quiet Town the way you know the story of the flood: a smaller basin, a failed song, and when travelers reached it the towers stood and the cisterns were full and the fields were tuned and nothing was broken and nothing was taken — only everything was gone, and the salt beneath the town was churned like a plowed field.

No keeper has seen one whole. They are known by subtraction: a direction in which the Drone goes woolen, a damped return on the far-listener's drum, a weight standing in the salt the way a figure stands in a doorway. On the still nights, when twenty voices hold the fence up from inside the Throat, the children are kept from the rim and are not told why, and feel it anyway through the ground — a ring of patience around the basin, waiting for a lapse that has never come. The keepers, who extend citizenship so widely — to leaves, to voices, to the dead — do not extend it there. Their sign for the pale ones is the sign for hunger.

---

And finally, the one resident that does not sound.

In the felt shrine at the rim, on its stone plinth, the jar of last water sits in the one protected silence in the loudest place in the world. Its warden is chosen for quietness of temperament, and the others revere her and tease her in the same breath: *keeper of the one thing in the city that does not sing.* She changes the felt by season and dusts the clay with a brush of moth-wing parchment, and presses her palm to the cool curve, and feels nothing, which is the point.

Everything else in the basin lives by translation — water into sound, hearing into touch, voice into chord, dead into clay, lake into song. The water in the jar refused. It stayed itself for ten thousand years, the last of the untranslated, the only thing in the basin that remembers the world before the music. It does not vibrate. In a city where to live is to tremble, the holiest thing is exempt.

So: the census, in one breath, the way the keepers will give it to you at the rim before you go. Twenty keepers. Four children. Moths in season, uncounted and uncountable. Nine moles, each known by its gait. Ten thousand listening leaves, every one a citizen. A hundred voices of the gone, thinning by the hour. One silence, sealed in clay, remembered. And the ears — four sets, at the rim, on loan from childhood, holding the whole roaring city at once and spending it gladly: the only hearing in the loudest place in the world.
