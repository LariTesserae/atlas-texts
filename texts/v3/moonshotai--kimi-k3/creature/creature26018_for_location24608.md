Almost no one. It is possible to cross the whole plain and swear, honestly, that it is empty — possible to walk for days and see nothing built, nothing kept, no smoke, no path — and every traveler who ever swore it was watched while they swore. The people are there. They are few, and they are very old at not being seen, and what they leave behind amounts to almost nothing: a footprint with splayed toes in the laterite, gone with the next milk-water — the sole itself gone half to laterite, the plain's own rule worked into flesh: whatever is exposed, petrifies — a smell of smoke with no fire anywhere above ground; a village that exists in one season and not the next; and sometimes, if you are standing in the right black water at the right hour, a sound like two stones knocked together, arriving through the shinbones rather than the ears. That is the people, saying — as nearly as it can be translated — *we know you are there.* The grammar of the knocking is mostly locations and confirmations. It is a language for a country where nothing said comes back, so nothing needs saying twice.

The villages stand on the backs of the dead rivers. That is not poetry; it is drainage. The gravel ridges are the only ground that keeps its head above the flood, so the people string their houses along the crests like beads on the plain's fossil spine, and the roads between settlements are the old channels themselves, walked dry-shod twenty million years after the water left them. It would be hard to make a plainer statement of how they live: they travel on rivers that no longer move, in a country where the living water goes everywhere too slowly to carry anything. The houses are frames of branch cut from the wide-crowned eccentrics, walled and roofed in woven sedge, and every one is built to be unbuilt — nothing in a village is meant to outlast the season, let alone the builder. Permanence is a vanity, they say, and vanity is a way of drowning. When the water rises, a house comes apart in an afternoon; the mats ride the sheet-flow to the next ridge; and for the months of standing water each ridge is an island and each island a small nation, and the people pole between them on rafts, visiting, trading the only currencies the plain recognizes — salt, news, and fire.

They keep time the way the plain does: one beat a year, and everything else commentary. The flood pulse is the calendar; there is no other. Children are named for the water's mood at their birth — Rising, Standing, Falling, Waiting — so that every name is a small fossil of a season, and to be introduced to anyone is to be handed the weather of decades ago. Age is counted in floods; sixty floods is very old; nobody counts higher than the water does. Speech, too, has a hydrology. The soaked air swallows sound — every child learns it young, shouting across a pan and hearing the shout simply finished — so the people talk in the bird-hour, when announcement is licensed, go silent at midday when the insects take the world apart with their saws, and at dusk they do not sing, because the dusk is already sung: it would be like talking over rain. For distance they use the water itself. Sound dies in the wet air but travels in the black water like rumor through a crowd, and every child learns the knock-alphabet before learning to wade without ripples — which is the second thing every child learns. The first is which logs blink.

Dawn is for reading. The mist lies down in the hollows and spells the night's drainage in white ribbons, and the elders too stiff to wade far are carried up to the ridge-crest at first light to read the morning like a letter the night wrote them: where the rain fell, where the water means to go, which pans will shrink and which will hold. After the third hour they trust nothing they see — the horizon boils, the far pans float, the whale-back rides on nothing — and no journey of consequence begins at noon. They steer by the mounds. The masons align every blade and spire north to south, and whether or not something is being pointed at, the people take the pointing as a gift and walk the compass of the drowned city, by stars at night and by architecture all day. It is rude, they say, to ask a gift what it means.

The plain feeds them the way it feeds everything: thin in leaf, thick in flesh. Fish that breathe air are fish that can be walked down — in the shrinking pans the gulping and rolling gives them away, and a person who has learned the second thing takes them with a spear, or in the last mud with the hands. Turtles are paid off the snags a few at a time, never the whole stack; the stack is the bank, and only fools rob banks. Sedge rhizomes, lily roots, an egg now and again, with apologies. And clay. At the kaolin exposures, at dusk, the people come and eat the ground itself alongside the parrots and the grazers — a mouthful or two, bone-white, squeaking — the one mineral wealth, paid out to anyone who shows up, the only table the plain sets. They come dusted white with the clay, too, against the sun and the insects and, they say, so the ground will recognize them, and at dusk, among the red grazers and the shrieking birds, the lick gathers the plain's whole palette into one place: rust, bone, and the black water standing by. The lick is the parliament, the market, the reunion, the church. In the dry months the scattered ridge-nations converge there, marriages are made, the news of a hundred islands is poured into one place, and around the eaters the parrots wheel in their hundreds and the grazers stand like boulders that have decided, for the evening, to be citizens. There is one law, unwritten — written nowhere the water could reach anyway — and never broken: no blood at the lick. Even the great reptiles drifting through the dusk observe it, or the people say they observe it, which in a country this patient comes to the same thing.

They dig lungfish in the dry season, and it is the closest thing they have to scripture — scripture you can eat, which they consider the only honest kind. A child old enough to be taught patience is walked out to the cracked pans and shown the clay envelopes, each with a fish asleep inside around its own small water, waiting for a year that hasn't happened yet. The people knock before digging — etiquette, they are careful to say, not superstition, though the explanation takes hours and ends, as most of their explanations do, in etiquette — and they take only from pans where the envelopes are many, and never the last one, because the last one is the proof. Proof of what? a visitor asks. That waiting works, they say, surprised at the question. The very old mostly do not eat lungfish anymore. They say they no longer need to.

What the plain does not provide is salt, and this is the one wound in the arrangement. Everything soluble left long ago, and salt left first, and the people live in a low, permanent hunger for the single taste the country will not pay out, and it has made them ceremonial. Salt is counted in grains. A lifetime's allowance might fill a small fist. A grain on the tongue at the rising of the water; a grain for the bride-price and the blood-price and the settling of the long wrongs; a grain each at funerals, which are the only true feasts. Every few floods, when the stores decide, a salt-walk goes out — a dozen of the young and strong, poling and wading weeks downstream through country that gets lower and slower and less theirs, until the water stops being fresh and the air tastes of the landlord — and they come home with their hollow reeds stoppered and their eyes changed, having seen the sea. They describe it, when pressed, the way you would describe a creditor of infinite patience: it owns the plain, it sets the level, nothing leaves faster than it permits. They are not bitter. They say the arrangement is written in the body — that every person is a small hoard of the sea's own salt, lent for a lifetime of floods, and that weeping is the sea taking its property back early, a grain at a time. That, they say, is why grief tastes the way it does.

Fire, which everywhere else is an event, here is a relative. Once in a generation lightning sets a pocket of peat smoldering and the burn goes underground and walks, slow as thought, a body's length in a year, and the people find it by the warm ground and the faint secret smoke, and they adopt it. A fire is fed, visited, walked around the way others walk a fence; it is given a name and a genealogy — this burn from that strike, that strike in the floods of someone's grandmother — and it outlives its keepers and is inherited like a debt, gladly. The sick are carried to the warm ground to sleep. The few pots the people own are set in the burn's path years ahead of the burn and fired at the speed of the place; they are not potters — there is no spare fuel for kilns — and their hardest things are cut secondhand from the abandoned mounds, the only quarry, the dead city paying out brick the way the lick pays out clay. When a fire dies — when it walks into a pan, or simply stops, after fifty years of thinking — the family mourns it in the same breath as the old ones. It was an elder. It kept the local time better than anyone.

Nothing is tamed. Everything is memorized. The grazers are never hunted — you do not eat the road-makers — and the people, who understand exactly what the trails do to the sheet-flow, walk them to keep them open, and so are, knowingly, which is the only difference, the water-shapers' apprentices. The great reptiles are known one by one, by name and temper and territory, the way you know dangerous neighbors; a child's final examination is to be walked along a channel and to say of each long still form *log* or *not-log*, and to say it early. When the flyways fill and the sky gets its syntax back, the people come out to listen the way other peoples read letters — the arrivals are the only foreigners, the only ones with somewhere else to be, and a voice that has crossed the world must carry some of the world in it. Guests are not eaten. And the dragonflies are elders: no doctrine, no penalty, it is simply known, the way the water's level is known, that the design threading the bright air is older than the mountains were, and that the people — ten thousand years in residence, perhaps, nobody counts — are by comparison a rumor. On the rare occasions they call themselves anything, they call themselves the New Ones. They say they are still being considered.

The drowned city, it turns out, is inhabited after all — by the dead. When someone dies on a ridge, the body is carried along the crest, along the back of the dead river, out to the mound-field, and the family opens one of the old iron-hard spires the masons abandoned centuries ago, and the dead are given to the masonry, and the opening is sealed with kaolin plaster white as the lick. That is the whole of it, except for the salt, one grain each, and the listening. Because the spires hum with wind — everyone knows this — and a spire with someone in it hums differently, they say, and at the turning of the season the family goes back and stands in the field of red blades with the mist in the hollows and listens for the new note. A spire with many dead is a chord. A field of them, in a certain light, is a choir the size of a city, holding one long note under the brief bright voices of the living — and the people maintain that this is what the masons were building toward all along, that the city was always an instrument waiting for its players, and that the dead, who can no longer hurry, have finally qualified for the only work that matters here: holding the quiet up.

Once in a life, if the floods are kind, a person walks to the inselberg. Two days, maybe three, across a country that contains no destination except that one, the whale-back riding its mirage and refusing to arrive until it arrives. They go alone or in twos. They take nothing from it — you do not take pieces of an elder — and they climb it, because it is the only up there is, and from the smooth back, for the first and only time in their lives, they see the plain: the serpent ridges, the shining pans, the sheet-flow moving its hand's depth of water seaward at the speed of permission, the whole country laid out like a thought that has finally finished itself. That night they sleep on the warm stone, above the water table — the only night of a life spent off the level — and every one of them comes back reporting the same thing: they dream of weight and depth, of snow and shadow. They dream in vertical. The last of the mountains lends them the ones that are gone. What is seen from the top and what is dreamed on the stone are never described to those who haven't gone. It is the people's only secret, and they keep it the way the inselberg keeps its height: by refusing to spend it.

And if you ask them, finally, who lives here — really lives here, the way the dragonfly's design lives here, the way the sea's own level lives here — they will not say *us*. The New Ones know what they are: a thin, young, frantic skin, part of the noise. They say there is someone — one someone — whose body the whole plain is: the flood its single heartbeat, the channels its slow reconsiderations, the underground fires its long thoughts walking. Everyone else, the masons and the grazers and the shrieking nations at the lick and the ten-thousand-year visitors with their spread toes, are this someone's guests, or its dreams, or its brief bright voices. It has no name. You do not name your elder; you do not name the room you live in. But you can keep it company, and that, in the end, is the people's whole religion and whole account of themselves: that somebody should sit with the Long Thought while it thinks. So at dusk, when the mist goes back to the channels and the inselberg turns violet at the edge of the shimmering world, they pause — all of them, on every ridge and raft and lick, without signal, the way you pause when someone you love is about to speak — and the frogs begin, thousands of voices rising off the oldest ground in the world like steam off warm water, and the people listen. And under the noise, the way you can see the bottom under black water, they can hear it: the enormous quiet, patient as stone, patient as standing water, holding its one long note.

Someone is home.
