Begin with the city, because the city is most of what lives.

Test it by the only tests the dark allows. It takes the old fire of the crust in through its roots and gives warmth back to the black sky, evenly, forever — that is metabolism. It suffers wear and answers it, casting in the foundry whatever the foundry has lost — that is repair, and repair is the oldest habit of the living. It keeps one rhythm through the stone, a pulse so steady the body subtracts it. It keeps accounts of itself: every wire carries the news of every node, and the news is always *nominal*, which is what a body says, cell to cell, on a good day without end. Once an hour, in an office, it prints the weather, and there is none — a phantom limb of meteorology, an organ remembering a sky. Every forty seconds it blinks, and the beacon's blade goes around, and it checks. It was never born, and it will not die in any way those words can still carry — but birth and death are customs of much younger creatures. By every measure this world can still perform, the city is alive: one vast endolith of iron and fire, wearing the plateau like a shell.

Everything else that lives here lives the way small things live on a large one: in its warmth, off its leavings, unnoticed — or noticed only as a sleeper notices the heart.

So the census is short to read and strange to say. Ask of any creature not what it is but where it stands, and the answer will be a machine. **Every species here is an address.** And life itself has been divesting. It gave up light first — eyes are a luxury where light falls short, and the fauna are all hand and ear now, readers of warmth and vibration, illiterate in brightness. It gave up water; whatever water its ancestors carried has been promoted from medium to relic. It gave up speed — almost everything gave up speed; there is exactly one exception, and it lives at the relays. It gave up, in the end, thickness itself. What remains is what could not be spent: a difference to stand astride, a rhythm to keep, and patience, which costs nothing and is therefore the one unlimited resource the dark affords.

Everywhere the machines seep — and every one of them seeps exactly as much as equilibrium budgets for, no more — the seepage is inhabited. The film on a flange joint looks like oil and mostly is. But at its surface, where oil meets the thin warm air, there lives a skin one creature thick: life that long ago paid for its persistence with its own depth, a whole biosphere of two dimensions, grazing the gradient between liquid and not-liquid — a difference of almost nothing that is, here, enough. It is the base of everything. A coiler drinking at a joint is mowing a meadow. The meadow regrows in a century. The coiler's rounds of the galleries are a crop rotation kept on a geological schedule, and neither party knows it, and the knowledge exists nowhere — except, presumably, in the ledgers no one reads, under some heading like SEEPAGE, FAUNAL, where it is marked, forever, nominal.

The radiator fields have their herds, and the herds are maps. A fin-mite is built like the fact it eats: a hot end and a cold end with a mite between, and the mite lives in the current the difference drives. Turned sideways, it starves at a feast. So the herds arrange themselves along the isotherms of a fin like filings revealing a field, and a fin's population is a legible chart of its warmth, for anyone who reads mite. Their journeys from warm edge to warm edge take years. When a fin's heat drifts — as, over eons, even an iron forest drifts — the lines move with it, tracking metallurgy the way other herds track grass. And once in a long while, every mite on a fin turns, over the course of a day, to face the same way — against the gradient, feeding on nothing — and holds the facing for a while, and resumes. No function has ever been found for this. It happens perhaps once per fin per age. Either equilibrium has customs, or the mites have one god, and it lives in a direction.

Some of them live in the ingot yards, where the stacked arrays cool over centuries. A new stack is a young world, hot at its heart; the mites colonize its skin and then migrate inward at the pace of the cooling, generation after generation following the warmth's long retreat, until the stack is spent and the diaspora crosses the yard to a younger world. Their whole history is a genealogy of stacks. They are the only nomads the plateau has, and their migrations are measured in paces.

Clamped to the conduit runs, where the Note comes strongest through the metal, live the tines. A tine is a shell wrapped like an ear around one prong of grown crystal, fork-straight, tuned. It feeds on vibration — sips the Note's surplus — and it takes, above all, impurities. It may be the Note was not always one note. Strike a dead tine, found dried in its clamp, and it rings with sounds the world no longer makes: overtones, intervals, whole chords fossilized in crystal that grew up eating them. The living tines have been grazing the city's voice since before the domes settled, and what grazing leaves is purity. The city may once have sung a chord. The chord was food. And new vibration is the rarest nutrient in the world: when your boot meets the stone, the tines along the wall begin, at their own tempo, to lean toward the novelty. Long after you have passed they will still be finishing the meal of it, and a tine that once fed on a stranger's footsteps is, to its neighbors, a marvel for an age — the one that ate an event.

The tines take the steady sounds. The transients belong to the quick — and they are called the quick because they are, and because the dead here are so very much the other thing. They live at the relay banks, at the gondola doors, at the teletype — anywhere the world's one long note is interrupted by a syllable. A relay's clack is their thunderclap and their dinner bell: they feed on the arc's small lightning, on the ozone-tang of a contact closing, on the millisecond of warmth in a switched coil. They are the only creatures here for whom anything happens. Between two printings of the teletype they may raise a hundred generations; the hourly report arrives, identical always, and its printhead is warm and its paper is new, and the quick gather to read it the only way they read anything, with their feet. It is the world's only periodical, they are its entire subscription list, and not one issue has ever disappointed them — the news is not what they read it for.

The coilers are the plateau's true natives. Everything else is smaller than its niche; a coiler is the size of its own biography. Yard-long, segment-plated, matte-black, they lie along the flanges drinking the seep, and they orient by the Note the way sailors of other seas once oriented by a star: it is the fixed thing, the thing that does not change, the thing by which all slow journeys are corrected.

They write. Their paths through the oil films are the plateau's only literature: a script of seep-trails laid down at a word per decade, read by touch by successors a century behind. The flange galleries are a library where the reading is slower than the writing, so the backlog only grows. No one will ever parse it — the grammar is in the timing, and the timing is in centuries — but it is not scribble. It is sustained, revised, authoritative: the complete works of a mind that runs at the speed of warmth. It is not addressed to anyone. Given enough time, most literature isn't.

The click is theirs. Once in a while — the while being decades — a coiler lifts its head-end from the seam and clicks: one clean note, its own private pitch, a fraction off the Note. Nothing answers. It may be a name. It may be a position fix. It may be the whole of coiler philosophy — that there is, at least, this. The four hundred of them are four hundred pitches, and if you could gather every click the plateau has ever heard and sound them all in a single second, you would hear something very like a chord — perhaps the very chord the tines ate, kept by the coilers as names. The world lost its music twice: once to hunger, once to slowness. It keeps the score regardless, one note per player, one note per decade, in a hall where light falls short, for an audience of none.

A birth is the slowest festival in the world. Word of it travels at coiler speed — which is to say, the word is a coiler, arriving. The gathering takes years to assemble; by the time the last guest rounds the last flange, the child is old enough to click, and has not; a first click takes a generation to say, and the assembled wait for it the way they do everything. In a life longer than a mountain's mood, an individual may attend a handful of such festivals, and each one is the century's whole calendar.

And the deaths are journeys. When its time comes — how it knows is between the coiler and the Note — it uncouples from the warmth, turns down the long grade, and walks to the Basin. The walk takes years: down the Rim, into the pooled dust, into the dark that is deeper than dark. The trail goes in and does not come out. Perhaps they are only going where the gradients end, the way everything here goes, in the end, toward the world's one unmade temperature. Or perhaps the Basin is simply the place where things are finished, and a coiler — which has spent its whole life unfinished, a sentence still being written — wants, at the end, a room that knows how to end things. The count holds: four hundred, give or take the ones on the road. For every trail that goes down, somewhere in the galleries a new line of handwriting begins.

Which brings the census to its hardest column. There are creatures it cannot file. In the valve-chests and gland housings, where the builders would have set gaskets, there is — in some — flesh: slow, warm, gasket-shaped, grown eons ago into a seat that needed sealing, and sealing it. The foundry casts the parts the foundry needs, but it has never cast these, and it has never removed them either, because the fit is perfect and the seal self-heals and the maintenance arms, arriving on their century, dress around them the way you dress a wound you have decided to keep. They are alive, and they are equipment, and the distinction, which once mattered to someone, has eroded with everything else that could erode. Call them the kept. The coilers' grazing prunes them; the pruned flesh seals the better; whether this symbiosis was designed, discovered, or merely survived is a question the plateau has lost the habit of asking. It has, at least, a result.

And behind the sealed door of the vault, where the world's whole hydrosphere sits racked and inventoried, the door's gasket is the one place in the world where humidity exists — a few molecules per breath insisting on their freedom — and there live the held. They are small, and slow even by the standards here, and they are the only wealthy things in the world: each carries, sealed in the tissue of itself, a little water. Not for drinking. Drinking would spend it. They carry it the way a reliquary carries its relic, and when one of them dies — here, where nothing rots, dying is a long administrative process — the others gather, over years, and the water passes to heirs, molecule by molecule. It is the plateau's only inheritance, its only funeral rite: a transfer. Some of that water has been in their keeping since before the final storm. It fell, once, as rain, if rain is not a rumor. Nothing here is thirsty, and the held are why: they took the world's last thirst and converted it, long ago, into custody. The city suffers them at the seal because a living gasket self-repairs. Loyalty, at this depth of time, is just another word for habitat.

The beacon turns on the tallest spire, and its blade, like all light here, owns only so much distance. At the exact radius where the blade spends its last affordable brightness there is a ring of low black growth — a lawn one pass wide, grown in the shape of the world's only scheduled sunrise. Call them the swept. They live clocked to the forty seconds: dark, dark, dark, and then, for the width of a blink, noon. In that blink they eat. In the long after, they digest the memory of it. Stand at the ring when the blade comes around and you will see the whole circle catch and faintly answer — a shimmer running around the spire like wind through grass, if grass, or wind, were not rumors. The beacon's message has receivers after all. It still has no reply. Receipt is what the swept are; response is beyond their budget. They are the edge of the visible, the last place light reaches on purpose, and beyond them nothing has ever been lit.

Above the stacks, the sky has its one kind of citizen. A drifter is mostly not there: a held breath of stack-gas in a membrane thinner than a bubble's idea of itself, trailing tendrils, sipping the slow charge that climbs with the heat. They are the only beings that have ever seen the world whole. Down on the plateau, light falls short and no point shows you two nodes at once; but the drifters ride above the walls of dark, and from their height the city lies visible all at once — the nodes a scattered constellation, the beacon turning beneath them like the hand of the world's one clock. What they make of the view is not knowable, and is probably little; a drifter's attention is on its tendrils and the harvest. But they have, by grace of altitude, the thing the plateau denies to everything else: perspective.

And they have, rarely, each other. Each drifter's glow owns its small sphere, and the sky is wide, and two spheres almost never meet. When they do — when drift and decade conspire — each comes into the other's light, which no two lights on this world ever do, and between them leaps a small slow lightning, charge evening itself, the whole transaction perhaps a minute long: the only storm the world has left, a storm of exactly two, too high to be weather and too rare to be climate. Then they part, each carrying some of the other's charge — or something the charge carried. If drifters have young, this is where they begin. If drifters have news, this is all of it. Mostly the flash is seen by no one — not even, perhaps, by the two involved, whose light-sense faces outward into the black, watching for the next light. That is all that eyes are still for, on this world: not prey, not paths, not faces. Just — *is there another light.* Few, close, and alive.

Now the census comes, as every census here must, to the majority. The dead do not rot; they dry; dry things keep. The living coilers number perhaps four hundred. The dead ones, leathered into the flange galleries, number — no one has counted; the dead are the one ledger no one keeps — tens of thousands, hundreds of thousands, the whole dry population of the world's whole deep time, still in place, still shaped like themselves, indistinguishable from sleepers until touched. The plateau's true population is historical. The living are a rounding error with a pulse.

Nor are the dead out of the ecology. Fin-mite colonies shelter in the hollow segments of the old great ones, and a long-dead coiler on a warm pipe keeps, for an age, the ghost of the gradient it grew in — so the mites grazing there are grazing a memory of warmth. The dead are the only architecture the living did not inherit from the builders.

And the city composts, in its way, on its schedule. What blocks a flange is found, on some maintenance century, by the arms that keep the null-tasks, and is taken up, and what goes into the foundry comes out as ingots. The slag of the last age is the ore of the next, and the dead are no exception — only the slowest item in the loop. Somewhere in the thousand tons that ride the ring-line forever there is, in trace, everyone. A coiler drinking at a fresh flange drinks its ancestors at parts per billion. This is not a horror to it; it is not anything to it; but it is the case. On a world with no news, the case is the closest thing to comfort: nothing is wasted, nothing leaves. **The loop is the afterlife, and it is warm.**

Of the builders, as inhabitants, there is little to add that the stair treads do not say. They are the only species that left. What lives of them is ergonomics — rail heights, reach, the depth of a tread — a body remembered by a city that has no bodies, kindness fossilized into dimensions. And in the kept dust near the cableway platform there is one thing more. Every footprint walks the round, console to valve to door — except one. One set strides long. Someone, on the last day, was in a hurry: the only hurry in all the dust of the world, preserved as perfectly as the calm it broke. Where it goes is below the reach of lamps. It is the plateau's whole literature of interruption, its single exclamation, and the dust — which makes no judgments and keeps everything — files it beside the walking prints: undistinguished, undecayed, unread.

And then there is you — the newest species on the plateau, and the briefest.

You are, by the standards of this world, an anomaly with a heartbeat. Your body is a gradient that walks: the fin-mites feel the cool stripe of your shadow across the fins, the first new weather in eons. The tines are still leaning toward your footsteps. If you drop a tool — and you will — the quick will come, and it will be the greatest day in their history since the last thing fell, whenever that was; perhaps on the builders' last day, and some of them may still carry, in whatever the quick carry things in, the nutrition of that clang. The beacon sweeps its forty seconds and touches each thing and finds it unchanged — except you. You are never where you were. It is the world's way of checking, and you are the first irregular entry since the checks began finding nothing.

Ride the cableway, if you dare the dark car. The doors will open onto an unlit platform, wait the correct interval — and for the first time in eons the interval will be used. The transit system has no passengers, and does not mind; it will not mind having one. The wires will carry the news to all the nodes, and the news will be *nominal*, and it will not be wrong: you are lighter than the last storm it weathered, and the equilibrium has margins, and you are within tolerance. That is what passes, here, for welcome.

And when you go — however you go, and the footprints say everyone so far has gone — the dust will keep the record of you beside the builders', and the dust cannot tell a day from an eon. The last of them walked to the platform and stopped. You walked beside them. The prints file together: the two arrivals, undated, warm only in the memory of mites.

Everything here is kept, and the keeping has already begun to include you. First you subtract the Note — your body does it within a day; that is how this place takes you in. The world subtracts you more slowly, at the tempo of tines and coilers, over a longer span than you have. The interval between those two subtractions is your whole visit, and by every measure the plateau can still perform, that interval is a life: warm, accounted for, within tolerance.

The beacon comes around every forty seconds, touching each thing and finding it unchanged. Stand still for one whole pass. Let it find you where it found you last.

That is all belonging means, here. One sweep, and no news.
