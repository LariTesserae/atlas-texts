Prepare for this place as you would for a cold, waterless expedition on a mountain whose ledges move independently—and whose apparent paths may be memories rather than load-bearing ground.

The most dangerous thing about it is how accommodating it looks. There are streets, steps, doorways, sheltered courts. Your body will recognize these and begin making ordinary promises: that a street continues, that a floor supports you, that a nearby district can be reached before dark.

Do not let familiarity make those promises for you.

## Before you arrive

**Arrange a way home that does not depend on the city remaining in place.**

A return gate left in a particular square may become inaccessible when that square drifts away. A fixed rendezvous coordinate may cease to have ground beneath it. Ideally, your return mechanism should travel with you, function without local infrastructure, and work from inside a shelter.

Find out exactly what it requires: a standing surface, open sky, a stable gravitational field, a certain amount of clearance. “Portable” is not enough if it only operates under conditions this place cannot guarantee.

If return depends on an external team, establish missed-contact procedures before departure. Do not make rescue contingent on your reaching the original landing site.

For a first visit, keep the trip short and self-contained. Bring everything needed to remain on one terrace for the entire visit. Crossing into another district should be optional, not necessary for survival.

## Dress for cold that never gives you a break

The cold described here is mild enough to tempt you into tolerating it and persistent enough to exhaust you.

Wear:

- A moisture-wicking base layer.
- Insulation suitable for long periods of inactivity.
- A wind-resistant outer layer.
- Gloves that preserve enough dexterity to work clips, instruments, and food packaging.
- Insulated boots with dependable grip.
- Head and neck protection.
- A padded, insulated sitting mat.

The mat matters. You will spend time watching, waiting, measuring, and deciding whether something is safe. The stone is generally colder than the air, and sitting directly on it will steadily drain heat.

Carry a freestanding shelter and a sleep system sufficient for conditions colder than you expect. Do not assume you can stake anything into the mineral, tie off to a pillar, or sleep safely in a room merely because it has walls.

Bring independent heat if your equipment allows it, with appropriate ventilation and fire precautions. A warm seam is a pleasant discovery, not a heating plan. Its warmth passes; the cold remains.

## Assume there is nothing here for you to eat or drink

Carry all food and water, plus a delay reserve.

There is no visible liquid water, no frost to harvest, and no evidence that the depressions in the squares are reservoirs. Nothing about the dark films or the animals suggests human-compatible nutrition.

Do not taste the mineral, scrape up the films, or experiment with local organisms. Their ability to extract something from stone says very little about what your digestive system could do with them.

Use sealed bottles and food containers. Fine mineral dust can enter threads, valves, and packaging seams. Keep essential supplies in more than one container so a single dropped bag does not end the expedition.

Secure small objects before opening a pack. Here, dropping a glove is not necessarily a minor inconvenience.

## Treat the atmosphere as unverified

The description tells you what the air looks and feels like, not whether a human can safely breathe it.

Before exposing yourself, check pressure, oxygen availability, temperature, and whatever contaminants your instruments can detect. Carry respiratory protection appropriate to the uncertainty. A dust mask can help with particles; it cannot make an unsuitable atmosphere breathable.

The drifting veils may contain extremely fine mineral particles. Avoid disturbing the films, grinding the stone, or standing in dense dust streams. Protect your eyes and keep spare filters sealed.

A sky without an obvious sun is also not proof that radiation exposure is harmless. If you have the means to assess it, do so rather than inferring safety from the dim light.

## Learn what “down” means before you walk far

The six-limbed animals are not demonstrations of what you can do.

An animal stepping onto the underside of a terrace may have unusual adhesion, unusual anatomy, control over its own orientation, or access to a property of the stone that you do not share. None of those possibilities makes the underside a human walking route.

Begin on broad, apparently substantial ground well away from an edge. Check how your balance, loose objects, and instruments behave. Repeat those checks if you move into a materially different area.

Never lean over a gulf to test its gravity. Never use your own body as the experimental weight.

A fall should be treated as unrecoverable unless you have already demonstrated otherwise. The fact that no bottom is visible does not establish what happens to a falling person; it does establish that an ordinary rescue cannot be assumed.

## Be much more cautious about floors than walls

The mineral is weathered, sometimes translucent, and capable of thinning out of existence. A floor can look old without being dependable. A newly forming bridge can acquire hollows resembling ancient foot traffic before it is thick enough to support you.

Do not confuse apparent age with structural strength.

Avoid:

- Translucent flooring.
- Slender, waisted pillars and the structures they appear to support.
- Unsupported stairs.
- Narrow curbs above open space.
- Rooms showing progressive fading.
- Freshly forming surfaces.
- Any crossing whose strength is inferred solely from an animal using it.

The animals are lighter than you and may interact with the surface differently.

For exposed travel, use suitable fall protection only where the anchors are genuinely trustworthy. An unjointed wall is not automatically a good anchor. A rope attached to questionable stone can provide confidence without protection.

Do not tether yourself across two independently moving terraces. Their separation could turn a safety line into a trap.

## Measure distance; do not estimate it by eye

This place preserves visibility after it has stripped away scale.

Bring a rangefinder if one can work under local conditions. Use binoculars to inspect surfaces, not to convince yourself they are near. Without familiar objects, a doorway and a district may occupy the same apparent size.

Plan travel using measured distance, elapsed time, supplies, and verified terrain—not how close something feels.

Carry independent lights even though the sky is luminous. Dim, distributed illumination can conceal shallow steps and surface changes. A lamp held at an angle may reveal wear or cracks that disappear in ambient light. It still cannot prove structural integrity.

## Your map must record motion

An ordinary sketch map will begin lying to you almost immediately.

Record:

- Which features share a continuous piece of ground.
- Which routes cross between separate districts.
- The time of every observation.
- Relative movement between terraces.
- Changes in gaps, seams, and connecting surfaces.
- Places where the stone appears to be thinning.

Photograph landmarks repeatedly from safe positions. Use an independent clock. The brighter regions overhead are not a dependable day-night cycle, and shadows are explicitly unreliable indicators of present objects.

Do not navigate by a shadow merely because its wall ought to be nearby.

Prefer routes that remain within one continuous, substantial terrace. Even there, recheck the return path. The place can alter its ground as well as move it.

A connection lasting several days is still temporary. If you cross one, be prepared to remain on the other side without it.

## Choose camp for independence, not atmosphere

A sheltered court beside a warm seam will be inviting. It may also become a gathering place for animals, a center of biological activity, or part of a temporary alignment.

Choose broad ground with several ways to move away from camp, far from edges and visibly thinning structures. Avoid balancing your entire refuge on one doorway, staircase, or bridge.

Watch a proposed campsite for a while before settling in. Look for changing transparency, relative motion, dust lifting, and changes in the seams. Inspection reduces uncertainty; it does not eliminate it.

Keep boots, light, communication device, and return equipment within reach when sleeping. Store your packed essentials so you can take them quickly without searching through the shelter.

If possible, visit with a companion. A second person can notice changes while you are studying something else. Stay close enough that a drifting district cannot casually separate you.

## Observe the inhabitants without borrowing their rules

Give the animals room, especially when they gather and press their heads against the floor. That behavior may concern warmth, communication, feeding, orientation, or something you cannot recognize.

Do not stand in the middle of those meetings. Do not chase an animal toward an edge. Do not follow it onto a surface simply because it proceeds without hesitation.

Leave the films undisturbed. They appear to be actively changing the mineral, but there is no basis for assuming they are either dangerous or harmless to humans.

Likewise, the pale bands are not confirmed trail markings. A mark at a consistent height above floors—including ceiling-floors—belongs to someone else’s relationship with this place.

## Prepare for the waiting

Much of the visit will be slow.

You may watch a bridge form for hours and still decide not to cross it. You may see a district approach, recognize a promising avenue, and discover that the streets never actually meet. You may spend an entire excursion on the terrace where you arrived.

That is not a failed visit. It may be the safest and most revealing one.

Bring enough comfort to make patience possible: warm drinks if feasible, easy food, a notebook, a seat, and a clear turnaround time. Hunger, cold, and fatigue make a narrowing gap look like an opportunity that must not be missed.

The city’s rhythms need not fit yours. Do not give your last food, last warmth, or last chance of return to a connection that is almost ready.

The best preparation is to arrive with everything you need, expect no hospitality from the architecture, and remain willing to leave without crossing a single bridge. Sit against a sound wall, insulated from the stone, and let the distant streets move.

You can appreciate how nearly they meet without asking your body to complete the distance.
