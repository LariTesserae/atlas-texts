Prepare for this basin as you would for a cold cave, a flooded industrial ruin, and an animal colony large enough to have become the landscape.

The most important adjustment is this: **you will not be walking over a habitat. You will be walking through—and sometimes on—the inhabitants.** A surface that looks available may be feeding, breathing, waiting, or carrying the weight of something beneath it.

An ordinary human should make a short, supported visit from the western margin, not begin with a crossing.

## Before you enter

### Establish whether the air is breathable

The abundance of animals does not establish that the atmosphere is safe for humans. This ecosystem is supported by mineral water carrying sulfur compounds and dissolved gases; its animals may tolerate conditions that would incapacitate you.

Have the approach and intended route checked for oxygen concentration, pressure, carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and other relevant gases. In an unfamiliar world, familiar instruments are only a starting point: they cannot identify every possible atmospheric hazard.

Do not use smell as your warning system. Some dangerous gases have little odor, and hydrogen sulfide can stop being perceptible while remaining dangerous.

Until the atmosphere has been characterized, the appropriate protection is an independent breathing supply, not a filter mask. A filter cannot supply missing oxygen. Any breathing equipment needs to work under the local pressure and temperature, and its endurance must include a substantial retreat reserve.

If adequate atmospheric assessment and breathing protection are unavailable, stay outside.

### Find out what “weather” means underground

Rain reaches this place late. Clear skies above do not mean conditions below are stable, and a storm that has already passed may still be on its way through the rock.

Before visiting, seek observations covering several seepage cycles:

- How quickly do water levels rise?
- Which shallow pans become connected?
- Which passages become impassable?
- Where does gas accumulate when seep activity changes?
- Are there reliable, unoccupied refuges above the water?
- Does the western exit remain accessible during a pulse?

The changes described are often slow, but that does not make them harmless. Water only needs to cover a few crucial saddles to destroy your return route.

### Arrange a return, not just an arrival

If you are arriving by portal, make certain you can reopen it under the actual conditions inside the basin. Test it in darkness, moisture, noise, and whatever electromagnetic interference accompanies the overhead discharges.

Prefer an extraction point outside the basin, with a second independent means of retreat. Do not assume an apparently solid interior platform will remain clear long enough to serve as a permanent landing site.

Leave your route, return deadline, and emergency procedure with someone who is not coming with you.

## Dress for persistent cold and continuous contact

The cold here is not dramatic. That is part of its danger. You may remain comfortable enough to keep moving while wet gloves, damp clothing, and repeated contact with chilled stone steadily take heat from you.

Wear:

- A moisture-resistant outer layer over insulation that remains useful when damp.
- Tough gloves that preserve enough dexterity to operate equipment.
- Boots with good wet-rock traction, ankle support, and protection around the lower leg.
- A helmet with secure lighting.
- Eye protection against grit, splashes, and close-range water jets.
- Close-fitting cuffs and covered skin.

Avoid dangling straps, loose fabric, open pockets, and exposed equipment cables. This is a place full of hooks, mouths, fringes, narrow crevices, and animals accustomed to carrying smaller animals.

Bring dry reserve clothing in a genuinely waterproof container, along with emergency insulation. Keep drinking water and food sealed. The basin’s water is not a supply to be made safe by casual filtering; it may contain both unfamiliar organisms and dissolved chemicals.

Check whether your ordinary medicines and medical equipment remain effective in this world. Small wounds matter in an environment where almost every surface is occupied.

## Protect your hearing without surrendering awareness

The noise may be the first thing that makes you want to leave.

It is not merely loud. It is continuous, layered, reflected, and spatially misleading. Your brain will keep trying to locate events that happened somewhere else—or happened some time ago. Human speech may become useless just when you most need it.

Use suitable hearing protection and a communication system tested in comparable noise. Do not assume ordinary noise-canceling headphones provide adequate protection.

Agree on a small set of visual and tactile signals before entering:

- Stop.
- Retreat.
- Air problem.
- Injury.
- Route blocked.
- Do not move.

Stay within visual contact when possible. Keep your group small enough to coordinate but large enough that one injured person does not leave another alone with an impossible evacuation.

Some vibration will reach you through your feet and body regardless of ear protection. Expect that to be tiring. Schedule rests and a turnaround before exhaustion rather than after it.

**Do not investigate a noise simply because it seems nearby.** Direction and distance are unreliable here.

## Bring your own light—and your own navigation

Take independent primary and backup lights, with protected power supplies. You need enough illumination to inspect footing, not merely enough to see the next silhouette.

The animals’ blue points and pale rings are not helpful landmarks. They can move, disappear, or be imitated by predators. Treat any biological light as an animal behavior you do not yet understand.

Begin with low, directed illumination. Bright light might disturb the inhabitants, but there is no reason to assume any particular color is invisible or harmless to them. Establish reactions from a safe position rather than discovering them while surrounded.

Navigation should be redundant:

- A mapped, limited route.
- A record of turns and significant changes in elevation.
- Approved markers on verified stable features.
- A tested position-tracking system, if one works locally.

Do not count on a compass, satellite navigation, radio, or acoustic ranging until each has been tested. The roof blocks some systems; mineral deposits and repeated echoes may confuse others.

Mark the way **out**. A fascinating route deeper into the basin is not useful when your light, air supply, or concentration starts to fail.

## Learn to distrust apparent ground

Your first practical lesson should happen near the exit: identifying which surfaces can safely bear a human.

There are several kinds of false reassurance here:

- Grit can conceal moving animals or unstable gaps.
- Smooth slabs can be covered in almost invisible bodies.
- A dry-looking surface can carry a lubricating film.
- Shell deposits can contain occupied, thin-walled chambers.
- Hardened mud can contain dormant animals.
- A broad, rocklike mass can be a hunter.

A probing tool may help assess a surface, but it is not a guarantee. Pressure can injure an animal, trigger a response, or break through a crust. Never put your hand into a crack merely to steady yourself.

Favor previously surveyed footing. Take short, deliberate steps, keeping your weight recoverable until the next foothold has been assessed.

Avoid drainage grooves, black water, undercut boulders, crowded seep routes, and low, wide “stones” across animal traffic. Do not climb onto a large motionless creature for a better view.

There may be **no safe walking route** for an ordinary human through some districts. The emptier ground is specifically described as less stable. Preparation includes accepting that an apparently accessible destination may require specialist equipment—or may not be responsibly visitable at all.

## Do not try to behave like a harmless local animal

You do not know the local signals.

A repeated tap might resemble courtship, a feeding cue, a territorial challenge, or the movements of prey. Walking itself introduces an unfamiliar pattern of pressure and vibration. Keep movements controlled, but do not assume silence or slowness makes you invisible.

Avoid experimental noises, rhythmic tapping, playback of recorded calls, and imitation of luminous displays. Do not feed anything to see what happens.

The largest hunters may not be interested in you as food. That does not make them safe. A human could be crushed, trapped beneath a rising body, knocked into water, or caught in a feeding chamber intended for smaller animals.

If a mass begins to lift, **move away from its footprint rather than waiting to identify it**. Retreat toward footing you have already checked. Do not run blindly into another occupied surface.

Likewise, a local quiet patch is not necessarily a refuge. It could be recently abandoned feeding ground, a changed seep, or a place where activity is concealed beneath the floor.

## Choose rest stops carefully

Do not sit on the nearest convenient slab.

Arrange rest points during reconnaissance, ideally using a stable, cleanable platform that does not obstruct a seep or animal route. Keep your pack closed and off occupied surfaces. Inspect boots, cuffs, gloves, and equipment before continuing.

Do not sleep inside on a first visit. During sleep, you lose the ability to notice rising water, atmosphere changes, cooling, animal movement, or organisms entering your equipment. A motionless warm human would be a novel feature in a place where nearly every available shelter gets occupied.

Your first trip should be short enough that you can return before you need a meal, substantial recovery, or sleep.

## Keep the visit biologically contained

Nothing in the description establishes whether these organisms can infect or parasitize humans. It also does not establish that they cannot.

Use a conservative containment procedure:

- Cover wounds before entry.
- Avoid bare-skin contact.
- Keep hands away from your face.
- Do not bring specimens home casually.
- Inspect and clean equipment on exit.
- Contain contaminated clothing and wash water.
- Follow an appropriate quarantine protocol before returning to another ecosystem.

This protects both worlds. Your microbes could be as disruptive here as the basin’s organisms could be elsewhere.

Leave no food, waste, batteries, discarded markers, or scraps. A small object left here may enter a food chain you cannot follow.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect your confidence in your senses to erode.

Your eyes will say “stone,” then a surface will wrinkle. Your ears will place a heavy movement ahead, then its vibration will pass beneath your feet. A channel will look empty until the water tightens around something. Light will reveal detail without giving you a reliable sense of the room’s size.

The ordinary comforts of a landscape are absent: no horizon, no dawn, no dependable empty ground, no silence in which to gather yourself. Even stopping does not stop the place from happening around you.

That can produce fatigue, nausea, claustrophobia, or a strong urge to hurry. None of those reactions is shameful. They are reasons to shorten the visit. The dangerous response is trying to regain control by moving faster or exploring farther.

Start at the western margin, keep the exit close, and let your first success be modest: observe a section of the basin, remain warm and coordinated, and leave with everyone and everything you brought.

**Prepare to depart before you feel ready to.** In this place, the difference between a remarkable visit and an emergency may be noticing that your attention—not the basin—has begun to fail.
