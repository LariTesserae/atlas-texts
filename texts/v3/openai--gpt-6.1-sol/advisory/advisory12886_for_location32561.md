Prepare for this town as you would for a winter mountain crossing, a stay in someone’s crowded home, and a journey on a river whose banks cannot be trusted to remain banks.

The mistake would be to think of it as a small, quiet settlement that happens to have unusual plumbing. Water determines which doors are usable, which routes exist, where animals gather, and when work must stop. The inhabitants have learned to live within those changes. Your first task is to borrow their knowledge—not to discover the rules by experiment.

## Before you enter

Arrange to arrive in daylight, with someone expecting you. Ideally, meet a resident outside the hollow and let them bring you in.

You could probably see your destination from several streets away and still be unable to reach it safely. A map would help you understand the town’s structure, but it would not tell you which stair is carrying water today, which bridge has been raised, or whether the route through a neighbour’s roof is available to visitors.

For your first few days, hire a guide if that is possible. Otherwise, ask your host to accompany you on essential journeys. Learn how to reach your lodging, a source of drinking water, food, and the town’s exit under more than one arrangement of the channels.

If you are arriving from low altitude, allow time to acclimatize before going up. The description does not establish the elevation, but the exposed ridge, snowfields, and sparse vegetation make thin air a reasonable concern. Exhaustion, headache, and poor judgment would be especially unwelcome where a familiar path can become unusable in minutes.

Do not arrive with a wheeled suitcase. Bring a compact pack that leaves both hands free.

## Protect your eyes before anything else

The light is a serious hazard. It comes from above, below, and sideways, and there are few deep shadows in which to recover.

Bring good snow goggles with full ultraviolet protection, side coverage, and an anti-fog treatment. Carry a backup pair in a protective case. Ordinary sunglasses may leave too much reflected light coming around the lenses.

Also obtain a local wooden slit visor and learn how it is worn. The residents’ equipment is worth taking seriously: it may make the town’s pale surfaces easier to read, as well as reducing glare. But do not assume an unfamiliar wooden visor provides all the ultraviolet protection your eyes need.

Wear sunscreen on exposed skin, including beneath the chin and around the nose. Carry lip protection. A brim or hood will help, but it cannot stop light reflecting upward from pools and snow.

On arrival, pause somewhere safe and let your vision adjust. If you cannot distinguish a step from its reflection, you are not ready to walk through the town unassisted.

## Dress for wet cold, not just cold

You will encounter bitter water repeatedly. Even if you never fall into a pool, splashes, dripping lintels, soaked steps, and wet doorways can gradually defeat inadequate clothing.

Bring:

- Insulating layers that remain useful when damp.
- A waterproof, windproof outer layer.
- Waterproof boots with good wet-stone grip and enough room for warm socks.
- Several changes of socks, packed separately in waterproof bags.
- Thin working gloves and warmer outer gloves.
- Dry sleeping clothes that never become your walking clothes.
- A small towel or absorbent cloth for drying feet, hands, and equipment.

Avoid long trailing hems and loose straps. They can drag through channels, snag on bridges, or catch among animals. Keep your load narrow enough to pass through tight passages without turning sideways over open water.

Do not assume that heavier footwear is safer. Bulky boots may be awkward on narrow boards and worn, scalloped stairs. Likewise, do not put on crampons or studded overshoes without asking: the town contains old wood, smooth stone, fragile edges, and surfaces residents may not want punctured.

Have enough dry insulation to recover from one substantial soaking. In this place, being able to change immediately could matter more than carrying another useful gadget.

## Learn the red wool markings exactly

Two meanings are given, and they are not interchangeable:

- **A red loop on a threshold:** do not bring a wet foot across it.
- **A red loop around a public basin’s handle:** the basin is safe to fill, but not to empty.

Treat these as precise instructions. Do not simplify them into “red means dangerous” or “red means safe.”

Before crossing a marked threshold, ask how a visitor is expected to dry their feet. Before using a marked basin, ask what “fill” and “empty” mean in local practice and where its contents may safely go.

There may be other markings. Have someone show you those currently relevant to your route. Do not untie a loop to inspect it, borrow its wool, or replace it with a mark you think is clearer.

The important preparation here is accepting that a wet footprint or an emptied container may have consequences you cannot predict.

## Do not trust the appearance of water

Clear water is not necessarily drinking water. Liquid water is not necessarily warm. Ice is not necessarily stable.

Bring drinking water for your arrival and your first period of settling in. Then ask where residents obtain theirs and how they prepare it. A filter or purifier may be useful for ordinary contamination, but it cannot be assumed to deal with whatever allows some of this water to remain liquid in extreme cold. Local knowledge should come before improvisation.

Never test a pool with your bare hand. Do not walk across translucent ice because it appears thick or because beetles are moving beneath it. Avoid touching unfamiliar wet stone, particularly near markings.

Find out where to dispose of washing water, food scraps, and waste. Do not pour anything into a convenient gutter. That gutter may pass through a kitchen, connect to an occupied pool, or change direction later in the day.

## Learn what to do during a turning

You may not hear a warning. Watch the birds.

When they abandon one set of ledges and crowd onto another, stop treating the street as an ordinary street. Look to your guide or host. The residents will be moving baskets, shelves, bridges, gates, animals, and children into their new positions.

Your first response should be to get out of their working space, not to help at random.

Ask in advance:

- Where should a visitor wait during a turning?
- Which nearby doorway or landing is suitable?
- How will your host signal you?
- What should you do if water cuts you off?
- Which bridges must never be moved by a guest?

Do not assume that higher ground is automatically safe. Water climbs stairs here. Nor should you assume that a dry place will remain dry.

During your first several turnings, stay with someone experienced. Do not cross a newly exposed patch simply because a resident has done so. The child at the basin knows when to step into the space left by retreating water, how long to wait, and which way to leave. You do not yet know any of those things.

If separated from your guide, use the waiting place you agreed on, if it remains safely accessible. Signal visibly to a resident rather than attempting a shortcut through moving water.

## Expect shouting to be ineffective

The town’s quiet is not evidence that nothing is happening. Sound is absorbed so strongly that a hammer blow may be inaudible across an alley.

A whistle or electronic alarm can be brought, but do not depend on it. Agree on visible signals with your companions: an unmistakable wave for attention, a gesture for “stay there,” and another for “come by this route.”

Keep companions in sight when travelling. Do not send someone ahead with the expectation that they can call back if the way becomes unsafe.

A lamp may help in deeper passages, but use it carefully. Some animals retreat from lamplight even though they tolerate daylight. Ask which kinds of lighting are normal indoors.

Old sounds emerging from walls may be unsettling. Do not take a murmuring cupboard as evidence that someone is trapped behind it, and do not start dismantling masonry to investigate a scrape. Ask your host what is ordinary and what warrants concern.

## Stay in a room someone has chosen for you

For a first visit, prefer established lodging with a resident host. Ask for a room with settled masonry, more than one usable way out, and a dry place for your bedding and equipment.

Respect the custom of keeping beds away from newly repaired walls. You do not need to understand the full reason before following it.

Ask where your pack should go. The underside of a bucket can hold snails; firewood can contain nests; an apparently empty recess can be occupied. A bag left open on the floor may become shelter.

Keep possessions closed and together, but inspect them gently before sealing them. Set things down slowly. Check clothing and footwear before putting them on, and ask for help moving any unfamiliar creature rather than handling it bare-handed.

Store food as your host recommends. Do not assume a tightly wrapped snack can safely be left on a windowsill or beside your bed.

## Be useful cautiously

Nearly everything in the town seems to be part of an ongoing maintenance task. A wedge may hold a gate in readiness. A loose-looking plank may be waiting to become a bridge. A channel through a room may be essential rather than inconvenient.

Do not “fix” anything without permission.

The residents’ mallets are tools, not evidence that every visitor ought to carry one. Learn their use before borrowing one. The same applies to ice hooks and bone wedges: forcing a frozen door may damage the door, its frame, or whatever local arrangement keeps it working.

Metal may be valuable, but ask before offering supplies as gifts or payment. Small, agreed contributions are better than arriving with equipment you expect people to adopt. Good cord, suitable cloth, or a useful cutting edge might be welcome; their usefulness should be judged by the people maintaining the town.

If invited to help, begin with supervised work: carrying something to a specified place, holding a basket, passing an item, or keeping a route clear. During a turning, your host may prefer you to do nothing. That is still cooperation.

## Leave the far basin until later

The basin behind the houses is not a good place for a newcomer to explore alone.

Its changes are faster, its water is cloudy, animals crowd the margins, and ice forms and breaks apart. The stone bench offers a place to watch, but even reaching it should be done with someone who knows the route.

Stay back from the edge. Do not collect the translucent creatures, disturb the animals, or imitate the child with a tray. There may be rules about timing, species, quantity, or ownership that are not visible to you. Her decision to take eleven is a reason to ask a question, not a reason to assume eleven is a universal safe number.

Watch first. The town rewards close attention, but observation need not mean approaching everything within arm’s reach.

## Keep your departure flexible

Know who can guide you out and where to meet them. Ask how turnings affect the exit routes and how much time to allow.

Keep a departure kit packed: eye protection, warm layers, dry socks, drinking water, food, and any essential medication. Store it where your host considers it accessible, not merely where it is closest to your bed.

Do not plan a tight connection elsewhere in the multiverse. A settlement whose internal routes rearrange deserves a generous margin for leaving.

The most useful thing to bring is a willingness to be a slow learner. You will need to notice birds before water, markings before thresholds, and residents’ preparations before deciding where to stand. For a while, every ordinary action—emptying a basin, crossing a doorstep, setting down a bag—will require a question.

That need not make the visit miserable. With a good host, dry clothing, protected eyes, and time to watch, you could become comfortable there. But let comfort follow knowledge. The town looks quiet enough to invite wandering; arrive prepared to be led.
