Prepare for this town as you would for a visit to someone’s home, a cold-water dive, and a stay high in the mountains—all at once. The mistake would be to treat it as a picturesque village that happens to be wet. Water is the street, the weather, the space between you and help. The houses are living organisms. Even the places where you can put your head into air require maintenance.

An ordinary human can visit, but should not arrive expecting to live as the residents do.

## Arrange your first visit before entering the lake

Make contact from one of the shore landings, or through someone who knows the town. Arrange a local guide, a particular house to visit, and a surface return point. Ask where visitors can safely enter, which air chambers are maintained for guests, and whether there is anywhere you can get completely out of the water.

That last question matters. The description offers seats where your head and shoulders can emerge, not necessarily dry rooms where your whole body can become warm. A roof pocket might be a perfectly comfortable sitting room for a resident and an inadequate place for a cold human to spend the night.

For a first visit, plan to sleep ashore. Have dry clothes, shelter from mountain wind, food, and a way to heat water waiting there. Returning to the surface soaked, chilled, and tired is not the end of the exposure.

Ask your host about:

- Water temperature at street level, not merely near the surface.
- Actual depths along the proposed route.
- The safest way through the root canopy.
- The northern outflow and any changes associated with rain.
- Which rooms have dependable ventilation.
- Local signals for assistance, departure, and “please do not touch that.”

Do not assume the residents’ ability to work underwater tells you what you can do. They may be adapted, unusually practiced, or using methods you have not yet understood.

## Bring your own breathing capability

The roofs are close to the surface, but the town is not uniformly shallow. Its lower streets approach a slope that disappears into deep water. More importantly, a canopy of roots and living roofs means that being near the surface does not always give you a clear route to it.

You should be trained for the equipment and conditions you intend to use. If you are not already a competent cold-water diver, make the first visit a short, professionally supported excursion—or remain at the landings until an appropriate arrangement is available. Do not improvise a town-wide breath-holding tour.

For an ordinary human using conventional equipment, bring an independent breathing supply, adequate reserve, and reliable backup arrangements. Obtain specialist advice about **altitude diving**: a mountain lake has lower surface pressure than a sea-level dive site, and your depth instruments, dive plan, and subsequent travel to higher elevations must account for that.

The silver air pockets are not automatically safe breathing stations. They are occupied spaces whose air quality depends on tubes that can become obstructed. A chamber can look inviting while containing too little oxygen or too much carbon dioxide. A normal smell is not proof of safety.

Use only chambers your hosts have confirmed are properly ventilated, preferably with an atmosphere check suitable for the space. Keep your own breathing equipment available. Do not plan your air reserve around an unverified pocket.

And although the streets are open, some rooms are overhead environments. For the first visit, stay in broad lanes and chambers with obvious exits. A welcoming doorway is not a reason to push through a narrow root passage.

## Dress for cold, slow movement

The water is cold enough that exposed stone can numb a hand. Visiting will probably involve pauses: waiting while someone finishes tying a sapling, watching a tool being used, sitting in a chamber to talk. Those pauses are likely to chill you more than steady swimming would.

Wear exposure protection appropriate to the measured temperature and visit length. A properly fitted drysuit may be preferable for a longer cold-water visit, but only if you know how to use it. Protect your head, hands, and feet while preserving enough dexterity to handle your own equipment.

Expect your useful endurance to be shorter than your enthusiasm. Loss of hand coordination, persistent shivering, or difficulty making simple decisions means the visit should end. Tell your guide early; do not wait until you can no longer operate a buckle.

Pack warmly for the shore as well. The landings are among wind-bent trees on a mountain saddle, not on a sheltered beach.

## Keep your equipment compact

This is not a place for trailing straps, loose cords, large swinging bags, or anything that readily hooks around a branch.

Secure hoses and accessories close to your body. Bring a small light even if you expect full daylight; the illumination becomes green and directionless in rain, and the spaces within old walls can be very dark. Carry suitable emergency cutting equipment, but understand that it is for freeing yourself, not for clearing a convenient path through someone’s house.

Use short, managed retainers where necessary, rather than tethering every object into a web of cords. Leave nonessential belongings ashore.

Do not imitate the workers’ waist stones. Their weights serve a particular task within a practiced local routine. For you, weighting should be part of a controlled buoyancy system with a reliable release—not an attempt to make standing underwater feel ordinary.

A bulky pack will be especially troublesome indoors. Bring a modest gift that can tolerate immersion, or leave it at the landing for your host to collect.

## Learn how to move without spending the town’s labor

The town’s spaciousness is maintained, not natural. Someone has repeatedly removed every shoot that would otherwise close a lane.

Your goal should be to pass through without undoing that work.

Maintain controlled buoyancy and stay clear of the moss floor. Use gentle, compact propulsion; vigorous fin strokes can damage growth, stir material into the water, and make visibility worse. Do not stand in a clipped meadow simply because it resembles a place made for feet. Ask whether it is appropriate.

Likewise, do not grab a root merely because it is within reach. Some are structural, some tender, and some deliberately guided into a shape that took years to achieve. Let your guide show you which handholds are meant to be used.

Do not:

- Scrape bark or trim a branch to make room for yourself.
- Move braces, collars, stones, or bindings.
- Push through abandoned houses.
- Disturb eggs, feed fish, or collect plants.
- Insert anything into ventilation tubes or shift their floating reed covers.

Clean your equipment before arrival and after departure. An unfamiliar seed, fragment of weed, or aquatic organism could become a lasting problem in a place where almost every loose cutting can root.

## Treat the northern and southern edges differently

At the north, the danger is the outflow. The gentle water elsewhere should not persuade you to approach the channel. Follow the local stand-off distance, and confirm whether rainfall changes it.

At the south, the danger is depth and the absence of easy recovery. The low willow rail marks a real boundary. Stay well inside it. Do not lean over for a better view, chase a dropped object, or let curiosity about the pale fish draw you down the uncultivated slope.

If you drop something there, tell your host. Accept that it may be lost.

Neither edge belongs on a casual first tour.

## Arrange communication before you need it

You will hear plenty underwater, but hearing a sound is not the same as understanding where it comes from. The waterfall’s vibration, knocks through the wood, and bubbling from houses may make the town seem busy without helping you locate anyone.

Agree on visual signals with your guide before entering. At minimum, cover:

- “I need air.”
- “I am cold.”
- “I want to return to the surface.”
- “I am caught.”
- “Stop.”
- “I am all right.”

Stay within sight. Do not rely on shouting, or assume that knocking against a wall is an acceptable way to call someone. It may be someone’s sleeping chamber, and the sound may travel farther than you intend.

Know your return route by landmarks. From below, the houses can merge into one another, and the bright surface is not a map.

## Visit at the town’s pace

The most interesting things here will not reward haste. A person guiding a root around a sleeping chamber is working on a timescale much longer than your visit. Watch without grabbing the work to help.

Ask before entering a house, sitting on a woven seat, touching a tool, or taking photographs. Do not interpret a wall’s permeability as permission to cross it. Fish may disregard the boundary between inside and outside; you should not.

If you want to contribute, let someone assign a task. Unskilled pruning is a particularly poor gift. Something straightforward at the surface landings may be much more useful.

Choose a short daylight visit first. Evening brings a change of use: people withdraw into the air pockets, tools come indoors, and the open streets become less supervised. Leave before your guide must choose between hosting you and settling their own household for the night.

## What to expect

The town may feel quieter emotionally than it sounds physically. There is no surf to keep announcing the water. Leaves barely move. Sunlight passes across old walls, and fish travel through domestic space as freely as weather through an open window.

That calm is likely to make you forget your limits.

Keep enough attention on your air, warmth, position, and companion that you can enjoy the rest. You may feel clumsy beside residents who know exactly where to brace a foot and which root will bear a hand. Allow yourself to be a visitor rather than trying to look at home.

The best preparation is an independent way to breathe, a warm place to return to, a local person who knows where you belong, and the willingness to leave while you still feel well. With those secured, you can give the town what it seems to ask of everyone: patience, careful movement, and respect for the open spaces someone has worked so hard to keep open.
