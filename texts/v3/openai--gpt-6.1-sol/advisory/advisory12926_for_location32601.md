Prepare for this as a visit to a **high, exposed limestone wilderness where shelter, water, and reliable communication are all scarce**. It is not lifeless or necessarily hostile, but almost everything that keeps its inhabitants alive is tucked into a crack, under a slab, or inside a small patch of vegetation. An ordinary human arrives without those adaptations.

The most important preparation is to make sure you can leave without having to find anything here.

## Before you arrive

Choose an arrival point on a broad, solid shelf, well away from the plateau’s rim and any visible opening. Ideally, your return mechanism should work from that same point without depending on local power, sunlight, or a distant signal. Carry a backup means of locating it.

Do not assume that a place with recognizable birds, mammals, and plants has an atmosphere suitable for you. Check pressure, oxygen availability, temperature, and basic atmospheric safety before stepping out of a protected arrival space. The light and clouds suggest substantial elevation, but they do not tell you how much oxygen a human can obtain.

If conditions are compatible with unassisted breathing, still treat the first visit as a short reconnaissance. Carry the supplies for an unexpectedly long stay, but do not plan to use them all.

Go with another person if possible. This is a poor place to be alone with a twisted ankle.

## Dress for cold air and fierce light at the same time

You may feel cold while getting badly sunburned.

The pale ground reflects light upward, so protection needs to cover more than the top of your head. Bring:

- UV-blocking sunglasses with side coverage, preferably with a spare pair.
- Sunscreen for exposed skin and protective lip balm.
- A brimmed hat that fastens securely.
- Long sleeves and trousers.
- An insulating layer and a windproof, waterproof outer shell.
- Gloves that still let you operate your equipment.

Secure hats, maps, straps, and other loose items. Wind that feels modest at ankle height may be considerably stronger around your shoulders or at the edge of a shelf.

Wear supportive boots with good grip on stone. The fitted gravel may look reassuringly stable, but the raised seams are trip hazards, and smooth limestone can become slippery where moisture collects. Trekking poles can help with balance; do not mistake a successful poke with a pole for proof that a hollow slab will support your weight.

## Prepare for the noise before you hear it

The underground roar is not incidental scenery. It will shape the whole visit.

Bring well-fitted hearing protection. If measurements show hazardous sound levels, use protection rated for the exposure rather than relying on ordinary noise-cancelling headphones. The description suggests strong low-frequency sound, which may remain physically noticeable even when your ears are protected.

Agree on hand signals before arrival:

- Stop.
- Come back.
- Danger ahead.
- I need help.
- Return now.

Carry radios, but test them on site. Wind and roar can overwhelm microphones, and terrain can interrupt reception. Keep companions in sight; a shouted warning is not a dependable safety system here.

The noise may also become exhausting without causing obvious hearing pain. Chest vibration, humming teeth, and the inability to hear ordinary speech could produce headache, nausea, anxiety, or simple difficulty thinking. Make decisions early, while you are still comfortable. Do not wait until you are overwhelmed to negotiate a return plan.

## Navigate by instruments, not apparent distance

That dark outcrop is farther away than it looks, and the ground between you and it contains obstacles you cannot yet see.

Carry a map or a means of recording your route, a compass, and a positioning system known to work in this world. Do not assume an Earth-based satellite receiver will have satellites to listen to. A local beacon at your arrival point may be more useful.

Record your outward route as you go, especially where you cross a ridge into another basin. Take photographs looking backward as well as forward. Rounded shelves can look distinct on approach and almost identical on return.

Use elapsed time and remaining energy to govern your excursion, not whether the destination seems “nearly there.” Turn back with a generous reserve. The return includes every concealed hollow you crossed on the way out.

Keep a watch and a headlamp accessible. The apparent steadiness of the light is a bad clock.

## Treat the stone as a surface over empty space

This is karst terrain: water has opened passages inside soluble rock. Some openings are obvious; others may be concealed beneath gravel, flakes, or thin roofs.

For a first visit:

- Avoid shafts, fissures, undercut edges, and suspect slabs.
- Do not jump across cracks merely because they look narrow.
- Do not crawl into cavities to escape wind or noise.
- Stay well back from the cliff rim, including sections that appear rounded and solid.
- Do not approach an opening just because its sound is interesting.

The loudest hole is not necessarily the largest, and the quietest one is not necessarily safe. Airflow tells you that spaces connect; it does not tell you where they lead or whether their roofs are stable.

Exploring the underground chambers would be a separate expedition requiring trained cavers, appropriate equipment, atmospheric checks, and a rescue plan. It is not a casual extension of a walk.

## Bring all your water and food

Visible moisture is not a usable water supply.

A thumb-deep pocket may be difficult to reach, may drain before you return, and may contain contaminants from birds, mammals, or the surrounding rock. Clear water is not necessarily safe water. Carry enough for the planned outing and a substantial delay. Cold, dry air and exertion can increase water loss even when you do not feel sweaty.

For a first visit, do not taste the plants, seeds, insects, or water. Familiar-looking life in another world is not evidence of familiar chemistry. Ordinary filtration or boiling also cannot be assumed to remove every unfamiliar hazard.

Pack food that is easy to eat with cold hands and hearing protection on. Keep it sealed, retain every crumb and wrapper, and do not feed the animals.

## Be careful where you stop

A bare-looking slope can conceal a whole bird colony. A comfortable stone lip may be someone’s nesting cover. A tiny cushion of leaves may support much of the life in that particular hollow.

Rest on visibly durable, unoccupied stone away from fissures, plants, and cliff ledges. Watch a proposed resting place for a while before approaching it. If birds repeatedly rise or circle because of your presence, withdraw rather than waiting for them to tolerate you.

Do not lift stones to see what lives underneath. Those stones are shelter, moisture traps, and sometimes the roofs of burrows. Leave the seed-cracking stones and probing stems where they are; their simplicity does not make them unimportant.

If you must stay overnight, bring a freestanding shelter suitable for exposed rock, with an anchoring plan that does not depend on driving pegs into soil. Do not assume you will find a sheltered campsite. Carry a means of packing out human waste rather than burying it in scarce soil or depositing it in a drainage crack.

## Know what should end the visit

Return promptly if anyone develops persistent headache, dizziness, nausea, unusual fatigue, or worsening breathlessness. Confusion, poor coordination, or breathlessness at rest warrants urgent evacuation to a safer environment. Do not diagnose every symptom as altitude illness: noise, cold, dehydration, and an unsuitable atmosphere can overlap.

Also turn back if cloud begins to erase your landmarks, footing becomes wet, communication fails, or either traveler feels too overloaded to make sound decisions.

**The best first visit is modest:** arrive safely, verify conditions, walk a short recorded circuit, observe a feeding patch or bird colony from a respectful distance, and leave with water and energy to spare.

This place will reward patience more than distance. You do not need to reach the far outcrop to encounter it. Stand somewhere secure long enough, and the apparently empty stone will begin to disclose its inhabitants—while the great noise continues beneath your feet.
