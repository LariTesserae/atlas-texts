Prepare for this place as you would for a working harbor, a tropical industrial city, and a ship on which you will be living for several days. Do not prepare as though it were a ruined city waiting to sink. People have made an ordinary life here. Your task is to learn which parts of that life are safe to imitate.

The most important adjustment is this: **water, buildings, animals, and machinery are not separate features of the landscape. They are one inhabited system.** Something that looks like a wall may also be a cooling surface. Something that looks like a puddle may be an opening into the city’s underside. A quiet passage may be less reassuring than a loud one.

## Before you arrive

Arrange your arrival at an established passenger terminal, preferably with someone local meeting you. Do not choose an arbitrary rooftop or apparently empty quay just because your means of multiverse travel permits it. The empty space may be a freight route, a retracting market floor, a storm overflow, or an area kept clear for reasons you cannot recognize.

Confirm three things in advance:

- **That you can safely breathe the air and eat local food.** Ordinary-looking water and animals do not establish biochemical compatibility.
- **Where you will sleep, and how to reach it without crossing an exposed bridge.**
- **How residents receive warnings.** Obtain an accessible version of the local alarm patterns before you begin exploring.

If your exit requires returning to a particular place, choose a sheltered, publicly accessible departure point and identify an alternative. A damaged span can close, and a perfectly functional district can become temporarily difficult to reach while gates discharge water.

For your first visit, pay for a local guide. Not chiefly to show you attractions, but to teach you how to walk.

## What to wear and carry

You will be damp. Plan to remain comfortable and functional while damp rather than trying to exclude every drop of water.

Wear lightweight, quick-drying clothes, with enough coverage to protect against abrasive railings, hot surfaces, and unfamiliar organisms. Bring spare socks and a dry sleeping outfit. Keep both in a sealed bag. A heavy waterproof coat may make the heat under the decks intolerable; a light, ventilated rain shell is more useful.

Your shoes matter more than almost anything else. Choose secure footwear with soles suited to wet ceramic and metal. Avoid loose sandals, smooth soles, and anything that catches easily in mesh. Waterproof boots help in shallow water, but once overtopped they can become buckets. Do not interpret good boots as permission to wade.

Carry:

- A compact waterproof bag, with essential items separately sealed inside.
- A small light for dark interiors and power interruptions.
- Drinking water from a verified safe source.
- Any essential medication, protected from moisture.
- A cloth for wiping lenses, screens, and your face.
- Eye protection for spray and wind-driven grit.
- Hearing protection that can reduce machinery noise without completely obscuring warnings.
- A little ready-to-eat food known to be safe for you.
- A local map or route aid that distinguishes **levels**, not merely streets.

Salt, condensation, and warm air will be hard on equipment. Favor sealed devices, protect charging contacts, and dry things whenever you get the chance. A device can become wet in a shaded corridor even when there is no rain outside.

Leave the umbrella behind unless locals recommend a particular kind. In canal wind, on narrow walks, among crowds and retracting awnings, it is likely to become an obstruction.

## Learn the warnings before the street names

The noise will initially flatten everything into one overwhelming impression. Residents hear information where you hear machinery.

Ask your guide to explain, at minimum:

- Gate movement warnings.
- Bridge or platform closure signals.
- Storm warnings and shelter directions.
- The meaning of colored public lamps.
- What pedestrians should do when a deck shudders.
- How an emergency is communicated when speech is inaudible.

Do not wear headphones while walking. Hearing protection is useful, but total acoustic isolation would remove one of the city’s main sources of situational information. If you cannot reliably hear low horns or distinguish signals, arrange a visual or tactile alternative.

Most importantly, learn to notice **other people noticing**. If a market suddenly pauses, pause with it. If every vehicle stops on a trembling bridge, do not attempt to squeeze through the gap. If workers look toward a canal whose appearance seems unchanged to you, give them room and wait.

A reduction in noise is not necessarily peace. It may mean something important has stopped.

## Walk as though the edge matters

Stay on established pedestrian routes. Use the guide cables on exposed bridges. Keep your hands available rather than carrying several loose purchases.

Do not step over a maintenance animal, prod it, or pull it away from a leaking seam. It is doing a job, and the surface beneath it may be compromised. Give repair crews more space than seems necessary.

Avoid:

- Water-covered stairs whose lower steps you cannot see.
- Railings or ladders not clearly intended for public use.
- Narrow ledges beneath inhabited structures.
- Canal edges during gate releases.
- Flooded rooms, even when there are fish in them and people living upstairs.
- Shortcuts through lift cores, pumping facilities, or apparently unused infrastructure.

A surface may be structurally sound without being safe to stand on during its normal operation. A passage can be dry now and carry water later.

Do not assume you can swim your way out of a mistake. The water may contain currents from gates, moving vessels, submerged structures, poor visibility, and animals large enough to damage hulls. If your itinerary includes boat travel, use the locally approved flotation equipment and learn what to do if you fall overboard.

## Choose your lodging carefully

For a first stay, choose an established guesthouse or hotel on a well-used passenger level, with a sheltered route to transit and staff accustomed to newcomers.

Ask about:

- Ventilation and nighttime temperature.
- Reliable drinking water.
- A place to dry clothing.
- Alarm visibility from your room.
- An alternative exit.
- Where guests shelter during storms.

“High” does not automatically mean “good.” Upper platforms may receive stronger wind, glare, and lightning exposure. Lower rooms may be cooler but noisier, damper, or more vulnerable to rising water. What you want is a well-managed building, not simply an impressive elevation.

Do not expect silence at night. Bring sleep earplugs, but establish how you will still receive emergency warnings. The engines may continue through the night; traffic and gate cycles may change rather than cease.

## Treat water and food as local knowledge

Nothing in the description establishes that tap water is unsafe, but neither does a sophisticated water system make every outlet drinkable. Ask which supplies are intended for drinking. Do not fill a bottle from an attractive overflow, bath outlet, cooling channel, or clear-looking fountain.

Food is likely to make extensive use of aquatic animals and cultured organisms. If you are unfamiliar with the local biology, begin with verified meals prepared by people who know your requirements. Avoid raw food until you understand the risks. A creature that resembles a shellfish may not share the chemistry of any shellfish you know.

If you need a plant-based diet, arrange it before arrival. There is almost nothing visibly green here; that does not prove plant foods are unavailable, but you should not count on finding fresh fruit or vegetables casually.

Keep cuts covered. Wash after contact with canal water. Do not touch the leathery colonies, copper-like growths, or “flowers” in gardens merely to discover what they feel like. They are unfamiliar living things, and some are part of the city’s working surfaces.

## Let the residents teach you how to handle a storm

The city manufactures weather as a consequence of its own activity. Do not think of rain as something coming only from a distant horizon.

Watch for the sequence: thickening cloud, wind in the canals, retracting awnings, markets beginning to fold. **Leave exposed routes when the preparations begin, not when the rain reaches you.**

Choose a proper public shelter. Do not duck into a drainage workshop, an abandoned lower story, or an opening beside a gate. During the storm, stay away from collector masts and exposed conductive structures.

Afterward, do not rush out simply because sunlight has returned. The stored rain still has to go somewhere. Gates may open in sequence, canals may surge, and floating structures may move substantially against their guides. The aftermath can be more dangerous at water level than the rain itself.

Allow generous time for travel. A detour is normal here, not evidence that the city has ceased to function.

## Respect the living infrastructure

The residents’ familiarity with animals does not mean the animals are tame.

Do not feed canal grazers or approach them in a small boat. Follow the pilot’s instructions when vessels slow near one. Leave nesting vents, hatcheries, cages, screens, and feeding shelves alone. What looks like miscellaneous clutter may protect someone’s food supply or keep a cooling intake clear.

Before photographing inside workshops or homes, ask. The absence of a clean boundary between habitation and infrastructure does not erase privacy. A fascinating room in a bridge pier is still someone’s room.

Likewise, do not mistake every leak, tilt, stain, or flooded floor for an emergency. Report anything concerning to local staff, but let them interpret it. The inhabitants have experience distinguishing tolerated conditions from dangerous changes.

## Prepare for the lack of relief

The hardest part may not be danger. It may be the absence of a quiet, dry, green place where your attention can loosen.

There will be glare outside and dimness within. Heat will linger under decks. You may have to stand close to strangers to understand them. Your shoulders may tense against the noise before you realize it. Even a garden will not offer the familiar comfort of leaves moving in wind.

Plan short excursions at first. Find a sheltered place where you can sit, drink, and stop navigating. Eat regularly. Do not spend your first day proving you can tolerate the conditions.

There will also be beauty, but much of it will arrive briefly: reflected light in a corridor, animals following a falling tide, the undersides of bridges turning gold after rain. Leave enough room in your schedule to notice it.

You are visiting a place where survival has become architecture and maintenance has become daily life. Go with sound footwear, protected belongings, a reliable local contact, and the willingness to stop when everyone else stops. Once you have learned that discipline, the city will become much more than a collection of hazards. It will become legible.
