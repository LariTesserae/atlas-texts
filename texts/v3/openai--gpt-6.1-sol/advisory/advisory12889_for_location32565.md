Prepare as though you are entering an abandoned industrial city, a delicate archaeological site, and the interior of a wind instrument at the same time.

The most important fact is not that the city is small. It is that **the city is still operating, and you do not know what its operation once made safe—or whom it once served.**

Its inhabitants are absent. Its shutters, roots, passages, and tuned mouths are not idle. Do not confuse those two things.

## Before you go beneath the stone

### Resolve the problem of scale

At your ordinary size, you can visit the broken corner and look in. You cannot walk the streets without destroying them.

Do not lift the paving stone to make access easier. It is the city’s ceiling, its source of shade, and part of its ventilation system. Removing it would change the light, temperature, pressure, and airflow throughout the basin. Even if nothing collapsed, you could ruin a functioning environment in seconds.

For a full visit, you need a reliable means of becoming small enough to use the doorways. Choose your size from a measured entrance, not from the overall dimensions of the hollow. Leave clearance for your helmet and pack. Someone who barely fits through a doorway may not fit through the covered passage beyond it.

Your equipment must change scale with you while retaining its function. In particular, establish that:

- Your breathing system works at the destination scale.
- Your eye and hearing protection remain effective.
- Your boots can grip fine mineral surfaces.
- Your communications can reach someone outside the stone.
- You can return to normal size **after leaving the hollow**, rather than accidentally expanding inside it.

A timed return is a poor sole safeguard. Being restored to ordinary size under a paving slab is not a rescue.

Have a companion remain above, clear of the stone, with a marked rendezvous point and a means of retrieving you without dismantling the city. If safe shrinking is unavailable, use a small remote observer. Looking in from outside is a legitimate visit.

### Observe a whole day before choosing your entry

This place has a daily cycle. Morning, noon, and afternoon are not simply different lighting conditions; they are different operating states.

Watch the broken corner from outside. Record airflow, temperature, sound, and any dust expelled through the opening. A tiny, loosely placed sensor is preferable to anything wedged into a crack.

Do not assume dawn is safest. The western streets roar in the morning. Noon brings intense light and the opening of the leaves’ red combs. Afternoon brings changing shutters and strong drafts. Night may be quieter, but it may also bring reduced ventilation and a greater concentration of seep gas.

Choose a visit window from measurements, not from how peaceful the courtyard looks.

## What to wear and carry

### Bring your own breathable atmosphere

There is an unidentified gas emerging from the floor. Its bitter metallic smell tells you almost nothing useful about its toxicity.

The plants consume it. That does not mean humans can tolerate it. Nor does greenery imply an oxygen-rich atmosphere: these organisms do not appear to work like ordinary terrestrial plants.

For a first entry, wear a sealed breathing system with an independent air supply. A filter mask is not enough for an unknown gas or an atmosphere with insufficient oxygen. Carry instruments that monitor oxygen, temperature, pressure, and airborne particulates, with as broad a chemical detection range as your equipment allows.

Treat the kilns and fissures as higher-risk areas. Do not lean over a seam to smell it.

Avoid exposed flames, sparking tools, and equipment that gets hot. You do not know whether the gas, airborne powder, or amber deposits can ignite.

### Protect your hearing before you cross the threshold

Do not enter first and put in ear protection once the sound becomes uncomfortable.

Bring properly fitted hearing protection, ideally with controlled external sound reproduction and a means of communicating that does not depend on shouting. A visual signal system and haptic alerts would be useful backups.

The noise is not merely loud music. It includes shrieks, rattling leaves, sudden pipe-clearing coughs, and a sustained low tone. Some of it may be transmitted through the ground and buildings. Your hearing protection will not necessarily prevent vibration from making you nauseated or disoriented.

Shrinking may also change how your body and equipment respond to the city’s acoustic frequencies. Test the protection at scale before committing to a long visit.

Do not chase complete silence. You still need to detect a pipe clearing nearby, a change in wind, or the onset of a dust cloud.

### Cover your eyes and skin

Bring impact-resistant goggles with adjustable tint. The former inhabitants wore smoked mica for a reason, but reproducing their eyewear is not proof that it will protect human eyes.

At this scale, a blade of sunlight may occupy an entire street. Move from cover to cover, and avoid lingering beneath roof slots at noon.

Wear close-fitting clothing, gloves, and a helmet. Mineral leaves may look like green ribbons, but their edges could behave like thin shards. Loose straps can catch on stems, cords, shutters, and pipe mouths. Keep your pack narrow.

Useful additional equipment includes:

- A dimmable, cool-running lamp.
- A temperature probe.
- A local map and independent navigation markers.
- A tether or retrieval line, if it can be routed without touching machinery.
- A small emergency shelter that does not require anchors.
- Water, food, and enough air to wait out an unexpected change in conditions.

There is no visible water here. Do not plan to find any.

## How to move through the city

Enter through the broken corner without widening it.

Your first problem will probably be the descent. The terraces may look gentle from above and become substantial ledges at street scale. Dust-polished stone may offer poor footing. Go slowly enough to learn how your boots behave before reaching the square.

Prefer visibly sound, open routes. Avoid roofs, foliage, and covered passages until you have inspected them. The city has adjusted to a sagging ceiling over generations; apparent elegance is not a guarantee of structural strength.

Watch the dust. It is one of your best indicators of conditions:

- Dust traveling steadily shows an active draft.
- Dust suddenly reversing may indicate a shutter change.
- A motionless pocket may offer shelter from wind, but does not establish breathable air.
- A rising yellow cloud after a pipe cough is a reason to retreat, not investigate.

Do not put your face near a chimney mouth. Do not stand in line with a pipe outlet. A modest discharge by the city’s standards could be a substantial blast to you.

The broad empty ground is not necessarily unused ground. It may provide airflow, separation from fissures, access to structures, or room for a function you cannot recognize. Cross it carefully; do not choose it automatically as a campsite.

## Leave the machinery alone

The temptation will be considerable. Most of the mechanisms can be understood by looking. You will see a weighted shutter and want to test it. You will see a blocked pipe and want to clear it. You may think opening one passage would relieve the noise.

Resist.

A shutter may divert hot air away from a roof garden. A narrowed chimney may regulate pressure elsewhere. A particular sound may once have carried information. The twice-repaired mouthpiece suggests that accuracy mattered.

**Do not retune the city for human comfort.**

Do not cut roots, pull vines from plaster channels, scrape amber, or remove the workbench tools. The amber is both an unknown material and food for the few animals still present. The knife resting in its fitted groove is part of a worksite, not a souvenir.

Keep introduced substances contained. A leaking drink, a dropped ration, even ordinary damp equipment could be a significant disturbance in a place this dry and small.

## Treat the recess as shelter, not sanctuary

The shaded recess beneath the porch sounds like the best place to pause. The roar diminishes there. Dust settles. You can feel the remaining hum through your feet rather than endure every note directly.

It is a sensible place to inspect from a distance and potentially use as a short resting point.

But its stillness is also a warning. Reduced airflow may allow gas to accumulate. Roots occupy it. Its steps and porch may be weakened. Check its atmosphere and structure before entering, and keep your independent breathing supply on.

Once there, you may find the city much easier to perceive. Outside, the noise makes everything seem violent. From the recess, individual actions become visible: a shutter lifting, a patch of light moving, a stem drawing across a doorway. Give yourself time to watch those changes without touching them.

## Be prepared for company—and for absence

Do not describe the city as dead while you are in it.

You have not established what its inhabitants were, how they communicated, or where they went. The shoulder-height hollows suggest a possibility, not an invitation to press your hands into them. Touching a resonant wall might transmit a signal you do not understand; it might simply damage old plaster.

If you encounter movement that appears deliberate, stop and give it space. Begin with slow, visible gestures. Avoid shouting or striking surfaces. You do not know whether either would be intelligible, threatening, or overwhelming.

Give the six-legged creatures the same consideration. Their pauses when the notes change may mean that sound is important to how they navigate or survive. Do not pick them up, feed them, or arrange for their paths to meet.

Prepare emotionally for the workbench. A carefully repaired object can make abandonment feel more intimate than a street full of ruins. You may find yourself inventing a last worker, a final task, an explanation for the empty houses. Let those remain possibilities.

Nothing here establishes that the departure was sudden, tragic, or even a departure.

## Leave before you have to

Set conservative turnaround limits for air, heat, noise exposure, and fatigue. Do not spend the last of your reserve reaching one more workshop.

Return before the day moves into an operating phase you have not observed. Confirm that your route out remains open; a passage that was comfortable on arrival may carry a hot draft later.

Once outside, inspect and contain any dust caught on your clothing. Check for attached fibers, leaves, or small creatures. Return to ordinary size well clear of the paving stone.

The best visit will leave almost no trace: no shifted shutter, no damaged leaf, no new obstruction in a street.

And expect the courtyard afterward to feel strangely quiet. You will be standing above a place whose entire weather, industry, and daily music fit beneath two human feet. The danger is that, having seen it, you will no longer find it easy to walk away without doing something.

Looking closely—and leaving it intact—is enough.
