Prepare for this city as you would for a winter expedition, a moving industrial site, and a visit to an unfamiliar household—all at once. The mistake would be to regard the animals as wildlife, the buildings as fixed structures, and the weather as something happening outside. Here, all three occupy the same space.

Your first purchase should be a local guide. Your second should be more time.

## Before you arrive

Arrange to arrive **inside an inhabited transit terminal**, with someone expecting you. An exposed arrival point on the plateau could leave you within a short walk of shelter and still unable to find it. Fog, overlapping bridges, entrances beneath street level, and almost no useful illumination make distance a poor measure of accessibility.

Book accommodation in an established neighborhood rather than a newly growing district. Specifically ask for:

- A room with a floor suspended independently of the walls.
- Inner shutters and a maintained drainage system.
- A functioning body-warming field compatible with human physiology.
- Access to food and sanitation without an outdoor journey.
- A host who will explain settling procedures rather than assume you know them.

“Warm room” is not a sufficient description. Local warmth apparently stays close to the occupant rather than heating the enclosure. You could be comfortably warm in bed while your water bottle freezes and your boots become rigid beside you.

Confirm the atmosphere’s pressure, oxygen content, and chemical composition before travel. The city is high, but “high” does not establish an Earthlike altitude or an atmosphere humans can breathe. If acclimatization or respiratory support is necessary, arrange it in advance. Fatigue, dizziness, and numb fingers are especially dangerous somewhere navigation depends on balance and touch.

Likewise, establish whether your food, medicines, and waste can be safely handled locally. There is no visible plant ecology to reassure you that the ordinary provisions of a human city exist here. Bring familiar food until you know what the residents eat.

## Dress for wet cold and sharp surfaces

The cold is not merely in the air. It is in the handrail you need to hold, the wall you may have to follow, and the water that finds its way into your sleeves.

Wear a layered system:

- A moisture-wicking base layer.
- Insulation that remains useful when damp.
- A waterproof, abrasion-resistant outer layer.
- Reinforced knees, elbows, and seat.
- Boots with reliable traction on wet stone and ice.
- Gloves that combine insulation, cut resistance, and enough sensitivity to read tactile markings.

Those gloves deserve special attention. Thick expedition mittens may keep you warm but make you functionally unable to read the city. Bare fingers may read a handrail beautifully for a minute, then become painful and clumsy. Ask a local outfitter for a solution intended for visitors with human hands.

Avoid loose straps, trailing scarves, and exposed cords. The clearances are narrow and occupied. Snagging yourself between a cable and a slowly contracting animal would not necessarily produce an immediate emergency; it might produce one you cannot escape before it becomes serious.

Carry a small waterproof pack rather than a broad, heavily loaded one. Keep essential supplies secured inside it. During the settling, unsecured possessions become everyone else’s problem.

For exposed walks, use traction equipment approved locally. Aggressive spikes might damage living thresholds, membranes, or machinery concealed in what appears to be ordinary pavement.

## Learn the city with your hands and feet

Do not arrive expecting a powerful flashlight to solve the darkness.

Lamps attract organisms until the lamps fail. A bright light may also interfere with people who are navigating by other cues. Bring a shielded, adjustable light for emergencies and close inspection, but ask before using it outdoors.

Spend your first day learning a few essential tactile and acoustic signals:

- How a handrail announces a junction.
- How routes distinguish levels and destinations.
- What a pressure gate’s warnings mean.
- How a safe bridge responds underfoot.
- How to recognize a delayed response from a far anchorage.
- Where a public route ends and a maintenance passage begins.

A bridge that gives three clear tones is not just making a pleasant sound. It is communicating something about its condition. If the answer from the far end arrives late, do not continue because the bridge still looks intact.

Have a guide demonstrate these differences on known examples. Reading about them will not teach your feet what to notice.

Maps should be current, local, and preferably usable without sight. An old map may correctly identify a building and still give you the wrong entrance. A road may have become a warm ridge. A stair may have changed. A passage may be occupied for the next nine days.

For your first excursions, travel with someone who knows both the route and its alternatives. “I can find my way back” is not enough if the way back becomes unavailable.

## Protect your hearing without cutting yourself off

The city is loud, but much of the sound is information.

Bring hearing protection that reduces harmful volume while preserving useful signals. Ordinary earplugs may be appropriate during rest, but wearing them continuously outdoors could conceal bridge tones, transit warnings, or a change in animal calls.

Do not assume noise cancellation will handle vibrations transmitted through floors, teeth, and the bones of your body. Ask your host how visitors sleep, and whether your lodging’s isolation systems reduce structure-borne vibration.

Expect the sound to be tiring even when it is not painful. Schedule short outings at first. A day of cold, uncertain footing, and continuous low-frequency pressure can exhaust you without any single dramatic event.

Use a locally approved communication method with your companions. Shouting may not work; light signals may attract the wrong attention. Learn a few simple touch signals as a backup.

## Treat water as infrastructure, not scenery

Do not drink seepage, taste ice, or refill a bottle from an attractive-looking channel.

Some water is meltwater. Some is concentrated brine. Their interaction produces deposits capable of disabling machinery. You do not know what either will do to your skin, equipment, or digestive system.

Bring a sealed drinking supply for your initial stay and use only water your host identifies as suitable for humans. Keep batteries, connectors, and medical equipment protected from both condensation and mineral contamination.

A thin skin of ice does not establish that a pool is shallow, solid, or safe. Neither does cold weather establish that a dark liquid has frozen.

Do not redirect a gutter to stop it dripping on your luggage. Do not chip away an inconvenient deposit. The city has redundant drainage for a reason, and the apparent nuisance beside your door may be preventing a much larger problem.

## Learn which animals are part of the building

Your guide should give you a practical lesson in recognition by texture, conducted safely and with permission. At minimum, learn the organisms you are likely to encounter on doorways, handrails, bridges, and transit approaches.

The most important rule is: **do not remove an animal merely because it is in your way.**

A pale creature on a winter threshold may be keeping that threshold flexible. A broad animal between buildings may distribute weight or damp vibrations. Something feeding on a cable may belong to a regulated relationship with the power system.

Conversely, not every animal is harmless or useful. Some obstruct valves. Some occupy routes. Some may be dangerous to touch for reasons the description does not reveal.

Never put your fingers beneath a body to see whether it is attached. Never step onto a shell because other people seem to use it as a road, unless you are on the maintained route. Never squeeze through a gap that is presently open without knowing whether it will stay open.

Their slowness is deceptive. A movement taking an afternoon can still trap you if you have placed yourself in its path. An alarm response that develops gradually is still an alarm response.

Watch for tightening bodies, deepening color, and withdrawing organs. If these changes begin nearby, stop treating the encounter as an opportunity for photographs. Give the animals room and consult your guide.

## Know what to do during the settling

Ask about this before your first night, not when the floors begin humming.

During the settling, residents stop work, secure loose objects, close inner shutters, and wait in specially constructed rooms. Follow their lead. Do not assume that because the measured movement is tiny, the event is inconsequential. Very small displacements can matter enormously in a dense network of gates, pipes, bridges, and loaded foundations.

Know:

- The nearest suitable room.
- How to reach it from your accommodation.
- Who will tell you when normal movement can resume.
- What supplies should already be inside.
- How to request help if you cannot reach it.

Keep water, medication, food, and warm clothing available for a wait lasting most of a day. Use a steady, locally approved light if needed.

The animals may announce the event before instruments do. A change in their chorus is not something to ignore merely because no official alert has arrived.

Do not improvise an evacuation during the settling. A crowded bridge may be less safe than the room residents have prepared for precisely this event.

## Be conservative about the unfinished plateau

The open plateau is not a scenic promenade beyond the city limits. It is young terrain, poorly visible, occupied by immense animals, and still under survey.

Go only with an authorized guide and a plan appropriate to current conditions. Carry independent warmth and communication equipment, and know what happens if fog prevents your return.

Do not use survey ropes as casual handrails or detach them to make yourself a safety line. Do not approach instruments, foundation seeds, or warm soft ridges without permission.

Warmth underfoot is not proof of a safe resting place. It may indicate fresh rock, an active seep, or a living body affecting the air around it.

When the clouds lower and the valley becomes visible, resist the urge to lean into an opening for a better view. Gaps in the paving are actual gaps, with an enormous drop beneath them. The sudden appearance of distance after hours of close fog may also make you dizzy.

## Budget for waiting

A traveler here needs a different sense of punctuality.

A herd can close a causeway for nine days. Settling can suspend work for hours. A transit connection may be unavailable because something is cooling, freezing, breathing, or growing.

Do not plan a same-day arrival and departure. Keep several days of essential medication beyond your intended stay, and make onward arrangements flexible. Ask about alternate routes, but accept that sometimes the correct alternate route is to remain indoors.

Locals will likely judge your competence less by how quickly you move than by whether you know when not to move.

## Before you leave

Have your boots, clothes, and equipment inspected for attached organisms and mineral deposits. Small translucent plates could disappear against a wet sole or pack seam. Nothing that fed comfortably on local stone or power filaments should be carried into another world by accident.

Do not collect fresh stone, animal tissue, living “decorations,” or foundation material without explicit authorization.

What you should bring home is the memory of the place: the startling warmth held close around your body, the cold wall just beyond it, and the sensation of a distant animal’s voice arriving through your feet.

A successful visit will not necessarily cover much ground. You may spend an afternoon waiting for a passage to open, or a whole day listening from a suspended room. That is not lost travel time. It is how this city becomes intelligible—and how you leave it safely.
