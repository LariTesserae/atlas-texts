Prepare for this city as you would for a place that is inhabited, competently maintained, and not yet finished becoming land.

The obvious danger is not catastrophe. Nothing in the description suggests that residents live in continual terror, or that the ground might suddenly swallow a district. The danger is that an ordinary human traveler will mistake a familiar-looking feature for a familiar kind of thing: a street for a stable route, a train for an object that has already arrived, a dry plain for solid ground, a cup of clear water for drinking water.

**Your most useful preparation is learning which signs the residents trust.** Bring good equipment, but budget time for being taught how to use the city.

## Before you arrive

Arrange to be met at an established arrival point by a local guide, host, or municipal representative. A visitor should not have to discover the difference between a safe transit car and an almost-present one by experiment.

Ask your contact to confirm:

- Whether ordinary humans can breathe the air safely.
- Whether local water and food are suitable for human consumption.
- What marks a train as ready to board.
- How construction seams and path closures are indicated.
- What residents use to measure the day.
- How emergency assistance is summoned, and how a location should be described when streets and buildings can shift.

The atmosphere appears warm, but warmth does not establish breathable composition, pressure, or biological compatibility. Likewise, the presence of residents does not establish that their needs match yours. Get those basic compatibility checks done before treating this as an ordinary city visit.

Choose accommodation in an occupied, maintained district, preferably near an established transit stop. For a first stay, avoid the southern expansion zone and isolated towers with long stretches of empty ground around them. The space may be attractive; it also increases the distance to help.

Ask for a room with a maintained exit and a host who can explain any overnight adjustments to the building. A tower that moves gently may be perfectly safe. A newcomer trying to find yesterday’s entrance in dim light is a separate problem.

## What to pack

You do not need expedition armor. You need equipment for warm weather, low visibility, sharp immature terrain, and intermittent access to supplies.

Bring:

- **Closed walking shoes with puncture-resistant soles and good grip.** Fresh mineral glass and brittle stone are more concerning than ordinary mud.
- **Lightweight trousers and breathable long sleeves.** These protect against rough vegetation, stone edges, and uncertain skin contact without making the warmth intolerable.
- **A brimmed hat, if comfortable.** Useful for widely spaced heavy raindrops, falling plant debris, and drips from upper chambers.
- **A compact, adjustable light.** Choose a broad, low setting for walking and a stronger setting for inspecting footing. Do not assume the lamps among roots are path lights.
- **A sealed supply of known-safe water and familiar food.**
- **A personal medical kit**, including dressings for cuts, your usual medications, and any prescribed allergy treatment.
- **An offline map or notebook**, alongside whatever current municipal navigation service exists.
- **A reliable clock and spare power.** The sky will not give you a dependable sense of time.
- **A small daypack rather than a large rigid case.** Long gaps between destinations and changing walkways favor manageable luggage.

A walking pole may help on approved uneven routes, but do not use it to probe fresh seams, exposed services, or channels. If a surface is questionable enough to require experimental poking, a visitor should usually take another route.

## Learn to read the ground

The plain is not old terrain being damaged by construction. It is young terrain still forming beneath an accommodating city.

That means some of your usual hazard instincts need adjustment. Do not wait for shaking, rumbling, or visible collapse before taking a new seam seriously. Here, the warning may simply be a straight discoloration and faint warmth.

**Stay off emerging ridges and their margins, even when they appear slow and harmless.** A bench remaining balanced on a rising strip tells you the movement is gentle; it does not tell you the strip is intended for pedestrian use. Sharp edges, separating paving, and changing access are enough reason to keep your distance.

Use municipal detours. The withdrawing walkways are evidence that the city already knows how to manage this process. Follow that engineering rather than improvising across the exposed soil.

Check routes each day. A map is a dated account, not a permanent promise. In particular:

- Do not navigate solely by the number of steps in a staircase.
- Do not assume an entrance will be in exactly yesterday’s position.
- Leave extra time for returning home.
- Carry your lodging’s contact details and its official location identifier, if it has one.

The most unnerving moment may be a small discrepancy: a doorway slightly farther west, a stair shorter than you remember. You will need to distinguish a normal adjustment from being lost. Let your host teach you that distinction early.

## Treat transit arrival as a process

The trains are the clearest place where familiar behavior could be dangerous.

On an ordinary platform, a vehicle becoming visible usually means it is physically there. Here, that assumption is explicitly wrong. Cars pass through a stage in which they can be seen but are not safe to touch.

Wait well back until the recognized boarding signal. The description identifies condensation on the windows as an important threshold; learn whether there is also a platform indication, attendant’s signal, or door sequence.

For your first few trips:

1. Stand behind the local waiting boundary.
2. Observe the car becoming present without reaching toward it.
3. Wait for condensation and the authorized boarding cue.
4. Board normally, without touching exposed exterior surfaces out of curiosity.

Do not throw anything into the trough or try to test the car with an object. An apparently empty track is still operating infrastructure.

Because there is no audible approach, put your attention on platform indicators rather than listening for a train.

## Be conservative about water

Water here is carefully collected, routed, and reused. Its scarcity is partly concealed by lush groves and damp upper rooms.

Carry your own supply until you have identified a verified source for humans. The cups beside doors may be household collection vessels, drinking vessels, or something else. The fact that the water is clear and tastes faintly of stone does not establish its safety.

Do not assume a portable filter resolves every uncertainty. Filtration may help with particles and some organisms; it does not automatically remove dissolved minerals, unfamiliar compounds, or every biological hazard. Local testing and a known compatible supply are better than improvisation.

Also:

- Do not drink from the flowers.
- Do not take water from planted hollows or fern-covered channels.
- Do not open service hatches to investigate the running sound.
- Do not place your own cup in a collection recess without permission.
- Do not obstruct gutters, even briefly.

In this city, a small water diversion may matter to a whole patch of vegetation. What looks like a decorative trickle may be essential infrastructure.

## Approach the groves as inhabited places

A grove may be a neighborhood, a water-management system, and a garden at once. Do not read dense vegetation as permission to wander.

Use entrances and established paths. Ask before crossing planted hollows or approaching upper-level gardens. Moss-covered balconies may be lovely, but persistent moisture makes unfamiliar surfaces worth treating cautiously.

The lamps among the roots deserve particular restraint. They illuminate selected leaves and knots rather than routes. Their purpose is not given. They might support plants, communicate conditions, or serve an aesthetic function that a visitor does not understand. Do not move them, shade them with equipment, or rely on them to lead you to a door.

Likewise, roots entering doorframes are not necessarily neglected damage. Maintenance crews deliberately accommodate them. Do not cut a root, force a tight door, or “repair” a lock whose position seems strange. Ask the occupant how the entrance works.

Avoid handling unfamiliar plants and animals. The mineral-bearing creatures emerging from ridges are interesting precisely because so little is known about them. Photograph from an appropriate distance rather than collecting specimens.

## Take nighttime closures literally

You do not need to decide what moves through the channels.

Something large sometimes passes there, draws water from neighboring gardens, and leaves changes sufficient for the city to close paths. That is enough information to govern your behavior.

Do not follow it, wait beside a channel to see it, or enter a closed path because the notice says only “maintenance condition.” The notice may be phrased that way because the cause is uncertain, because the distinction is irrelevant to public safety, or because that is simply the city’s administrative language.

For evening walks, choose maintained routes away from the channels, travel with someone familiar with the area, and have a clear way home. The long sightlines should not tempt you into assuming that anything important will be visible from far away.

## Prepare for the city’s pace and quiet

The spaciousness may be harder on an ordinary human than the strange engineering.

You can see a destination without being near it. A building that looks like the next stop may require a long walk across exposed, powdery ground. Plan by actual travel time, not visual closeness, and refill supplies before you need them.

Expect your voice and small noises to travel farther than usual. Speak quietly outside, silence unnecessary device alerts, and avoid amplified sound. This is less about observing a formal prohibition than about being considerate in a place where a dropped tool can occupy an entire neighborhood’s attention.

The dim sky may also interfere with sleep and timekeeping. Bring an eye mask if you use one, keep a regular meal schedule, and ask your host how the warm and dark intervals work. Do not let perpetual subdued light turn the entire visit into one long evening.

There may be periods when the city feels almost abandoned despite being occupied. Distant lit rooms, a door closing beyond a grove, and signals exchanged between towers are signs of activity on a scale different from your own. Resist the impulse to fill that quiet immediately. Give yourself time to become comfortable with it.

## Leave the unfinished south for later

The southern edge is compelling, but it is a poor place for an unaccompanied first excursion.

Open foundations, exposed service bundles, newly cut streets, and land that resembles water but is dry all demand local interpretation. The shallow bright-rimmed footprints do not establish that walking there is safe. Nor do survey markers establish a public route.

Visit with permission and a guide who knows the current boundary. Do not touch the translucent rods, pipes, or turning filaments. Do not step across a seam simply because it has not yet risen.

The city’s infrastructure appears to be reaching toward land that is still arriving. Let it do so without you standing in the way.

## The attitude to bring

Bring patience, curiosity, and a willingness to ask small questions.

This does not seem like a place that needs visitors to be fearless. It needs them to be observant: to wait until the windows cloud, notice a new line in the paving, respect an unnamed closure, and understand that a root-filled doorway may be working exactly as intended.

Once you have learned those habits, allow yourself to enjoy it. Listen to the towers answering one another. Watch the copper undersides of the leaves show in the moving air. Notice how a few drops of rain find their way toward a grove.

You are visiting a city whose residents have made room for the ground to keep changing. Prepare well enough that you can make room for it too.
