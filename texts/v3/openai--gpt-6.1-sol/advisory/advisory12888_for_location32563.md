Prepare for this place as you would for a cold, partially flooded industrial facility that is still operating—and whose operating plans are not entirely reliable.

The crucial fact is not that it looks alien. It is that **the basin is a machine, and most of its landscape is machinery doing a job**. A bank may be curing. A floor may be adjusting its slope. A quiet channel may be waiting for a gate to open. Nothing needs to notice you in order to kill you.

Your first visit should be short, guided, and mostly above the waterline.

## Before you arrive

Find someone who has worked a recent shift in the basin. Not someone who remembers its layout from years ago, and not someone who has only studied the kiosk maps.

You want a maintenance worker who knows:
- Which raised routes are currently continuous.
- Where you can shelter without obstructing equipment.
- How much warning precedes a flood, a refrigeration discharge, or a floor adjustment.
- Whether the bells follow a predictable schedule.
- Which doors can be opened from inside.
- Where a person can be recovered if they fall into a channel.

Ask specifically about the orange flags and chalk dates. Those may be more useful than the official diagrams, but their meaning should come from a worker, not your interpretation.

Arrange your return point on a verified stable, dry service platform, well away from the intake, casting gantry, and yellow-post corridors. If you travel by portal, do not assume the floor beneath it will retain its elevation, or that the route back will remain open. Have an independent way to leave, or a local escort with access to one.

Establish a missed-check-in procedure before entering. “Come looking for me” is inadequate in a place where someone can be visible from a platform and still be unreachable.

## Dress for cold water, not merely cold air

The basin will wet you from above, below, and sideways. Ceiling water will find the gap behind your collar. Condensation will wet equipment that never touches a pool. Holding a metal rail will drain heat from your hands.

Wear:
- Moisture-wicking base layers and substantial insulation.
- A waterproof outer shell with sealed seams.
- Insulated, waterproof boots with good traction on wet metal.
- Warm gloves with enough dexterity to operate doors and communications.
- A suitable industrial helmet and eye protection.
- Hearing protection compatible with your communications system.

Carry spare gloves and a dry insulating layer in a genuinely waterproof bag. A second pair of gloves is particularly valuable: wet hands make every subsequent task harder.

Have any traction aids approved locally. Aggressive spikes might help on ice but catch in grilles, damage surface systems, or make certain walkways worse.

Avoid loose scarves, dangling straps, and unsecured clothing. Keep necessary lanyards short and arranged so they cannot snag machinery. The fans are far overhead, but there are plenty of closer things capable of catching a traveler.

Do not dress for intentional wading. Waterproof boots are protection against accidental wetting, not permission to cross a channel.

## Bring equipment that remains useful in noise and vapor

Carry two independent lights. A broad light helps you read the immediate floor; a narrower beam helps examine a route ahead. Neither will let you see through unlimited condensation. Very bright illumination may simply return a wall of glare.

Bring a locally compatible radio or communication device, and test it **inside the basin’s noise**, not just at the entrance. Ordinary shouting will fail near the pumps. Agree on a few simple hand signals: stop, retreat, hazard ahead, assistance needed.

Useful additional equipment includes:
- A compact first-aid kit and emergency insulating shelter.
- A waterproof watch or timer.
- A current route record that works without a network.
- Enough food and drinking water for a delayed return.
- A means of identifying your party under poor visibility.

Do not drink the basin water. Clear water over a pale panel is still industrial drainage containing whatever has washed off the overhead systems and surrounding surfaces.

Have local personnel assess the atmosphere and electrical hazards. The downward drift of breath is an observation, not proof of any particular gas, but this is an enclosed environment with ventilation districts and machinery exhaust. If atmospheric monitoring is required, use equipment suited to the actual hazards and know what its alarms mean. A generic mask or unfamiliar detector is not a substitute for that assessment.

Likewise, visible submerged heating wires should make you cautious, not curious. Do not probe panels, open cabinets, or lean on damaged electrical installations.

## Learn to read changes rather than scenery

Here, the most important landmarks are temporary states.

A bridge being dry tells you what it is doing now. A pump being silent tells you almost nothing about what it will do next.

Move from one verified safe stopping place to another. Before leaving a platform, know where you will stop and how you will retreat. Avoid routes whose entire safety depends on a single low crossing remaining exposed.

Obey the stated rules literally:

**Straight lines of sweating on a panel:** do not cross it. Keep clear while its heating and expansion cycle develops.

**Water suddenly stops in a channel:** do not enter. Treat the pause as a possible precursor to incoming flow.

**Blue lamps between yellow posts:** stay out. Do not attempt a quick crossing. Freezing wet clothing or footwear to the floor would turn a brief mistake into an immobilization emergency.

Also watch for less formal warnings: fresh cracks, shifting reflections, rising water against supports, or a newly altered drainage direction. If you cannot interpret a change, stop on known safe ground and ask your escort.

The small crustaceans retreating into joints and the reeds folding may indicate changes before you feel them. Notice those signals, but do not make animals your primary warning system.

## Keep well away from the intake

The intake deserves more caution than its apparent depth suggests.

Shallow water can still carry you off your feet, and grilles can trap a person against a flow they cannot overcome. Near-freezing water also compromises useful movement quickly. The slow movement of a glove toward the intake is not reassurance; it shows that transport is occurring even when the surface looks unremarkable.

Stay outside locally established exclusion boundaries. Do not approach to retrieve dropped equipment.

If someone falls, summon the site’s rescue response. Do not turn one casualty into two by entering the flow without training and appropriate equipment. Any rescue gear you carry should be part of an actual plan, not a length of rope you hope will become one.

## Treat warmth as an operating condition, not hospitality

The felt gardens and pale reeds will be tempting. They are almost the only things here that resemble a place made for living.

But a warm pipe joint may be leaking. A heated panel may be about to move. The disabled exchanger still warms intermittently, which means “disabled” does not necessarily mean isolated.

Use only designated heated shelters for recovery. Do not warm your hands on unfamiliar pipes or fresh cast material.

Leave the vegetation undisturbed. Its habitable territory may be only a few centimeters wide. Moving a pad, lifting an access panel, or taking a specimen could destroy the tiny combination of heat, moisture, and shelter that keeps it alive. Clean your equipment before and after visiting as well; do not introduce organisms or carry these ones into another world.

## Make a plan for the bells

For a first visit, observe the bell interval from a vetted dry platform with a protected route back. Do not go to the shoreline to see the climbing water closely.

Ask whether the operating cycle is predictable, but prepare for uncertainty. The reeds folding is a reason to check your position, not to wait for a better view.

When the bells begin, stop moving unless your escort directs otherwise. Let the interval pass. Do not touch the rising films, stand beneath wetted overhangs, or assume ordinary drainage behavior describes what is happening.

The account establishes that water climbs, then retreats when the machinery resumes. It does not establish what happens to a person caught in that motion.

The sudden quiet may also be disorienting. Resist the impulse to remove your hearing protection or walk toward a newly audible sound. The machines return in layers, and the first restart may not be the one nearest you.

## Prepare for the scale of it

Two kilometers under a hidden roof will feel larger than two kilometers outdoors. You will have few useful horizon cues. Machinery heard nearby may actually be beneath you or far across the basin. A visible platform may have no accessible connection to yours.

Budget for fatigue and a slower return. Cold, loudness, poor light, and continuous footing decisions consume attention even when nothing dramatic happens.

Expect the abandoned kiosks to feel intensely inviting. Their abrupt quiet may bring relief so strong that you linger too long. Before entering one, confirm that it is an approved refuge and that its exit will remain usable.

Use its maps as information, not authority. A predicted channel is reason to avoid an area; the absence of a predicted channel is not a guarantee.

There is real beauty here: moss tracing a buried circuit, little gardens surviving in failed seals, clear water briefly revealing the workmanship beneath it. Give yourself permission to look—but look from somewhere safe.

**The best preparation is to arrive with a local guide, dry insulation, reliable communications, and no need to prove that you can cross the basin.** Visit one accessible district, understand a little of its rhythm, and leave while you are still warm and alert. The basin will continue making itself after you go. You do not need to witness every stage.
