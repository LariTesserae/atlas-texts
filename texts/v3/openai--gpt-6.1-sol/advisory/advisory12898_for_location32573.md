Prepare for this country as you would for a hot, enclosed wilderness—but with the additional constraint that **almost everything you could stand on, lean against, or accidentally damage is alive**.

The description makes it sound hospitable. There is light, shelter, water, and room to walk. None of those things, however, has yet been shown to meet a human need safely. Your first visit should be a short reconnaissance with an easy return, not an attempt to settle in.

## Before you enter

Your most important piece of equipment is a dependable way out. Arrive with a known exit, a return deadline, and someone outside who expects to hear from you. Do not make your ability to leave depend on finding another shelter, another pool, or a corridor that looks promising.

Bring your own drinking water and food. For an initial visit, assume that nothing local is edible and that no local water is potable. The country may eventually prove generous, but it offers no familiar ecological evidence by which to judge that generosity: no soil, no litter, almost no feeding animals, no obvious history of human use.

Useful equipment would include:

- A portable oxygen and carbon-dioxide monitor, plus temperature and humidity measurement.
- Broad-soled, flexible footwear with good grip but no spikes or aggressive cleats.
- Lightweight clothing covering your skin, gloves, and clear protective glasses.
- A headlamp, even though the country appears thoroughly illuminated.
- A compass, inertial navigation aid, camera, and an offline mapping method.
- Freestanding, removable route markers.
- A closed sleeping system, insulating mat, and opaque eye mask.
- Sealed containers for food, waste, and anything you bring back.

Enter with clean gear. Remove seeds, soil, insects, and organic debris from your boots and pack. In a landscape where nothing has become soil, a handful of foreign dirt is not necessarily an insignificant introduction.

Do not bring a machete as your answer to difficult terrain. If you cannot pass without cutting, regard that route as closed.

## Establish what the air is doing

The place is full of light and living plantlike tissue, but that does not guarantee breathable air.

A continuous roof could restrict ventilation. Living tissues may consume oxygen as well as produce it, and the low corridors could have very different air from the broad hollows. Nor do you know whether this biology behaves like terrestrial vegetation.

Take readings at the entrance, at head height, close to the floor, and before entering enclosed shelters or descending into deep passages. A normal reading at one location is not a certificate for the whole basin.

A particulate mask may help with airborne material, if there is any. It will not correct low oxygen or dangerous carbon-dioxide levels. If the atmosphere proves unsuitable, an ordinary traveler should withdraw rather than improvise an extended visit.

The smell of warm sap is worth noticing, but do not use smell as a safety instrument. Familiar-smelling air can still be hazardous.

## Treat the heat as an exposure, not a discomfort

The most consequential detail may be that **there is no cool side of anything**.

On an ordinary hot day, you can seek shade, sit against cooler ground, or wait for evening. Here, the roof admits radiant heat, the floor supplies warmth, and the hollows offer no stated relief. A shelter may protect you from contact with the surroundings without cooling you at all.

Carry enough water to leave without drinking locally. Walk slowly, keep your load modest, and schedule stops before you feel exhausted. Do not assume that the apparently dry surfaces mean low humidity or effective evaporative cooling.

Bring a thermometer rather than judging conditions by how pleasant the light looks. Yellow-green brightness could make the whole place feel mild even while your body steadily accumulates heat.

Until you have observed a full cycle, do not count on night bringing cooler temperatures. The country may track the world above, or it may buffer its changes. Your watch is more trustworthy than your expectation of dusk.

## Learn how to walk again

The floor is not ground with vegetation on it. It is vegetation serving as ground.

A broad bark plate might support your weight comfortably; a similar-looking plate might bridge a space full of delicate stems. The fine felt underfoot may be resilient, or it may conceal openings. Test uncertain surfaces gently before committing your weight.

Take short steps. Favor broad, supported areas over narrow roots and folded leaf margins. Avoid jumping down from ridges: besides the risk to your ankles, you could crush a surface that has no loose substrate underneath it.

Keep your pack narrow and stow loose straps. This is a landscape where apparently empty space contains threads, tendrils, and folded blades. A dangling buckle may catch something you cannot see.

A walking pole could help, but give it a wide, soft tip and use it for balance rather than probing by force. Do not hammer tent stakes into the floor, drive pitons into roots, or carve steps into a slope.

If you break a surface and expose the green, white, or translucent pink tissue beneath, stop. Do not peel it farther to find out what is underneath. You have made a wound, not uncovered a trail.

## Navigate without depending on wear

The country is readable from a high place, but close up its repeated forms could become confusing: bundled roots, leaf sheaths, pale slopes, branching corridors.

There are no worn paths to reassure you that a route goes somewhere useful. Your own footsteps may leave no durable trace. Take photographs at junctions, including a view back toward the direction you came from. Record travel time and bearing between recognizable features.

Do not rely on satellite navigation beneath the roof. Test radio communication rather than assuming it will carry through miles of packed living material.

Use removable markers resting on firm surfaces—not ribbons cinched around stems, paint, or scratches. Count them out and collect them on your return.

The description says the country does not visibly grow. That is useful, but it is not yet proof that a passage will remain unchanged indefinitely. Observe the same junctions on your way back. Any clear change in their geometry is a reason to shorten the visit and reassess your navigation plan.

## Approach water conservatively

The pools are inviting partly because they are so clean-looking. Clear water, however, says little about dissolved chemistry or biological safety.

Treat local water as an emergency resource until tested. Filtration and disinfection can address some familiar hazards, but neither automatically makes an unknown sap-associated liquid safe to drink.

Do not climb up to disturb ceiling beads. Do not pull aside the fine roots lining a pool, deepen its basin, or try to connect neighboring pockets. Water here appears confined to particular spaces; altering those spaces could injure the surrounding growth.

If sampling is permitted, take a small sample with clean equipment from an accessible edge. Keep soap, food residues, sunscreen, and bodily waste away from the water.

Those shallow pools may be the entire accessible supply for whatever lives nearby.

## Treat the shelters as someone’s work

The chambers are the strongest evidence that the country is more than an unusual wilderness. Their regularity suggests intention, even though you cannot identify the maker.

An empty shelter is not necessarily an abandoned shelter. A clean floor does not establish that nobody uses it.

Before entering, announce yourself in an ordinary voice and wait. This is not a guarantee of communication; it is simply a reasonable courtesy in a place that appears prepared for occupants.

Inspect the chamber without rearranging it. Do not pull at wall recesses to find out whether they open. Test the sleeping shelf gradually before putting your full weight on it. Use a clean mat between yourself and the leaf.

Initially, remain near an unobstructed entrance. A two-entrance shelter may be preferable for visibility and access, provided both passages have been checked.

Do not light a fire inside—or elsewhere in the basin. Dry, papery surfaces and an uncertain enclosed atmosphere make combustion an especially poor experiment. Bring ready-to-eat food for the first visit.

Do not “improve” a chamber by widening its doorway or trimming its walls. Its useful shape may depend on relationships you cannot see.

## Prepare for brightness without openness

This place could be psychologically tiring in a way that is easy to underestimate.

You can see far, but never the sky. You can enter shelter, but not darkness. You can hear your own activity, but little that establishes the scale or rhythm of the world around you.

Bring an opaque eye mask and keep a deliberate sleep schedule. If staying longer becomes justified, use a freestanding light-blocking sleeping enclosure with adequate ventilation rather than trying to darken the living walls.

Maintain regular meals, check-ins, and a written log. Note headaches, unusual fatigue, anxiety, or difficulty judging time. Do not dismiss those symptoms as merely the strangeness of the place: heat and poor air can also affect attention.

There is no need to fear the silence, but it removes familiar information. No wind in branches tells you about changing weather. No ordinary animal chorus marks evening. You will have to supply your own routine.

## Leave very little behind

Do not handle the pale animals or collect their cast skins during an initial visit. Avoid leaning against a creature simply because it resembles the root beneath it.

Pack out all waste, including human waste. Nothing here suggests a familiar decomposing soil layer into which leftovers can harmlessly disappear. Do not scatter food “for the animals,” and do not leave gifts in the shelters without knowing who might receive them.

On departure, inspect your clothes and equipment for attached organisms or plant fragments. Keep any authorized samples sealed and isolated until they can be examined safely.

Above all, resist interpreting the country’s unfinished appearance as a request for completion. The half-unrolled leaves, suspended root tips, and almost-trunks may be healthy, stable forms—not things waiting for a traveler to help them along.

The right first visit would be modest: measure the air, walk a short mapped route, observe a water pocket, inspect a shelter respectfully, and return while you still have ample reserves.

**Prepare to be a careful guest, not a pioneer.** This country offers room without offering emptiness. The safest way to visit is to remember that distinction every time you put down your foot, your pack, or your hand.
