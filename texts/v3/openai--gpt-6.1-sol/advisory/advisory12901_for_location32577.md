Prepare for this place as you would for a warm, rain-soaked forest crossed with a floating wetland—but remember that neither comparison quite works. The ground is also the plumbing, the structure, and the habitat. What looks like a hillside may be a roof. What looks like a fallen tree may be holding up the route you intend to take.

Your first visit should be short, lightly loaded, and easy to reverse. The greatest danger is likely not an obvious monster or a dramatic collapse. It is making an ordinary assumption about what can bear your weight.

## Before you arrive

**Establish that a human can safely breathe there.** The description suggests familiar biological processes, but not necessarily familiar atmospheric chemistry. A high plateau may have thin air even when it is warm and richly vegetated. Deep decomposition could also create pockets of poor air in enclosed hollows.

If your travel equipment allows it, measure oxygen, pressure, and potentially hazardous gases at the arrival point. Carry a compact air monitor into chambers and depressions. Do not assume that a shelter occupied by local animals is safe for human lungs.

Arrive during the brighter part of the day, at a known landing place rather than wherever the scenery looks inviting. Ideally, choose a clearing with a history of supporting visitors and a route to shelter that has been checked recently.

If there are local guides, hire one. Their most valuable knowledge may be how the ground *feels*: which kind of yielding is normal, which kind signals a hollow, and which roots should never be loaded. A map cannot teach that.

If no one knows the terrain, treat the first visit as reconnaissance. Do not plan an inland crossing.

## Dress for perpetual dampness

Expect to be wet even when it is not raining. Leaves will empty onto you; your hands will touch wet supports; the ground may release water under your feet.

Wear:

- Quick-drying layers rather than absorbent, heavy clothing.
- Long trousers and sleeves for contact with unfamiliar plants and animals.
- Lightweight gloves with good grip.
- Footwear with a broad, stable sole, secure fastening, and moderate traction.
- Gaiters to keep fibers, small animals, and loose plant material out of your boots.
- A rain shell with generous ventilation.

Rigid mountaineering boots and aggressive spikes are not automatically useful here. They may punch through a surface that a broader sole would distribute your weight across. Conversely, very soft shoes may offer too little protection from hidden stems. Test footwear at the arrival site before committing to a route.

Carry spare socks and a dry sleeping layer in genuinely waterproof bags. In a warm, humid place, feet can deteriorate long before you feel cold. Clean and dry them whenever you have a secure opportunity.

Do not confuse warmth with protection from exposure. An exhausted person wearing wet clothes can still become chilled, especially near the windier rim.

## Pack lightly, but keep the essentials dry

A heavy pack adds stress to every step and makes self-extraction harder. Bring enough to manage a delay, not enough to build a settlement.

Useful equipment would include:

- Two walking poles, one suitable for gently probing ahead.
- A headlamp and backup light; daylight beneath the crowns may be inadequate even at noon.
- Waterproof navigation equipment and an independent backup.
- A reliable communication or return device.
- A water-treatment system appropriate to the local water chemistry.
- Sealed food, with extra for an unplanned overnight stay.
- First aid supplies emphasizing wound cleaning, dressings, and foot care.
- A compact emergency shelter and insulating pad.
- Rope and a harness, **if you know how to use them**.
- A small rescue tool for freeing yourself from tangled equipment, not for casually cutting living supports.

Keep critical equipment attached securely. A dropped lamp or communicator may disappear into a slit in the ground without making a sound.

Before departure, leave someone your route, turnaround time, and overdue procedure. If your multiverse transit can be fixed to an arrival point, make that point unmistakable and protect its power supply from moisture.

## Learn how to walk again

On arrival, spend time testing a small, safe area.

Notice how the surface responds to a stationary foot, a step, and a shift of weight. Watch whether water merely wells up or whether the surrounding vegetation starts to move. Probe gently ahead, but remember that a pole samples only a narrow point. Firm resistance could be one stem spanning an empty chamber.

Move slowly enough to keep your balance without grabbing whatever is nearest. Use short steps and avoid jumping.

**Do not assume that another person’s successful crossing proves the route can carry you.** They may be lighter, may have found a supporting cord, or may have weakened the mat.

Particular warning signs include:

- A surface that continues sinking after you stop moving.
- Water rising rapidly rather than simply wetting a footprint.
- Cracks or openings widening around your feet.
- Movement extending well beyond where you stepped.
- A hollow sound or sudden loss of resistance beneath a familiar surface.
- A route visibly lifting or shifting ahead of you.

None of these signs has a certain interpretation without local knowledge. Their practical meaning is simpler: stop adding load, and retreat if you can.

If you begin to break through, avoid frantic stamping. Spread your weight and work back toward the last surface that supported you. Your companions should not rush into the same weak patch. A rope helps only if the rescuer and anchor are secure; a conveniently placed root is not automatically a safe anchor.

Avoid entering concealed water. The flooded hollows are occupied, and the underside of a floating mat may be a maze of roots and air chambers rather than an open space from which you can easily climb out.

## Navigate without trusting the path behind you

The land has few sharp landmarks, and footsteps begin disappearing almost immediately. A ridge may be difficult to recognize from either side. Streams are particularly poor guides: they divide, disappear, and migrate.

Use position fixes where available, but expect canopy interference. Supplement them with compass bearings, elapsed travel time, photographs, and a route log. Record junctions from the direction you will see them on the return.

If markers are needed, use removable, non-damaging ones. Do not cut blazes into plants that may be structural.

Set a conservative turnaround time. Returning through unfamiliar green-brown twilight will take longer than walking outward in daylight. Carry enough light to inspect the ground, not merely enough to see the next trunk.

Old route information should be treated cautiously. A hundred-year-old map might be valuable history and terrible walking advice. Even a recent route could change with water levels or root growth.

## Water and food require more caution than their abundance suggests

Clear water is not necessarily clean water. Here, much of it has passed through warm biological material and may have circulated within the same district for a very long time.

Bring an initial supply of known-safe water. Use locally validated treatment if available. Ordinary filtration or boiling may address some biological hazards, but neither guarantees removal of dissolved toxins or unfamiliar plant compounds.

Do not drink from beads being lifted along stems, translucent bulbs, or apparently convenient cavities. Those may contain concentrated secretions rather than ordinary water.

Likewise, do not taste the plants because local animals graze on them. Their digestion may depend on adaptations or symbionts you do not possess. Carry your own food, and keep it sealed to avoid attracting animals or introducing foreign organisms.

## Treat shelters as living spaces, not empty buildings

The woven chambers may be the most comfortable places available: dry floors, drainage, warmth, and protection from prolonged rain. They may also be active animal shelters or structures whose behavior no visitor fully understands.

Before entering:

1. Watch for repeated animal traffic.
2. Check the air.
3. Identify an unobstructed exit.
4. Inspect the floor and roof without pulling or cutting.
5. Keep your first stay brief.

The leaves turning inward around a seated visitor need not mean danger. It could be a response to heat, moisture, touch, or something else. But it is enough reason not to make your first experiment an overnight sleep.

Do not wedge a living doorway open or trim growth for convenience. Move elsewhere if a chamber is changing around you.

Avoid fire. Wet vegetation is not proof against combustion, and a fire in buried organic material could be difficult to detect or extinguish. Bring ready-to-eat food or a heat source explicitly approved for the site.

## Remember that the ground carries sound

The animals here often perceive movement through the structure beneath them. Your footsteps, dropped pack, or repeated probing may announce you more clearly than your voice.

That does not mean you should attempt perfect silence. It means you should travel without unnecessary pounding and remain attentive to vibrations. Stop occasionally. Let the ground settle. Listen through your soles as well as your ears.

Do not handle sleeping animals disguised as root knots, reach into warm cavities, or investigate a patch of vegetation that lifts from below. Give unseen occupants room.

A sudden local shudder is a reason to pause, not hurry closer.

## Stay well back from the edge

The rim offers the clearest view and perhaps the least forgiving footing.

A grassy-looking overlook may be an overhang of roots and accumulated growth, with no rock immediately beneath it. Cloud conceals both the drop and possible breaks in the descending shelves. Do not approach until you have reliable knowledge of the supporting structure.

Never descend toward the lowlands merely because the next green shelf looks reachable. The shelves may have no continuous route between them, and their undersides shed water into unseen space.

## Leave the place biologically intact

Clean your boots and equipment before arrival and again before returning home. Do not bring seeds, soil, untreated wooden gear, or loose organic waste. Pack out food scraps and human waste.

Here, disturbing a plant is potentially disturbing infrastructure. Digging a latrine, cutting a walking stick, or clearing a campsite could damage drainage or weaken a surface. Use established visitor facilities if any exist; otherwise, bring a self-contained sanitation system.

Take no living samples without informed permission and proper containment.

Above all, prepare to be patient. This country will not offer firm ground simply because you need it, or an obvious path simply because you are lost. Allow time to test, observe, and turn back.

The right first visit ends with dry reserve clothing still unused, an intact return route, and the feeling that you saw less than you hoped—but learned how to place your feet.
