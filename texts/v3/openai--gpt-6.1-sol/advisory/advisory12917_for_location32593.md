## Prepare for a submerged landscape, not a ruin you can walk through

The most important fact about this place is that **there may be no usable surface above you**. Pale light is not a promise of open air. What looks like an outdoor avenue may lie beneath a thick mat of vegetation; what looks like an open courtyard may have a submerged roof just beyond visibility.

An ordinary human should not arrive here with a lamp and the intention of exploring. The first visit should be a reconnaissance from a verified refuge, with a short route and a dependable way back.

The basin’s quiet is misleading. It does not need predators, violent currents, or mysterious forces to be dangerous. Cold, depth, entanglement, lost visibility, and distance are enough.

### Establish somewhere you can actually survive

Before entering the water, confirm your arrival arrangements:

- **Will you arrive in air, on a platform, or already submerged?**
- **What is the depth and pressure at the arrival point?**
- **Is the water chemically compatible with your equipment and skin?**
- **Can you return without reaching a particular doorway or touching a particular object?**
- **Does the return mechanism work underwater, while you are wearing gloves, and if local visibility falls to zero?**

If your passage between worlds opens directly into this basin, treat its underwater operation as something to test before committing a person. A device that needs an unobstructed doorway, a dry surface, or several minutes of careful adjustment is not an adequate sole escape route.

Ideally, establish a dry, heated staging area with breathable air, communications, medical supplies, and equipment servicing. This might be on a verified accessible shore or in a purpose-built habitat. Do not assume the basin has a nearby shore merely because its terraces are shallow.

**If no secure refuge or dependable extraction is available, send instruments rather than yourself.**

### Learn the water before trusting it

The lack of animals is a reason to investigate, not a reason to relax.

It may reflect the basin’s isolation, low oxygen, unusual chemistry, or something specific to its ecology. The description does not establish which. Plants growing there do not prove the water is safe for humans.

Use a remote probe to measure temperature, depth, water chemistry, and conditions along the intended route. Check for acidity, salinity, and contaminants that could affect seals, metals, suit materials, or exposed skin.

Do not drink the water. Do not breathe from pockets trapped beneath ceilings. Such pockets may contain little oxygen and gases released by decomposition; their composition and pressure are unknown.

The water’s oxygen content also does not provide you with a breathing supply. Whatever lives here has adapted to this place. You have not.

### Dress for sustained cold, not merely immersion

This is cold water with no obvious warm layer to retreat into. The danger is cumulative: you may feel capable at first, then find your fingers clumsy and your decisions slower while the way home remains long.

Use professionally selected cold-water diving equipment, including a suitable drysuit, insulation, hood, gloves, and properly maintained breathing apparatus rated for the conditions. Equipment fit matters. A glove that keeps you warm but prevents you from operating your return device is not suitable.

Have thermal contingency plans, warm shelter after exit, and strict exposure limits. If you have never used this equipment, learn in a familiar environment first. This basin should not be where you discover how a drysuit changes your buoyancy.

Nor is ordinary recreational diving experience sufficient for its enclosed passages. **Entering beneath roofs, bridges, or vegetation that blocks ascent is overhead-environment diving**, requiring specialized training, equipment, and planning.

For a first visit, stay outside those spaces.

### Carry redundancy—and keep the expedition small

A reasonable human expedition needs trained companions, surface or habitat support, redundant breathing capability, independent lights, navigation instruments, and a rescue plan suited to the actual access point.

Useful equipment includes:

- A primary light and independent backup lights.
- Depth and exposure-monitoring instruments appropriate to the breathing system.
- Navigation and mapping equipment.
- Suitable line equipment, used by someone trained in its deployment.
- Accessible tools for dealing with plant or line entanglement.
- Underwater communications where feasible.
- A remote camera or small survey vehicle for inspecting openings before anyone enters.

Do not treat extra equipment as permission to take extra risks. Everything you carry also has bulk, snag points, operating demands, and failure modes.

Plan breathing reserves, exposure, and any decompression obligations with qualified dive professionals. “We can turn back when we have used half our air” is not an adequate plan for a cold, obstructed environment where the return journey may suddenly become slower.

### Expect visibility to disappear

The water appears quiet partly because the sediment has been left alone.

Your arrival changes that.

A fin stroke near the floor, a hand on a leaf-covered step, or contact with a wall can release silt that removes every visible landmark. Fine material may remain suspended long after you have lost your sense of direction.

Maintain controlled buoyancy and stay clear of the bottom. Do not walk along the paving in weighted boots. Do not use the stairs as stairs. Their missing lower steps and accumulated mud make them unreliable even before you consider the damage your passage would cause.

The architecture will tempt you to navigate as you would on land: through that opening, across that court, past those columns. Resist this. Repeated walls and terraces can look almost identical, and gradual darkness can make a distant structure seem merely obscured.

Record your route. Use reliable navigation references rather than memory alone. Where a guideline is appropriate, it must be installed and managed by trained divers, not casually trailed through plants and crumbling masonry.

### Treat the gardens as obstacles, not handholds

The long leaves and layered dead stems may catch equipment without appearing to move. Some are attached far from where they touch you. Pulling one strand could disturb an entire mat.

Avoid dense vegetation and entrances concealed behind it. Do not force your way into plant-filled chambers. If you cannot see the opening clearly, you cannot assess its clearance, structure, or exit.

Likewise, do not lean against masonry. These walls are slowly losing mortar and settling. A surface that has remained undisturbed for centuries may still fail under one visitor’s weight.

An isolated click or scrape deserves attention. It need not mean a major collapse, but neither should you dismiss it because the sound is small.

### Let the first journey be deliberately uneventful

Choose one open, surveyed area near the refuge. Visit briefly. Photograph a wall, a pavement joint, perhaps the exterior of the surviving stairway. Then return.

Do not make the farther colonnade your objective simply because it remains visible. Here, every completed vista reveals another one. The place has no obvious summit or central chamber to tell you that you have arrived.

Set turnaround criteria before entering: elapsed time, breathing reserves, cold, visibility, communications, equipment performance, and signs of instability. A companion should know these limits and be able to end the visit without debate.

### Prepare for the emotional weight of it

This place may feel less like a drowned city than a world patiently assembled from human-sized actions.

The hand-shaped pipe rims and sheltered chisel marks will bring its makers close. The avenues continuing beyond sight will make them impossibly distant again. You may find yourself wanting to follow one more wall to understand what all that labor was for.

That desire is probably the basin’s strongest lure.

Photograph rather than collect. Leave sediment, vessels, roots, and fallen plants where they lie. Their arrangements are part of the record, not clutter hiding it.

Expect your own breathing to be among the most persistent sounds you hear. Expect your lights to reveal only a small part of something immense. And remember that **a successful visit is not one in which you find the end of the basin. It is one in which you return safely, having disturbed very little.**
