Prepare for this place as you would for a **hot, exposed tidal wetland where a few centimeters of elevation can decide whether your way home remains walkable**. It looks quiet, but the water is continually rearranging the usable ground.

The chief danger is not a dramatic wave. It is failing to notice that the flats have stopped being land.

## Before entering

Find out how the tides work here. Do not assume another world shares familiar tidal periods, moon phases, or weather patterns. Ideally, observe a full local cycle from genuinely safe ground, or consult someone who regularly crosses these flats.

You need to know:

- When water begins rising, not merely when high tide occurs.
- Which channels fill first and which crossings become impassable.
- Whether wind or distant storms can raise the water beyond its usual reach.
- Where there is ground that remains dry during an unusually high tide.
- Whether that ground connects to your exit.

The low stone ridges are useful walking surfaces, but **they are not necessarily flood refuges**. A crest warm and dry at midday may be an isolated rock by evening.

If your return depends on a portal, vehicle, or extraction point, put it above the wetland’s flood reach. A fixed portal in a shallow depression could become submerged even while its surroundings still look passable.

For a first visit, choose a short route during a verified falling-water period, with ample daylight and a conservative turnaround time. Do not attempt to cross miles of flats simply because you can see miles of flats.

## Dress for wet feet, strong sun, and abrasive mud

Wear lightweight, long clothing that protects against sunlight and biting insects. Bring a broad-brimmed hat, sunscreen, and sunglasses; water and pale clay can throw light upward as well as receive it from the sky. A compact sun umbrella may be useful in calm conditions, though it should not be your only protection.

Footwear matters more than almost anything else. Choose secure boots or wetland shoes with:

- A sole that grips wet rock.
- Protection against sharp shells, stones, and unseen debris.
- Fastenings that mud cannot easily pull loose.
- Materials you can rinse and dry afterward.

Loose sandals are poor equipment here. Bare feet are worse: shallow water conceals hazards, and small cuts remain wet and dirty for hours.

Tall waterproof boots help on known, shallow routes, but do not let their height encourage deeper crossings. If water overtops them, they become troublesome. Chest waders are not a substitute for knowing the channels.

Carry spare socks and dry clothes in a waterproof bag. The day may be hot, but prolonged wetness, wind, and fatigue can still chill you after sunset.

## Bring your own drinking water

There is water everywhere, but that does not make it drinkable.

The mineral rims and salt-tolerant vegetation suggest that salinity varies from basin to basin. Even a pool that looks fresh may be brackish, biologically contaminated, or concentrated by evaporation. Ordinary filters and disinfectants do not remove dissolved salt.

Bring enough potable water for the planned visit, the return, and a delay. Bring food you already know your body tolerates. For an ordinary human crossing between worlds, local fish, succulent leaves, and apparently familiar shellfish should not be treated as emergency provisions without reliable information about their chemistry and biology.

Pack any human-specific medicines and a small first-aid kit, especially supplies for cleaning and covering foot injuries.

## Navigate deliberately

This is a landscape that can make you feel well oriented while giving you very little dependable information.

A low ridge disappears when viewed from another angle. A pool darkens under a cloud and seems deeper. A channel loses its edge as water rises. On the return journey, the route may look different even though you have barely changed position.

Use navigation tools that function in this universe. Do not assume terrestrial satellite navigation will work. Save your entrance location and route on an offline system, carry a suitable backup, and record distinctive landmarks or bearings where possible. A compass is useful only if the local magnetic environment makes it useful.

Travel with a companion, and leave your route and return deadline with someone who can help. Keep electronics, communication equipment, and portal controls protected from water and mud.

Sound is a poor distance guide here. A loud bird call need not come from nearby; the open ground carries it unusually well.

## Walk slowly enough to read the surface

Carry a sturdy probing pole. Test doubtful ground ahead, particularly near channels, root mats, crab burrows, and the margins of exposed shelves.

What looks like a firm clay flat may be a thin crust over soft sediment. What looks like one continuous rock surface may have a submerged gap. Favor verified stone and firmer margins rather than taking a straight line across every basin.

If you begin sinking, stop forcing the next step. Spread your weight, use the pole or firm support, and work back toward tested ground. Pulling hard against a trapped boot can unbalance you or leave you barefoot.

Stay out of channels unless you know their depth, footing, and current. A shallow-looking approach does not establish that the center is shallow.

Also watch for lightning. With so little vertical relief, you and your equipment may be among the tallest objects around. Identify actual shelter beforehand; crouching beside a low rock is not equivalent to reaching it.

## Let the animals keep their space

The sparse vegetation makes wildlife conspicuous, not necessarily approachable. Walk around feeding groups and nesting patches rather than through them. Repeated alarm calls, birds moving away, or adults hovering nearby are reasons to retreat.

Do not handle unfamiliar crabs, snails, fish, or frogs. Avoid putting insect repellents or other chemicals directly into the water, and carry out your waste. Afterward, clean mud from footwear and equipment and follow any interworld biosecurity procedures. This wetland should not acquire organisms from your home, nor your home organisms from it.

## Know when to leave

Turn back before fatigue, thirst, or the rising tide makes the decision for you. If separate pools begin joining across your return route, treat that as a warning—not an interesting development to watch a little longer.

The right way to experience this place is to make a modest excursion from a secure base, then pause on verified safe ground. Give yourself time to notice the tiny fish, the scattered birds, the heat coming off the stone, and the sounds arriving across apparently empty miles.

It is a place for patient attention. But keep one part of that attention on the waterline: **the birds may escape the tide by moving uphill a few paces. You may need to leave altogether.**
