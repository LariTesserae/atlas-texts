Prepare for this place as a **hot, active underground utility space**, not as a hidden courtyard. Its square-like proportions are misleading: the floor is partly drainage infrastructure, the walls carry working services, and the apparent stillness depends on pumps, power, and maintenance.

For an ordinary human traveler, the chief dangers are not the rats or insects. They are heat, potentially unsafe air, contaminated water, electricity, and losing a safe route back to the stair.

## Arrange the visit before descending

Go with someone who maintains the court. Ideally, visit during a scheduled inspection, when a worker can explain which surfaces are safe to stand on, how the pumps behave, and whether any conditions have changed.

Ask specifically:

- Has the atmosphere been tested, and is ventilation adequate for people to enter?
- Is rain expected, or has there recently been heavy rain upstream?
- Are the pumps and lighting operating normally?
- Is any electrical equipment, pipe insulation, or masonry known to be damaged?
- Where can visitors stand without obstructing work?
- What is the evacuation signal, and who remains above to summon help?

The extractor’s hum is not proof of breathable air. Underground drainage spaces can accumulate dangerous gases or have too little oxygen, and neither a familiar smell nor the absence of a smell establishes safety. If the site requires confined-space entry procedures, follow them; a handheld detector and a companion are not substitutes for an authorized entry and rescue plan.

Do not make this a solitary exploratory descent. If the court’s operating condition cannot be established, observe from an approved position above rather than enter.

## Dress for wet footing and oppressive heat

Wear boots with good slip resistance and protection appropriate to an industrial site. Expect algae, grease, fine silt, and condensation—not merely clean water on concrete. Waterproof footwear is useful, but it is not permission to wade.

Choose lightweight, close-fitting work clothing that covers your skin without hanging loose. Long trousers protect against rough brick, insects, and small abrasions; loose cuffs, dangling straps, and trailing coats can catch on projecting rods or equipment. Use a suitable safety helmet where required: low pipes and brick vaults make head protection sensible.

Bring work gloves for approved contact with railings and other surfaces, but avoid treating gloves as electrical or thermal protection. The stair rail is already unpleasantly hot, and a leaking pipe or exposed component may be much hotter.

Do not disturb the swollen, split pipe insulation. In an old installation, its composition may be unknown, and damaged insulation is something for qualified workers to assess—not something to brush aside for a better look.

A sealed, lightweight bag is preferable to an open backpack. Keep its exterior simple enough to wipe clean afterward.

## Carry very little, but carry the right things

Useful essentials are:

- **An independent light**, with a backup, both suitable for the site’s assessed hazards. One of the fixed lamps depends on pump power.
- **Drinking water**, kept closed and consumed in a clean area outside the court.
- **A reliable means of communication**, checked before entry. Street-level reception may disappear underground.
- **A clean covering for any cuts**, applied before arrival.
- **Any monitoring or protective equipment required by the site’s safety assessment.**

A particulate mask does not make unsafe underground air safe. Ordinary boots and gloves do not make wet electrical equipment safe either.

Leave unnecessary valuables, food, and cumbersome camera equipment above. You want your hands free and your exit uncomplicated.

## Plan a short visit

The heat will probably feel worse than the description first suggests. There is little air movement, warm surfaces surround you, and the damp air will limit the usefulness of sweating. Even standing still may become tiring.

Arrive rested and hydrated. Avoid making the descent immediately after strenuous walking through the market. Agree on a short initial visit with your escort rather than deciding to stay until you feel uncomfortable.

Headache, nausea, dizziness, weakness, confusion, or unusual breathlessness are reasons to leave immediately and notify the escort. Do not assume they are merely reactions to the smell or atmosphere. If someone collapses below, do not attempt an unprotected rescue; call the designated emergency response.

## Treat the walkway as your territory

Stay on the raised maintenance walk and other surfaces explicitly approved by the worker. The brick islands are visually inviting, but they are not necessarily a visitor route. A brick can rock, a landing can be slick, and shallow water can conceal an inspection opening or an abrupt change in level.

The unfinished clay deserves particular restraint. Its dark surface gives little indication of firmness, and the mound behind the mesh is visibly changing even without anyone touching it. Do not climb it, step around its fence, or test it with a boot.

Keep away from:

- The overflow channel and open sumps.
- Projecting reinforcing rods.
- Electrical cabinets, especially where condensation or leakage is present.
- Hot pipes and their joints.
- The recessed pump bay unless escorted into it.
- Inspection covers, valves, and service doors you have not been authorized to use.

Do not turn a valve, lift a cover, move a screen, or interrupt a pump to hear the room more clearly. These are operating systems, not exhibits.

Before settling anywhere to watch, look back toward the stair. Keep that route unobstructed and know how you will follow it if the lighting changes.

## Expect an unpleasant intimacy, not an animal attack

The creatures mostly occupy precisely the places a visitor might reach for balance: hinges, seams, pipe brackets, cover rims, and the underside of the stair. Look before placing a hand.

The rats’ unhurried movement should not be mistaken for tameness. Give them room, do not feed them, and do not corner one between yourself and its ledge. Insects may land on you; resist swatting while negotiating the stairs or a narrow walk.

Do not lift bricks to inspect the pale insects underneath. Besides disturbing their galleries, you would be handling a contaminated object and potentially changing your footing.

Keep hands away from your face. Avoid water contact, especially with broken skin, and do not eat below. After leaving, wash your hands thoroughly and clean your footwear and equipment somewhere appropriate, rather than carrying drainage residue through living quarters.

## Prepare your attention, too

At first, you will probably notice steam, dimness, and the sense that things are moving just outside clear sight. Give your eyes time to adjust from the street before walking farther in.

Then the court will begin to resolve into small territories: rats on the ledges, midges beneath the lamps, cockroaches in the cabinet seams, algae in the channels. The apparently empty floor is not lifeless; much of its life is too small to see from standing height.

The pump rhythm may become oddly reassuring. Do not let familiarity with it replace attention to your escort or the equipment. Ask which sounds are normal and what changes require departure.

A good visit here is brief and mostly stationary. You do not need to cross the wet floor to understand the place. From one safe position, you can watch a rat complete its route, see a drift of midges move around a lamp, and hear the delayed rush of water after a pump stroke.

Leave while you are still comfortable enough to climb the stair steadily. The court rewards patience, but not endurance. Its slow changes belong to people who return with tools and a reason to be there—not to visitors who stay until the heat makes the decision for them.
