Prepare for this place as an active geological disaster that happens to look, at intervals, like a landscape.

The greatest danger is not a spectacular eruption. It is that you will accept something as settled—a floor, a ridge, a distance, a way back—and continue acting on that assumption after it has ceased to be true. Here, the country can change faster than your understanding of it.

For an ordinary human traveler, the safest visit would be short, well supported, and mostly conducted from outside the rising ground.

## Before you cross

**Do not assume that the air is breathable simply because the place has wind.** Thin air, mineral vapor, and an unfamiliar biosphere are three separate uncertainties. Establish atmospheric pressure, oxygen availability, temperature, and gas composition with instruments before exposing yourself.

The bitter smell of the fissures is not a useful safety test. Some dangerous gases have little smell; others overwhelm your ability to notice them. Sheltered hollows may collect gases that are less concentrated on an exposed ridge. A place that feels pleasantly out of the wind may be the worse place to breathe.

Ideally, you should arrive with:

- An independently supplied breathing system, with enough reserve for a delayed departure.
- An enclosed suit suitable for the measured pressure and temperature.
- Active cooling, not merely insulating clothing.
- A helmet with eye protection against fine abrasive dust and flying mineral fragments.
- Multiple lights, independently powered.
- Thermal imaging and ordinary visible-light imaging.
- A surveying or ranging instrument that does not depend on a local satellite network.
- Continuous communication with someone remaining outside the hazardous area.

Check gravity, too. The description gives you a sense of height, not a measurement of weight. Your pack, your ability to recover from a stumble, and the energy required to climb back out all depend on it.

If you cannot establish these basic conditions, this should be a remote observation visit, not a walk.

## Make your way home independent of the mountains

Your exit is the most important piece of equipment.

A portal fixed to a slab of stone may move with that slab. A beacon may remain intact while a fissure opens between you and it. A vehicle can be perfectly operational and still become unreachable. Do not count “the equipment is working” as equivalent to “I can leave.”

If your travel system permits it, use an exit that can be activated from your present position and does not require a stable patch of local ground. If it does not, keep the main crossing point on the last level mineral plain, well back from the abrupt shelves, and confirm that this lower ground is itself stable.

Have someone watch that crossing continuously. They should be watching its surroundings as well as its status.

Set departure conditions before entering:

- A crack appears anywhere along the return corridor.
- The exit area shifts, tilts, or begins venting.
- Ground movement accelerates.
- Visibility drops below what you need to recognize nearby edges.
- Cooling, breathing, communications, or lighting loses redundancy.
- You experience headache, confusion, unusual fatigue, or impaired coordination.

These are reasons to leave, not reasons to stop and investigate.

Give yourself a generous turnaround time. Do not spend the reserve on getting closer to the blade at the center.

## Dress for heat from below

This is not ordinary high-altitude cold. Conventional mountain clothing may trap exactly the heat you need to lose.

You need protection against hot surfaces, radiant heat, sharp glass, and warm abrasive dust. Boots should have soles rated for the actual temperatures you measure, with puncture resistance and secure footing on both smooth and granular rock. Gloves should allow you to handle equipment without exposing skin. Knees and elbows need protection because a minor stumble could become a contact burn.

But no boot makes newly forming crust safe.

A darkened skin over a fissure may be cooler at its surface while remaining weak or hot underneath. A thermal image helps you identify some hazards; it does not certify that a surface can bear you. Nor does an apparently cool reading establish what lies under a slab.

Watch your cooling system’s performance continuously. In thin air, familiar ways of shedding heat may not work as expected. You could become dangerously hot without the surroundings ever feeling like a furnace.

Carry all your water. There is nothing here to refill from, and the mineral deposits should not be treated as ingredients, medicines, or emergency electrolytes.

## Bring light that reveals depth

A headlamp alone is inadequate for black cliffs, black floors, and black openings.

Use a broad light for nearby ground and a separate beam for inspecting the route ahead. Raking light can help reveal slight rises, cracks, and surface texture that frontal illumination conceals. Range measurements matter because extraordinary clarity in thin air does not mean your distance judgments will be good.

The central plain is especially deceptive. From above, its landmarks look gathered together. On foot, one of them is hours away. That difference is not just inconvenient: it can consume your breathing supply and heat budget before you recognize your mistake.

Do not use the copper horizon band as your only clock. Its return may suggest a cycle, but you have not established that the cycle is regular or that it corresponds to anything like an Earth day.

## Choose a modest route

For a first visit, I would stay near the lower margin, observe the shelves from a setback, and avoid entering the basin.

Do not choose the western heights merely because they offer the best view. Reaching a superb vantage point is not worthwhile if you must cross unstable shelves to get there.

Avoid:

- The bases of cliffs and columns.
- Overhangs, especially those supported by visibly hot joints.
- Glass faces that could shed fragments outward.
- Narrow shelves with no alternative retreat.
- Recently skinned fissures.
- Domes, even small ones.
- Any ground already developing a tilt.
- The edges of fresh clefts.

A gap that is easy to step over is still a warning, not a convenient crossing. Once you cross it, your return depends on it remaining small.

Ropes deserve particular caution. A rope helps only when its anchor and the terrain around it remain dependable. Here, a tether can connect you to a moving block or pull you toward a widening break. Do not improvise ordinary climbing systems on fresh pillars simply because the stone looks solid.

If you have reliable hovering or flight technology, it may remove some ground-contact risks, but it introduces others: sudden winds, airborne dust, poor visibility, and hot plumes. Use it only within tested operating limits, not as a general exemption from the landscape.

## Listen, but do not wait for a loud warning

The quiet is part of the hazard.

A small click, a strained creak, or pressure felt in the chest may accompany movement on a scale that your instincts expect to hear as a roar. Train yourself not to dismiss these signals because they sound unimpressive.

During the visit, stop periodically and compare the landscape with recorded reference images. Check fixed markers against one another. Is a wall leaning differently? Has a notch disappeared? Are two formerly separate ridges overlapping?

Continuous instruments can help detect movement, but no reading guarantees the next few minutes. The absence of visible change is not proof of stability.

If the ground begins moving beneath you, do not linger to discover the mechanism. Withdraw along the last verified route while it remains usable. If that route is compromised, use your independent extraction system rather than improvising a crossing over fresh crust.

## Do not use the stone structures as refuges

They are tempting precisely because they offer things a human recognizes: walls, roofs, shade, an interior.

But their comfort is not evidence of safety. One has already split with the ground. Another has a warm crack through its floor and an inward-leaning wall. A roof can be structurally sound while everything supporting it is becoming unsuitable.

The interiors may also trap mineral gases. Entering them adds an overhead collapse hazard and obscures your view of surrounding movement.

Observe them from outside. Do not straighten wedges, test the slabs, or move stones to see what is beneath them. Their purpose is unknown; their present instability is not.

If windblown dust becomes severe, use your protective equipment and withdraw. Do not let the availability of a dark, cooler room persuade you to shelter under a leaning roof.

## Treat the dark patches as living until you know otherwise

They may be organisms, colonies, mineral processes, or something for which human categories are inadequate. You do not need to settle that question during a first visit.

Do not touch them, peel them up, heat them, or collect their drifting scales. Avoid standing across the warm seams they occupy. Their relationship with escaping heat may be much more precise than it looks, and a hand-sized patch could represent a substantial amount of local life.

Keep your suit sealed around airborne scales as well as dust. On return, isolate and inspect exposed equipment before bringing it into an inhabited environment. Cleaning procedures should be chosen to avoid both contaminating home and casually destroying material from the site.

The salt branches deserve similar restraint. You already know a touch can collapse them. There is little to learn from confirming that with your fingers.

## Prepare for the emotional pull of the place

You will probably want to stay.

The landscape offers events that seem almost impossible to witness anywhere else: a ridge acquiring a new outline, a plain becoming a slope, bare ground still holding the heat of its creation. Because much of this occurs quietly, it may feel intimate rather than threatening. You may find yourself waiting for one more seam to light.

Build your limits around that temptation. Arrange for an outside observer to call the turnaround. Record from a safe position rather than following each new event. Bring enough magnification that curiosity does not always demand proximity.

And do not camp there on a first visit. Sleep would mean surrendering awareness while the route, floor, and skyline continue to change.

The right ambition is not to cross the basin or reach its most striking formation. It is to spend a little time seeing the country clearly, disturb almost nothing, and leave while leaving is still straightforward.

Here, a successful journey may consist of standing well back from the first shelf, watching a red joint brighten, and recognizing that you have already come close enough.
