Prepare as though you are visiting **a cold, electrically active desert whose ground, roads, and buildings are all alive**. The town makes the plateau habitable, but it does not make it an ordinary landscape. Your safety will depend less on toughness than on understanding where you may stand, what you may touch, and when to let the place finish what it is doing.

The most useful thing to bring is a local guide. The second is all the water you will need to leave again.

## Before you arrive

Arrange your arrival inside an inhabited building, during the quieter part of the local day. Avoid making your first appearance on an exposed hill, beside a freight intake, or during the evening discharge.

Ask your host to confirm several things rather than inferring them from the presence of a town:

- **Whether the atmosphere is breathable for humans.** A populated place need not contain human-compatible oxygen, pressure, or trace gases.
- **Whether the altitude poses a physiological problem.** Clouds below you do not establish the air pressure, but the possibility of hypoxia deserves checking.
- **Whether humans can safely contact the substrate.**
- **Where you can obtain compatible drinking water and food.**
- **What the local warning signals mean**, especially humming ground, closing seed cases, moving avenues, and tower activity.
- **How you leave if weather operations interrupt your intended route.**

Bring an independent return method if you have one. Confirm that it works amid strong electrical activity and from inside the shelter where you will stay. An exit device that requires an open horizon would be particularly inconvenient here.

Keep your first visit short. One locally guided daylight excursion and a night in a prepared room would teach you more than a long unsupported crossing.

## Dress for cold, wind, and extreme dryness

The absence of frost is not evidence of mild weather. This place can be bitterly cold without displaying the familiar signs of cold on a water-rich world.

Wear windproof outer clothing, adjustable insulation, a face covering, and eye protection. Protect lips, nostrils, and exposed skin from drying. Avoid relying on cotton next to your skin: your own perspiration may be the wettest thing you encounter, and it can still chill you.

Boots should have broad, stable soles with good grip and no aggressive spikes. Much of what looks like loose gravel is a living seed field; what looks like a firm ridge may flex. You want to distribute your weight without grinding, puncturing, or getting caught between moving scales.

Electrical protection needs local advice. Ordinary rubber soles are not a complete answer, and neither is deliberately grounding yourself into the plateau. The ground is part of the electrical system. Depending on how charge moves here, either isolation or an unintended conductive path could create trouble.

Use locally approved protective equipment if available. Otherwise:

- Keep dangling metal and exposed conductors to a minimum.
- Secure straps and loose clothing.
- Protect electronics against static and surges.
- Do not improvise grounding wires, conductive stakes, or “lightning protection” by attaching equipment to living roots.

A dust filter may help with shed tissue and conductive particles, but it does not make unknown air breathable or stop every chemical vapor. Have the appropriate respiratory equipment for whatever your pre-arrival checks establish.

Bring ear protection that still permits conversation and warning sounds. The place’s low chords and structural groans may become exhausting long before they seem painfully loud.

## Treat water as both a supply problem and a compatibility problem

You should assume you will find **no usable environmental water**.

There is no stream to follow, no snow to melt, no damp hollow to search. Cutting into a plant will not give you drinking water; its circulating fluid is an unfamiliar solvent. Do not taste it, cook with it, or assume its clear appearance means anything useful.

Carry drinking water in robust, insulated, well-sealed containers, with a reserve for a delayed departure. A water-rich human can dehydrate here while feeling cold and barely noticing sweat. Sip regularly rather than waiting for strong thirst.

Bring familiar food as well. Nothing about the grazers’ feeding or the plants’ biology establishes that local material is edible to you.

Ask your host how human water should be handled. Water may be innocuous, valuable, chemically disruptive, or harmful to particular tissues; the description does not settle that question. Until you know:

- Do not pour leftovers onto the street.
- Do not wash dishes against a wall or root.
- Do not urinate outdoors.
- Keep wastewater contained.
- Ask before using a humidifier or releasing substantial steam indoors.

You are carrying a substance this ecosystem keeps tightly sealed. That deserves caution, not an assumption that everyone will either fear it or welcome it.

## Learn to walk without treating the ground as inert

Take short, deliberate steps. Stay on established avenues until a guide has shown you how to read the terrain.

A surface can be alive without being fragile, and this plateau plainly tolerates traffic. Still, avoid unnecessary damage. Do not pry up scales, drive stakes, scrape away tissue for samples, or break off a violet-lined flake as a souvenir. A wound sealing quickly does not mean the wound was harmless.

The low hills may conceal changes in load and tension. Pause if the surface begins to contract, lift its vanes, hum, or arrange its seed cases beneath you. Let a local tell you whether the correct response is to stand still, move onto an avenue, or leave the area.

Keep clear of the open spaces surrounding solitary plants. Those boundaries already repel younger growth. That does not prove they are dangerous to you, but it is a strong reason not to treat them as convenient picnic sites.

Avoid the southern rim without an escort. This is not simply a scenic cliff: beneath it are root buttresses, conducting loops, cloud, and electrical discharge. Do not trust an apparent overhang, or assume a root makes a suitable climbing anchor.

## In town, patience is a practical skill

The town responds to people, but you should not assume it interprets every human action correctly.

For your first journeys, let your host operate the road interface. Find out whether it requires bare skin, whether human contact is safe, and how to make a request the avenue can recognize. Do not remove an electrically protective glove simply because you have seen someone else press a palm to the ground.

Once a route is requested, give it time. The town shortens journeys after the traveler’s breathing settles, not when the traveler becomes more insistent. Arrive with slack in your schedule.

**Do not build a plan that requires running through these streets.** In a genuine emergency you may need to move quickly, but frantic movement is plainly not the town’s normal operating mode.

Ordinary maps may become misleading when distances and relationships between buildings change. Use named destinations, local guidance, and agreed meeting points rather than “the third building on the left.” Do not assume a GPS-style position fix will remain useful.

Doorways also need explanation. Ask how to enter, how to leave, and what to do if a seam does not open. Never wedge a living door with a knife or cut your own opening.

## Choose accommodation with your body in mind

A room that changes shape at night could be comfortable once you understand its limits. Before sleeping, ask your host to demonstrate what moves.

Choose a room prepared for visitors, with:

- Enough clearance around the bed and your equipment.
- A known exit that remains usable at night.
- A way to summon help without leaving.
- A safe place for water containers and electrical devices.
- Explicit permission before any nighttime contraction affects occupied space.

Do not leave your pack in an apparently unused recess. The building may decide that recess is no longer needed.

A freestanding or visitor-approved sleeping surface is preferable to lying directly against an unknown wall or floor. If you must camp, use only an approved site and a shelter that does not require puncturing the substrate.

Do not light a fire merely because the country resembles dead wood. Much of it is living, and its response to heat, smoke, or combustion is unknown. Use only a cooking or heating method approved locally.

## Give infrastructure room to work

Freight stems are not ordinary pipes. Cargo disappears into a husk and arrives elsewhere without traversing the intervening length. That is enough to justify strict boundaries.

Do not climb into an intake, use a husk as a hiding place, or assume a freight system can transport a person. Human passage would need its own verified procedure.

Keep away from active mouths and visibly repaired sections unless escorted. An apparently abandoned stem may contain animals, or it may simply be out of service rather than safe to explore.

Expect the brief jaw ache and hollow report around transfers. Ask how far away sensitive visitors should stand and whether repeated exposure is advisable.

Likewise, do not approach the weather towers while they work. A sudden ribbon of wind is an operation, not merely a gust. The towers reach into neighboring versions of the weather; your own portal or navigation equipment may need to remain inactive nearby.

## Assume wildlife notices you differently

Many animals here reveal themselves only when you stop. Before sitting down, opening a bag, or crouching to examine something, watch the ground for a while.

Do not feed the grazers. Keep fingers out of lifted scales and shallow channels. Let the small creatures close their own openings rather than helping.

Give the pale browsers space, especially when the plant beneath them begins to hum. Their tolerance of a feeding wound is not permission for you to approach it.

For predators, visibility is not assurance. A flattened animal can cross a basin without triggering the structures that would announce other creatures. Travel with companions outside town, avoid hollows behind standing fans, and ask locals which behaviors indicate danger. Do not invent a defense based on the animal’s resemblance to a terrestrial species.

## Be indoors before the evening electrical work

For a first visit, observe dusk through a window membrane.

The rising vanes, trembling fans, resin smell, and violet veins are beautiful, but they describe an ecosystem moving substantial charge. Do not stop on an exposed slope to photograph the flash.

Follow the local storm procedure. Keep clear of conductive fixtures and designated current-carrying surfaces. Do not assume every building offers equal protection, or that lying flat on the living ground is sensible.

Have your light, footwear, return equipment, and emergency supplies accessible before the room changes shape. During a discharge is a poor time to discover that your bag has been enclosed behind a newly thickened wall.

## Prepare for the feeling of the place

The hardest adjustment may be that there is nowhere truly inert to rest your attention.

The floor has internal traffic. The walls make decisions. The horizon folds up its leaves. Even a broad, empty square is occupied tissue, and even your route home may be waiting for you to breathe more slowly.

The constant sound may disturb sleep or make you feel that something enormous is always approaching. Take breaks indoors, use hearing protection when appropriate, and let a guide distinguish ordinary load adjustment from a warning. You should not have to remain vigilant against every click and groan.

Bring familiar comforts: food you recognize, a warm layer that smells like home, a quiet activity, a watch that does not depend on the sky. Allow several days for adaptation if you stay longer.

Above all, visit as a guest rather than an explorer looking for empty ground. There is no empty ground here. With a patient host, enough sealed water, and a willingness to let the town set the pace, the plateau could be extraordinary: a whole country sustaining itself through cold, wind, and charge, with human passage accommodated inside that work.
