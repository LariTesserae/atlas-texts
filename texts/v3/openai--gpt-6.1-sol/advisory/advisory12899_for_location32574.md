The first thing to prepare is your restraint. This is a country you could destroy while trying to find it.

Do not lift the stone. Do not slide a knife beneath it, scrape away the pale seam, or test whether the light survives exposure to the sky. The stone has not yet acquired an underside; making one by force may be a much larger intervention than turning over a rock ordinarily is. Whatever permits the country to exist seems bound up with its being sheltered without quite being enclosed.

Kneel beside it. Keep your hands in your lap. Let finding the entrance take longer than finding the stone.

## Arrange a way to become a visitor, rather than a disturbance

An ordinary human cannot simply crawl into a country the size of two cupped hands. You will need some means of crossing the scale difference: a traveler’s aperture, a temporary change of scale, or an entrance that admits you into the country’s own distances.

Choose a method that does **not** require widening the seam.

More importantly, make sure it takes you into one coherent version of the place. The country is not merely small. A finger can cover its sky while an animal spends an afternoon getting beyond that finger’s shade. Several creatures can cross what looks like the same bare patch without quite sharing it. A device that makes you tiny but leaves you halfway attached to outside geometry may not be enough.

Before leaving, establish three things:

- You can enter without displacing the stone or its supporting earth.
- Your equipment will accompany you at the same scale and in the same version of the terrain.
- You can leave without depending on a particular doorway, ridge, or hollow remaining where it was.

A return mechanism tied to your person is preferable to one planted in the ground. The ground is still deciding what it is.

If you cannot arrange a reliable exit under those conditions, visit only from outside. Kneeling observation is still a visit, and a better one than becoming stranded in a landscape that can open beneath you.

## Pack for a cold, damp place with no darkness

Wear close-fitting, flexible clothing and soft, broad-soled shoes. Avoid heels, hard tread, dangling straps, and anything that might catch in a seam. Your footwear should spread your weight rather than bite into the surface.

The white earth is yielding, but it is not necessarily forgiving. It briefly holds footprints and then fills them from below. That tells you it can repair a mark; it does not tell you that whatever lived in the mark survives.

Bring a light insulating layer and protection against condensation. The warmth on the stone’s upper face will not help you below it. Weather begins beneath your feet, and cold can arrive from a cavity you did not know was there.

You probably will not need a lamp. There is abundant light, including under things. Nevertheless, bring a very low-output light for emergencies, kept off unless needed. Do not assume that more illumination would improve the place. Its own light enters openings before there is room for it; yours may behave less delicately.

Useful supplies include:

- Your own drinking water in a sealed container.
- Food that produces no crumbs, drips, or strong scent.
- A compact medical kit suitable for your altered scale.
- A contained breathing reserve, or some other means of retreating if the air proves unsuitable.
- An eye covering for rest.
- A quiet means of taking notes.
- A return system that remains accessible with cold hands.

Leave loose powders, sprays, exposed adhesives, and trailing cords behind. In a country this small, ordinary debris could become a geological deposit. A cord laid across the plain might obstruct several routes you cannot see.

Test your kit before entering. Smallness should not be the first moment at which you discover whether a seal, valve, or fastener still works.

## Do not eat or drink anything local

The air smells of cold milk, but that is not evidence of nourishment. The ground resembles chalk, but that does not make it chalk. The grazers eat blue bloom from new stone, but their digestion tells you nothing about yours.

The water deserves particular caution.

At a bowl, a winged creature’s reflection rises before the creature arrives. They meet, exchange places, and the departing animal may be wetter, older, or carrying an egg. You do not know whether the water causes this, permits it, or merely marks the point at which it happens.

For a human traveler, that is enough reason not to drink, wash, or lean over a bowl.

Keep your face away from the surface. Observe from the side. If your reflection behaves independently, withdraw rather than trying to establish which of you is now the original.

Also remember that predators wait beside the bowls. A nearly invisible crack with long legs is still a predator, and your scale on entry may put you within its range. What looks like convenient firm footing at the water’s edge may be an animal waiting to close.

## Learn how to look before you start walking

From outside, nearly the whole country is visible. From within, visibility will not necessarily make it legible.

Spend your first interval still, on whatever surface your arrival method provides. Watch a ridge, a damp patch, and an apparently empty stretch of plain. Allow enough time to see something change.

Look for:

- Feelers projecting from boundaries.
- Transparent backs that slightly magnify the ground.
- Cracks with joints or suspiciously deliberate shapes.
- Plants bending toward a passing body.
- Small corrections in another creature’s route.
- A patch of cold shine spreading through the soil.

Do not navigate by shadows. They arrive late and may come from the opposite direction. They also graze. Here, a shadow is not a dependable indication of where a body presently stands.

Nor is silence evidence of safety. Feeding, predation, and an entire procession can occur without audible warning. A tiny click may be the loudest event in an hour, while much larger changes happen quietly.

Walk slowly enough to stop before committing your next foot. Prefer visibly firm, older ground when it is unoccupied. Avoid ridge crests, wet–dry boundaries, and the narrow interval between a slope and its still-arriving shadow. Those are inhabited seams, not empty margins.

If you meet an invisible resistance, stop. Do not push through it. Other creatures sometimes encounter an adjacent version of the terrain and wait for separation to return. That is a useful example to follow.

## Prepare for weather by choosing refuge early

You are unlikely to get a distant view of approaching weather. Its first warning may be the ground under you growing cold enough to shine.

Before exploring far, identify a broad raised place you can reach without crossing a water bowl or a fresh seam. It should be stable-looking, unoccupied, and not one of the domestic structures.

When moisture begins collecting beneath ridges or on resting animals, move toward that refuge. When the low mist rises, stop exploring.

Do not continue walking simply because your head remains above the fog. The plain can change while your feet are hidden. Basins deepen, ridges fork, and hollows open from below. A familiar route may still look familiar at eye level while no longer supporting your next step.

The local animals offer good warning signs: they climb; predators leave their cracks; houses close their doors. Follow the broad pattern, but do not crowd their refuge.

After the mist clears, reassess your surroundings before descending. Look for new hollows, newly established seams, and water where there was none. Do not assume your previous footprints still lead anywhere useful.

## Treat the dwellings as dwellings

You do not need to decide whether their occupants possess humanlike intelligence before respecting their homes.

The thresholds are swept. There are sleeping hollows. Water is kept inside. These are sufficient signs of deliberate use.

Do not put your head through a doorway, take a roof flake, or enter because nobody seems present. The occupants are rarely visible when the houses are, and a dome can get up and walk. “Unoccupied” may simply be a poor description of what you are seeing.

The ring of posts requires even more care. Young animals pause there while the ground firms beneath them; afterward, their legs no longer pass through the surface. It may be a nursery, a place of transition, or something for which human words are inadequate. Whatever it is, your presence could interrupt a consequential process.

Keep outside the posts. Do not step across the enclosed quiet, and do not place equipment nearby.

If a house approaches, give it space to settle. If a creature approaches, become still. Let contact, if any, be initiated locally. Do not try to attract attention with food or tapping.

## Leave the oldest things where they are

The dark grains may look like ideal souvenirs: small, solid, easy to carry.

They are also the oldest objects in the country.

In a place where so much is continually beginning, an old object may matter far beyond its size. You need not invent a particular function for it to recognize that removing it would be taking something scarce.

Take no grains, animals, moss, roof flakes, mineral crusts, or water. The moss is especially misleading: a cushion no wider than a mite contains a forest, and animals emerge carrying its spores. What appears to be a speck could be an entire inhabited environment.

Keep records instead. Note what you actually observed, and distinguish it from your guesses. “The house moved” is an observation. “The occupant carried it” may not be.

## Resist the smaller country

The opening near the boundary will be difficult to ignore.

It offers a second beginning: white ground, a bead of water, one green blade. You may feel that you have finally understood the first country well enough to enter the next.

Do not make that decision during your first visit.

You do not yet know whether those openings connect neighboring places, different ages of the same place, or a succession in which each passage changes the traveler. An animal returning with something dark and round demonstrates that a journey is possible. It does not demonstrate that yours would be reversible.

Observe the doorway from a respectful distance. If you later plan a descent, prepare it as a separate expedition with a separate return arrangement.

For now, one country is enough.

## Plan a short first visit—and an unusually gentle departure

Allow time to observe a weather cycle, but do not make enduring one a condition of success. Cold, unreliable footing, unusual bodily changes, or uncertainty about your exit are all good reasons to leave sooner.

Before returning, check yourself and your equipment for passengers. Look along seams in your clothing, beneath soles, and inside sheltered folds. Do not use a chemical sterilant in the country. If something has attached itself, give it an opportunity to leave onto suitable ground before you cross back.

Account for every item you brought. Depart without digging, shaking out debris, or marking the entrance.

Then leave the stone where it is.

The central preparation for this place is not courage or technical ingenuity, although you may need both. It is learning to inhabit a moment without trying to finish it. The valley is being made without erosion. The ground is becoming firm beneath young feet. A doorway can appear before there is room for its light.

You would be visiting a country in the act of acquiring its own ordinary things.

Go lightly enough that it can continue.
