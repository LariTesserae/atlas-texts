Prepare for this place as you would for a vast, occupied machine room in which almost every surface is also someone’s habitat.

It does not look hostile. That is not the same as being forgiving. Its chief danger is that it appears to have made provision for everything, and you may mistake that provision for an invitation.

## Before entering

Find out whether visitors are expected, and what counts as a visitor route. The broad sill, signs, stairs, and handrails suggest that someone is meant to move through this landscape, but they do not establish that every accessible surface is safe for an ordinary human. A ladder may be intended for a maintenance organism, a specialized machine, or a worker equipped for pressures you cannot tolerate.

If there is an operator, guide, or entry protocol, use it. Ask four questions first:

- Is the atmosphere safe for human breathing, including near ventilation outlets and inspection shafts?
- Which routes are rated for human weight and ordinary footwear?
- Are there restrictions on introducing biological material?
- How does a visitor request help and return to the entry point?

Do not assume that the steady gauge or amber lamps mean “normal.” They may mean normal, caution, occupied, or something with no human equivalent.

The animals’ apparent comfort tells you little about your own requirements. They belong to this atmosphere and water chemistry. You do not.

## Dress for warm water, not a rainstorm

The difficult combination here is warmth, persistent moisture, and very little opportunity to dry.

Wear lightweight clothing that will not become heavy when wet. Avoid trailing straps, loose hems, and anything that can catch in a slot or brush against a feeding seam. Bring a light rain layer for the cloud districts, but do not spend the whole visit sealed inside heavy waterproof clothing. In warm, humid air, that can make heat stress worse.

Footwear matters more than almost anything else:

- Closed, protective boots with dependable grip on wet ceramic and metal.
- Secure fastenings and good ankle support.
- No exposed hooks or metal cleats.
- Clean soles without grit embedded in the tread.

Ankle-deep water is deep enough to conceal a plate edge, a drainage opening, or a small animal. It is also shallow enough to tempt you into careless walking.

Bring spare socks in a waterproof pouch, a compact towel, and dressings for blisters. Do not walk barefoot, even if the ceramic feels pleasantly warm. You would be putting unprotected skin onto an unfamiliar living and industrial surface.

If biosecurity procedures are available, follow them rather than improvising with disinfectant. Clean equipment is sensible; leaving chemical residue in the pools is not.

## Carry your own small, closed support system

Assume that nothing in the landscape is available for your consumption.

Bring drinking water in a sealed container, food that produces few crumbs, your usual medication, and a basic first-aid kit. Carry out everything you bring in, including food scraps and hygiene waste. Establish where visitors may use a toilet before taking a long walk.

The water may look cleaner than any stream you have seen. That does not make it potable. The dissolved compounds feeding the seams might be harmless to you, harmful to you, or simply outside familiar biology. Boiling would not necessarily solve that problem.

A useful day pack would also contain:

- A waterproof route map or an offline navigation device.
- A light for reading signs and finding footing in shadow—not for entering shafts.
- A working communications device, tested at the entrance.
- A watch and enough battery reserve for an unexpectedly slow return.
- Any atmosphere-monitoring or protective equipment required by the entry assessment.

Keep the load modest. You want your hands free and your balance good. There is no benefit in carrying a survival expedition’s worth of equipment if the safer choice is to make a short, controlled first visit.

## Navigate by identifiers, not scenery

This is a place where you could become lost without ever feeling lost.

There are no hills to orient you, no coastline to follow, and no distant landmark guaranteed to remain visible. A bank of pipes may look exactly like the one you passed an hour ago. The clouds are stationary, but many districts may have similar clouds. The uniform light may offer no useful sense of time.

Before leaving the sill, record its route number and adjacent signs. Photograph junctions, keeping arrows and identifiers in the frame. At each turn, confirm not merely where the onward route goes, but how you would recognize it on the return journey.

For a first visit, choose one observation platform or a short loop. Set a turnaround time. Do not allow “everything is accessible” to become “I can always find another way back.”

If you lose your route, stop at a clearly identified, designated access point and ask for assistance. Wandering onward through repetition is likely to make the problem larger.

## Walk as though the ground is occupied—because it is

Give yourself time to learn to see the animals.

Initially, a snail is a cap, a crab is a drainage fitting, and a group of fish is a discoloration in a pool. Your eye will need practice. Until then, walk slowly enough to reconsider each foot placement.

Use designated walkways wherever possible. Avoid stepping on porous strips, grazing films, channel lips, and translucent panels unless they are explicitly part of the walking route. A surface can be structurally strong and still be biologically important.

Do not make the animals demonstrate how accommodating they are. A crab withdrawing from your foot is not permission to keep testing its clearance. An ivory animal shifting aside is not an invitation to enter the herd.

Keep a comfortable distance from the larger creatures, especially where their bodies obscure smaller ones. They may be calm, but they are heavy, and their narrow legs stand on slippery ground. Avoid getting between them, underneath them, or between a mouth and its feeding seam.

Do not feed, pet, collect, or reposition anything. Even a motionless swimmer may be occupying a particular flow of warm water rather than resting at random.

## Respect the machinery’s quiet

The silence is deceptive. Noise has been suppressed; force has not.

Stay outside gate clearances, even when a gate seems motionless. Keep fingers away from narrow gaps and do not lean on moving assemblies. A piston moving almost imperceptibly can still exert immense force.

Do not open service doors, climb machinery shells, or descend inspection shafts merely because access exists. Below the walking surface, the risks could include pressure changes, unsuitable air, heat, and moving equipment. None of those needs to announce itself loudly.

Similarly, do not test the self-repairing rail with a blade or scratch your name into it. The ability to repair damage does not make damage inconsequential—and you have no reason to assume the repair process is safe in contact with your body or belongings.

## Watch yourself, not just the landscape

Persistent humidity may make it difficult to judge how much you are sweating. The warm surfaces offer little relief, and walking through shallow water can be more tiring than it looks.

Drink regularly. Take breaks at designated dry or raised resting places. Treat headache, dizziness, nausea, unusual fatigue, or worsening coordination as reasons to end the visit, not as part of the atmosphere.

Avoid sitting or lying directly among the animals and seams. Besides obstructing habitat, you could become chilled, overheated, exposed to unfamiliar substances, or simply difficult to locate.

## Prepare for the feeling of it

The unsettling part may arrive only after the novelty has passed.

At first there is abundance: shells, eyes, warm bodies, water suspended in wings. Then you begin to notice that nothing accumulates, nothing seems wasted, and no visible process leads to a different tomorrow. The place is alive without displaying the kinds of change by which humans usually recognize life.

You may feel unusually conspicuous. Your warmth, breath, footsteps, and shadow are small disturbances in a landscape whose exchanges appear exquisitely matched.

Do not conclude that you are unwelcome. The description gives genuine evidence of room being made for passage. But neither should you assume that the system can absorb anything you choose to do.

For a first visit, arrive rested, travel with a companion, and give yourself permission to leave while you are still comfortable. You do not need to cross the continent to understand something of it.

Stand on the platform. Watch one basin long enough to distinguish its occupants. Notice the drop and the fish, without putting your hand between them.

The best preparation is to bring everything you need, introduce as little as possible, and move slowly enough to see where your next step belongs.
