Prepare for this place as you would prepare for a hot, dry cave whose floor is still being made—but remember that the cave is narrower than your fingernail, and that most of its inhabitants can disappear between grains of dust.

The first decision is not what to pack. It is **at what size you intend to visit**.

At ordinary human size, you can reach the stone, examine the entrance, and perhaps send an instrument inside. You cannot enter without breaking open the landscape you came to see. If your means of multiverse travel does not include reliable miniaturization, plan an observational visit. Do not pry the blocks apart.

If you can become small enough to enter, you will need more than a smaller version of mountaineering equipment. You will be entering a place where heat, air, sharpness, and the movement of your own body matter differently.

## Before approaching the seam

The outside is already hazardous: altitude, intense glare, mountain wind, and loose volcanic footing. Bring the equipment appropriate to a high, exposed summit, including eye protection, secure footwear, weather protection, and whatever altitude support you require.

Arrange your return before you descend into the crack. Establish a rendezvous on stable ground away from the leaning blocks. A block that looks motionless during a short visit is not necessarily securely seated, and you should not use the seam itself as an anchor.

Find out what you can about the drilling below. The description does not establish that an eruption is imminent, or that the machinery is about to disturb this particular stone. It does establish that industrial activity reaches you through the mountain. Ideally, visit during a known, steady operating period, with notice of blasting, drilling changes, or shutdowns. A sudden quiet is not automatically a sign of safety.

At the entrance, measure rather than guess:

- Air temperature and the temperatures of individual surfaces.
- Oxygen and any hazardous gases.
- Airflow, including changes when the wind shifts.
- Vibration in the rock.
- Whether the mouth and nearby flakes move under those vibrations.

A reading taken outside cannot certify the interior. Neither can one comfortable ledge.

## If you are going inside

Choose a travel size that leaves room for your protective equipment and a turn-around maneuver. “Small enough to squeeze through” is not small enough to visit safely.

Also confirm how your transformation handles heat exchange, breathing, vision, and strength. Do not assume that shrinking preserves ordinary human tolerances. At a very small scale, your relationship to the surrounding air and surfaces may be radically different. Your equipment must be designed for that condition, not simply reduced along with you.

The essential kit is a **sealed, abrasion-resistant, thermally protected enclosure with independent life support and an independent way out**.

That could be a suit or a tiny vehicle, depending on your technology. It should have:

- A known-duration breathing supply.
- Active cooling with enough capacity for the planned route and a delayed return.
- Temperature sensors at every point likely to touch the rock.
- Protection against dust and brittle, cutting edges.
- Low-intensity, controllable lighting.
- Navigation that does not depend on seeing the entrance.
- A distress signal that can reach someone outside.
- Retrieval or return capability that does not require you to crawl back while injured.

Do not rely on evaporative cooling, a damp cloth, or exposed drinking water. This is a landscape that removes moisture before it can collect. Any cooling system also has to put its waste heat somewhere; it must not simply discharge it into the chamber you occupy.

Test the whole arrangement somewhere accessible before committing to the seam.

## Expect the ground to be dangerous in several different ways

The floor is not a continuous slab. It is a stack of fragments, with passages underneath.

A patch that appears flat may shift, admit a foot, or conceal a drop into hotter material. A thin shelf may be a roof over another animal’s route. Something apparently wedged in place may be held there by a balance your touch can upset.

Move slowly, testing contact points without loading them heavily. Do not pull on projecting flakes or use a glassy underside as a handhold. Fresh basalt edges can be sharp enough to damage your suit, and a torn seal here is more serious than a cut.

Map temperature as carefully as shape. A navigable corridor may become unusable halfway along because its floor is too hot. At this scale, a few steps can take you from tolerable stone to a surface your equipment cannot safely touch.

For a first visit, remain near the entrance and use remote sensors to explore farther in. **The fresh crack at the back is a turnaround boundary, not a destination.** It is actively admitting heat and gas, and there is no described refuge beyond it.

## Bring your own air and water

The mineral smell is information, not a reliable warning system. You cannot identify the gas mixture by smell, and you should not assume that air supporting mites will support a human.

Carry all the water you need inside a sealed system. There is none available to collect, and adding water would change the place: it could disturb dust, affect microbial films, or wet deposits that have formed under dry conditions.

Avoid open flames, chemical smoke, loose powders, or anything that draws local air into your breathing circuit.

The entrance’s slight ventilation is useful, but uncertain. Wind can change. A sheltered pocket can retain a different atmosphere from the passage beside it.

## Learn to navigate without a horizon

Your familiar landscape cues will mostly disappear. There is no distant view, little light, and no dependable distinction between a wall, a floor, and the underside of a fallen chip.

Record your path as a sequence of junctions, temperature boundaries, and distinctive rock shapes. Use retrievable navigation aids rather than paint, powder, or permanent markers. Do not trust footprints: animals can cross them, and vibration can move the dust.

A bright lamp will not make the whole seam legible. It will produce hard shadows and reflections from crystals and glossy surfaces. Use a narrow, adjustable beam, with enough side lighting to reveal edges before you touch them.

Keep the return route comfortably within your life-support range. Distance alone is a poor measure of how long escape will take.

## Prepare for sound you feel through your body

The drilling will not merely be a noise outside your shelter. It will arrive through whatever supports you.

Hearing protection may help with the wind’s confined roar, but it will not remove vibration transmitted through your boots, hands, or vehicle. Some isolation between you and the stone would be valuable. So would an instrument that lets you distinguish a change in machinery from a loose flake trembling nearby.

Do not interpret every new vibration as impending collapse. Equally, do not let a continuous hum become so familiar that you stop noticing changes.

Expect the experience to be tiring. Darkness, restricted movement, heat management, and persistent vibration can consume attention even while you are apparently doing very little.

## Treat the inhabitants as residents

The seam is sparse in food, not in life. A fragment of moth wing or seed husk may support a substantial part of the community around it.

Do not move debris to make a comfortable standing place. Do not brush away a dusty web because it resembles mineral crust. Avoid the beetles’ feeding site and the cooler, slightly more humid pockets used by springtails.

At a reduced size, some animals may also become obstacles or potential hazards. You do not need to assume they are aggressive; you do need enough clearance to avoid being caught between a moving body and a sharp wall. Watch passages before entering them.

Keep your emissions contained. Breath, warmth, moisture, food crumbs, and suit leakage could create a conspicuous new resource—or destroy a small refuge. A careful visitor leaves no supplies behind.

## What to be ready for emotionally

This place may feel less like a cavern than like being inside the unfinished surface of a mountain.

There will be no single temperature, no reliably empty ground, and almost nowhere that is merely background. What looks inert will move. What looks solid will have routes beneath it. A tiny change in position may alter everything that makes a spot habitable.

You may also feel an urge to go farther because the visible distances are so small. Resist that urge. A recess only a little way beyond your lamp can still be beyond safe reach.

The best first visit is short, cool by local standards, and close to a known exit. Stay long enough to notice the animals separating themselves from the dust, and to feel the distinction between wind in the opening and machinery in the stone. Leave before fatigue makes that distinction difficult.

Your aim should not be to reach the deepest chamber. It should be to enter without breaking a route, overheating a refuge, or becoming another fragment the seam retains.
