Prepare for this city as you would for a wet industrial port, a high-altitude settlement, and a place where the ground beneath your room is still being made and unmade.

Do not prepare for a wasteland. There is abundant life here, and people plainly know how to live among it. But almost none of its familiar signals will mean quite what you expect. Warm water is not necessarily clean water. A bare courtyard is not necessarily neglected. A quiet drain is not necessarily a welcome improvement.

Your most useful preparation will be learning which kinds of wetness are ordinary, and which kinds make residents get up and leave.

## Before arrival: establish that you can live here

For an ordinary human traveler, the first questions are atmospheric and biological, not cultural.

Confirm through a reliable local or interworld service that the air has a breathable composition and pressure, the gravity is tolerable, and local food can nourish a human. The city’s height matters, but “high basin” does not tell you how much oxygen you will have. Warm weather can also disguise the physical strain of altitude: you might be short of breath while feeling none of the cold you associate with mountains.

Do not treat birds, fish, or familiar-looking shells as proof of biological compatibility. Find out whether visitors can safely eat local organisms, whether any adaptation or vaccination is recommended, and whether imported food is permitted.

Bring a modest reserve of food and drinking water known to be safe for you. It should buy time to make arrangements, not become an attempt to remain sealed off from the city.

Because this ecosystem is so unlike a plant-based one, follow its biosecurity rules carefully. Clean soil, seeds, spores, and hitchhiking insects from your equipment before arrival. Do not bring a living plant as a gift. You cannot know whether it would fail harmlessly or introduce something troublesome.

## Pack for persistent damp, not extreme weather

You will probably spend much of your visit warm, damp, and slightly dirty.

Bring:

- **Shoes with excellent wet-stone traction.** Rounded stairs, slippery films, mineral mud, and polished thresholds will matter more than deep tread designed for loose earth.
- **A spare pair of shoes and plenty of socks.** Give footwear time to dry. Small blisters and softened skin become much more troublesome when everything stays humid.
- **Light, quick-drying clothes**, with a breathable rain shell rather than a heavy coat.
- **A warmer layer for the rims and exposed embankments.** The basin may be sheltered while the heights are windy.
- **A brimmed hat and sunglasses.** Lake glare will be strong, and the hat has a second, less elegant purpose beneath crowded roosts.
- **A washable outer bag**, with waterproof inner pouches for documents, medication, and electronics.
- **A small flashlight.** You want to distinguish a shallow groove from a drain opening, particularly where the pavement and watercourses follow the same line.
- **Basic first-aid supplies**, including wound dressings and blister care.
- **Hearing protection for industrial visits.** Earplugs may also help at night, but first learn how your lodging communicates alarms.

Avoid long hems, dangling straps, and footwear you are reluctant to soil. Keep your hands available on stairs.

A walking aid can be useful, but it should have a nonslip tip. You are visiting a city of softened stone, not climbing a mountain trail; a sharp trekking pole might damage surfaces or be unwelcome indoors.

## Choose lodging by drainage and maintenance

For a first visit, stay in an established lodging house on an inhabited upper terrace, with staff accustomed to outsiders. You want convenient transport, a known drinking-water supply, and someone who can explain the building’s water arrangements.

Ask practical questions:

- Which taps or supplies are potable?
- Is the indoor channel suitable for bathing, or only for washing and heat?
- Where should you go if the street floods?
- What does a drainage problem sound or look like here?
- Whom do you notify about a change in flow?
- Which alarm means you should leave immediately?

Do not choose a room solely because it looks dry. Dry foundations are apparently difficult to maintain here, and a cheap, dry-looking basement may be less reassuring than a well-kept room with a deliberately managed channel.

Expect sound through the building: pump strokes, moving water, perhaps a steady hiss beside the bed. Ask what is normal before deciding you cannot sleep with it.

**Do not plug a channel, close an unfamiliar sluice, or block a groove to make your room quieter.** What looks like an inconvenience may be part of the building’s drainage system.

Store belongings off the floor. Use the furniture’s raised feet as a clue about where yours should be.

## Treat water as several different substances

The city contains drinking water, household water, industrial water, warm spring water, culture water, and waste-bearing water. To a newcomer, some of these may look identical.

Learn the local markings and terminology before using them.

Until you have reliable guidance:

- Drink only from a verified safe supply.
- Do not assume clear water is potable.
- Do not use an indoor channel to clean your teeth or rinse contact lenses.
- Keep cuts out of lakes, gutters, and feeding beds.
- Bathe only in places explicitly intended for it.
- Do not rely on a camping filter to make mineral-rich or industrial water safe.

Boiling may address some biological hazards, but it does not remove dissolved metals or many chemical contaminants. A local treatment system designed for this water is preferable to improvisation.

The shallow lakes are not inviting swimming holes merely because they are warm. They collect sediment, animals, runoff, and industrial activity. Their beauty is largely something you see from the terraces.

## Learn the routes before wandering

A map here should show more than streets.

Find one that includes stairs, public crossings, transport stops, terrace elevations, and any restricted waterworks or industrial areas. Confirm that it is current. In a city where shorelines grow and passages open behind cellar walls, an old map can remain visually convincing after it has ceased to be useful.

For the first day, explore with a resident guide or keep to a straightforward route between your lodging, a transport stop, a market, and a viewpoint.

Watch how locals cross wet surfaces. Notice which side of a staircase they favor and where they refuse to put a foot. The deepest worn hollow is not necessarily the safest foothold.

Do not enter a tunnel simply because rails run into it, or follow a drain because it resembles an alley. Freight passages and water passages have become entangled over centuries. Some spaces may also contain dangerous gases or insufficient oxygen. A particulate mask does not protect against those hazards; restricted underground visits require appropriate local supervision and equipment.

Treat new seepage, suddenly muddy water, a changed drain sound, or water emerging somewhere unfamiliar as reasons to alert someone—not as curiosities to investigate yourself.

## Give the animals room

The large hairless working animals sound slow, but slow does not mean safe. An animal the size of an ox can pin you against a wall without moving quickly.

Do not imitate a handler prodding one with a pole. Locals know individual animals and understand which will move. You do not.

Keep clear of tether lines, harnesses, capstans, and the space between an animal and its load. If one is sleeping across your route, wait or go around.

With smaller creatures, resist the urge to classify everything as vermin. Some are useful enough to be legally protected. Ask before removing an animal from a room, killing something in a drain, or collecting a shell.

Do not feed the birds. Cover food outdoors and avoid lingering directly below densely occupied ledges. Wash your hands before eating, particularly after using railings or touching masonry beneath roosts.

You will probably get bird droppings on something. Prepare to clean it rather than letting it ruin your day.

## Arrange food rather than guessing

This city’s food system begins with bacterial growth, but that does not mean you should taste a culture film or assume every product made from it is safe.

For your first meals, use an establishment recommended for compatible visitors. Ask what is in dishes and how they are processed. Thorough cooking may reduce some risks, but it cannot make incompatible biochemistry or dissolved contaminants harmless.

Expect familiar categories—staples, rich foods, preserved foods, market snacks—to have unfamiliar sources. There may be excellent local cuisine here. Approach it with curiosity after compatibility has been established, rather than with either disgust or reckless confidence.

Also check your longer-term nutrition. The absence of visible plants does not prove a deficient diet, but it does mean you should not assume your usual dietary needs will be met automatically.

## Prepare for the absence of green

The most disorienting thing may not be the smell or the animals. It may be that nothing sprouts.

There is no grass softening an abandoned rail bed. No tree marking a square. No moss making an old wall look old. Neglect accumulates mineral dust, shells, feathers, and sediment instead.

If you are accustomed to looking for vegetation as a sign of comfort or health, the city may initially feel depleted even when it is functioning well. Give yourself time to learn its other signs of life: birds settling on a roof, grazers gathering at an outfall, pale cultures growing in a maintained gallery, workers reading the color of dredged sediment.

For a long visit, familiar music, photographs, or other reminders of home may help. So will occasional trips to the rim, where there is wind and distance, even if there are still no trees.

## A sensible first day

Arrive in daylight. Check into your lodging, establish your safe water supply, eat a known-compatible meal, and take a short guided walk. Learn the route back before the lamps come on.

Save the low lakes, culture galleries, industrial works, and long intercity journeys for later. Allow more time than the apparent map distance suggests: stairs, rain, slow freight, and an immovable animal may all intervene.

Above all, arrive willing to pay attention.

This is not a city that has defeated water. It has built an extraordinarily elaborate accommodation with it. A good visitor learns enough of that accommodation to stay out of its way—and eventually to recognize why a channel running quietly through a house can be a source of comfort rather than alarm.
