Prepare for this city as you would for a hot, very loud wilderness with excellent public services—not as you would for an ordinary urban holiday.

Its conveniences are real. So are the kilometers of living ground between them. The most important habit to acquire is this: **a place can be easily accessible without being nearby.** When the transit system pauses, that distinction becomes your whole day.

## Before you arrive

Confirm that an ordinary human can tolerate the atmosphere, gravity, food, and transfer system. The description establishes that people live here; it does not establish that their physiology is yours. In particular, ask whether the momentary pressure behind the eyes during a transfer is harmless for human visitors, and whether any medical conditions make transfers inadvisable.

Arrange your first arrival **inside an occupied building**, preferably your accommodation, rather than at a recess on an unfamiliar path. You want somewhere to sit, cool down, test your hearing protection, and learn the warning signals before you have to navigate.

Choose accommodation for its engineering rather than its view. Ask for:

- Effective cooling, including during transit shutdowns.
- Maintained vibration dampers.
- A genuinely quiet interior room.
- Drinking water stored on site.
- A host or staffed desk that receives municipal warnings.
- A clear procedure for remaining in the building during underground movements.

A spectacular upper room may be lovely, but “the hospital is visible from the balcony” is not the same as “the hospital is accessible in an emergency.” Ask what happens when someone needs medical care during a transfer suspension.

Get a local orientation early. Children here know which stones hum dangerously and which vegetation conceals breeding grounds. None of that knowledge will be obvious to you.

## Pack for heat, sharp rock, and relentless noise

### Footwear and clothing

Bring closed boots with puncture-resistant soles, good traction, and protection around the toes and ankles. Fresh rock can open an ordinary shoe, and a maintained path may acquire a new edge or crease.

Lightweight, loose clothing that covers your skin is a better starting point than shorts and sandals. It keeps unfamiliar plants off you and offers some protection from rock dust and abrasion. Choose fabrics that dry quickly; brief rain will not necessarily cool the air enough to make wet clothing comfortable.

A broad-brimmed hat may help with exposure, but do not mistake dim light for a mild environment. The ground itself is a heat source. If possible, choose footwear and a daypack that do not trap heat unnecessarily.

### Hearing protection

Take sound protection seriously before you hear the city.

Bring properly fitted earplugs and protective earmuffs, with spares. Consumer noise-canceling headphones may improve comfort, but should not be your only defense against potentially damaging sound.

Ask locals or your host for measured noise levels and recommended exposure limits. The roar’s familiarity to residents does not prove it is safe for your ears.

Protection also has limits: low-frequency vibration may still be felt through your body and the building. If the noise produces pain, dizziness, nausea, or persistent pressure, retreat to a damped interior rather than simply adding louder music.

Use written messages, a text device, or agreed hand signals with your companions. You should not need to uncover your ears whenever someone wants to say something.

### The small essentials

Carry:

- More drinking water than you expect to use between stops.
- Suitable electrolytes and a modest supply of familiar food.
- A small first-aid kit, including dressings for cuts and blisters.
- A reliable light with a broad beam for uneven ground.
- An offline map and written destination addresses.
- A charged communication device and backup power.
- Any essential medication, kept with you rather than in distant luggage.

A lamp matters even in daytime. The light is diffuse, the greens are nearly black, and hollows can conceal footing. A polished gleam may catch your attention while a dangerous edge remains difficult to see.

## Learn the transit system before relying on it

The transfer chambers are what make this dispersed settlement a city. Learn their etiquette and failure procedures with the same care you would give unfamiliar rail travel—but remember that a chamber may connect you to somewhere physically hours away.

For your first trips:

- Use known public entrances.
- Check the destination before confirming.
- Sit or hold the handrail as instructed.
- Allow time for the pressure sensation to settle.
- Check where you have actually arrived before stepping out.
- Know whether your destination has shelter, water, and a return connection.

Do not assume that every entrance is interchangeable or that a small recess will be easy to find again. Save its address and location.

Ask how shutdown notices reach visitors. **The disappearance of the lowest mechanical note is not a dependable warning for someone wearing ear protection or someone who has not yet learned the city’s sound.** You need a visual or electronic notification as well.

When a yellow line appears around a transit door, do not rush through in the hope of beating the suspension. Follow the local procedure. If you are already in an occupied building, staying there is likely to be much more sensible than beginning an improvised walk home.

Build slack into your plans. Keep enough necessities with you to spend several unexpected hours at the market, a friend’s building, or another safe destination. If medication must be taken on schedule, carry it.

## Walk as a visitor, not as someone who grew up here

The maintained paths are your first choice. Open-looking ground is not necessarily stable ground, and lush-looking growth is not necessarily a safe place to put your weight.

Do not step on broad leaves simply because local children do. Their confidence comes from knowing the species, the season, and the particular patch.

Avoid:

- Newly exposed ledges and fresh fractures.
- Vapor emerging from a cleft.
- Stones identified as humming or otherwise hazardous.
- Moving survey lights and areas with changing markers.
- Slab edges with cavities beneath them.
- Burrow entrances, even neat or apparently unoccupied ones.
- Dense hollows whose inhabitants you cannot identify.

Learn exactly what the marker colors mean. Do not invent an interpretation from their resemblance to signals at home.

When underground movement begins, stop trying to make progress and follow local instructions. Do not go closer to photograph a new crack. The machinery need not produce a conventional earthquake for the surface to become unsafe.

Distance deserves respect here. A tower on the horizon is a landmark, not a reasonable spontaneous destination. Before any long walk, know its duration, its sheltered stopping places, and whether it still works as a plan if transfers stop.

For your first outings, walk with a resident guide. They can show you what matters far more efficiently than a field guide full of names.

## Treat the vegetation as water infrastructure

This city’s greenery is not evidence of abundant water. It is evidence of extremely effective water retention.

Do not collect condensation just because it appears unattended. Household shares are regulated, and the surfaces producing it may be part of an established collection arrangement.

Bring or obtain verified drinking water. Neither droplets on a walkway nor moisture inside a swollen plant joint should be assumed safe for humans.

Be gentle around the plants. Avoid pulling creepers off rock, disturbing husks tucked into fissures, or shifting leaves to get a better view. What looks like incidental litter or awkward growth may be helping a root system capture its next drink.

After rain, resist the urge to wander everywhere. The brief wet interval is when plants unfold feeding structures and animals emerge to drink. It may be the most beautiful time to be outside, but it is also a time of intense biological activity.

Watch from the path. Let the droplets belong to the things that need them.

## Do not rely on hearing to notice animals

Here, the landscape masks movement. A hunter can break a stalk during a chorus without announcing itself to you.

That does not establish that the wildlife is dangerous to humans, but it does mean that “I’ll hear something coming” is a poor plan. Ask which species require distance, whether any bite or sting, and what to do during an encounter.

Do not reach into cracks, burrows, or dark foliage. Do not handle a grazer that resembles shed bark. Avoid feeding animals and keep food sealed.

An animal flattening itself or withdrawing is not an invitation to inspect it more closely. Give it room.

## Expect the first day to be tiring

The combination of heat, dimness, vibration, and noise may consume more attention than you expect. You may find yourself irritable or exhausted without having walked very far.

Keep your arrival day simple: one transfer, one short guided outing, an unhurried meal, and rest. Learn what a sheltered room feels like before testing how much exposure you can tolerate.

Plan quiet breaks deliberately. Check yourself for headache, thirst, dizziness, ringing ears, and overheating rather than waiting until you feel unmistakably ill.

There is also a subtler adjustment. Your ordinary judgments of scale may keep failing. The city can feel almost empty while being socially dense; a distant window can be minutes away until suddenly it is hours away. Resist the impulse to fill that apparent emptiness with walking.

## The best way to visit

Give yourself enough time to experience both sides of the place: its intimate civic life and its enormous physical separations.

Visit the market by transfer. Then take a short, guided walk from it. Watch a rainfall from shelter, and step out afterward along a known path. Spend an evening in a damped upper room looking across the plain as the flying animals begin to call.

Do not make the machinery’s next movement into an attraction you need to approach. You can learn a great deal from a safe window.

The essential preparation is to abandon the assumption that a city must have subdued its ground. This one has made a workable life above something still becoming. Its paths, dampers, water rules, markers, and transit pauses are not inconveniences around the real experience.

They are how you get to have the experience—and come home well.
