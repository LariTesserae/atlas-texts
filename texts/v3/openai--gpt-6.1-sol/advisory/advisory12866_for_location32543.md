Prepare for this place as you would for a very small garden that has survived by admitting almost nothing unnecessary—including visitors.

The main difficulty is not finding shelter, water, or a safe path. It is recognizing that all three already belong to something else. A sheltered hollow is a nest. A dry ledge is a snail’s resting place. A few drops of water may be the entire supply of a plant whose roots you cannot see.

You can probably visit safely. You should arrive ready to need very little.

## Before lifting the stone

**Get warm, drink, eat, and use a toilet elsewhere.** There is no indication that the courtyard offers any ordinary human facilities. Its cup is not necessarily a drinking vessel anymore, and its water is neither abundant nor yours to take.

Wear insulating layers that do not shed much fiber, with a close-fitting outer layer. Avoid a trailing scarf, loose straps, broad coat hems, or anything that might catch a branch when you turn. The plants fit their space tightly; your clothing should do the same.

Bring:

- **Thin, warm gloves with a good grip.** The entrance ring is painfully cold. You need enough dexterity to handle the stone without dropping or scraping it.
- **Clean, compact footwear with dependable traction.** The paving may be mostly dry, but the lowest stair has ice beneath it, and roots have shifted some stones. Clean soil and seeds from your soles before arrival.
- **A small, securely closed bottle of water.** Drink outside if possible. Keep it away from the planting and channels.
- **A reliable way to measure your visit**, preferably one you can check without a bright screen or an alarm.
- **A small backup light**, for the approach and the exit rather than for illuminating the garden.
- **Whatever return equipment your multiverse journey requires**, kept on your person and reachable without unpacking.

Leave bulky luggage outside in a safe place. A room-sized courtyard with only inches between its inhabitants will make a large pack feel much bigger than it did in the street.

If you travel with someone, take turns entering. This is not a place improved by a group.

## Make the exit straightforward

Before descending, understand how the entrance stone moves and how you will replace it. Do not assume that lifting it from the passage means it will be equally easy to lift from below.

Check that you can get back through the opening in your winter clothing. Notice the steps, the position of the ring, and whether the stone needs to remain supported. Do not improvise a prop using a pot, the stool, or a piece of the little bridges.

An outside companion can be useful here: someone who knows where you are and will check on you after an agreed interval. They should not keep calling down. The courtyard’s silence is part of what you are visiting.

For a first visit, plan a short stay—perhaps ten or fifteen minutes—and extend it only if you remain warm, comfortable, and certain of your way out.

## Enter more slowly than seems necessary

Pause on the steps before committing your weight.

The courtyard has several scales of passage at once. What looks like an awkward bit of paving may be a water channel. What looks like debris may be a bridge. A raised flagstone may be the roof of an occupied chamber.

Choose your next foothold before moving your foot. Avoid pivoting on planted soles: the twisting motion can be more destructive than a straight step.

Do not put a hand against the wall automatically to steady yourself. Ferns occupy its joints, roots occupy its peg holes, and even a plain-looking brick may have been worked into part of the garden’s infrastructure.

Once inside, stand still for a little while. Let your eyes separate leaves from animals, pottery from lichen, and pathways from drainage grooves. The bright light helps you see, but it does not make everything immediately legible.

## Do not improve anything

This may be the most important preparation: leave your gardening instincts outside.

Do not water the plants because the garden seems dry. Its moisture is distributed with extraordinary precision. A bottle poured into a gutter could turn an individually supplied root system into a flooded one, fill a dry nest chamber, or wash fine soil out of a place where it has held for years.

Do not:

- Prune with the shears.
- Straighten a bent branch.
- Remove the leaf from the cup.
- Repair the broken-bottomed pot.
- Clear roots from a pipe.
- Move a bridge so that you can step more easily.
- Pick a berry, flower, or “small sample.”

The description suggests a place in which damage has become structure. A broken object may now be more useful than a whole one would be.

Nor should you leave food for the bird or the whiskered animal. Their lack of alarm is not a request for feeding. It might reflect familiarity with visitors, a very different pace of response, or simply the fact that you have not yet done anything alarming.

Assume the place is cared for, even if you never meet its keeper. Treat the tools and seats as someone’s possessions.

## Be careful about what your body brings in

An ordinary human produces a considerable amount of warmth, moisture, vibration, and loose material for a space this small.

You do not need to hold your breath or behave as though your presence is contamination. Just avoid leaning close over delicate plants or breathing repeatedly onto the same cold surface. Do not shake out a wet coat inside. Avoid smoking, flames, heaters, and sprays.

Keep snacks wrapped. Even crumbs would be a substantial addition to some of the lives here.

The seats may be usable, but inspect them first. Look beneath the slab and around the stool’s shortened legs. If sitting requires displacing the shears, brushing through ferns, or placing your boots over channels, remain standing instead. There is no obligation to make yourself comfortable at the garden’s expense.

## Do not rely on the light to tell you when to leave

The courtyard’s brightness does not seem to behave like ordinary daylight. Clouds barely affect it; shells and whitewash remain luminous. You may have no useful sense of afternoon becoming evening.

The motionless water bead and the unusually settled states of flower, fruit, and wood also deserve attention. They do not prove that time is altered. They do mean that the usual signs of change may be poor guides here.

Use your planned visiting interval. If you have a companion outside, compare elapsed time afterward rather than assuming your impressions were accurate.

Leave sooner if you become clumsy, shiver persistently, lose feeling in your fingers, or find yourself unusually sleepy. Brightness can make a cold space feel less physically demanding than it is. Silence can make staying seem effortless.

## What to expect

You may initially feel that you have discovered an abandoned place. Resist the urge to claim it.

Then you may feel watched—not menaced, necessarily, but noticed. The bird’s eye, the face between the stones, the tools laid ready beside a cup: these suggest that entry does not confer privacy.

After a while, the most striking experience will probably be a change in scale. A trembling drop becomes an event. A handspan bridge becomes a piece of civic engineering. The distance between two leaves becomes occupied space rather than emptiness.

Let that happen without trying to force a revelation. You do not need to touch the glowing shells, wait until the drop falls, or locate the caretaker to have visited properly.

On leaving, check that you have taken everything you brought. Retrace your steps, handle the cold ring carefully, and restore the entrance as you found it, provided doing so is safe.

The right preparation is enough warmth, enough water, and enough certainty about your return that you can spend your visit asking nothing of the courtyard. It already appears to be sustaining a great deal.
