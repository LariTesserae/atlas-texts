Prepare for this plateau as you would for a place where **the landscape may permit your presence without necessarily permitting your departure**.

The cold, the height, and the absence of water are familiar problems. You can pack for those. The harder problem is that almost everything here seems to have reached a condition in which it can remain indefinitely: a mouth around a leaf, a beak inside a cracked egg, a person with a bone across their knees. None of these things looks forcibly restrained. That should make you more cautious, not less.

Your first visit should be short, accompanied, and deliberately unfinished.

## Before you ascend

Arrange your return before you arrange your exploration.

If your means of traveling between worlds can open a passage independently of local geography, bring it. Verify that it works from the approach, and test it again shortly after reaching the plateau. Do not save that test for when you need to leave.

If your only way out is the path you came up, leave someone below the last unmistakably ordinary stretch of country. Give them a firm turnaround time and instructions that do not require them to come looking for you. A rescue party could become several more people sitting quietly among the animals.

Travel with one companion if possible. More people will make it difficult to pass through the narrow spaces without touching anything. Alone, however, you may have no one to notice that you have stopped completing an action.

Before entering, agree on a few plain checks:

- Can each of you take three deliberate steps and stop?
- Can each say something new, rather than repeat the last observation?
- Can each pick up an item from your own pack and put it away?
- Can each still explain why you intend to leave?

These are not proven defenses. They are ways to notice a change while you can still act on it.

Set a limit measured by equipment and supplies, not by how long the visit feels. This is a place where a very small sound can receive an enormous amount of your attention.

## What to carry

### Everything you need to stay alive

Bring all your water. There is no reason to expect springs, rainfall, or usable moisture in the basins. Do not treat the salt deposits as evidence that water is merely out of sight.

Carry ready-to-eat food in small portions. Avoid anything that requires a fire, a long preparation, or local ingredients. You should not have to establish a household here merely to have lunch.

Dress for persistent dry cold:

- Insulating layers beneath a wind-resistant outer layer.
- Gloves that preserve enough dexterity to operate your return equipment.
- Warm, well-fitting boots with dependable grip on smooth rock.
- Eye protection against glare and fine mineral dust.
- A face covering for any powder you disturb.

The absence of wind does not mean the powder is harmless. Something that has lain undisturbed for a very long time may become airborne when you kneel, drop a bag, or brush against a seam.

Bring a compact first-aid kit and your usual emergency medicines. Do not expect to find useful plants, fuel, or help quickly.

### Instruments that disagree usefully

Carry a watch, a second timer using a different mechanism, and a simple written log. A compass and positioning device are worth bringing, but neither should be treated as authoritative.

The geography is not merely difficult to recognize. It changes the relationship between route and destination. A correct bearing may lead you along a slope whose length belongs to an animal.

Record sequences as well as landmarks:

> Shelter with chipped bowl. Passed to the left of two shell-backed animals. Basin with branching channel. Returned by the same side.

“Walked east for twenty minutes” may be much less useful.

Photographs can help distinguish familiar objects from similar ones. Take them without assuming they will explain what happened. A photograph of a shadow may record a fact your map cannot accommodate.

A rope is not a guaranteed route home. If distance expands around a creature, the rope may simply become insufficient. Do not attach lines to residents, animals, trees, or structures.

## How to move through it

Walk on exposed stone, with short, deliberate steps.

What first appears to be texture may be a living body. Examine a place before putting your boot or hand there. Avoid stepping over animals even when the alternative looks inconvenient. An animal’s apparent length may not tell you how far you must travel to get around it.

Make your first excursion a small loop near your arrival point. Return through it promptly. If you come back to the same shelter from an unexpected side, do not interpret that as an interesting puzzle to solve before lunch. It is enough evidence to shorten the visit.

Do not use the rim beasts as destinations. Their role seems less like that of landmarks than of things holding the visible world at its present extent. Walking toward one for hours without drawing nearer is not a challenge that better stamina will overcome.

Keep well away from the outer edge. The plateau offers unusually poor depth cues: broad slopes, distant valleys, mountains flattened by height. Stable-looking stone does not make a fall survivable.

### Treat shadows as occupied space

Ordinarily, a shadow tells you something about an object. Here it appears capable of participating in the arrangement itself.

Avoid crossing the long shadow of the plated beast. Avoid walking through the larger grove shadow beneath the seven small trees. Do not stand in a notch where an animal seems precisely fitted.

If a crossing is unavoidable, pass straight through without pausing, and have your companion watch your own shadow afterward.

The fact that your shadow can join another and fail to leave immediately deserves practical respect. You do not need to know whether it is dangerous before deciding not to repeat the experiment.

## Do not try to finish anything

You will see an egg that appears ready to hatch. You may see an animal apparently trapped in another animal’s jaws. A creature may seem one movement away from relief.

Do not intervene on appearance alone.

Nothing described here indicates ordinary distress. The white animal inside the larger mouth is uninjured; both are attending to something elsewhere. The beak in the egg has remained visible beyond any normal expectation. Pulling, prying, feeding, or repositioning may impose an event on a relationship you do not understand.

The same caution applies to objects:

- Do not turn over the bowl.
- Do not borrow the hammer.
- Do not unravel the fiber.
- Do not lift the stones pinning the marked hides.
- Do not sit in the place left between the three enclosed animals without invitation.

That last space is particularly tempting. It is comfortably human-sized in a place that otherwise accommodates you only narrowly. It may be hospitality. It may be someone’s place. Until you can tell, leave it open.

The empty sleeping hollows near the rim are not campsites simply because they are empty.

## Meeting the people

Approach slowly, stop at a respectful distance, and announce yourself in a normal voice.

Their lack of a visible turning movement does not mean they have failed to notice you. Their quietness does not establish that they are unable to speak. Allow a response without demanding one, but do not wait indefinitely for a response that may already occupy its own long interval.

Ask practical questions before metaphysical ones:

- “May we pass here?”
- “Where should we avoid walking?”
- “How do visitors leave?”
- “Is there somewhere we may rest briefly?”

Do not assume the marked hides are primitive writing, incomplete maps, or instructions intended for you. They may be records of feet, paths, bodies, or obligations. Copy them only with permission.

If a resident offers you a place, food, or an object, establish whether accepting it changes what is expected of you. Hospitality in another world can be sincere and still have consequences an ordinary human cannot meet.

## Watch what the place does to your attention

The danger may arrive as patience.

At first you will wait for a foot to lower, a mouth to close, a person to turn. Later, you may stop expecting completion and become comfortable watching the interval itself. That could be an extraordinary experience. It could also make leaving feel unnecessarily abrupt.

Watch for these changes:

- You have been holding something without using or putting it away.
- You repeatedly postpone departure until one small event occurs.
- Your companion’s movements seem intrusive.
- You find yourself choosing a posture because it fits a hollow, wall, or gap.
- You can remember your departure plan but no longer feel inclined to carry it out.

Leave at the first such sign. Do not wait to determine whether it is merely fatigue.

Eat, drink, speak, and change position on your agreed schedule. Keep these actions simple and fully completed. A visitor who is cold, dehydrated, and absorbed in observation will be poorly equipped to distinguish exhaustion from something stranger.

## Leaving

Leave while you are still curious.

Do not take souvenirs. Even a loose pebble has settled into a place here; taking an animal, seed, hide strip, or pinch of salt would be a much greater presumption. Your records and memories are sufficient.

Once below, check your body, equipment, and companion. Confirm that ordinary actions still proceed normally. Look at your shadow in ordinary sunlight—not as a ritual, but because you have a specific reason to notice whether it behaves as it did before.

The plateau need not be hostile to be unsuitable for a prolonged human stay. It may be a refuge, an immense accommodation, or a world in which continuance matters more than change. Its inhabitants may belong there completely.

You do not.

Go prepared to be quiet, careful, and astonished. Keep enough of your ordinary life with you—thirst answered by drinking, a question followed by another question, a walk that ends somewhere—to recognize when it is time to return.
