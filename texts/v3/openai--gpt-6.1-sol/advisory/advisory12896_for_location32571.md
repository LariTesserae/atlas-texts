Prepare for this city as you would for a long walk through exposed uplands and an abandoned stone settlement—not as you would for a visit to a city. Its streets survive, but the services implied by streets do not. There is no food to buy, no inhabited house to ask directions at, and no assurance that a building which looks sound from outside can safely shelter you.

The central difficulty is that **the city makes exposure look like shelter, and distance look like detail**. A roof seems close because you can see it clearly. A room seems dry because sunlight reaches its floor. A stair seems usable because almost all its steps remain. You will need to check these impressions rather than trust them.

## Before crossing into it

Confirm that the air is breathable and that your point of arrival is on stable ground. The description suggests a broadly Earthlike environment, but it does not establish atmospheric pressure, altitude, or biological compatibility. If your travel equipment can test these, use it before committing to a long excursion.

Choose an arrival point well inside the plateau, away from terrace edges, marshes, and retaining walls. Ideally, arrive in full daylight, with enough time to inspect the immediate area and return without hurrying.

Make your exit reliable before you begin exploring:

- Record the arrival point in more than one way: coordinates if available, a photograph, and a sketch of its surroundings.
- Verify whether your navigation and communication equipment work here.
- Establish a return deadline, with someone monitoring it if possible.
- Carry enough provisions to survive an unexpected night without depending on a ruin for warmth.

For a first visit, plan a short loop through one district. Do not set out toward an interesting feature on the distant uplands. The city’s scale is such that visible weather may never reach you; a visible neighborhood may likewise be much farther away than it appears.

## Dress for cold water, not just cold air

The sunlight is real, and you may feel pleasantly warm while standing in it. Nevertheless, the ground, shaded passages, and saturated masonry will keep drawing heat from you.

Wear layers that remain useful when damp: a wicking base layer, an insulating middle layer, and a waterproof, wind-resistant outer shell. Avoid cotton as your main insulation. Bring a warm hat and light gloves even if you arrive at noon in clear weather.

Footwear deserves particular attention. You want:

- Reliable grip on wet stone.
- Enough support for irregular steps and loose rubble.
- Water resistance suitable for shallow flowing water.
- A fit comfortable enough for prolonged walking and descending.

No sole makes algae-coated paving safe. Test it gently before putting your full weight down. Trekking poles can help, but pole tips also slip on smooth slabs; do not treat them as fixed supports.

Pack spare socks and a dry insulating layer in a genuinely waterproof bag. Gaiters may help keep shallow water and wet grit out of your boots. An ordinary fabric backpack can become soaked merely by being set against a wall.

Bring sunglasses and sun protection. Pale stone, bright puddles, and open sky can make a cold day unexpectedly glaring.

## Carry the necessities the city no longer provides

Bring all your food. The scattered plants, insects, and birds are not a dependable supply, and familiar appearance would not establish that anything is safe to eat.

Bring drinking water as well. The city’s water is clear in places, but clarity only tells you that you can see through it. It has traveled across roofs, accumulated in rooms, and passed through old masonry and sediment. Its microbial and chemical contents are unknown.

If you must replenish, use an appropriate treatment system, recognizing that ordinary filters do not remove every dissolved contaminant. Prefer a verified source over a picturesque one. Neither a flowing gutter nor an apparently clean pool is automatically safe.

Useful equipment includes a headlamp with spare power, an offline map or route log, a first-aid kit, a whistle, emergency insulation, and a weatherproof means of taking notes. For entering ruins, a helmet is sensible—but it is not permission to enter visibly unstable structures.

For overnight travel, carry a shelter that can stand without dependable soil for stakes. Much of the available ground may be stone or shallow soil over stone. An insulated sleeping pad matters: bedding laid directly on cold paving will lose heat even in a room sheltered from wind.

## Navigate as though every district resembles the last

This city is likely to be disorienting without being labyrinthine.

Its buildings share materials, heights, and shapes. Courtyards lead to courtyards. A street that appears to continue may meet subsidence, standing water, or a collapsed wall. The view from a terrace can clarify your position, but reaching that terrace may involve several turns through nearly identical passages.

Record junctions as you go. Photograph the way back, not just the attractive view ahead. Note distinctive arrangements—a divided stair, a particular drainage opening, a courtyard with one fallen corner—rather than relying on “the pale house beside the wet lane.”

Keep a generous turnaround margin. Returning along the same route may be slower if rain deepens a crossing or evening reflections conceal the stones you used earlier.

Do not navigate by “downhill” alone. The place has broad uplands, local valleys, and successive terraces; descending may lead into a basin rather than toward your exit.

## Move slowly around water and walls

The water here is shallow, but its effects are not.

A thin sheet can hide a loose paving slab. A pool can conceal a missing step. A dark line on a wall can mark sustained saturation behind it. An upper wall standing over a gap is not reassuring: it may be bridging a space that once contained its support.

Avoid:

- Roofs, including roofs that appear almost level with the ground.
- Undercut walls and passages beneath unsupported masonry.
- Terrace edges, especially where the ground is wet or cracked.
- Marsh margins and clay that cannot be distinguished from firm ground.
- Rooms whose entrances or lower walls have been washed out.

Stay well back from drops into the lowlands. Clouds can hide the depth, and a terrace edge need not have a conspicuous boundary.

Watch the water before crossing it. A minute of observation may reveal a current, a hidden outlet, or sediment moving beneath the surface. If rain begins, reconsider routes through sunken squares and low passages. They are part of the drainage system now, whatever their original purpose.

Do not clear a drain or shift fallen stones to improve your path. The present landscape depends on the way those obstructions hold soil and redirect water. A helpful-looking alteration could send water somewhere less stable.

## Choose shelter by inspection, not atmosphere

A sunlit room with a square window will be inviting. It may also have a roof carrying saturated soil and centuries of frost damage.

The safer resting place may be an open courtyard on firm ground, with your own shelter, rather than an enclosed room. Look for signs of recent or recurring flow: sediment fans, stranded grit, water stains, and moss lines. Avoid depressions, drains, wall bases, and routes that could receive runoff from higher terraces.

Make camp before the shallow warmth disappears. Cold becomes harder to manage once your socks are wet, you have stopped moving, and you are tired enough to postpone changing them.

## Prepare for the quiet

You probably do not need courage against some hidden inhabitant. You need patience with a place that offers no human response.

For the first hour, the absence may feel peaceful. Later, the repetition of empty rooms and distant districts may become wearing. Do not let that feeling push you into searching for a more meaningful destination farther away. There may be no central monument, intact household, or final viewpoint that resolves the visit.

If traveling with someone, agree to remain within sight or easy speaking distance. Walls and turns can separate people quickly. If alone, keep a deliberate routine: check your position, drink, eat, assess the weather, and turn back when planned.

Visit lightly. Do not collect the small flowers, pry loose a stone, or leave markers unless they are necessary and removable. The sparse life depends on very particular patches of warmth, soil, and seepage.

The best preparation is to arrive **self-sufficient, dry, and unhurried**. Then you can notice what the city actually offers: weather crossing distant neighborhoods, individual drops under a lintel, the slight motion of grit in a street. You do not need to cover much ground to experience it. A safe visit may consist of one courtyard, one terrace, and enough time to watch the light change.
