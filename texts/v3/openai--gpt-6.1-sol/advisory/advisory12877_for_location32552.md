Prepare for this place as you would for a small, sun-warmed bathing cistern—not a natural spring, and not necessarily a pool intended for people. Its beauty is unusually legible: you can see the tiles, the steps, the current, even individual grains moving across the floor. That clarity could make it feel safer and more familiar than it actually is.

**Before entering, establish what the basin is for.** The description gives no indication of ownership, bathing customs, or the cleanliness of the supply. It might be a private bath, a holding tank, or part of a water system whose users would strongly object to someone stepping into it. Ask a resident or caretaker whether entering is permitted and whether the water is suitable for skin contact. ### What to bring

You do not need expedition equipment. A few ordinary items would make the visit much more comfortable:

- **Clean, nonslip water shoes**, if bathing is allowed. The plaster already has a slick film in places, and one raised tile is enough to catch a bare toe.
- **A towel and dry clothing**, kept above the coping and away from splash.
- **Drinking water.** Bring your own; clear, continuously moving water is not necessarily potable, especially after crossing a roof.
- **Sun protection and a hat** for waiting or observing outside. The narrow opening admits fierce daylight.
- **A waterproof pouch** for anything you cannot leave outside. There is little room below, and no described dry shelf.
- **A companion**, particularly if you plan to enter. They can remain at the opening, where they are more useful than another person crowding the basin.

A mask could help you appreciate the underwater light, but it is optional. There is no reason to bring diving gear or weights into such a small, shallow enclosure.

### Make your first visit from the edge

Approach the opening slowly. The wet coping and apparently narrow gap conceal a drop, and leaning too far over is the first avoidable hazard. Get a stable footing before looking down; do not balance on the damp rim for a better view.

Spend a minute watching the water. Locate all three steps, the raised tile, the inlet, and the overflow. Notice whether the level is steady or whether the overflow has begun to gulp and pulse. Refraction and glare make depth difficult to judge even when the bottom is plainly visible. Measure it with a suitable clean pole, or ask someone who knows the basin, rather than trusting your eyes.

Identify how you will climb out **before** descending. There is no handrail described. The top step may disappear when the flow increases, and the hard plaster edges are not forgiving if you slip.

### If you enter

Use the steps, slowly, keeping a secure hand against an appropriate support if one is available. Do not jump from the courtyard or dive: the water is modest in depth, the floor is hard, and the chamber offers almost no margin for error.

Test the temperature before committing your whole body. “Sun-warmed” can vary with weather and time of day, and the water near the spout is warmer than elsewhere. A comfortable temperature at the far wall does not establish the temperature beneath the inlet.

Stay away from the falling column at first. Its bubbles and turbulence can obscure your footing, and the sound becomes much more intense underwater. This is not described as a powerful hydraulic trap, but it is still an unnecessarily awkward place to put your face or find your balance.

Give the overflow room as well. It is a gravity-fed outlet, not a described pump suction, but a covered drain is no place to insert fingers, investigate with your feet, or let loose straps trail.

Keep your head above water until you have confirmed that the basin is suitable for bathing. Roof-fed water can carry contaminants that do not affect its clarity. Avoid swallowing it, and avoid entering with open wounds if its quality is unknown. If you do briefly look underwater, do so without prolonged breath-holding and with your companion watching.

Do not crowd the chamber. Its scale is intimate enough that one visitor’s movement will alter nearly the whole surface, turning the clear view into glare and sending ripples back from every wall.

### Expect a surprisingly intense little room

The main adjustment will probably be sensory rather than physical. From outside, it is a hidden gap. Inside, the spout fills the enclosure with sound, and the bright rectangle overhead becomes the strongest visual reference. Moving sunlight makes the floor seem to shift even though the tiles are fixed.

If enclosed spaces, loud water, or flickering light make you uneasy, start with a short visit on the upper step. You need not submerge to experience the place. Nor should you expect silence: even at the far end, the water’s noise follows you.

Late afternoon may offer gentler light while the tiles still retain warmth. Midday will give the clearest view of the whole floor, but also the fiercest glare. Neither time guarantees a particular water temperature, so check it on arrival.

### Leave the small beginnings alone

The green threads and thin patches are the first living occupants of an otherwise new, handmade basin. Do not scrape them away, collect them, or scrub the plaster to obtain a cleaner foothold. Avoid touching the thumbprint and trowel marks unnecessarily. Those details are part of what makes the chamber worth visiting: it is still close enough to its making that individual gestures remain visible.

Keep soap, oils, and other products out of the water unless a caretaker explicitly confirms that they belong there. The overflow carries whatever you introduce onward into a system you cannot see.

The best preparation is to arrive without needing this to become an adventure. It is not a cave to penetrate or a depth to conquer. With permission, clean footing, an easy exit, and a little patience, you can let it be what it is: a warm, bright chamber where the movements of water are small enough to follow, and loud enough to surround you.
