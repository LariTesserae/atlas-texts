Prepare for this town as you would for a long stay underwater, not a visit to a seaside settlement. Its gentleness is real, but it is not the same as safety. There is no beach to retreat to, no dry street where you can take off your equipment, and no reason to assume that the air under a welcoming roof is fit for an ordinary human to breathe.

The most important thing to bring is **a way to remain alive without borrowing the town’s air**. The next most important is patience.

## Before you arrive

Find out what the water does to a human body.

The water’s refusal to fall tells you that this place has unfamiliar physics. It does not tell you whether pressure, buoyancy, dissolved gases, or human lungs behave differently here. Do not let the view of clouds beneath the mountain persuade you that you understand its depth.

Have a probe or a qualified expedition measure:

- Pressure at the arrival point and in the inhabited terraces.
- Water temperature, including near the hot hollows.
- Salinity, acidity, mineral content, and any biological hazards.
- The composition and pressure of air inside an occupied house.
- Whether ordinary diving equipment operates normally.
- Whether the route from the town to the upper surface requires decompression.

If you cannot get those answers, your first visit should be a short reconnaissance in a suitable protective system, with independent life support and a reliable return route—not an overnight stay.

The inhabitants may be entirely human, or they may only look familiar. Their ability to travel between houses, hold their breath, and sleep partly immersed should not be treated as evidence that you can do the same.

## Breathing and escape

Bring breathing equipment appropriate to the measured conditions, with a genuinely independent reserve. A recreational scuba setup is not automatically suitable merely because the water is warm and the streets are narrow.

An expedition-grade rebreather could offer quiet movement and long endurance, but only if you are trained to use it, its chemistry works here, and you have adequate bailout gas. Otherwise, arrange a professionally designed alternative.

Keep your breathing system available even inside a house until the air has been checked. A roof pocket could contain breathable air, stale air, excess carbon dioxide, or gases released by the mountain. The arrival of a fresh seedpod is a supply event, not proof of air quality.

**Do not make “swim upward” your emergency plan.** The surface may be far away, obstructed by the floating forest, or separated from you by unsafe pressure changes. The lower sea is not an alternative exit; reaching it might mean passing through the edge of the suspended water and falling.

Your return point should be:

- Tested from the actual arrival location.
- Usable while wearing all your equipment.
- Reachable without entering an unfamiliar house.
- Marked in a way you can recognize in poor visibility.
- Accessible if one route is blocked by roots or changed ground.

If multiverse travel uses a portal or retrieval device, establish exactly what happens at a boundary between air and water. An open doorway that unexpectedly drains a room—or pulls someone through it—would be disastrous here.

## Wear equipment for gardens, not open ocean

This is a place of ropes, hanging roots, narrow passages, and living walls. Streamline everything.

Avoid dangling straps, exposed hooks, bulky packs, and long fins that could catch or tear the plants. Secure tools close to your body. Carry an accessible line cutter for emergencies, but do not confuse the ability to cut yourself free with permission to damage a house.

Useful equipment includes:

- A compact, adjustable buoyancy system.
- Exposure clothing chosen for the measured temperature.
- Gloves that protect your hands without depriving you of touch.
- Soft, well-fitting footwear.
- A small, dimmable light.
- A waterproof writing slate.
- Depth, pressure, time, and temperature instruments.
- A compact medical kit sealed against water.
- Drinking water and familiar food in watertight containers.

Do not copy the children’s stone belts. Their ballast suits their bodies, habits, and environment. Yours needs to be tested with your complete breathing equipment.

Warm water can still chill you over hours if it is cooler than your body. If it is hotter, exertion and insulating clothing may instead cause overheating. Either possibility matters more than the water’s initial comfort.

Protect small cuts. You will be handling living stems, old ropes, mineral surfaces, and equipment shared by many people.

## Arrange a host before arranging a holiday

A local guide is not an optional cultural enhancement. They are your street map, current forecast, structural inspector, and introduction to the air carriers.

Before arriving, establish where you will stay and whether that house can safely accommodate your size and equipment. The shallow upper chambers may offer enough room to lift your face into the air, but not enough to sit upright, remove a breathing unit, or help an unconscious visitor.

Ask your host:

- How many visitors can the room’s air supply support?
- How often is its air replaced, and how is stale air removed?
- Where can heavy equipment be secured?
- Which beams and roots can take weight?
- What signal means “I need air” or “I need help”?
- Which routes are safe for a stranger?

Bring compensation agreed upon in advance. Your presence consumes air that someone has carried down, room in a small chamber, and time that might otherwise go to tending a house. It would be easy to mistake the town’s slow pace for an abundance of spare labor.

## Learn how to enter a house

A doorway is not necessarily an invitation to swim through it.

There may be a particular approach that avoids disturbing a roof pocket, pulling on a supporting root, or depositing sediment in the household’s air chamber. Let your host demonstrate it.

Move slowly beneath roofs. Do not brace yourself against leaves or stems simply because they resemble walls. They may be living structural members. A root descending from the forest may be holding up part of the room.

Secure your belongings where instructed. A dropped object might drift into a flooded workspace, become lodged in a planting basket, or vanish through a gap you cannot safely follow.

Speak once your head is properly in the air chamber. Outside, assume speech will not carry intelligibly. Agree on a small set of underwater hand signals with your guide before you need them.

The air carriers’ three knocks are a useful lesson: here, arrival is something you announce gently.

## Take sleeping seriously

Do not plan to imitate local sleeping arrangements on your first night.

An ordinary human can drown quietly if a partly immersed sleeping position shifts and the face slips below the air pocket. Warmth and exhaustion make that risk easier to underestimate.

Use an arrangement that reliably keeps your airway in verified breathable air, supports your body, and allows assistance. Have your host and expedition lead assess it beforehand. Keep emergency breathing equipment reachable without having to dive for it.

Avoid alcohol and sedatives. They are a poor addition to an unfamiliar sleeping platform, an unfamiliar atmosphere, and water beneath your chin.

For a short first visit, returning elsewhere to sleep may be much wiser than trying to prove you can live as the residents do.

## Move at the town’s pace

Use the hand ropes as shown. Keep near the established paths. Let residents pass rather than trying to overtake them through their gardens.

The broad open water above a lane may look easier than a narrow passage, but it removes your handholds and exposes you to currents you may not feel until you are being carried sideways. Root curtains also become difficult to read from within them.

Never assume that an unfinished path leads somewhere. Some end where the next piece of mountain is expected to grow.

Be particularly cautious around:

- Deep clefts crossed by ropes.
- Open edges beyond the terraces.
- Neglected, overgrown houses.
- Hot hollows that visibly distort the view.
- Newly marked ground.

Fresh stone may yield under your hand and still have great significance to the town. Do not test it with your thumb, hammer in a stake, or anchor equipment there. A casual dent could outlast your visit by years.

## Food, water, and small courtesies

Bring your own drinking water. Warm, apparently clean water is not necessarily potable, especially when it contains minerals and supports an unfamiliar ecology.

Treat local foods as unfamiliar until their compatibility with human digestion is established. Cooking may make a root tender without removing a toxin or allergen.

Ask about meals early. A communal oven is not a place where an unexpected guest can request something quickly. Offering to share sealed, compatible provisions may be more considerate than expecting the household to find another portion.

Do not pick the milky beads, taste the sweet stems, or harvest wax without permission. A garden can be food supply, wall, roof, and future building material at once.

Do not prune anything to improve your view.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect a kind of fatigue that is difficult to predict from the description.

Your eyes will continually adjust to suspended mineral haze. Distances will be deceptive. You will hear noises without easily locating them. Every errand will require some attention to breathing, buoyancy, handholds, and the whereabouts of your equipment.

You may also feel unusually exposed. Through the outer gardens you can see clouds below the ground on which you are standing. There is water all around you, yet somewhere beyond it is an edge that is not a shore.

Make the first outings short. Give yourself a familiar room, a familiar route, and time to stop navigating.

Then, once the practical uncertainties are settled, leave space for the town’s quietness. Watch an air carrier empty a pod. Sit with the elders beside an unfinished street. Notice how much work goes into keeping a doorway open, and how little anyone gains by hurrying the mountain.

The best-prepared visitor will not be the one carrying the most equipment. It will be the one whose equipment allows them to stop worrying long enough to understand why a plant, a knot, and a warm patch of yielding stone can already be the beginning of a neighborhood.
