Prepare for this city as you would for a cold mountain journey—but treat the ability to leave as more important than anything you might carry.

The landscape is welcoming in its arrangement. It has resting places, sheltered gardens, manageable stairs, water within reach of each neighborhood. It was built by people who understood the effort of carrying a load uphill. Yet none of those accommodations is necessarily available to you now. A bench may still support you, but a cistern may not yield water. A doorway may be open, but the air beyond it may not admit a breath.

The central question is not simply whether time has stopped. It is **what happens where your continuing life meets things that no longer change**.

## Arrange a return before entering

Do not make your first visit a one-way arrival, and do not rely on reaching a particular building to leave.

Ideally, your means of return should:

- Travel with you and operate independently of the city.
- Work without local electricity, combustion, running water, or help.
- Return you to a place where someone expects you.
- Be usable without a long sequence of delicate actions.

If your method permits a brief visit to the outskirts first, take it. Arrive on a broad, dry, level patch away from people, suspended water, vegetation, and steep drops. Stay close to the arrival point until you know that walking, breathing, and returning work normally.

An observer on the other side is useful only if you have established that communication crosses the same boundary you do. “They will retrieve me after ten minutes” is not a sufficient plan when you do not know whose ten minutes will pass.

## Bring a self-contained margin of ordinary life

Dress for a high, cold, exposed settlement: wool or insulating layers, a wind-resistant outer garment, gloves, a hat, sturdy boots with reliable grip, and protection for your eyes and skin against strong mountain light.

Choose a compact backpack rather than a wheeled case. The streets are stairs, and even the larger routes leave little reason to expect wheels to help. Keep your hands free. A heavy expedition load would make the porters’ resting courts useful to you, but it would also make a fast retreat harder.

Carry:

- Drinking water from outside the city.
- Ready-to-eat food that needs no cooking.
- A first-aid kit and any personal medication.
- An independent light.
- A notebook, pencil, and camera.
- A familiar watch or timer.
- A small insulating pad, so you can rest without depending on a local furnishing.

If available, suitable breathing equipment and someone competent to help you use it would be a sensible precaution for an initial survey. The description does not establish that the air is unbreathable, but it does not settle the matter either. Ordinary breathing requires air to move, and your body requires ongoing chemical processes. A person who can walk here may bring those processes with them—or may merely be able to take a few steps before discovering the exception is incomplete.

Do not plan to replenish anything locally on your first visit. Clear water, full gardens, and neatly stored goods are not the same thing as usable supplies.

## Make the first visit short and modest

Your first objective should be to discover whether you can safely be present, not to reach the upper terraces.

At the arrival point, pay attention to simple things:

- Can you breathe comfortably?
- Does your own clothing move normally?
- Can you hear your own voice and footsteps?
- Does your watch advance?
- Does a small amount of water **you brought** pour normally into **your own** cup?
- Can you return immediately?

Use your own objects for these observations. There is no need to poke a suspended droplet, bend a leaf, or move someone’s hand to establish that something unusual is happening.

These checks will not give you a complete theory. Your watch may measure only the interval you experience; your water may behave differently from water already in the city. They can nevertheless distinguish a place in which you can conduct a cautious visit from one in which you should leave at once.

If breathing becomes difficult, movement feels unexpectedly resistant, or your return mechanism behaves differently, stop the visit. Do not push farther in hoping that the main square will be more normal.

## Do not mistake stillness for structural safety

The city looks extraordinarily well preserved. That does not tell you how it will respond to a visitor’s weight.

A stair may bear a porter without moving because the porter and stair are held in the same condition. Your foot introduces something new. Whether the stone behaves like ordinary stone under that new load is an unanswered question.

Favor broad ground-level routes. Avoid roofs, narrow bridges, retaining-wall edges, unsupported balconies, and the sharply fractured mountain rock. Do not use a motionless tree, person, or suspended object as a handhold.

The dry paths are preferable to wet stairs and channels. A patch of arrested water might behave as liquid, as an obstacle, or in some way neither comparison captures. You need not find out with your full weight.

Remember also that a roof reached directly from the uphill slope may have a substantial drop on its downhill side. The city’s low profile from above is deceptive.

## Leave the water alone

Water is the most inviting resource here and the one I would be most reluctant to disturb.

Do not drink from the cisterns, scoop up the river water, walk through the projecting overflow, or put a finger into the curve between the jug and bowl. Its transparency tells you little about what contact would do.

Perhaps a cupful would become ordinary water once removed. Perhaps it could not be removed. Perhaps touching it would allow part of a suspended event to continue. None of those possibilities is established by the view, and the inhabitants should not bear the cost of finding out.

Give suspended streams and droplets extra room as you pass. The arc at the table is not merely a curiosity: it is part of someone’s unfinished action.

## Treat the inhabitants as people in an interrupted day

Nothing described suggests that they are dead. Their expressions belong to work, play, and attention, not to a shared catastrophe. You should behave as though they may resume their lives, whether in a moment or long after your departure.

That means:

- Do not reposition people or animals.
- Do not take tools, food, clothing, or “souvenirs.”
- Do not enter houses merely because no one can stop you.
- Do not rearrange a burden to make a porter look more comfortable.
- Do not handle children or inspect bodies.
- Photograph with the restraint you would use around living strangers.

The porter leaning into his straps is particularly easy to misread. You may want to relieve him of his load. But if the event resumes, his balance and effort may depend on that load remaining exactly where it is.

There is no evident emergency that requires you to intervene. Apparent kindness can still disturb a situation you do not understand.

Move around animals as carefully as you would around resting ones. Give the fox its passage and the roof goats their space. Their inability to yield makes courtesy more necessary, not less.

## Navigate by your own record

The sunlight will not help you judge how long you have been walking. Neither will smoke, meal times, passing traffic, or changing weather.

Mark your route in your notebook rather than on the city. Note junctions and distinctive buildings; take photographs looking back toward the arrival point. Set a conservative turnaround interval using your own timer, but also heed fatigue, cold, hunger, and breathlessness.

The sustained sounds may be disorienting. A watery murmur that never changes and a wooden note that never fades are not reliable signs of active machinery, running water, or someone nearby. Do not pursue the faint vibration solely because it seems to promise an explanation.

There is no visible ordinary region beyond the city. Walking downhill is not a demonstrated escape route.

## Expect the emotional difficulty to arrive slowly

At first, the city may feel peaceful. No crowd presses you onward. Nothing deteriorates. The gardens are rich without engulfing the stone, and every open space seems intentional.

After a while, that peace may become loneliness. You can see people but cannot share their afternoon. A child’s expression remains available to your gaze long past the point at which looking would normally become intrusive. The city offers all the signs of habitation without the reciprocal movements that make a visitor feel welcome.

A companion can help you remain grounded, provided you stay together and have enough supplies for both. Keep the first visit brief even if you feel well. Fascination is a poor measure of how long you should remain.

My intuition is that this is not a ruined city or an abandoned one. It is an inhabited place whose ordinary life is inaccessible to you. Prepare to witness it, not to use it.

Come warmly dressed, provisioned from elsewhere, and able to return. Walk lightly. Let the woman keep her hand between the threads, the bird remain just above its stone, and the water keep its bright curve between jug and bowl. Leave their next moment intact.
