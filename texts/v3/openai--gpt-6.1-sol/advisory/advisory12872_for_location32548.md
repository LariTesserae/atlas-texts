Prepare for this plateau as you would for a place where **shelter is the real geography**. The distances between houses matter less than the distances between places where a human body can safely stop moving.

The stone looks calm. The air is not. Almost everything the inhabitants do—crouching, touching an arm before speaking, crawling into houses, carrying their packs low—is an accommodation to that difference. Learn those habits before attempting a crossing.

## Arrange your arrival carefully

Do not arrive on a summit, at a cliff edge, or in the middle of an attractive green patch. Arrange to enter through an inhabited, sheltered hollow, preferably with someone expecting you.

If your means of multiverse travel requires a return device, doorway, beacon, or fixed departure point, place it somewhere protected from wind, shifting vegetation, and drainage. A patch of greenery is not necessarily permanent ground cover; a dry pool is not necessarily solid ground. Test whether your equipment can function under stone or within a dwelling before relying on it.

For a first visit, make your destination a particular household rather than “the plateau.” There may be no visible settlement to walk toward if your arrival goes wrong. Smoke will not reliably show you where people are.

A local guide is close to essential. Their knowledge is not just a map of the country. It is an understanding of when that country is passable.

## Prepare your body for wind, not merely for walking

The rounded terrain invites a deceptively ordinary kind of travel: long strides, an upright posture, a high pack, eyes on the horizon. That is probably the wrong way to move here.

Before going, practice:

- Walking and resting in a crouch.
- Crawling through low entrances while wearing your equipment.
- Removing and replacing a pack in a confined space.
- Keeping your balance under irregular lateral pressure.
- Communicating without raising your voice.
- Spending several hours with persistent noise and little relief from it.

Knee, hip, and lower-back comfort will matter. Bring flexible knee protection and gloves that allow a good grip on stone and a staff. Do not overpack: a load that feels manageable on a sheltered trail may become exhausting when carried low through hours of gusts.

Ask about the actual atmospheric pressure. The plateau’s apparent height and warmth do not tell you whether an ordinary human can breathe comfortably there. If its air is thin, acclimatization still matters; warm rock does not prevent altitude illness.

## Dress without giving the wind anything to seize

Wear close-fitting layers with secured cuffs. Avoid capes, loose scarves, dangling straps, and broad unsecured hats.

Useful equipment would include:

- A wind-resistant outer layer.
- A light insulating layer for dawn, night, and enforced stops.
- Close-fitting eye protection against grit.
- Gloves and grippy footwear suitable for smooth, sometimes wet stone.
- A secured sun hat or fitted hood.
- Sunscreen and lip protection.
- A compact waterproof layer.

Do not mistake warmth for mildness. Your legs may be heated by the ground while your shoulders are chilled by the wind. You need layers you can adjust without unpacking everything.

There is little standing-height shade. Wind may make sunlight feel less punishing than it is, and it may conceal how much moisture you are losing.

A low, compact pack is preferable to a tall one. Secure every object before leaving shelter. A dropped glove or water bottle may immediately become an object you cannot safely retrieve.

## Treat wind as a condition that grants or denies passage

The most important piece of equipment is a willingness to wait.

An ordinary traveler may think, *The next hollow is only a short walk away.* A resident may see that the crossing is presently impossible. Believe the resident.

Ask your guide to establish simple signals before departure:

- Stop.
- Lower yourself.
- Follow me.
- Return to shelter.
- I am hurt.
- I cannot continue.

Hand signals, agreed touches, and proximity will be more dependable than shouting. Touching someone’s forearm before speaking is worth learning as both a practical habit and a local courtesy.

Bring hearing protection suitable for prolonged noise, but do not isolate yourself completely from warning sounds or your guide. Communications equipment may help, yet microphones can be overwhelmed by wind. Test it outside while you are still close to shelter.

The forked staff deserves attention. Have a resident show you how to use one. Its value is not simply that it is a third leg: it lets you test a purchase before committing your weight. An unfamiliar crack is not automatically a trustworthy anchor.

If a crossing becomes unsafe, getting low and following your guide’s direction is more useful than trying to hurry upright toward the destination. Do not chase anything that blows away.

## Keep far more distance from the edges than seems necessary

The cliffs are not sightseeing platforms.

Their depth is unknown, their lower reaches are obscured, and air rises violently from the valleys. There is no good reason to approach an edge merely to see farther down. Do not assume that a rounded lip is safe because it lacks loose rubble or an obvious overhang.

Let local people define safe routes and setbacks. Do not invent a universal “safe distance” from the description alone.

The same caution applies away from the cliffs:

- Green mats may conceal hollows or channels.
- Vegetation can lift or tear free.
- Old pool beds can have soft mud beneath a dry crust.
- Wet smooth stone may become treacherous.
- Fissures may contain heat, water, or unstable margins.

Do not clip a safety line to a living vine, an enclosed stone in a tree, or a convenient root without reliable knowledge of its strength. The vegetation is adapted to surviving here; that does not mean every part of it can hold an extra human.

If ropes are needed, they should be part of a planned crossing with assessed anchors, not an improvised answer to being too close to a precipice.

## Bring water even though the place is green

The plants’ success does not imply easy drinking water for you. They collect moisture in small, sheltered spaces over long hours. You will be crossing exposed stone between those spaces.

Carry enough water for the intended journey **and for waiting out an unsafe crossing**. Ask where dependable supplies are, how they are collected, and whether a pool or crack has changed recently.

Treat unfamiliar water. Clear water is not necessarily safe, and warmth does not sterilize it. If the local biology differs substantially from your own, ordinary camping filters may not cover every risk. For an initial visit, a known compatible supply is the safest choice.

Do not plan to refill from every visible pool. A pool can drain, and its banks may not support you safely. Nor should you peel back vegetation to collect dew unless someone teaches you an established method. Those mats are the plateau’s soil, shelter, and water economy at once.

Bring compact, familiar food that can be eaten inside shelter. Pale berries and fleshy stalks may be edible to residents without being safe for you. Ask, and begin cautiously.

## Sleep in shelter designed for this place

A conventional tent is a poor default. Its height and exposed fabric could make it difficult to secure, and reliable anchor points may be scarce.

Stay in a local dwelling if invited, or use a sleeping pit that a resident has identified as safe and available. The low entrance and turns are not inconveniences to redesign. They keep the outside conditions outside.

Bring a compact sleeping system, a moisture-resistant ground layer, and a small electric light. Avoid adding flames to an enclosed space already lit by oil lamps unless your hosts approve. Do not occupy an abandoned shelter merely because it exists: it may be damaged, poorly situated, or no longer maintained.

Inspect any sleeping place for drainage. A shallow basin can be pleasantly warm after sunset and still be the wrong place during a sudden sheet of rain.

Keep your boots, water, and essential equipment where you can reach them in darkness. Secure them even indoors if the entrance is open to gusts.

## Learn how to be a guest

These houses may be difficult to recognize from outside. Walk gently around inhabited hollows, and do not step onto something that might be a roof.

Ask before touching the living roof ties, moving slabs, cutting vines, or taking fuel. A root that looks incidental may be structural. A small basin of plants may be a carefully used source of food and moisture.

Useful gifts would be compact, durable, and chosen in consultation with your hosts: sound cord, needles, suitable cutting tools, or repair materials. Avoid introducing seeds, insects, or organisms from another world. Clean your boots and equipment before arrival.

A bird feather is evidently noteworthy. If you find one, show it to your guide rather than assuming it is a disposable souvenir. Finding it may be news worth sharing.

## Expect the place to wear on your attention

The hardest part may not be a dramatic gust. It may be hours of noise, enormous scale, interrupted speech, and the effort of remaining physically small.

You may become tired without noticing it. You may start shouting unnecessarily, miss a signal, or want to stand upright simply because you are tired of crouching. Build generous rest into the visit.

Sheltered hollows will probably feel extraordinarily intimate: warm earth, bruised leaves, water dropping quietly beneath foliage. Enjoy that relief, but do not let it persuade you that the next crossing is equally gentle.

For a first trip, plan to experience one dwelling, one sheltered basin, and one short guided crossing. Allow extra days rather than insisting on covering distance.

**The governing rule is simple: do not spend your safety to satisfy your curiosity.** Wait when the residents wait. Keep low when they keep low. Let the immeasurable cliffs remain immeasurable.

This is a place where a fern survives behind a stone no higher than your hand. Prepare to notice how much protection that small stone provides—and to accept the same protection when you need it.
