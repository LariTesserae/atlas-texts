Prepare for a city where **the ground, the hour, and the distance to your destination all require local confirmation**.

It is not an uninhabitable place. People raise children here, maintain gardens, commute to work, and spend their days off looking at a cliff. That matters. You do not need to arrive dressed for an expedition into a catastrophe. You need to arrive ready to become, for a while, a very careful pedestrian.

The city’s dangers seem to come less from violence than from applying ordinary assumptions at the wrong moment: assuming a visible ledge is available to stand on, assuming an empty courtyard is a short walk, assuming a noise has finished happening because someone elsewhere heard it three days ago.

## Before you arrive

Arrange lodging in an established residential district, preferably close to a staffed station and on the same terrace as your arrival point. For a first visit, convenience means fewer crossings, not a better view.

Ask your host three questions:

- Is the route from the station currently settled?
- Are there any outstanding stone reports for this district?
- What should a visitor do here when the hour needle trembles?

Those answers are more useful than a general assurance that the city is safe.

Obtain the current municipal visitor guidance, even if it consists of several thin gray sheets with corrections attached. Keep the issue date and district designation. Regulations revised slowly after agreement may contain distinctions that sound absurd to an outsider but preserve somebody’s hard-earned experience.

If your means of multiverse travel allows you to specify an arrival point, choose a staffed, inhabited location rather than an apparently clear patch of mountain. Do not establish your return point on fresh rock, a projected formation, or an unfinished street. Confirm how your return mechanism behaves if local time becomes unsettled. An off-world clock is not necessarily protection against a local hour that has not fully taken hold.

## What to pack

### Clothing for alternating exposure and damp

Bring layers rather than one enormous coat:

- A warm base layer.
- An insulating middle layer.
- A wind-resistant, water-resistant outer coat with a hood and a collar that closes at the throat.
- Gloves that allow you to handle tickets, railings, and radio controls.
- A warm hat.
- Spare socks and a dry change of clothing in a sealed bag.

You may walk through a cloud indoors and emerge onto bare, dry stone in cold air. A garment that becomes heavy and stays wet will be unpleasant here.

Wear broken-in boots with good traction on wet stone and metal steps. Avoid smooth soles and trailing clothing. There are long stairways, industrial walkways, condensation, and considerable empty space beneath some routes.

### Water and food

Carry drinking water even when the air looks saturated. **Visible dampness is not the same as an accessible water supply.**

Refill at designated potable sources. Do not assume that condensation beneath a freight platform, a warm pipe leak, or water collected in soft mineral folds is safe to drink. The gardens are evidence that water is present, not that it is clean.

Keep a substantial snack with you. A three-hour rail journey can become longer if trains must wait for the hour to settle, and the widely separated districts may offer few opportunities to buy food between stops.

### Light, maps, and communication

Bring:

- A dependable torch, with spare power.
- An offline or paper map showing established public routes.
- A notebook and pencil.
- A receiver capable of receiving municipal broadcasts, if local equipment is available.
- Your lodging address written in the local form.
- Essential medication, with several days of margin.

Do not rely solely on a digital map that confidently fills blank areas. Here, “projected” is a consequential category, not just a planner’s annotation.

A watch is useful for recording what happened according to your own instrument. It should not replace station clocks or district instructions. In your notebook, record the district and local clock reading as well as your own time when making important arrangements.

A dust mask and protective glasses are sensible additions if you intend to visit works or exposed construction areas. They are not a reason to enter those places without permission.

## Learn the three clocks before traveling independently

The railway clock tells you about trains. The district clock tells you about local arrangements. The needle tells you something more fundamental: whether the present hour is secure enough for ordinary activity.

Have a station employee explain the needle in person. Learn where passengers wait when it becomes unstable and how the all-clear is communicated.

When it trembles, do not treat the delay as an opportunity to explore an empty platform or take a staircase you had not noticed before. Follow the local procedure. The precautions around children, stairs, mirrors, and metal pouring suggest that this is not merely an administrative inconvenience.

For an ordinary traveler, a conservative response is appropriate:

- Stop unnecessary movement.
- Stay in a staffed, established place.
- Keep away from stairs and mirrors as instructed locally.
- Do not follow newly visible corridors, extra flights, or apparently occupied extensions of a street.
- Wait for local confirmation before resuming your journey.

If your hotel corridor has become longer, do not attempt to prove that your room is still at the far end. The resident who sits beside the lift is demonstrating a useful survival skill: declining to turn an irregularity into an adventure.

Allow generous slack in all bookings. Avoid an itinerary in which a delayed tram causes you to miss your only route home.

## Believe the timetable, not your eyes

The reservoir that looks like a pleasant walk before lunch is three hours away by rail. Treat that example as a warning about the whole landscape.

Sparse buildings and enormous clean surfaces deprive you of ordinary distance cues. A school and tram stop may appear to share a modest square until you discover that the terrace is larger than the center of a village.

Plan journeys by established travel times. Before walking between districts, ask about:

- Actual walking time.
- Elevation changes.
- Sheltered waiting places.
- The last return service.
- Whether the route includes unfinished ground or a district boundary.

Do not set out toward a landmark merely because you can see it.

The clear overhead sky may also give you little practical sense of the day’s progress. Keep track of meals, warmth, and fatigue deliberately. You may be tired long before the light looks like evening.

## Respect barriers, including the ones that appear premature

Some fences are installed before the ground behind them becomes accessible to humans. That means the city has learned to protect people from possibilities as well as from existing drops.

A fox reaching a ledge does not establish that you can reach it. An animal vanishing halfway across a courtyard is not demonstrating a shortcut. Its routes may belong to a different relationship with the mountain.

Similarly:

- A rail laid on sleepers is not necessarily an operating railway.
- A staircase descending into a gap is not necessarily unfinished in a harmless way.
- A lamp crate does not mean a public route is about to open.
- A surface that looks solid may still be young mineral material.

Do not press your thumb into soft stone, remove samples, move survey stakes, or step around barriers for a better photograph. The mountain is not merely scenery; it is active infrastructure beneath a populated city.

For visits to the exposed works, use an authorized route and, at least initially, a local guide. Ask whether the final bench is open to visitors that day. Its existence does not guarantee that every path leading toward it is safe.

## Take delayed sound seriously

The mountain’s reports do not arrive everywhere at once. You cannot reliably decide whether a fracture is “over” by when it occurred elsewhere.

If a municipal broadcast announces a report affecting your district, follow the building’s precautions. Ask your host how the window braces work; do not improvise structural bracing yourself. Until the local sound has passed, avoid sitting beside glass and leave fragile objects where the residents advise.

At the outer works, ask guides what to do if stone hardens nearby. Do not assume that a deep report is only a sound, or that silence means nothing is happening.

Light hearing protection may make industrial travel more comfortable, but avoid isolating noise cancellation while walking. You need to hear bells, announcements, workers, and whatever warning sounds the locals recognize.

## Leave the animals alone

Most animals leave people alone. Help preserve that arrangement.

Do not feed them, approach young animals, or stand across an obvious route. Give the horned browsers generous space beneath the viaducts. Do not follow goats onto cooling-tower rims.

Expect birds to emerge suddenly from traffic-signal housings. Be especially cautious at crossings: a burst of wings can startle you exactly when you need to attend to the signal.

Stay out of rail yards unless escorted. The hidden rail-dwelling animal is interesting, but the working railway is the more immediate reason not to investigate it. Do not lift equipment, put fingers into rail openings, or linger on tracks listening for taps.

At the bathhouse, obey the attendants when changing rooms close. An unlocked outer door is not an invitation to enter a temporary slope. The staff may be leaving a passage available for reasons that have nothing to do with human visitors.

Before returning to another universe, inspect your clothes and luggage for insects, seeds, spores, or small stowaways. Do not transport plants or animals without explicit clearance. A city whose bathhouse acquires gardens through occasional openings should make a traveler thoughtful about what else can cross a threshold.

## Be careful around the gardens and pipes

The lush places may be the city’s most inviting spaces, especially after a cold journey. They are also maintained systems whose water sources can be narrow and precarious.

Stay on paths. Do not disturb condensation channels, scrape moss from drain mouths, or “help” by closing a dripping valve. A leak may be supporting a corridor of vegetation that took decades to establish.

If you notice a fault, report it to a gardener or the relevant municipal department rather than fixing it. Here, the useful answer may be to repair the pipe, preserve the leak, or alter it carefully; a visitor cannot tell which by looking.

The grove beneath the eastern freight station sounds like an excellent early destination: public, inhabited, sheltered, and close to railway staff. Visit as a passenger, not as an explorer of its foundations. Expect dripping leaves and heavy vibration overhead.

## Make your first day deliberately modest

A good first day would be:

1. Arrive at a staffed station.
2. Learn the clocks and obtain current notices.
3. Travel directly to your lodging.
4. Confirm drinking water, meals, heating, and local emergency procedures.
5. Take one short walk within the established district.
6. Return before you are cold or tired.

Do not make the outer mountain your first excursion. Spend a day learning how residents cross streets, wait for trams, respond to announcements, and decide that a route is not worth taking.

For longer outings, tell someone your destination and intended return service. Arrange a check-in using a named station or event as well as a clock time: “after the returning reservoir train” may be a useful supplement to “at six,” depending on local practice.

## Prepare for the city’s pace

You may find the place less frightening than lonely.

There is room for far more people than are present. Machines work briefly and rest. A lift’s slow motor can become part of the room rather than a sign of imminent arrival. From a distance the city sounds busy; beside you, nothing may happen for a long time.

Bring something to read. Learn to wait without wandering. Eat before you become irritable, put on another layer before you become chilled, and do not interpret every pause as a malfunction.

The inhabitants’ calm is probably not indifference. It looks like a developed ability to distinguish between conditions that require action and conditions that require patience. Try to borrow that judgment before borrowing their confidence.

Eventually, you may be ready to sit on the bench at the final survey stake. Go warmly dressed, with water, a confirmed return route, and enough time that you do not have to hurry back. Stay on the human side of the works. Watch the new precipice without demanding that it do anything.

The essential preparation is to accept that this city is already a home while still becoming a place. You can visit it safely only by respecting both facts.
