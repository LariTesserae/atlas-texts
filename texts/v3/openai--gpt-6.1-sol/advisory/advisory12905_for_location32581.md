Prepare for this hollow as you would for a visit to a crowded, working household that happens to contain a small neighborhood. It is not a cave, a ruin, or an underground wilderness. Almost everything you might mistake for clutter is somebody’s equipment, route, storage, or carefully managed patch of life.

Your main difficulties will be heat, footing, air quality, and not knowing where to put yourself. Your main protection will be a local person who can show you how the place works.

## Before descending

Arrange to be met at the entrance rather than finding your own way through. Ask your host three practical questions:

- Where should I leave my bag?
- Which water is for drinking?
- How do I get back to the square if the usual route is blocked?

The hollow is small enough to tempt you into thinking you can navigate it by sight. You probably cannot. A door you can see may be reached by a bridge from another level; an apparently direct stair may end at a drying rack. Learn a route as a sequence of turns and landmarks, not simply as a direction toward the exit.

If you use a multiverse transit device, establish your return point somewhere clear and stable, preferably above in the square. Do not assume that an empty-looking corner below will remain empty. It may be where baskets go during rain, where a door swings, or where someone needs to stand to operate a bucket.

For a first visit, choose a dry morning. The retained warmth will already be noticeable, but you will avoid the afternoon’s accumulation of heat and see the household’s routes before evening makes the recesses harder to read.

## Dress for warmth and awkward movement

Wear light, breathable clothes that allow you to turn sideways, crouch, and climb without loose fabric catching on anything. Avoid trailing hems, dangling straps, and jewelry that can hook a cord or wicker edge.

Your shoes matter more than almost anything else. Bring closed-toe footwear with a flexible, grippy sole. The court has polished patches, damp depressions, and little channels; the stairs have been worn into scoops. Heavy expedition boots may be unnecessarily clumsy, while smooth-soled sandals leave you vulnerable to slipping, sharp fragments, and having a foot stepped on.

Carry very little. A slim bag held close to your body is better than a broad backpack. You should be able to turn in a shoulder-width passage without sweeping anything off a shelf.

Useful things to bring include:

- A modest supply of drinking water until you know the local source.
- A small light with a low setting.
- A clean cloth for sweat and dusty hands.
- Any personal medication, kept dry and accessible.
- A few dressings for minor cuts.
- Hearing protection if sustained noise bothers you.
- A well-fitting particulate mask if clay dust or smoke aggravates your breathing.

Keep these tucked away rather than hanging from your belt. Anything dangling will find a ladder, a jar handle, or a projecting shelf.

Do not dress as though the place is permanently dark and cold. Daylight reaches it, and the brick retains heat through the night.

## Treat the air as a real consideration

Warm air is not necessarily fresh air. There are cooking fires, a charcoal kiln, grinding work, animal enclosures, and recesses where air barely moves.

Ask where visitors should stay while the kiln is operating. Avoid lingering in its room or in stagnant corners near combustion sources. A particulate mask can reduce inhaled dust, but it does **not** protect you from carbon monoxide.

If you develop headache, dizziness, nausea, unusual weakness, or confusion, go up into open air promptly. Do not simply sit down in the coolest niche and wait. Those symptoms may have several causes, but an enclosed place with charcoal burning is not somewhere to dismiss them.

If you have asthma or significant sensitivity to smoke, arrange a short first visit and keep an uncomplicated route out. A discreet portable carbon monoxide detector could be useful, though it should supplement—not replace—local guidance and your own judgment.

The pale powder from grinding clay is another reason not to make yourself helpful too quickly. Do not dry-sweep, blow dust off an object, or lean close to watch grinding without asking.

## Learn how to occupy space

The most important local skill may be standing somewhere that does not interrupt three jobs at once.

A broad landing may be a passing place. A clear strip of court may be the route for a dragging basket. The space beneath a shelf may be needed when someone lifts a load. Ask where to wait, and stay there until you understand the traffic.

Watch how residents turn their shoulders at corners and divide loads before entering narrow passages. Copy that economy of movement. Keep your hands free on unfamiliar stairs, and do not assume every rail or cord is a handhold. A balcony rail might support the fig; a cord might control a light board.

Give priority to people carrying hot vessels, full buckets, or awkward loads. Do not stop them on a stair to ask a question.

The dogs are also part of the traffic problem. Let a resident show you how to get past one sprawled across a route rather than confidently stepping over it while balancing on worn tile.

## Never block a drain, even briefly

Learn the small channels crossing the court before setting anything down. They may look too slight to matter in dry weather, but they are what allow the hollow to survive a shower without becoming a basin.

Do not park a bag across one, stuff a rag into a wet seam, move a splash plate, or rearrange overlapping drain tiles. Likewise, do not “fix” the leaking pipe joint or remove the cloth beneath it. The leak supports a deliberately maintained patch of moss, and downstream water passes through other useful arrangements.

Here, a defect may have become part of the system.

The same applies to structural repairs. A fiber binding, shaped sherd, or patched step is not an invitation to test how loose it is. Never dig into the mound or pry at a wall. There is no reason to expect open ground behind it; there may be older construction, packed rubble, or another occupied space.

## Be a considerate guest around food and animals

Ask before touching drinking jars, tank covers, food baskets, or water-handling equipment. Clear-looking water is not proof of safety, and a cool storage niche is not automatically a place to put your own provisions.

Wash your hands before eating, especially after handling animals, dusty surfaces, or wicker. Follow whatever washing arrangement your host uses without contaminating stored water or leaving waste in a gutter.

Do not feed the cats, dogs, birds, hens, rabbits, or toads without permission. The animals have places in the household’s economy, even where they appear to be doing nothing. The toads’ bowl is pest control, not a curiosity to pick up and inspect.

For a multiverse visitor, biosecurity deserves attention too. Arrive with clean footwear and equipment, not mud, seeds, or insects from your previous stop. Do not bring live plants or animals as gifts. Carry away no moss, snails, feathers, or potsherds without explicit permission and appropriate precautions for your next destination.

## Expect noise, not silence

You will hear grain, mortars, birds, footsteps, buckets, fuel, conversation, and the occasional sharp crack from the kiln. Voices from the square may sound close while conveying nothing intelligible.

You may find this tiring even if you enjoy busy places. Plan a short first visit and a break aboveground.

Learn a few simple gestures with your host: wait, follow, move aside, come up. During rain, spoken instructions may become useless. Do not shout from one level to another unless that is how your host tells you to communicate; an unfamiliar shout could sound like an alarm.

## During a storm, stop being a tourist

When the roof openings turn gray and people begin hanging baskets up, get out of the working routes. Follow your host to a designated dry place.

Do not stand beneath a shaft to watch the rain arrive, cross a gutter for a better view, or approach a tank mouth. The water enters in hard streams, and the drainage depends on remaining unobstructed. Offer help only if someone can give you a clear task.

Afterward, wait until people resume moving before assuming the floor is safe. The hollow may dry quickly overall while individual tiles and polished steps remain slippery.

## What the visit will feel like

At first, you may feel that you are continually in the wrong place. Everything is close; several activities overlap; the walls return sounds without making their sources obvious. You will see nearly the whole hollow and still fail to understand how to reach half of it.

Then the order should begin to emerge. Food has a particular height. Smoke has preferred routes. Light is adjusted by hand. Damp is tolerated in some places and prevented in others. Even the smallest green patch receives care.

Bring curiosity, but resist the urge to improve things on sight. This is a place shaped by long use, not by neglect.

The best preparation is to arrive compact, rested, and willing to be taught. Leave with a better understanding of how people live there—not with a fragment of their wall in your pocket.
