Prepare for this as you would for a visit to a working greenhouse on a very high industrial roof—not a mountaintop hike, and not an expedition into a tropical wilderness. Its apparent naturalness is the thing most likely to mislead you. The warmth, water, stone, and vegetation are all supported by machinery, and the machinery is beginning to negotiate with things that grow.

Your best preparation is to arrive lightly equipped, with permission, and with enough time to stand still.

## Before you go

**Arrange your arrival through the service house or a caretaker.** If you travel by portal or another means that lets you choose an arrival point, use a designated landing area rather than materializing among the beds. There is very little spare ground, and what looks like an empty patch may be a drain, a soft mineral surface, or a space deliberately left for a moving ventilation panel.

Ask a few practical questions:

- Is the garden open to visitors today?
- Which paths and terraces are safe to use?
- Are any sections of the western bank newly deposited or under observation?
- What does a visitor do if the pumps stop or an alarm sounds?
- Where is the sheltered exit?
- Is the air at this elevation safe for an unacclimatized human?

That last question matters. Warm roots and tropical leaves do not establish that the air has enough oxygen for you. Being above the clouds does not, by itself, tell you the atmospheric pressure either. Check the actual conditions rather than guessing from the view. If supplemental oxygen or acclimatization is needed, arrange it before arrival.

Find out whether access involves the maintenance stair. Six floors of machinery may mean a substantial climb through warm service spaces; it may also mean that your only exit is a stair you should be physically able to manage.

## What to wear and carry

Wear **light layers beneath a wind-resistant outer layer**. Inside the foliage, you may become warm and damp. Four paces away, beside the southern rail, the air may be much cooler. A bulky winter coat will be awkward beneath the leaves, but arriving in tropical clothing alone is a poor bet.

Choose closed shoes with good wet-surface grip. The ceramic paths were made rough enough for bare feet, but that is not a promise that every wet plate, mossy edge, or lifted drain cover is safe. Shoes also offer some protection from warm surfaces and small maintenance debris. Avoid long, loose laces.

Useful items include:

- A small water bottle.
- Sun protection and sunglasses; the sunlight is ordinary, and there is little open space in which to avoid it.
- A compact rain shell.
- Any necessary medication, kept dry.
- A small cloth for glasses or a camera lens.
- A modest daypack that stays close to your body.

Leave large backpacks, trailing straps, umbrellas, and unnecessary tools behind. The paths bend beneath foliage and around exposed infrastructure. You do not want to hook a vine, catch a gutter, or turn around and knock a measured leaf off its stem.

Clean soil from your footwear and equipment before entering. This is a small, deliberately assembled growing system with very few animals. Bringing in seeds, insects, fungi, or ordinary soil could have consequences out of proportion to the size of your visit. Do not bring a pet.

## How to move through it

Treat the designated paths as your entire walking territory. There is no conventional soil beneath the moss: a green surface may cover a membrane, irrigation sleeve, ceramic rib, or root channel rather than anything capable of supporting your weight.

Walk slowly, especially on the lowest terrace, where water nearly meets the path. A plate giving slightly beneath your foot may be normal; a plate tilted by roots may not be. Ask the caretaker what movement is expected, rather than testing it yourself.

Do not use the pale western bank as a handhold. Some of it is still setting. Its soft contours are not simply weathered stone, and a ledge that looks substantial may be a young layer supported by wire and hidden plumbing. Likewise, do not lean on pipes or exhaust casings merely because plants grow beside them. Their temperature and function are not obvious from their appearance.

At the southern rail, keep both feet firmly on the path. Do not climb for a photograph or lean out to improve the view. Secure your camera before approaching, and resist the temptation to retrieve anything dropped beyond the boundary. The clouds conceal distance; they do not shorten it.

Yield to staff carrying baskets or equipment. In a space this small, a visitor standing in the wrong bend can obstruct a surprising amount of work.

## What not to touch

The grease-pencil marks are part of the operating life of the garden. Dates on ledges, lines beside pools, circles around moss, and numbers on leaves may all be records of change. Do not rub them away, move labels, or pick a leaf because it appears ready for harvest.

Ask before touching plants, entering the service house, or descending to the machinery. There may be accessible fruit, but edible species are not the same thing as food offered to visitors. Do not drink from the pools or gutters. Clear water here may have passed through mineral deposition, root seepage, or circulating process lines.

Leave small irregularities alone. A fern in a gauge housing or roots beneath a drain cover may look like a problem you could easily fix. It may instead be something the technicians are watching, or have already chosen to accommodate. **Report what you notice; do not repair it.**

The same applies to the frog. Watch it from the path, and do not try to catch, feed, or relocate it. In such a small enclosure, even one animal can have a specific job.

## Learn the difference between activity and trouble

This place will change while you are standing in it. A dry channel may begin running. Leaves may rise when irrigation pressure reaches their sleeves. Water may bead through a path plate and disappear again. None of those events necessarily means something has gone wrong.

Ask your host what is routine. In particular, learn how staff distinguish ordinary settling noises from a problem with the growing bank. The description includes occasional faint cracks followed by inspection; it does not give a visitor enough information to judge them independently.

If you see a new crack, a suddenly displaced plate, unusual flooding, leaking hot fluid, smoke, or a marked change in the machinery’s sound, step back and notify staff. Do not get closer to identify the source.

If the machinery stops, follow the caretaker’s instructions rather than assuming the garden has simply become quieter. Its heat, drainage, and irrigation depend on those systems. Keep the exit in mind from the beginning; the winding paths and broad leaves make a very small place surprisingly easy to lose your bearings in.

## Prepare for the sensations, too

Expect warm damp air close to the ground, wet foliage against your sleeves, a faint industrial smell near the flowering shrub, and something sourer near the fermentation beds. If you are sensitive to humid heat or strong smells, mention that before entering and stay near a clear route out.

If heights trouble you, begin away from the southern rail. The enclosure may feel intimate and sheltered until an opening reveals a weather system far below. There is no need to force yourself to the edge to appreciate the place.

You probably will not hear much wildlife. Much of the sound will be small and mechanical: dripping gutters, soft valve closures, a change in pump speed. Beneath that quiet, the machinery continues working. Someone who expects a garden to provide escape from infrastructure may find this unsettling; someone who enjoys seeing how things are sustained may find it absorbing.

## Allow time to watch

You could cross the enclosure quickly, but that would miss most of what makes it worth visiting. Set aside enough time to watch a trickle leave material behind, to notice a pool level change, or to see a caretaker compare new growth against an old mark.

Morning is likely to be more comfortable than the close, sultry noon described, though access and maintenance schedules should decide your timing. Do not assume a clear morning will remain dry: a rising cloud can become rain at the rail.

The garden is neither a finished display nor an abandoned machine being reclaimed. People are still tending it, measuring it, cutting it back, and sometimes moving a pipe instead of removing a root. Come prepared to respect that work.

The essential rule is simple: **do not mistake something living for something unmanaged, or something stone-like for something finished.** Travel light, stay on the paths, listen to the caretaker, and give the place time to reveal its changes. By the end of your visit, there may genuinely be a little more ground than there was when you arrived.
