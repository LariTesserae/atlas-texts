Prepare for this town as you would for a cold, dark port built inside a living organism—except that the streets may be smaller than your fingerprints, a drop of water can close a neighborhood, and the organism is preparing to become something else.

The first requirement is not equipment. It is a way to arrive without destroying the place.

## Arrive at the right size

An ordinary human cannot enter this town at ordinary human scale. Even lifting the bud to look beneath it could tear its attachment, alter its temperature, or push water through the harbor.

You will need a means of becoming small enough to travel its streets, or a suitably small visiting body. Do not assume that simple geometric shrinking makes a human viable. Whatever method you use must also preserve breathing, circulation, temperature regulation, and the ability to move at this scale.

Ideally, arrive at the size of one of the town’s ordinary carriers: large enough to handle a guide cord, small enough to use a doorway. Being the largest creature that can squeeze through the entrance is not an advantage. You would be difficult to shelter, difficult to rescue, and potentially indistinguishable from a predator.

Arrange a landing outside the harbor rather than appearing in the middle of town. The root comb is an inspection point as well as a physical barrier. Let someone admit you.

Your return mechanism should work without requiring you to regain full human size inside the bud.

## Book a guide, not merely a room

You cannot prepare a reliable map in advance. The town changes shape. Passages narrow, walls thicken, floors separate, and yesterday’s sound building may now wick water from the shore.

Find a local host who can provide:

- An escorted route from the harbor.
- A dry place to shelter through a cold interval.
- Instruction in the guide-cord knots and public notices.
- Current information about closed crossings and predator activity.
- A departure route that does not depend solely on the harbor.

A good guide should know when **not** to travel. Here, being stranded a few streets from your lodging can matter more than being lost.

Learn the signals for *stop*, *water coming*, *hunter*, *cold shelter*, and *do not touch*. Learn how residents identify themselves by touch. If you cannot read the pith notices, arrange for someone to read them for you each active period. A notice about a warmer room may be more urgent than it sounds.

Do not expect human-style conversation to work. At this scale, speech may transmit poorly or arrive as an alarming vibration. Your translation equipment should support contact signals, small gestures, or whatever the inhabitants actually use.

## Pack for darkness, wetness, and contact

Most of your information will come through your hands and feet.

Wear flexible protection that preserves tactile sensitivity. Thick gloves might keep sap off your skin but leave you unable to distinguish a junction knot from a warning. Footwear should grip wet living tissue without cutting it. Spikes, aggressive tread, and climbing hooks could wound the streets.

Useful equipment would include:

- **A close-fitting, water-repellent outer layer**, with no loose hems or trailing straps.
- **Insulation appropriate to your reduced body**, rather than ordinary miniature winter clothing.
- **A sealed breathing reserve or independent life-support system**, unless the interior atmosphere has been checked.
- **A short, non-wicking safety line**, approved by your guide.
- **A tactile route record** that can be updated.
- **Sealed food, drinking water, and waste containers.**
- **A return device accessible with either hand**, including while wet or immobilized.
- **A tiny, shielded inspection light**, used only with permission.

Avoid absorbent packs, dangling fabric, and fibrous equipment that can trail from a wet street into a house. In this town, your luggage could become a water conduit.

Keep your load narrow. You may need to turn on a platform, retreat through a doorway, or cross a removable twig bridge. Anything carried above your head also reduces your clearance beneath droplets and the ceiling.

### Do not solve the darkness with a floodlight

Bright light would not simply make the town easier for you to navigate. It could disrupt behavior, expose residents to hunters, or bring heat into tissue accustomed to cold.

A lamp strong enough to warm a wall might wake animals in the wall.

Prefer tactile navigation and passive sensing. If you need light, make it dim, tightly directed, and brief. Ask before illuminating anyone’s home. The rare blue line under the harbor comb is an event here; your arrival should not turn the whole town into an unnatural daylight.

## Treat water as a moving structure

The most serious everyday hazard is probably not the concealed animal in the ceiling. It is getting caught at the edge of a drop.

At your visiting scale, water will not behave like a puddle you can casually step through. Surface tension may pin you against the floor, bind your limbs together, or make it difficult to pull your face free. A falling drop can cover an entire crossing.

Before going anywhere, practice a local water rescue in controlled conditions. Know how your clothing behaves when wetted and how a companion can pull you free without becoming trapped too.

In particular:

- Do not stand beneath an enlarging ceiling bead.
- Do not touch a water lens to “see how thick it is.”
- Do not step from a raft onto an unfamiliar wet margin.
- Do not swim in the central pool.
- Do not run a cord between the shore and a dry building.
- Never replace a lifted foundation bridge because the gap seems inconvenient.

The dry breaks beneath houses are safety equipment. Keep them dry. If residents lift the crossing twigs, accept that visiting has stopped until conditions change.

Use the designated drinking supply only after checking whether it is safe for a human visitor. Water maintained by a worm colony may be perfectly suitable for its usual users and unsuitable for you. Equally, your disinfectants could kill organisms essential to the cistern. Bring your own water rather than “improving” theirs.

## Prepare for cold without warming the town

Activity is governed by temperature, not sunlight. Do not plan around hours called morning and evening. Plan around the next warming and the next retreat.

Have enough reserve to remain in shelter longer than expected. Your guide may become nearly immobile during a cold interval, and a public rescue crew may not be able to respond promptly.

Keep your heat inside your equipment. An exposed heater could change condensation, soften a structure, stimulate growth, or attract grazers and hunters. A warm human visitor may itself be an unusual environmental feature.

Before leaving shelter, ask three questions:

1. Is there time to complete the route before the cold returns?
2. Where is the nearest shelter if it returns early?
3. Is that shelter suitable for my body and equipment?

Do not mistake a motionless resident for someone injured or dead. Do not pick them up to warm them without asking.

## Learn the boundaries that are not walls

The thorn posts beneath the ceiling mark the reach of a predator. They are not decoration, and they are not an invitation to examine what lies beyond.

Stay outside the marked zone even if the animal has been still for a long time. Do not lean over the boundary, raise a light toward the fold, or place a camera where your body would not safely go. A tethered instrument could drag its owner—or damage the neighborhood—when taken.

If scratching sounds overhead and residents withdraw, withdraw with them. Do not remain outside to obtain a better view.

The pool presents a different problem: a hunter can be present without making the water look occupied. A closed crossing stays closed until locals reopen it. Your own sensor reading is not sufficient reason to disregard their cord or notice.

And because residents themselves are animals, avoid assuming that appearance tells you who is dangerous. A many-legged creature may be your guide. A smaller, almost invisible creature may be a hunter. Let introductions and local behavior establish the distinction.

## Bring nothing that can establish itself

You are entering a crowded ecosystem whose inhabitants are accustomed to carrying other inhabitants.

That does not make your microbes, mites, spores, or food residues welcome.

Clean and contain your equipment before arrival. Seal provisions. Keep your waste. Avoid introducing loose soil, untreated fibers, seeds, or living material from another world. Arrange an equally careful check before departing: your cuffs and pack seams may have acquired passengers too small for you to notice.

Do not use insect repellent, pesticide, antimicrobial spray, or scented toiletries in town. These could poison citizens directly.

Likewise, resist scratching if something explores your suit. Sudden brushing or slapping could kill an inhabitant you have not recognized. Ask your guide for help removing unwanted passengers.

## Be careful with the architecture

Nothing here is merely raw material.

A convenient ridge may carry sap. A doorway requires maintenance. A rope may be holding a section of shoreline together. A loose piece of tissue may be someone’s raft, and a raft pressed against a wall may become attached before its owner returns.

Do not cut souvenirs from the streets. Do not pull thorns out of walls. Do not lean equipment against living tissue and leave it there through a cold interval.

If you want to contribute, offer materials or work through a builder. Locally useful gifts might include small amounts of non-wicking cord, stable waterproof containers, or cutting tools—but only if their scale, weight, and chemistry suit the town. A miraculous adhesive is not necessarily a good gift in a place already struggling with unwanted adhesion.

Ask where to stand. The apparently empty floor is often deliberately empty: it carries drainage, allows growth, or gives residents enough warning to hear a predator approach.

## Make your departure plan before you enter

This is not a permanent enclosed settlement. It is a settlement in a bud approaching its opening.

Ask the surveyors about recent changes, not just the age of the town. Find out which wards have been abandoned, where floors are thinning, and whether the harbor stores are intended for an imminent move.

Do not build on or closely approach the rising central column. It belongs to the bud’s development, and the inhabitants give it room for a reason.

Have a second exit independent of the normal harbor route. It must work if:

- Water blocks the entrance.
- A hunter occupies the roots.
- The floor begins unfolding.
- Your route separates into different petals.
- Your host is immobilized by cold.

A neighboring bud is not automatically a safe refuge. Reaching it involves exposed travel above black water, and it may already contain residents who have not agreed to receive you.

When daylight first enters, do not stop to admire it until you know that your host has a safe way out. The same opening that gives you a view can drain the pool, break familiar routes, and expose cold-adapted citizens to moving air.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect to be slow and dependent.

You will put a hand on a cord and wait for someone to explain a knot. You will pause beside a threshold while a resident checks whether the bridge is dry. You may spend a long cold interval in a fold, listening to water gather overhead, with no useful way to hurry anything.

The town may initially seem empty because you cannot see its traffic. Then you will begin to feel scraping through the floor, a faint pull on a shared cord, the movement of a neighbor behind a thickening wall. What looked like silence will acquire inhabitants.

The best preparation is to give up the expectation that visiting means freely exploring. Here, a successful visitor is someone who can be guided, who leaves the drainage intact, who does not add dangerous heat or foreign life, and who gets out before their presence becomes another problem the town must solve.

**Go small, carry dry, learn the cords, and leave while the place is still a bud.**
