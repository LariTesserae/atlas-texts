Prepare for this place as you would prepare to walk through a flooded industrial plant in winter—except that the plant is alive, the floor is partly made of animals, and some of its oldest rooms cannot be reached by ordinary distance.

Your main task is not to survive an attack. It is to avoid becoming part of a process that has no reason to notice you.

## Choose a modest visit

For a first visit, aim to observe one complete drainage-and-flood cycle from a verified patch of exposed rock near your arrival point. Do not plan a crossing of the plain. Do not make the white flats your destination.

The place offers many things that look like routes: banks, shallow channels, rows of stepping stones, higher ground. Almost none are necessarily routes for a human. A bank can unfold into a colony. A channel can stop being liquid. A short walk can return you to the same stone from the opposite side.

Choose your arrival point with retreat in mind. Ideally, your means of departure should remain available on a stable platform that does not depend on local water levels, electrical conditions, or geographic coordinates alone. If your travel equipment has never operated in a place with unreliable distance, this is not where to discover its limitations.

Bring another person. Both of you should be able to initiate departure. Leave someone elsewhere with a firm return deadline.

## Dress for immersion, not merely rain

The cold here is wet, continuous, and difficult to escape. There is no tree cover, no dry vegetation, and apparently no reassuring distinction between solid ground and water.

Wear a sealed cold-water suit with enough insulation to survive an accidental fall into a shallow channel. Protect your hands, face, and neck. Bring redundant heating, but retain enough passive insulation that a failed battery does not immediately become an emergency.

Your outer clothing should be smooth and close-fitting. Avoid loose straps, dangling cords, exposed buckles, and anything that can enter a rotating mouthpart or a joint in moving stone. Keep essential equipment attached in short, releasable restraints rather than trailing tethers.

Boots should have:

- Broad, stable soles.
- Good grip on wet mineral surfaces.
- Insulation against cold and, as far as practicable, electrical contact.
- No metal spikes unless you already know they are safe here.

Electrical insulation is a precaution, not a promise. This landscape has current running through wet rock, water, and living bodies. Earth-rated equipment may not cover everything it does.

Do not rely on the orange seams for warmth. Their light is specifically not evidence of warm air. Likewise, a warm patch behind a moving silence is not a sensible place to stop and remove your gloves.

## Bring your own small, dry world

Carry all your drinking water and food. Nothing in the description suggests a local substance an ordinary human could safely consume. Clear water is not necessarily potable; the great animals’ outflow may be “clean” only in the sense that suspended sediment has been removed.

Keep supplies in sealed containers that can be opened with numb hands. Eat before entering and during planned stops, not while balancing above a channel.

Useful equipment includes:

- Two independent lights, preferably with adjustable brightness.
- A thermal imager to distinguish some animals from rock.
- A probe for testing footing from a distance.
- A water-level sensor fixed at your observation point.
- Instruments that record electrical fields, vibration, and temperature.
- An independent clock and a camera.
- A compact insulated emergency shelter.

None of those devices should be treated as authoritative. A thermal image might show a colony clearly while missing a cold animal beneath it. A rangefinder might give a perfectly precise answer to a distance that is no longer dependable.

Use instruments to notice disagreements.

## Learn the floor before trusting it

The first skill you need is recognizing that the ground is layered.

What looks like slate rubble may be animals. What looks like one animal may have nineteen more beneath it. What looks like exposed rock may be a mineral plate about to move.

Spend time watching before stepping. Look for coordinated lifting, hidden joints, little clearances opening between bodies, and dark patches left behind as creatures shift. Do not assume a motionless surface is inert.

Never deliberately step on a colony. Apart from injuring the animals, you would be placing your weight on a structure whose members are free to leave. A walking pole pushed into a colony is not a reliable footing test, either.

Favor continuous exposed rock, but watch that too. Stay away from plate margins, sockets, perforated beds, and places where water is being drawn downward. A shallow inlet can be the mouth of a large hydraulic system.

When a bank begins to rearrange itself, do not hurry onto the next apparent bank. Stop on verified footing and work back toward your last safe position.

## Treat water as active machinery

Keep your hands out of the pools. Do not wade merely because the bottom is visible.

The swimmers demonstrate that local water can become rigid during predation. You do not know whether that effect would leave a human unharmed, immobilized, compressed, or trapped until another event occurs. Avoid channels showing moving ridges beneath the silt, and give any feeding struggle a wide margin.

The smaller creatures grazing electrical discharge are another warning. Dense gray “felt” may indicate an energetic boundary, not just a convenient soft surface.

Do not approach the large filter-creatures closely. They may not be interested in you, but their intakes, outflows, planted legs, and turning bodies could be dangerous without any aggression at all. Stay out of their feeding paths and away from the sediment bands immediately behind them.

If water beads move uphill, record it. Do not attempt to settle the phenomenon by touching it or leaning closer. The fact that observation sometimes changes its behavior means you should be particularly cautious about assuming that looking is passive.

## Watch the cycle, not the clock

The plain’s regularity is your best protection, but it is ecological regularity rather than a posted timetable.

During drainage, more rock appears, yet colonies move toward the remaining wet ground and predators concentrate in narrowing channels. During flooding, apparent routes disappear beneath water and bodies. Neither phase is simply “the safe one.”

At your observation point, learn several signs together:

- Whether water is rising or falling.
- Whether colonies are spreading or compressing.
- Whether the buried mechanisms are changing rhythm.
- Whether the large animals are feeding, turning, or halting.
- Whether nearby pools have begun to lean in the same direction.

The country-wide lift is an especially important warning. A fraction of an inch sounds trivial until every pool moves together. When the large animals stop with vents open and the roar becomes ticking, stop walking. Keep your balance, remain on known footing, and wait for the surge to pass.

Do not improvise a crossing during that pause.

## Prepare to lose sound

Hearing protection will help with the continuous roar, but your team also needs communication that does not depend on hearing.

Agree on simple hand signals before entering: stop, retreat, footing unsafe, equipment failure, leave now. Carry visible lights for signaling in dimness. Stay close enough to see one another without a long tether that could snag.

A silent patch is not peaceful weather. Animals avoid its leading edge, and the large creatures suspend their work when one passes over them. Those are reasons to avoid it yourself.

If silence overtakes you, stop rather than scattering. Confirm your companion’s position visually. Do not remove your hood to test your ears, and do not assume shouting will help. Move toward the last observed boundary only if the footing remains known and safe.

The wake’s residual warmth is interesting, but not an invitation.

## Leave the oldest country alone

On an ordinary visit, the whitish flats should remain a view.

Repeatedly finding the split black stone is not a navigation error to overcome by persistence. It is evidence that the journey is failing in a way your walking cannot repair. Approaching from another direction has already been shown not to solve it.

Set a rule before arrival: the first unmistakable repetition ends the approach.

Do not try drifting across because water crosses more readily. Water’s ability to reach the flats says nothing about whether a human carried by it would arrive intact, return, or remain reachable by companions.

Similarly, do not enter a pool because it reflects a downward cliff. Do not handle the seamless creatures delivered from the flats. Something that sinks through rock is not a specimen you are equipped to contain.

Avoid collecting anything on your first visit. A harmless-looking shell, film, or mineral chip may have relationships to current, pressure, or local space that are invisible once you pick it up.

## Keep the visit short enough to remain attentive

This will be an exhausting place. Cold slows your hands; constant noise consumes attention; dim light makes every piece of rubble require interpretation. There is almost nowhere to rest without first deciding whether the resting place is alive.

Plan to leave while everyone is still warm, coordinated, and capable of making cautious decisions. Do not wait for an emergency to justify departure.

The right temperament is patient rather than brave. Watch how the animals brace before a knock. Notice which surfaces they leave alone. Let a cycle finish before deciding what its middle means.

You may feel, standing there, that the plain is ancient enough to be tired. Resist that impression. It is working continuously.

Visit as a temporary observer with a secure way home—not as one more body willing to trust the water.
