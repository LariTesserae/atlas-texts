Prepare for this as a visit to an operating installation whose workings happen to be alive—not as a cave expedition, and certainly not as a place to explore by touching things.

The chamber’s apparent stability is misleading. Everything is moving, sounding, redistributing, or holding something in position. You do not know which of those activities keeps the place habitable. Your first responsibility is to avoid becoming a new load in that system.

## Before entering

**Establish a way out that does not depend on the chamber remaining unchanged.**

Have someone remain outside, with an agreed return time and a clear plan for a missed check-in. If your multiverse travel equipment allows extraction without walking back through the room, verify that capability before descending. Do not assume a device will work underground merely because it worked in the desert.

Make your first visit short: long enough to assess the entrance and perhaps one accessible aisle, not long enough to reach every interesting object. The abandoned work shelters suggest that previous occupants came for shifts, but they tell you nothing about what protection those occupants needed—or whether they were human.

Arrive hydrated, rested, and with your supplies already organized. This is a bad place to discover that your spare light is at the bottom of a bag.

## Dress for edges, not just dust

Ordinary hiking clothes are inadequate. A mineral edge that can divide hanging cloth under its own weight deserves more respect than a sharp rock.

Wear:

- A close-fitting, abrasion- and cut-resistant outer layer, with no loose cuffs, straps, or dangling accessories.
- A helmet with eye protection; preferably a full-face shield over sealed goggles.
- Protective gloves that still allow you to operate equipment reliably.
- Sturdy boots with puncture-resistant soles and good lateral support.
- A compact pack whose contents cannot shift and pull you off balance.

No wearable material should be treated as proof against these blades. Protection is there to make an accidental brush less disastrous, not to permit squeezing through a gap.

Keep your silhouette small. A bulky backpack may turn a passage that fits your body into a trap. Avoid carrying anything across your shoulders or strapped upright behind your head.

**Do not brace yourself against the walls.** That reflex—catching your balance with an outstretched hand—is likely to be one of the greatest immediate dangers.

## Bring your own breathable environment

The air’s warmth may feel welcoming before its dryness and dust become apparent. Comfort is not evidence of safety.

At the entrance, check oxygen, temperature, and whatever atmospheric contaminants your equipment can detect. Familiar instruments may not identify unfamiliar mineral compounds, so a clean reading has limits.

A well-fitted particulate respirator would help against suspended dust, but it would not supply missing oxygen or necessarily protect against gases. For a first entry into an uncharacterized atmosphere, an independent breathing supply is the safer choice. Understand its duration and leave a generous exit reserve.

Carry water in a closed container. Plan to drink somewhere you can do so without exposing your mouthpiece or bottle to dust. Do not rely on finding moisture inside, and do not sample the floor’s greasy-feeling material.

Bring a compact trauma kit, including bleeding-control supplies, somewhere reachable without removing your pack. Any cut contaminated by this dust should end the visit and receive proper cleaning and medical assessment.

## Make light and navigation independent

The orange lamps are landmarks, not dependable illumination. Their severed cables are a particularly good reason not to draw conclusions about how they work.

Bring two independent lights:

- A broad, diffuse light for seeing your immediate surroundings.
- A directional light for inspecting the next section of passage before entering it.

Bright mineral edges can conceal the dark spaces behind them. Look at the floor, ceiling, and both sides of each opening; a passage should not be judged from its glowing outline.

Your footprints disappear, so they cannot guide you home. Record the route using photographs and a simple sequence of turns and distinctive features. Check how the return route looks while you can still see the entrance.

Do not casually lay a guide cord through the chamber. It could snag, be cut, cross an animal route, or touch an electrically active root. If a line is necessary, its placement needs deliberate assessment rather than the assumption that it is harmless.

Likewise, do not scatter markers onto the floor. You have no evidence that dropped objects stay where they land.

## Move without asking the floor to hold you differently

The granular bed responds unusually to pressure. It might support a walking person perfectly well while behaving badly under a knee, a narrow equipment foot, or a prolonged stationary load.

Begin with cautious steps near the entrance. Watch how the surface responds beneath both feet. Do not jump, stamp, drag your boots, or test the ground with repeated blows.

Avoid kneeling and sitting directly on it. A knee-shaped depression is unpleasant; a depression fitted around a limb or piece of equipment could be much worse. Do not assume a familiar folding chair will be safe just because it spreads your weight.

Move slowly enough to stop, but do not freeze in a constricted aisle if a broad plated animal approaches. Give it room. Its sensory fringe appears to read the floor, and your boots may be a large, noisy obstruction to it.

Never step over one unless the alternative is an immediate threat to your safety. Never lift one to clear a route.

## Protect your hearing without making yourself oblivious

The chamber is loud enough to conceal speech and potentially loud enough to damage hearing. Bring hearing protection, ideally something that preserves useful environmental awareness.

Arrange hand signals before entering. Use visual check-ins with your companion; do not depend entirely on radios, whose performance underground is uncertain.

Avoid speakers, alarms, ultrasonic rangefinders, or other devices that introduce unnecessary vibration. The small animals coordinate through the mineral, and the larger ones appear to sense through the floor. Sound here is not just background noise. It is part of how the inhabitants interact.

A sudden change in the room’s sound should be a reason to withdraw, not an invitation to investigate. Particularly concerning would be a widespread pause, a new pounding pattern, or colonies shifting in response to your movement.

## Leave the infrastructure alone

Do not touch the engine casing to experience the chamber’s combined vibration. It is an intriguing observation, but deliberately coupling your body to an unknown mechanism gains little useful safety information.

Stay clear of the flywheel and belt. The railing is a boundary, not a handhold. Do not turn the crank, open the inspection hatch, adjust the gauge, or introduce lubricant.

The needle near the red mark is neither proof of imminent failure nor reassurance that someone has set it correctly. You do not know what it measures.

Treat all cables, including severed ones, as potentially energized. The plants’ relationship with electrical leakage makes the roots another reason to keep your hands and equipment away from the fittings. Do not clip a light to a collar or use a cable as an anchor.

Leave the lunch tin closed. Its untouched latch may mean nothing more than that the animals cannot grip it, but opening someone else’s abandoned container in an unfamiliar ecosystem is an unnecessary experiment. The glove and benches are evidence to observe, not equipment to borrow.

## Do not approach the final gap

The most important detail is not that there is darkness beyond it. It is that living creatures are exerting themselves to keep it open.

That does not establish whether opening it farther would be dangerous, whether closing it would be dangerous, or whether it is a passage at all. It establishes that **its present width is being actively maintained**.

Do not insert a finger, probe, camera, lamp, or listening device. Do not remove the animals for a better view. Do not pry the blades apart or try to help the creatures. You cannot yet distinguish assistance from disruption.

There is no need for a first-time visitor to reach that wall.

## Know when to leave

Leave promptly if:

- Breathing becomes uncomfortable or your air monitoring changes.
- You develop headache, dizziness, unusual fatigue, or confusion.
- Dust increases noticeably.
- Your route becomes harder to identify.
- The floor stops releasing a footprint as expected.
- Animals begin crowding around your boots or equipment.
- The sound changes markedly.
- Any blade, fitting, or cable shifts.
- You suffer even a minor injury that compromises your movement.

Do not wait for several warning signs to agree.

On returning to the surface, inspect your boots, clothing, and gear for attached washer-creatures, mineral fragments, and filament growth. Contain contaminated items until they can be cleaned safely. Nothing should leave the chamber with you merely because it is small enough to overlook.

The hardest preparation may be resisting the desire to make something happen. This room offers an engine that invites operating, a tin that invites opening, and a gap that invites looking through. Yet its defining quality is activity without visible accumulation. You cannot safely infer that it will absorb your interference in the same way.

Go prepared to observe very little, touch nothing, and leave while the chamber still sounds as it did when you arrived.
