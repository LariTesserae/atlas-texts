Prepare for this as a visit to an active geological machine, not as a dive to a picturesque ruin.

The basin offers nearly every cue that a human mistakes for safety: a visible edge, a floor, small rooms, plants, an animal moving calmly through the water. But the edge does not reliably separate places, the floor is being remade, the rooms contract, and the animal’s apparent ease may depend on capabilities you do not have.

**For an ordinary human, the sensible destination is the water outside the rim. Entering the hollow should be a separate decision, made only after observation—and quite possibly never made.**

## Establish whether you can survive the surrounding water

“Near the summit” tells you nothing useful about depth here. There is no surface overhead, so you cannot assume an ascent will lead to air, lower pressure, or rescue.

Before arriving bodily, use a remote instrument package to determine:

- Ambient pressure, temperature, and water chemistry.
- Whether ordinary breathing gases, seals, electronics, and structural materials behave normally.
- Whether the surrounding water truly remains quiet through several major changes in the basin.
- Whether your means of returning works from this location, rather than merely having worked on departure.
- Whether instruments and elapsed-time records agree across the rim.

Do not breathe the local water, drink it, or expose your eyes to it. Warm water can still be chemically hostile, and suspended mineral dust makes even otherwise benign water unsuitable for an unprotected human.

If pressure or chemistry cannot be established, you need a vehicle that maintains a known internal environment. A conventional diving suit is not a substitute for knowing what it must withstand.

If your travel equipment can only deliver you into open water with a tank and fins, choose another destination.

## Bring a small refuge, not an ambitious expedition

The best visiting platform would be a compact, pressure-rated submersible or equivalent sealed travel capsule, with remote manipulators and probes. It should have independent propulsion, redundant life support, thermal protection, and a tested means of leaving without using the basin’s floor or rim.

The important word is **independent**. Your safety should not depend on a rock remaining a rock, a doorway remaining in the same place, or a quiet interval lasting long enough to finish a task.

Useful equipment includes:

- Protected cameras, low-light imaging, and adjustable external lights.
- Pressure, temperature, and acceleration sensors that record rapid changes, not just averages.
- External acoustic sensors, so you can study the booming without transmitting its full force into the cabin.
- Short-range mapping instruments, backed by visual observation.
- Replaceable protective surfaces against abrasive grit.
- A recoverable probe and expendable probes, with a strict limit on how much risk their recovery warrants.
- Navigation and recording systems that do not rely solely on landmarks inside the hollow.

Loose straps, trailing hoses, exposed joints, and decorative equipment are liabilities. Mineral flakes will find seams; moving stone will find anything projecting from your outline.

Do not plan to stand on the floor. “Underfoot” is a descriptive convenience here, not a promise that footing will exist.

## Treat the rim as a force boundary

An arm reaching inward is pulled at the elbow. That is enough evidence to reject casual contact.

The danger is not simply that the water is denser or that there is a strong current. Different parts of your body may be subjected to different forces while you straddle the boundary. A suit capable of supporting you in either region might still fail to protect you during the crossing.

Test with instruments before testing with a person. A probe should cross slowly, recording force and acceleration, then attempt to withdraw. Repeat that test during different phases of the booming and floor movement. One successful crossing proves very little in a place this changeable.

**Do not brace a hand on the rim, hook a foot beneath it, or tie your lifeline to it.** Fresh fracture faces and pieces larger than their apparent source make the pale stone a particularly poor anchor.

A tether from an independent vehicle might help retrieve a probe. It could also become a trap if a current fold catches it or a boundary changes around it. Any tether system needs controlled payout, measured tension, and a release procedure. “We have a rope” is not an extraction plan.

## Observe the rhythm without trusting it

Spend your first visit outside, watching.

The basin appears to have recurring events: amber bulbs brighten, deep blows arrive, chambers briefly quiet, then water surges into them. These may provide useful warnings. They do not yet provide a safe timetable.

Record several cycles and several large structural changes. In particular, watch for:

- Bulbs brightening beneath the ledge.
- Cracks widening or their light intensifying.
- Sudden vertical flow.
- Smooth sheets forming where currents meet.
- Upper stones shifting on the roofless enclosure.
- Ridges lifting from the floor.
- Bright cracks appearing across open water.

The bulbs may be an excellent local alarm. They may also react too late for you, or respond only to some events. Use them as one warning source, not as permission to approach.

The interval after a boom is especially deceptive. Quiet water in the chambers looks like relief precisely before the rooms inhale. A human could reach a doorway, feel the noise diminish, and decide that shelter has been found—then be pressed against the back wall.

Do not build your plan around making it into a room before the next blow.

## The chambers are not accommodation

None of the three enclosures should be entered by a human on an exploratory visit.

Their doorways persist, but that is not the same as remaining usable. Their joints spread, their interiors flood violently, and at least one chamber is shrinking. The roofless structure offers no dependable overhead escape if its stones rearrange.

The finned creature’s visit is not reassuring. It leaves with a changed body. Perhaps it was injured, perhaps it adapted, perhaps something else happened. You do not know which, and none of those possibilities establishes that the room is suitable for a mammal.

The white fragments at the threshold should remain unidentified until examined remotely. You do not need to decide whether they are teeth in order to keep your fingers away from them.

More broadly, resist the impulse to interpret something built “for use” as something built **for your use**.

## Protect yourself from the sound—and from what makes it

Ear protection alone will not address this environment. The concussion travels through water and bone; the slower blows are felt in the teeth. Even if you could make the sound comfortable, the pressure events producing it might still be physically dangerous.

A sealed cabin should be tested for transmitted vibration and pressure shock. Monitor cumulative exposure, not merely the strongest event.

Treat new tooth or jaw pain, dizziness, nausea, confusion, visual disturbance, or difficulty coordinating movement as reasons to leave. Do not dismiss them as ordinary seasickness or nerves.

The dry clicking that follows movement deserves particular caution. The description suggests brief gaps between a visitor and the surrounding water, but does not establish their cause. Do not assume it is harmless cavitation, an animal, or a friendly response. If it begins around your vehicle or equipment, record it and increase your distance. Do not provoke more clicks for the sake of a clearer sample.

## Expect your senses to disagree

This place would be deeply unsettling even before it became dangerous.

Looking outward gives the bodily certainty of a precipice while the water supports you. That conflict can cause an involuntary reach for the rim or a sudden attempt to swim away. Rehearse remaining still and checking instruments when your body announces that you are falling.

The current sheets create another problem: a wall can appear within reach and then retreat. Do not steer by apparent distance through them. Likewise, a second rim felt beneath the first, or a doorway opening onto the outer slope, should be treated as a boundary disturbance—not a convenient shortcut.

Keep tasks simple. Use checklists and explicit abort conditions. If accompanied, agree beforehand that either person can end the visit without debate. This is not a place to negotiate whether an alarming sensation is “probably nothing.”

## Leave living things alone

Do not handle the green straps or amber bulbs, and do not follow the pale creature.

Their survival demonstrates that they belong here, not that the basin is broadly habitable. The creature may divide currents around itself through anatomy, behavior, or something your instruments have not measured. Its calmness cannot be borrowed.

Keep lights modest and avoid directing exhaust or propulsion at the plants. Do not dislodge growth from cracks to collect it. Aside from harming scarce organisms, you could remove one of the few visible indicators of approaching change.

## Make the first visit deliberately modest

A successful first visit would be:

1. Arrive in verified quiet water well outside the rim.
2. Confirm that departure remains available.
3. Observe through several booms and at least one substantial rearrangement.
4. Approach only as far as the measurements justify.
5. Send a probe inward, if conditions permit.
6. Withdraw before fatigue, curiosity, or equipment wear begins making decisions for you.

Return immediately if the supposedly quiet exterior becomes forceful, your position reference becomes inconsistent, your vehicle develops unexplained clicking or vibration, or your exit becomes uncertain.

You do not need a mineral sample, a look inside a chamber, or a photograph from the floor for the visit to count.

The strongest temptation here will be the apparent nearness of everything. A hundred paces across is a human-sized place. You can see the rooms, the ledge, the glowing crack you want to inspect. Yet those short distances contain forces that throw stone, crush currents flat, and alter the basin’s own boundaries.

Prepare to admire it from farther away than feels satisfying. The little hollow near the summit is not waiting to be explored; it is continuously making and unmaking the conditions under which exploration would be possible.
