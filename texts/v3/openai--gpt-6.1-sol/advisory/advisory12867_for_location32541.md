Prepare for this as a visit to an **underwater conservatory with no usable surface**, not as a swim in a beautiful pool.

The garden looks gentle. Much of it probably is. But its gentleness belongs to the plants: warm water, steady nutrients, no biting mouths, no abrupt disturbance. An ordinary human brings needs the room was not necessarily built to accommodate—air, room to turn, a reliable way out, and an instinct to grab something when frightened.

Your preparations should keep those needs from becoming the garden’s problem.

## Before going up the tower

Arrange the visit with whoever maintains the garden. You want a guide who knows its current layout, not merely someone who has visited before. This is a room that changes without ever looking busy. A passage that was clear last season might now contain a membrane, a redirected root, or a new frame.

Ask specifically about:

- Water depth, pressure, temperature, and chemistry.
- The operating cycle of the entrance vestibule.
- Whether visitors use ordinary diving equipment or a local breathing system.
- Approved routes, handholds, and places to rest.
- Scheduled cartridge replacements, pressure changes, and maintenance.
- How an unconscious visitor would be recovered.

The last question matters. The exit is a door that equalizes, not a beach someone can drag you onto. There should be a real rescue procedure, equipment, and someone outside who knows you are inside.

Do not infer the pressure from the garden’s position. Being kilometers above the ground does not tell you the pressure inside a sealed, water-filled chamber. Nor does the modest size of the room mean it is a simple shallow dive. Its most important diving characteristic is that **you cannot ascend directly to breathable air**.

If you have no underwater training, seek a purpose-built escorted visitor arrangement. A guide beside you does not, by itself, make an overhead dive suitable for a novice. If the tower has no safe provision for ordinary visitors, admire the garden from the vestibule or postpone entry.

## Bring a way to breathe that does not depend on the garden

Those white bladders under the roof are an atmospheric reserve, not an invitation to put your face into the canopy.

You do not know their gas composition, whether they can support a human breath, or what damage opening one would do. The pale bubbles held in the little cups are likewise part of the garden’s functioning. Leave them alone.

Use an approved breathing system, with appropriate redundancy and sufficient reserve for delays at the inner door. Have it checked against the actual conditions. A familiar piece of equipment may not behave as expected in unfamiliar pressure or water chemistry.

A closed-circuit system might reduce bubbles and disturbance, but it is not something to learn for this visit. Use equipment you are trained to operate, and let the maintainers decide what the garden can tolerate. Exhaled bubbles could become caught in foliage or interfere with the ceiling structures.

Keep the rest of your equipment compact:

- A well-fitted mask and a secured backup.
- Exposure protection chosen for the measured water temperature and planned duration.
- A small, restrained light.
- A means of communicating with your guide.
- Any emergency equipment required by the local diving procedure.

Avoid dangling straps, decorative attachments, loose camera tethers, and bulky accessories. Here, a loose loop does not merely catch on a rock. It can catch on a transparent ribbon you have not noticed yet.

## Arrive clean

The lack of animals is one of the garden’s defining conditions. Treat it as something to preserve.

Have your equipment cleaned according to the conservatory’s protocol, especially if it has recently been in a sea, lake, or another living installation. Do not bring untreated plants, sediment, food, or water from elsewhere. Avoid unapproved lotions and chemicals that can wash off your skin.

Do not try to sterilize your kit with an improvised chemical rinse just before entering. Residue can be its own contamination. Follow the local procedure.

You should arrive with everything secured before stepping into the vestibule. Waist-deep warm water is a bad place to discover that you still need to search a bag for your mask.

## Learn how to move without using the room

Your most valuable skill will be controlled buoyancy.

The terraces are not necessarily walking surfaces. Some are young cast terrain; others conceal roots, nutrient ports, or replaceable components. The bronze rails may support plants rather than people. Even the thick growth on the doorframe stops at the seal for a reason: the doorway is working machinery.

Ask which surfaces can bear a hand, a foot, or a person’s weight. Do not decide by appearance.

Move slowly enough that you rarely need to correct yourself abruptly. Use the propulsion method your guide recommends for the available clearance. Broad kicks can push water through several layers of delicate growth even when your fins touch nothing.

**Never push through a leafy screen simply because you can see daylight beyond it.** Some screens contain joined chambers with their own water pockets. What looks like foliage in your path may be a living partition.

At narrow points, wait for the guide to show you the opening. If there is no approved opening, there is no route.

The thicker water near the service niche deserves particular care. It may provide a comfortable place to remain upright, but a change in resistance could also make movement unexpectedly tiring. Use the steps only with permission. Leave the wheel, tools, gauge, and glossy terrain-forming patch untouched.

## Plan the exit before admiring the view

From the threshold, you can see almost the whole garden. That will make it seem easier to navigate than it is.

Once you descend beside a terrace, broad leaves may hide the door. Light comes from several directions, shadows hang in the water, and near evening the cloud cover removes the outside horizon altogether. A room that initially looked legible can become a succession of very similar green spaces.

Agree on the route, turnaround point, communication signals, and what to do if separated. Know how to recognize the vestibule from inside. Do not improvise a shortcut beneath a terrace or through the ceiling growth.

Schedule your first visit for ordinary daylight and stable operating conditions. Save the enveloping evening clouds for a later visit, when the loss of visual reference is something you can appreciate rather than something you must solve.

## Prepare for how quiet it is

The warm water may make entry feel almost effortless. Do not let that sensation persuade you that the place is harmless.

You will hear your breathing more than the building. You may find yourself listening for animals that never appear. Nothing will scatter when you approach. That absence can produce an odd feeling of permission, as though nothing minds your presence.

It is better understood as vulnerability. The lace-like margins exist because nothing bites them. Your sleeve may be the roughest event they have encountered.

Give yourself time to stop somewhere approved and simply look. Notice the manufacturing seams under mineral growth, the clean white terrace, the old frames nearly absorbed into stems. The garden is not an imitation of wilderness. It is infrastructure becoming habitat, with every addition negotiated through crowded water.

A camera is optional. A patient attention span is more useful.

## Leave without a souvenir

Take no leaf, gas cup, mineral flake, or apparently loose fragment. Even dead material belongs to the garden’s circulation and filtration. Report anything you damaged or snagged, however slight. A nearly invisible tear may matter more here than a conspicuous scuff elsewhere.

Your first visit should be short enough that you leave with comfortable reserves of air, warmth, and concentration—not just enough to get through the door.

The right ambition is not to reach every corner. It is to spend a little time inside a place that has almost no unoccupied space, and to leave it with no new space forced open by you.
