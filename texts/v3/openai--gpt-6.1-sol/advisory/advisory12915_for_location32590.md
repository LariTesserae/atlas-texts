Prepare for this hollow as you would for a high-altitude rock basin with no dependable shelter, no fuel, and no margin for arriving tired. Its stillness is misleading. The stone moves, the footing changes, and the abundant light does not mean the place is warm.

The safest visit would be short, in a small group, with a known route back below the notch well before dusk.

### Before you go

**Establish that its atmosphere is safe for humans.** The description suggests thin, cold air, but it does not establish breathable composition or pressure. A multiverse traveler should check both before leaving a protected arrival point. If the air is compatible but the altitude is high, acclimatize somewhere lower first. A few minutes’ distance across the hollow could become a substantial effort when climbing, balancing, and breathing all demand attention.

Do not plan your visit around reaching the summit. From within the hollow you cannot see it, and the enclosing walls may conceal difficult terrain. Your destination is the hollow itself; the summit is a separate expedition.

Check the weather beyond the basin, not merely inside it. The walls can make conditions feel calm while wind, cloud, or deteriorating visibility closes the exit. Clouds rising through the notch deserve particular attention: that narrow opening is both your view of the world and likely your way out.

If you control your means of return, place it on stable ground away from the walls, the drop, and loose slabs. Verify that it remains usable when cold and wet.

### Dress for cold stone and fierce light

Wear layers that let you manage exertion without becoming damp:

- A moisture-wicking base layer.
- An insulating layer substantial enough for a prolonged stop.
- A windproof, weather-resistant outer layer.
- Warm gloves, with a pair that allows a secure grip on rock.
- Supportive boots with good traction, rather than light shoes.

Bring a warm hat, sun protection, and sunglasses suited to intense glare. Pale rock, water, and ice will return light toward your face from several directions. Your eyes may be uncomfortable even when you are standing in shade.

A climbing helmet is sensible here. Small flakes fall from above, and a stumble between close slabs could bring your head against an edge. It is not protection against a large slab collapse; staying out of its path matters more.

Carry a closed-cell sitting pad. The hollow offers many surfaces that look like seats and almost none that will spare you the cold. A hand warmed by sunlight can still lose heat painfully through its fingertips; the same will happen to someone resting directly on stone.

### Bring everything you expect to consume

Carry your own drinking water, plus a reserve for delay. The clear basin is not proof of safe water. Its apparent lack of life does not rule out microorganisms, and dissolved minerals may be invisible. Ordinary filtration and boiling do not reliably remove dissolved salts or metals.

If local water is part of your emergency plan, bring a way to assess and treat it appropriate to this world. Otherwise, regard it as something to observe rather than something to drink.

Bring food that can be eaten without cooking. There is no fuel to collect, and the old blackened patch is evidence that someone carried fuel up—not evidence that a fire is easy or safe here. For a longer expedition, carry a stove suitable for the pressure and temperature, and use it in open air, never under the little stone roof.

An insulated bottle and accessible snacks will make it easier to keep drinking and eating when cold or breathless.

### Move as though every route must be rediscovered

The hollow is small, but it is not simple. Close slabs interrupt sightlines and sound; a companion can become invisible and inaudible only a few passages away.

Stay within sight of one another where possible. Carry radios, but test them in the actual terrain rather than assuming they work. Agree in advance on what to do if separated. Do not split up casually to explore different passages.

Photograph the notch and the route from it as you enter. Note distinctive large features, not just small heaps of stone that could shift. The three little stacks may help identify the historic approach, but they are clues, not a guarantee that the route remains safe.

Place weight gradually. Avoid jumping between slabs, pulling on leaning stones, or squeezing through gaps where a small movement could trap you. A stone that held you in the morning may rock later. Thin flakes are especially poor handholds.

At the notch, treat the chipped footholds as old workmanship whose present strength is unknown. If the approach involves exposed climbing, bring appropriate equipment and the skill to use it; do not let the existence of footholds persuade you that the climb is ordinary walking.

### Do not count on the old shelter

The enclosure is evidence of human endurance, not comfortable accommodation. Its roof is low, heavy, and unmortared. Freeze–thaw cycles and small movements may have changed how the slabs bear on one another.

Admire it from outside before considering entry. Do not shift wedges, remove packing chips, or lean against the walls. Leave the vessel, handle, and stone markers where they are.

For an unplanned delay, carry your own emergency insulation and weather protection. If an overnight stay is unavoidable, choose a site outside likely rockfall paths and away from precarious slabs or drainage cracks. That may mean leaving the hollow rather than sleeping in it.

### Give yourself permission to leave early

Begin retreat before the beautiful last light through the notch. That light arrives close to the period when cooling, freezing water, and changing stone stresses become additional hazards.

Leave sooner if clouds obscure the exit, footing becomes icy, stones begin shedding nearby, or anyone develops headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual exhaustion, or difficulty thinking. Breathlessness at rest or poor coordination calls for urgent descent and assistance.

Finally, make room in the visit for stillness—but choose the place carefully. Sit on your pad on stable ground, clear of overhead flakes, and listen. The basin ice may sound like a tiny bell. A drip may be the loudest event for minutes.

You do not need to touch the curling mineral shelves or crawl under the roof to experience this place. Its intimacy comes from paying attention. Bring enough protection that you can notice the small changes without depending on the hollow to keep you alive.
