Prepare for this town as you would for a cold, wet mountain trek—but remember that you are visiting people’s homes, not camping in an abandoned forest. The greenery may hide it from above, yet almost every usable surface belongs to someone, supports something, or carries water somewhere important.

Your main problems will be **staying dry enough to remain warm, moving without falling, and learning which routes are safe today**. None of these can be solved entirely by equipment. Plan to rely on local knowledge.

## Before you arrive

Arrange a guide or host if you can, and approach in daylight. The town is small, but its paths are layered: a passage might run under a house, split around living bamboo, or continue through a gallery that appears private to an outsider. During rain, the usual route may cease to be a route at all.

Ask about conditions upstream, not merely whether it is raining in town. Three streams converge here, and a clear patch of sky overhead does not tell you what water is coming down the mountain.

Leave yourself time to be delayed. This is a poor place for an itinerary that requires crossing one particular bridge at one particular hour.

For a multiverse traveler, the description gives no reason to expect unusual physics or supernatural hazards. Do not let that make you casual about the ordinary ones. Wet bamboo over a ravine is quite enough.

## Pack small, and waterproof the contents

A broad pack will catch on shutters, laundry, projecting stems, and other people. A heavy one will make the stairs more dangerous. Carry a narrow load, keep both hands available, and secure everything that dangles.

Useful essentials include:

- **Footwear with reliable wet-surface grip.** Expect slick stone, muddy paths, and polished bamboo. No sole makes all of these safe; short, deliberate steps still matter.
- **A compact rain jacket with ventilation.** A long, loose poncho can snag or obscure your feet on stairs. If you use one, keep it controlled.
- **Quick-drying clothing and a warm layer.** The chill is likely to come from dampness as much as air temperature.
- **One protected set of sleeping clothes.** Seal it in a waterproof bag and do not spend it on daytime comfort.
- **Spare socks, packed separately.**
- **Waterproof bags inside your pack.** A pack cover alone will not protect against water entering from underneath or running down your back.
- **A headlamp**, with protected spare power. It leaves your hands free and helps distinguish a stair tread from flowing water.
- **A modest first-aid kit**, including supplies for cleaning cuts and covering blisters.
- **Insect repellent and suitable leg coverings** for forest paths.
- **A reliable way to treat drinking water**, plus a bottle you can fill from a source your host recommends.
- **A light sleeping liner**, if accommodation is uncertain.

Keep your documents, medicines, return mechanism, and any indispensable off-world equipment in independent waterproof protection. Preferably keep the critical items on your person rather than in a bag that might be set down, stolen by a monkey, or dropped into a stream.

Do not assume that anything left under a roof will become dry. A roof prevents direct rain; it does not remove the moisture from the air.

## Learn how the town carries weight and water

For your first few journeys, follow someone who knows the way.

The settlement’s floors, bridges, walls, roots, and living bamboo form a maintained system. Some parts may look improvised but be thoroughly dependable; others may look substantial while awaiting repair. You are unlikely to tell the difference immediately.

Ask your host:

- Which routes are open now?
- Which bridge or lower passage closes first during heavy rain?
- Where should a visitor go if water rises or the slope becomes unsafe?
- What warning do people use?
- Which handrails and platforms are meant to bear weight?

**A pole across a bridge is a closure, not an invitation to test your balance.** Do not step around it because the span still appears intact.

Similarly, do not clear a drain, shift a plank, cut a root, or move a stone simply because it seems helpful. Here, redirecting a little water can send it through somebody’s kitchen. A convenient branch may belong to the bamboo holding the slope.

On a narrow bridge, wait for loaded residents to pass. Avoid stopping on landings where goods are being hauled upward by rope. If you want to admire the ravine, first find somewhere that is not part of the town’s circulation.

## Take rain seriously without treating it as a catastrophe

The residents do not abandon their lives when rain begins. They change routes, move goods, and carry out practiced repairs. Follow their example rather than either panicking or deciding that their calm means there is no danger.

Watch for changes: water suddenly becoming muddier, debris arriving in unusual quantity, new cracking or movement in a wall, or a stream swelling rapidly. Report these to your host. Do not go investigate the unseen source of a booming sound in the ravine.

Do not automatically assume that “higher” means “safe.” An upper path may lie below an unstable slope; a lower building may have a carefully chosen refuge nearby. Let local experience guide you.

If you are caught away from your lodging, ask for shelter rather than forcing a crossing. There is no prize for getting your own bed wet instead of accepting a place to wait.

## Treat the water as scenery until its drinking safety is established

The streams may look clear and cold, but they pass through a densely inhabited place with livestock, washing, food waste, and runoff. Appearance is not evidence of safety.

Use a drinking source identified by residents, and treat water when needed. If you cannot establish biological compatibility between your home world and this one, be especially cautious about untreated water and unfamiliar raw foods.

The cookshops are likely to be your easiest introduction to local food. Freshly cooked broth and hot meals are generally a more sensible starting point than wet raw greens. Ask about ingredients, particularly herbs, rather than assuming that a familiar-looking plant is familiar.

Wash your hands before eating. Small cuts deserve prompt cleaning in a place where nearly everything you touch is damp.

## Give the animals room

The macaques are accustomed to people and interested in food. That makes them more troublesome, not more trustworthy.

Keep food inside a securely closed bag. Do not feed them, display snacks while passing beneath roof ridges, or try to reclaim stolen fruit by grabbing an animal. Ask a resident for help if one takes something important. Keep your return device well out of their reach.

Chickens, ducks, and goats will also affect your footing. A duck occupying a drain is a reason to slow down, not to jump onto an uncertain surface.

For the upper forest paths, cover your legs and check yourself for leeches afterward. Remove attached leeches gently rather than burning them or applying irritants. Clean any bleeding site. Avoid pushing bare hands through vegetation you cannot see into.

At night, close food containers and check bedding and shoes. Lamps attract insects; an open shutter beside a bright light may become a busy entrance.

## Be a considerate guest in a crowded place

Privacy and access are likely to be negotiated closely here. One household’s laundry shades another household’s window; a neighbor’s gallery may become a public detour when stairs flood. That does not mean every doorway is yours to enter.

Ask before using a passage that goes through a home. Ask before photographing people or close views into rooms. Do not spread your belongings across a washing platform or leave wet boots where they block movement.

Useful questions are simple:

- “Where should I put this?”
- “Is this way open?”
- “Can I help without getting in the way?”

If you offer help, take direction. Carrying baskets, fetching something, or moving goods to a designated shelf may be welcome. Repairing unfamiliar bamboo construction is not a good opportunity to demonstrate confidence.

Bring an agreed means of payment. Do not assume that off-world currency, gadgets, or exotic materials will be useful or welcome. If you want to bring a gift, ask an intermediary what is actually needed.

## Choose lodging for dryness and safe access

The best room is not necessarily the one with the finest ravine view. Favor a place with a reasonably dry sleeping platform, a way to ventilate smoke, and access to routes your host considers dependable in bad weather.

Learn the nighttime route to the toilet before dark. Put your lamp, footwear, warm layer, and essential equipment where you can reach them without searching. Ask where to dispose of waste; do not assume a drain carries everything safely away.

Hang damp clothing only where your host directs. It should not obstruct a shutter, drip on someone’s bedding, or crowd a cooking fire.

Finally, prepare for the sensory experience. The town will seldom be quiet: water beneath floors, wings under roofs, voices through thin partitions, frogs after dark, bamboo rubbing overhead. You may feel enclosed when mist erases the far slope, then unexpectedly exposed when sunlight reveals the ravine.

The right attitude is **patient attention**. Watch where residents place their feet, what they move when rain strengthens, and which repairs they make without ceremony. This town survives not by keeping the mountain out, but by continually making room for its water and growth. Your safest visit will come from learning a little of that practice—not trying to overcome it.
