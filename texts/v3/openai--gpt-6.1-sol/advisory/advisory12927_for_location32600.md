Prepare for this as you would prepare to enter a living electrical component in near-vacuum—not as you would prepare to visit a garden.

The first difficulty is getting there alive. The more important difficulty is arriving without changing what you came to see.

## Decide what “visiting” means

At ordinary human size, you cannot enter. You can look into the broken insulator, but even a fingertip would occupy much of its interior. Your body would be an approaching weather system: warm, moist, electrically conductive, carrying dust and organisms into a place that has apparently excluded all of them.

The safest visit would therefore be remote: a very small optical instrument held outside the opening, sending its view to you in a protected vessel some distance away. Even that instrument must be designed not to warm, shade, illuminate, or touch the garden unnecessarily.

If your means of multiverse travel allows you to become small enough to walk among the plants, do not assume that shrinking solves the problem. It merely exchanges one set of dangers for another. You will need to know what happens to your metabolism, heat output, breathing, weight, and susceptibility to electrical fields at that scale.

**If your equipment cannot keep you alive while keeping your presence negligible, remain outside.**

## Prepare for the tower before preparing for the forest

The summit is above the old mountains, in air too thin to trust. Bring complete life support:

- A sealed pressure environment with its own oxygen supply and carbon-dioxide removal.
- Thermal control that does not depend on the surrounding air.
- Navigation and propulsion that work without wind or aerodynamic lift.
- An independent means of returning to your departure point.
- Redundant power, communications, and position keeping.

Do not plan to climb the tower in ordinary mountaineering equipment. The lower structure disappears into darkness, its condition is unknown, and the destination is part of an operating transmission system. There may be no useful rescue route from below.

Approach without taking hold of a conductor, spindle, wire, or apparently convenient fitting. You do not know their voltage, their relationship to one another, or whether your vehicle could bridge something that has remained separated for ages.

The system’s steadiness is not evidence that it is harmless. It may simply mean that nothing has disturbed it.

## Leave your warmth behind

Thermal preparation is central.

The garden is in equilibrium: ceramic, foliage, machinery, and the air within the hollow share a stable cool temperature. A human hand would not merely make the place uncomfortable. It could alter tissues, electrical behavior, and the intimate arrangement of surfaces on which the whole garden depends.

Measure that temperature remotely before attempting entry. Do not guess from the appearance of the sky or the altitude.

Any visiting capsule or suit must present an exterior close to the garden’s temperature while keeping its occupant at a survivable one. More difficult still, it must account for the heat produced by the occupant and equipment. Insulation delays heat transfer; it does not make waste heat disappear.

For a short visit, a pre-cooled thermal reservoir might be sufficient. For a longer visit, you would need some means of rejecting heat away from the hollow without creating another disturbance. If your travel technology offers neither, your visit should be brief and external.

Do not vent warm breathing gas into the garden. Do not bring an exposed lamp, battery, or motor close to a leaf.

Here, **heat is a form of trespass**.

## Do not bring ordinary garden supplies

Nothing here needs watering.

Leave behind misting equipment, nutrient solutions, soil, and every instinct that says a plant must be thirsty because its surroundings are dry. These organisms use a waxy electrolyte and draw charge through roots reaching the spindle. Introducing water could change conductivity, occupy microscopic spaces, or damage tissues whose chemistry you do not understand.

Likewise, do not assume the plants would benefit from more light, fresher air, or additional room.

This is not visibly a garden waiting to flourish. It has already filled its available world. Its continued life consists, at least on the timescale you can observe, of maintaining what is there.

Prepare your equipment to be exceptionally clean and free of loose material:

- No exposed skin, hair, or fabric lint.
- No soil-bearing footwear.
- No lubricants that can creep, evaporate, or shed.
- No loose fasteners or detachable accessories.
- No unnecessary biological material.

The complete absence of animals should be respected as a condition of the place, not interpreted as an invitation to introduce one. That includes an unsealed you.

## Plan for electrical and mechanical contact

The vegetation is connected to a working electrical system. Treat exposed metal, glowing root contacts, and the plants themselves as potentially significant parts of that system.

Do not borrow power from the wires. Do not ground the spindle. Do not turn off the conductor to make the visit “safer.” Its load supplies both charge and the continuous vibration to which the garden is fitted.

A shutdown could be as disruptive as a surge.

The sound requires its own preparation. Outside, you may hear little. Inside, the vibration reaches every surface. A quiet-looking leaf is not an acoustically isolated place, and the bolt head is not a silent refuge.

Use vibration isolation for any miniature passenger compartment. Test communications and instruments against a sustained, narrow mechanical resonance. Do not rely solely on microphones for navigation or diagnosis; their readings may be dominated by the tower’s note.

You should expect the hum to become bodily rather than merely audible. Depending on your scale and your equipment, it may feel as though the floor, walls, and your own frame are participating in one uninterrupted tone.

Bring a way to leave before that becomes intolerable.

## There is no path

If you enter at reduced scale, do not imagine yourself strolling between trunks.

The forest is crowded through its full volume. Leaves overlap, stems occupy grain-sized gaps, and the deeper cavities are already inhabited by vegetation. A passage that looks open from the entrance may narrow behind a translucent blade or lead directly to an exposed contact.

Map the interior optically before moving into it. Maintain a known route back to the broken lip. Do not force a leaf aside to investigate what lies behind it; that leaf may be bracing another structure or shielding a contact.

The bolt head is the only visible open ground, but it is not necessarily a landing pad. Its alloy refuses plant attachment. That tells you nothing about its electrical potential, its temperature under contact, or its ability to support you safely. Its slightly different pitch also suggests that it would transmit the tower’s vibration differently from the surrounding ceramic.

If a visit requires standing somewhere, establish that somewhere before departure. Do not arrive expecting to improvise.

Ideally, your means of entry should allow you to remain suspended without exhaust, downwash, contact, or significant electric-field disturbance. Without such a capability, remote viewing may be the only responsible choice.

## Learn to see without lighting the place

Your first view will probably be disappointing: a broken edge and darkness.

Allow time for the interior to resolve. Use sensitive, passive imaging rather than immediately adding illumination. The green veins and violet contact points are dim, but they are enough to reveal the garden on its own terms.

If additional light is essential, use the lowest measured exposure that serves the purpose. Do not assume a wavelength invisible to human eyes is harmless to these plants.

Expect your sense of distance to fail. A dark groove will look like a valley; overlapping leaves will suggest a far canopy. Keep an explicit scale reference in your display. At miniature size, the same scene may feel vast, but feeling vast does not make it spacious.

There is no horizon inside. Almost every view ends in another surface.

## Prepare for a visit without an event

Nothing may move while you are there.

You may see no unfurling, no falling dust, no visible renewal. Do not interpret this as lifelessness, equipment failure, or a need to stimulate the garden. Its activity may be slower or subtler than your visit can resolve.

Prepare to attend to arrangement instead:

- The way a transparent trunk passes through an old screw hole.
- The roots occupying the maker’s identification marks.
- The exact clearance between foliage and bare alloy.
- The different pitches carried by bridge, bolt, and ceramic.
- The pale leaf whose edge coincides with the broken lip.

Do not sample a leaf. There is no casual pruning here. In a fully occupied system, a tiny loss can create a space and alter relationships that have remained settled for an immense time.

## Establish an abort rule

Leave immediately if you detect a temperature change, electrical instability, condensation, displaced foliage, or any object shed from your equipment. Your response should be withdrawal, not an improvised repair.

Do not bring a tether that can drag through the plants. Do not leave a marker to prove you were there. Record externally what you need to remember.

The ideal visit ends with no visible difference between arrival and departure.

What you are preparing to encounter is a forest whose weather is an electrical load, whose bedrock is fired ceramic, and whose entire history has settled into the space available to it.

Go prepared to be kept outside. If you are admitted, go prepared to take up almost nothing.

The pale leaf at the entrance does not tremble. Your task is to leave it that way.
