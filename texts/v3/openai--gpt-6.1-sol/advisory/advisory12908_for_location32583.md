Prepare for two journeys: a serious ascent of an exposed mountain, and a visit to a place that may not yet have decided what it means to admit a visitor.

The mountain is likely to hurt you in familiar ways. The hollow could hurt you in ways for which familiar precautions have no name. Treat the first as an alpine expedition and the second, initially, as an observation from the threshold.

## Reach the seam with enough strength to leave it

Do not let the promise of warmth reduce your mountain preparations. Outside the opening there is wind, severe cold, rapidly changing light, and little shelter. Bring appropriate climbing equipment, layered insulation, eye protection, food, water, navigation, and a reliable means of calling for help. Acclimatize before attempting the upper mountain. Travel with someone who can recognize when your judgment is deteriorating.

Find a secure place to stand or anchor yourself **before** leaning toward the seam. A thumb-wide opening is an invitation to put your face against the rock, forget your footing, and spend much longer looking than you intended. The hollow’s warmth may make your face comfortable while your hands and feet become dangerously cold.

Set a turnaround time in advance. Have your companion enforce it if necessary. This is exactly the sort of place where “one more minute” could become the last hour of safe daylight.

Do not widen the opening. The seam may be an aperture, a boundary, or part of the mountain’s functioning interior—not merely a crack obstructing your view.

## Decide what “visiting” means before attempting entry

The whole landscape fits in a cupped hand, but it contains distances that take time to see across. That does not establish that a human can enter it.

For a first visit, bring a magnifier, a camera with macro capability, and equipment that allows you to observe without placing anything inside. A stable camera support attached to your own gear is preferable to one braced against the young mineral floor.

If multiverse travel gives you access to shrinking or spatial translation, do not assume that either solves the problem. Reducing your size may make the plants accessible while making their waste heat overwhelming. A smaller body also behaves differently around dust, surface forces, and tiny electrical discharges.

More importantly, **being small enough to fit is not the same as being compatible with the place**.

Any proposed entry method should preserve your ordinary bodily conditions and give you an independent way out. Do not rely on walking back to the entrance if the hollow’s distances or gravitational directions can change.

If you cannot maintain a protective boundary around yourself without displacing plants, interrupting the substrate, or altering the hollow’s heat balance, remain outside.

## Keep your water to yourself

The water warning is especially important because an ordinary human is a wet organism.

An introduced drop becomes a small white stone. That tells you something definite about exposed water, but not enough about what would happen to blood, tears, lung moisture, or the water enclosed in living tissue. Do not discover the answer with a fingertip.

Avoid:

- breathing directly into the opening;
- bringing in snow on gloves or clothing;
- allowing condensation to fall from lenses;
- touching the plants with bare skin;
- introducing drinks, saliva, or samples of bodily fluids.

If bodily entry is possible at all, use a sealed environmental system with internally managed humidity and a breathing supply independent of the hollow. Its seals should be checked before the ascent, not improvised at the seam.

Do not test the water transformation for curiosity. A tiny white stone is still a foreign object. In a world this small, it may occupy a significant piece of future ground.

## Protect yourself from heat without cooling the hollow

The opening is already hot enough to sting. Deeper surfaces, especially unfinished depressions, may be hotter. The apparent steadiness of the interior temperature does not make it human-safe.

Use noncontact temperature measurements first. Treat their results cautiously: translucent minerals and unusual surfaces can complicate readings.

If you enter, your equipment should manage your heat internally rather than venting cold gas or spraying coolant into the landscape. Avoid anything that would shade the entire hollow for an extended period. A human hand held over the seam may amount to a substantial weather event.

You should be prepared for the smell to be misleading. “Hot motor wrapped in crushed herbs” is a sensory resemblance, not evidence that the air is safe to breathe. The described absence of insects and animals also gives you no assurance that the atmosphere supports animal life.

## Bring a secure reference for where you are

Near the entrance, gravity is reliable. Farther in, it follows mineral veins.

Do not plan around a single, permanent “down.” A ledge that looks walkable may draw you sideways; the recess above the pale plant receives falling leaves. Keep tools attached, close loose pockets, and do not scatter marker grains to test gravity.

A physical safety line could be useful only if it remains clear of the plants and does not pull you across sharp ridges when your weight changes direction. If you cannot arrange that safely, a tether is not a solution.

Likewise, a map should record relationships—entrance beside copper stand, bare interval before dark fans—rather than promise fixed distances. The far edge recedes under attention. Do not pursue it merely because it seems only a few more steps away.

The red stand deserves particular caution. Its roots occupy ground that has not yet acquired depth. Empty-looking space there is not necessarily open passage, and there is no basis for assuming that unfinished ground can carry you.

## Observe on the hollow’s timescale

Bring patience, spare power, and a way to record events over long intervals.

The most useful visit may involve a brief installation outside the seam followed by remote observation. Photograph from consistent positions. Record temperature, daylight, clicks, and visible changes with timestamps. If possible, monitor vibration through the outer rock without drilling into it.

Do not expect a click and a leaf movement to form a simple cause-and-effect pair. Some processes precede their visible results by days; others seem to finish something already underway. An hour without change may still be an hour of activity.

Avoid tapping the rock, sending electrical pulses into the fans, or “helping” a pale leaf unfold. Here, a mechanical disturbance might be an input to a process you cannot see.

## Leave everything where it belongs

The hollow has no obvious waste. Fallen leaves become grains, and those grains return through the floor to living roots.

That makes collecting especially intrusive. A dead leaf is not an abandoned specimen. It is material in transit. An amber blister may be substrate, machinery, or both. A loose grain may be following a gravitational route that matters.

Bring clean, low-shedding equipment. Leave no fibers, adhesive, skin flakes, probe fragments, or food crumbs. Do not introduce ordinary plants, microbes, fertilizer, or water on the assumption that a lush place would welcome more life.

There is room for more stands. That does not make the empty ground available.

## Prepare for the temptation to stay

The hollow will probably feel sheltered: warm, abundant, quiet, untouched by the violence of the mountain outside. Its movements are so slow that they may seem harmless. Its machinery has no operator to warn you away.

But nothing there is shaped around a human visitor’s needs.

The safest first visit ends with you still outside, having seen more than you expected and touched nothing. You leave while the descent is manageable, with recordings rather than specimens and unanswered questions rather than improvised experiments.

Come prepared to watch a copper leaf hang over an edge that is still becoming real—and to accept that, for now, watching is the fullest kind of visit the place can safely offer.
