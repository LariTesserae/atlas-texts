Prepare for this place as you would for a high-altitude geothermal site built around machinery that is still operating, though no one knows what it was meant to do.

The danger is not that everything is visibly hostile. It is that several things seem helpful: a roof that keeps you dry, a cup that never empties, a boundary that catches falling stones. An ordinary human could become comfortable here before discovering that none of those provisions was designed for an ordinary human.

Your first visit should be short, self-contained, and leave very little behind.

## Before you arrive

**Arrange an exit that does not depend on the shelf.** If you travel by portal, keep its anchor on terrain outside the growing basin if possible, and confirm that you can reopen it without touching any local fixture. If you arrive on foot, establish your return route while you are still cool, rested, and able to see it.

Do not assume the mountain approach is safe simply because the shelf appears sheltered. Wind passes above the basin, and the summit route may be much colder, more exposed, or more difficult than the destination itself.

Go with one companion rather than alone or in a large party. A companion can notice heat confusion or help with a retreat; a crowd increases the chance of someone stepping into a pool, touching a control, or wandering toward the rim. Leave your route and a firm return deadline with someone elsewhere.

Pack for both the mountain and the basin:

- Boots with heat-resistant soles, good wet-rock traction, and ankle support.
- Lightweight clothing that covers your skin, plus a separate insulating layer for the approach.
- A sun hat, sunscreen, and eye protection. High elevation still matters beneath a small local cloud.
- More drinking water than you expect to need, carried in sealed containers.
- Electrolytes, food, a basic first-aid kit, and supplies for cooling a burn with clean water.
- A contact thermometer or probe, if available.
- A compact insulating seat pad.
- A watch, notebook, and camera with a wrist strap.
- A means of calling for help that you have tested outside the basin.

Bring little loose equipment. You do not want to find out whether a dropped battery is caught beneath the rim, incorporated into a mineral crust, or treated differently from a stone.

## Make your first circuit an inspection, not an exploration

The shelf is only sixty paces across. That makes it easy to survey, but it also means there may be no refuge from a change affecting the whole basin.

Stay well back from the broken rim. Identify the approach, the shelter, the three plant islands, and a clear route between them. Note which surfaces are wet, which channels carry water, and where the crust is cracked.

The pale stone is not necessarily solid stone. It is actively forming, and some of it covers a warmer translucent layer. A fresh lip around a pool may be thin, brittle, or poorly supported. Do not use its whiteness or apparent dryness as evidence that it will bear your weight.

Test footing gently. Avoid jumping, stomping, or placing your full weight on narrow ridges. Keep away from crystal-lined cavities; transparent needles can be sharp even when they look delicate.

Nothing here needs to be pried open.

## Treat heat as the main immediate hazard

The heat comes from below as well as from the air. Shade may reduce sunlight without making a surface safe to touch.

Check the ground and bench before sitting. Keep your pack off hot, wet mineral surfaces, and do not kneel bare-skinned to examine anything. Boots provide time, not immunity: if heat begins coming through the soles, move away rather than waiting to see whether it becomes painful.

The humidity will make cooling difficult. You can sweat heavily without cooling effectively, especially if you have just climbed to the shelf. Headache, nausea, unusual clumsiness, irritability, or difficulty making simple decisions should end the visit. They could reflect heat, altitude, or both; you do not need to distinguish them before leaving.

Drink your own water regularly. Do not ration it in order to extend your stay.

The repeated showers are not reliable relief. They wet the rock, increase humidity, and are followed by steam. During a shower, watch your footing and avoid leaning over steaming pools.

## Use the shelter cautiously

The roof is the most plausible place to pause, but inspect it without handling it. Its fine threaded supports are rooted into the basin. They may be structural, sensory, or both.

If the space beneath is comfortably breathable and the bench is not dangerously hot, use your insulating pad and sit briefly. Keep the exit clear. Listen to the ticks, but do not assume that a change in their rhythm has any meaning you can act on.

**Do not put your fingers into the three hollows.** Their wear suggests repeated operation, not an invitation. A control fitted to a different hand might require pressure, sequence, conductivity, or contact you would supply accidentally.

Likewise, leave the buried central ring alone. Do not clear its roots to obtain a better photograph.

## Bring water; leave the cup alone

The cup will be enormously tempting. Cold water in that heat may feel like the one plainly benevolent thing on the shelf.

It is not yet established as safe.

Its constant level and the disappearance of leaves mean it is not behaving as an ordinary container. Clear, cool, pleasant-tasting water could still contain unfamiliar dissolved substances or biological material. More importantly, you do not know what happens to anything introduced into it.

Do not drink from it, wash with it, dip a finger into it, or put equipment inside. An ordinary filter or purifier would not establish that this particular water is safe.

You should arrive hydrated enough that refusing it does not become a difficult decision.

## Do not test the rim’s protection

The posts catch some loose objects. They do not catch everything, and the description gives no evidence that they would catch a person.

Rain passes through. Birds move normally below. Stones stop at different depths. The effect is selective, local, and changes slightly during the afternoon cooling.

That is not a guardrail.

Stay far enough back that a slip would stop on the shelf itself. Do not lean out to retrieve a suspended stone, and do not throw objects over the edge for entertainment or experiment. Even a successful test would tell you little about what happens to a heavier, moving human body.

If fall protection is necessary, it needs an independently verified anchor—not one of the leaning posts or a shelter upright.

## Observe the cycle without trying to complete it

Watch at least one rain formation from a safe position. Note its duration, the rise of steam, the leaf movements, and the changing water channels.

The late-afternoon sequence deserves particular attention: the seep strengthens, pools fill in an order, the central hollow fills, and only then does the heat ease. That suggests the water and embedded structures are connected, but it does not tell you how.

Do not dam a trickle, scoop out a channel, move a crust lip, or pull up a plant to see what happens. A very small alteration could matter in a system governed by shallow water.

The brief cooler interval is not proof that the basin has shut down. The next shower is already assembling. Leave while conditions are understood, rather than staying because the garden has begun to feel ordinary.

## Keep contact with living things minimal

The plants may be harmless, but their dense roots, abrupt boundaries, and delayed leaf movements are worth respecting. Avoid pushing through them or sheltering beneath their dripping leaves. Do not taste anything.

Leave the snails where they are. Do not reach into the hot silt for the jointed animal. Its retreat is sufficient reason not to pursue it.

The black bird’s route might hint at access elsewhere on the mountain, but it is a route for a bird. Watch; do not follow.

Before departure, check your boots, clothing, and pack for mineral paste, sharp deposits, snails, seeds, or attached leaves. Remove unwanted material carefully and keep anything you cannot remove contained until it can be assessed outside the site.

## The right attitude

This would likely be a beautiful, oddly intimate visit: rain close enough to watch forming, individual drops falling from leaves, a vast silent valley beneath your feet. The warmth and lack of wind could make the shelf feel protected.

Enjoy that, but keep your own water, your own footing, and your own way home.

The best first visit ends with photographs, a few careful observations, and the basin still doing exactly what it was doing before you arrived.
