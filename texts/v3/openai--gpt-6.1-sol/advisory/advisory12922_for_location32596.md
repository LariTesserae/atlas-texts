Prepare for this hollow as you would for a wet industrial site whose floors, walls, and supports are alive. It is not quite a garden, not quite a building, and certainly not a patch of wilderness you can cross by choosing any convenient foothold. Its apparent solidity depends on water moving, stems swelling, roots gripping, and somebody continuing to make yesterday’s cuts.

The most important preparation is to arrange a visit with whoever maintains the pump. This is a place where a guide’s knowledge of one troublesome leaf may matter more than all your general outdoor experience.

## Before descending

Spend a little time at the upper lip. You can see the extent of the hollow from there, and that overview will become harder to recover once you are beneath the leaves.

Identify the maintained descent, the machinery shed, the clearing, and your way back out. Notice where the pumped water arrives at the rim. If possible, ask the caretaker:

- Which surfaces currently support a person?
- Does the pump cycle change the firmness of the path or clearing?
- Has anything recently split, shifted, or grown across the route?
- What does the loud metallic squeal signify?
- Is there a signal for visitors to stop moving or leave?

Do not assume the squeal is either harmless routine or imminent failure. The description gives you no reliable way to distinguish those possibilities.

Arrive in daylight, preferably with enough time to leave well before dusk. Evening may be beautiful here, but beauty is not a substitute for being able to distinguish a worn walking surface from an unworn leaf over concealed water.

If you are traveling between universes, verify the basics at the rim rather than inside the hollow: breathable air, tolerable temperature, and any known risks from local water, sap, or spores. Nothing about the place establishes poisonous vegetation or dangerous microorganisms, but unfamiliar biology deserves ordinary caution. The unusual plant mechanics are not a reason to invent exotic hazards; they are a reason not to assume that familiar-looking leaves behave like familiar plants.

## What to wear and carry

**Wear light, close-fitting clothing that tolerates heat and persistent damp.** Loose sleeves, dangling straps, and long coat hems are poor companions for exposed belts and shafts. Expect wet cuffs, condensation in seams, and stains from plant fluids, rust, and grease.

Bring:

- **Closed footwear with good wet-surface grip.** Broad, reasonably flexible soles are preferable to heels or aggressive spikes that concentrate weight or puncture living surfaces.
- **Thin work gloves**, useful around rough roots and ironwork, but not permission to reach near moving machinery.
- **Clear protective glasses**, especially if you will be close to dripping vegetation or maintenance work.
- **A small headlamp**, even for a daytime visit. The water and your next foothold may be hidden in shadow.
- **A waterproof bag or liner** for your phone, notes, identification, and return equipment.
- **Drinking water from a known safe source.** The hollow is wet, but its water has passed through plant tissues and working machinery.
- **A modest first-aid kit**, particularly dressings for cuts and something to clean them.
- **A charged means of communicating with your host or someone outside.**

Keep the pack small and secured close to your body. A large pack can catch in the low root-lined bridge or press against plants you are trying not to damage.

Do not bring pruning tools with the intention of clearing your own way. Cutting is how the path is maintained, but it is also an intervention in the structure. A stem that obstructs your shoulder may be supporting a roof, holding a pipe, or keeping a water passage open.

## Learn how to stand here

Your first adjustment will be accepting that **green does not mean soft, and brown does not mean sound**. A broad leaf may be an established walkway. A dry-looking mound may have yielding living tissue beneath its outer plates. A root mat may carry you comfortably—or conceal a gap.

Use the maintained route and the pale, worn centers of designated walking leaves. Those signs suggest repeated use, not a guarantee of present strength, so follow your guide’s judgment.

Move slowly enough to feel changes underfoot. Avoid jumping, running, or dropping heavily onto a surface. If a step begins to yield, shift your weight back rather than testing how far it will sink. Do not step into a puddled panel merely because the water is shallow; the puddle tells you little about what supports it.

The yellow railing is a route marker and possible handhold, not necessarily a dependable fall-arrest system. It disappears into growing wood, changes angle, and may have compromised attachments. Use it lightly unless the caretaker confirms it is sound.

In the clearing, give people room. Six paces across is not much space when the floor is a living leaf and some of its firmness appears to change with the valve cycle. Avoid gathering everyone around one edge of the table.

## Treat the pump as working machinery

The flywheel’s partial turn and backward settling make it especially easy to misread. A pause is not a shutdown. Something that appears to have stopped may move again, or reverse.

Stay clear of:

- The flywheel and its sweep.
- Both belts, including the vine belt.
- Rollers, rods, levers, and piston assemblies.
- Clamped stems and their working surfaces.
- Any place where plant tissue and metal are pressing against one another.

The living belt is not safer to touch than the rubber one. Likewise, a swollen stem inside a sleeve is part of the power system, not a convenient branch to hold.

Do not turn valves, loosen clamps, oil components, test the electrical switch, or help yourself to the shears. Ask before touching even apparently idle equipment. The pump supplies water to the younger growth above; interfering with it could damage the hollow well beyond the shed.

The electrical installation deserves particular caution in this humidity. Its leaf hood is not evidence that it is electrically safe. If you see damaged wiring, sparking, or exposed connections, keep away and tell the caretaker.

For a first visit, observation is plenty.

## Pay attention without becoming alarmed by everything

The hollow will probably feel more enclosed than its small size suggests. You will know that you could see the whole place from above, yet at ground level you may not be able to see the water a foot away. Leaves and root crowns break up the space. Warmth, the vase-water smell, and the machinery’s uneven motion may make the air feel heavier than it is.

Expect to hear hidden things very close to you. A seed case clicking or a stem releasing a post may sound disproportionately sharp. Droplets shaken loose by the squeal may feel like sudden rain beneath the roof.

Give yourself a moment to acclimatize rather than rushing through this discomfort. Take breaks at the rim if you become overheated, nauseated, dizzy, or simply overwhelmed. There is no benefit in proving you can tolerate the hollow longer than your body comfortably allows.

Learn a few ordinary sounds from the caretaker. The goal is not to classify every creak, but to recognize a change: a new grinding noise, a suddenly altered pump rhythm, water rising onto the path, or several supports shifting together. If something changes sharply, stop, keep clear of machinery, and follow the caretaker’s instructions rather than investigating the source.

## Respect the place’s peculiar housekeeping

Here, neglect becomes architecture.

Stationary objects acquire collars, sheaths, and roots. A door becomes unusable not necessarily because it is broken, but because it has not been opened often enough. Old wreckage remains useful because living growth has incorporated it into the load-bearing structure.

Accordingly:

- Do not pry open the nearly engulfed locker.
- Do not remove rusted brackets, splintered wood, or apparently abandoned metal.
- Do not pull roots off a handle or pipe to make it look tidier.
- Do not place your belongings in joints, crevices, or beneath leaves.
- Keep bags and clothing away from freshly cut edges and exposed sap.
- Leave the ledger in its protected box unless invited to examine it.

It would also be courteous to arrive with reasonably clean shoes and equipment, and to clean them again before entering another living environment. Plants that establish themselves in the joints of other plants make transporting seeds or root fragments an especially thoughtless souvenir practice.

## Plan your departure

Keep your return device or essential documents on your person, not resting on the table. Count what you brought in and what you carry out.

Do not assume the descent will look exactly the same on your return. Growth is visibly active over hours, although there is no evidence that a visitor will be suddenly engulfed. The more immediate concern is a changing surface, a new obstruction, or a route that looks unfamiliar from below.

A first visit should be short: long enough to watch several pump cycles, cross the root-lined bridge with guidance, and see how the clearing responds when the valve opens. An overnight stay would require an explicitly maintained sleeping place. Do not improvise a bed on a broad leaf simply because it held you while standing.

The best thing to bring, after suitable shoes and a knowledgeable host, is patience. This hollow rewards looking twice. On the first look, you see machinery disappearing into vegetation. On the second, you begin to see a maintained partnership: stems shaped by clamps, a moving shaft keeping its own opening clear, old broken things still holding water passages apart.

You do not need to fear that partnership. You do need to avoid stepping into it as though you were outside it.
