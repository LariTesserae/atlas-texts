Prepare as though you were visiting a city inside a precision instrument—except that the instrument is also everyone’s home, and nobody can stop it while you find your footing.

The principal danger is not that the city will harm you. It is that an ordinary human body will change the city simply by being present.

## First, arrange to arrive at the right size

At your normal size, this is not a place you can enter responsibly. The chamber is barely wider than your reach, and a house fits on your thumbnail. Your fingertips would be demolition equipment. Your breath could move loose material. A sleeve brushing the roofs could redirect the water on which whole districts depend.

Do not try to lift the limestone slab. Its weight and apparent insignificance are part of what protects the chamber. Moving it could change the load on the stone, the ventilation, the light, or the course of a seep.

If your means of multiverse travel allows a reduced-scale arrival, **arrive no larger than an inhabitant, with your effective mass reduced accordingly**. A human-sized weight concentrated into a tiny body would be disastrous. You also need assurance that the transition itself produces no pressure pulse, heat, displaced water, or abrupt movement of air.

Ideally, arrange your arrival with a local guide who can identify a safe receiving place. An empty-looking ledge is not necessarily unused: it may be a drainage route, a structural brace, or the clearance beneath a falling droplet.

If you cannot solve the size and arrival problem, remain outside. Listening to the rush beneath the stair is a legitimate visit. Opening the city up for a better look is not.

## Dress for wet stone and narrow passages

The inhabitants tolerate the cold. You should not assume that you will.

Wear a close-fitting insulating layer beneath a smooth, non-shedding outer garment. Avoid loose hems, dangling straps, open pockets, and anything that can catch on a wall. At this scale, a loose fiber is not negligible debris; it may become a bridge across a notch or an obstruction in a drain.

Your footwear should provide grip on wet limestone without shedding grit or leaving a coating. Do not bring cleats or sharp-edged soles. The routes are worn and polished, and sometimes the correct crossing is a single projecting pebble.

Useful features include:

- Enough flexibility to turn sideways, crouch, and climb.
- Covered fastenings that cannot hook mortar seams.
- A secure place for every object you carry.
- Insulation that remains effective in persistent damp.
- Gloves thin enough to feel whether a surface is solid.

Avoid a bulky backpack. Some streets pass through load-bearing walls, and there is no reason to expect them to accommodate either a human traveler’s proportions or their luggage.

You may find that even after shrinking, your shoulders are too broad and your ribs too deep for ordinary routes. Discuss that with your guide before you enter, not after you become lodged in someone’s doorway.

## Bring very little, and keep it contained

This city appears to operate with almost no margin for stray material. Repairs use fragments already in the chamber. Water brings in dissolved stone and carries an equivalent amount away. That does not prove a dropped crumb would destroy the balance—but it gives you every reason not to find out.

Carry only what you need for a short visit:

- A sealed drinking-water supply.
- Food in a container that can be opened without scattering crumbs.
- A small medical kit, with nothing loose.
- A low-output, controllable light, if the residents permit it.
- A means of communicating despite the noise.
- A reliable departure method that does not depend on finding your way back unaided.
- Sealed containment for all waste, including used medical materials.

Do not count on finding food. Nothing visibly grows here, and the description does not establish what the inhabitants eat or where their membranes, bones, and other organic materials come from. Their provisioning may depend on arrangements unavailable to you.

Do not drink from the pools. Clear water is not necessarily safe water, especially after a long passage through mineral-bearing stone.

Clean your equipment before arrival. Soil, seeds, skin flakes, and lint are all imports. A place with no visible vegetation is not an invitation to introduce some.

## Make your first visit short

The city’s dampness is not weather that will pass. You will not wait out a shower, find a sunny street, or hang your coat somewhere breezy. Nothing in the description promises a dry guest room.

Cold, wet clothing and sustained noise can make you tired sooner than you expect. Plan a brief first visit with a conservative turnaround point, and leave while you still have the attention to cross safely.

Establish your exit arrangements in advance:

- Who will escort you?
- What signal means you need to leave immediately?
- Where can you wait without blocking movement or water?
- What happens if your usual route becomes unavailable?
- How will you depart if you are injured or too cold to walk?

Do not rely on shouting for help.

## Learn to communicate before you need anything

The sound will be one of the largest facts of the visit.

You may imagine a tiny city as having tiny noises. Here the water sounds fill the available space: clattering beneath the central stair, hissing through drains, breathing under the eastern quarter, cracking into the civic cup. There is nowhere reliably quiet enough for a whisper.

Have a local teach you the basic signals for:

- Stop.
- Wait.
- Safe crossing.
- Do not touch.
- I need help.
- I need to leave.

Residents speak close to one another’s ears or touch wrists. Ask before adopting the latter. You do not yet know whether different touches carry specific meanings.

At crossings, follow the local heel-strike convention, but learn its force and timing rather than improvising. A signal should announce your presence, not shake a thin bridge.

Hearing protection may help, but complete isolation would deprive you of information. Entire districts are recognized by sound. What seems like undifferentiated roar to you may tell a resident precisely where they are.

## Treat water as a right-of-way

The most important civic rule is simple: **do not interfere with a flow because it inconveniences you.**

A trickle that crosses a path may have its own name and rights. You are not entitled to dam it, step into it, bridge it with something from your kit, or scrape it aside.

Wait for your guide to show you the projecting stone used for crossing.

At the civic center, keep everything securely fastened. Nothing goes inside the drop cup. None of its seven overflow notches may be altered. Do not lean over it with loose equipment, and do not put a hand under the falling drop just to experience its force.

There are houses that cannot escape a diverted flow through an upper doorway. Your momentary curiosity could become someone else’s flood.

Likewise:

- Do not wipe moisture from a wall.
- Do not clear a channel.
- Do not straighten a tilted piece of masonry.
- Do not remove a fragment from beneath an arch.
- Do not “repair” anything without explicit local direction.

Much of this city’s stability is made of arrangements that look wrong to an outsider. The cracked arch is supported by neighboring houses. The tilted chimney drains well. A fallen lintel creates useful dry storage.

Here, tidying can be a form of damage.

## Walk as if appearances are unreliable

The pools reflect streets and arches so clearly that a submerged image may look like a passage. Follow the children’s rule: touch a surface before committing your weight to it.

But do not test an unfamiliar surface by stamping on it. A thin paving slab may already be dark with water underneath. Use the method your guide demonstrates.

Stay on known routes. Do not reach overhead to steady yourself; the ceiling nearly touches the highest roofs, and droplets need their clearance. Do not brace against a house merely because its wall is conveniently close. The mason who works with both feet against the opposite building knows where that pressure can go. You do not.

If you drop something, stop. Point it out. Let a resident help retrieve it rather than lunging across delicate paving or reaching into a drain.

## Be a guest, not a collector

The city is crowded, but most people remain indoors. Open doors offer brief glimpses of work and domestic life; they are not invitations to look inside.

Ask before observing closely, entering, photographing, or touching tools. Avoid flash. The gray light is sufficient for the residents, and a sudden bright source would be an intrusion even if it caused no physical harm.

Do not bring unsolicited gifts. Food, cloth, metal, or new tools might be welcome—or might be difficult to store, unsafe to use, or contrary to the city’s careful handling of material. Ask first.

Take no mineral souvenirs. Do not chip out a bead, pocket a loose tile fragment, or collect water. Something that appears spare may already have a use.

Give the six-legged creature room, as everyone else does. Do not feed it or touch it. Leave the shell beneath the bridge alone too. Its ambiguous condition is not a problem a visitor needs to settle.

## Expect intimacy without quiet

What may be hardest is the absence of ordinary human distance.

There is little room to stand aside. Streets pass through buildings. Wastewater travels beneath a neighbor’s floor. Conversation happens near the ear or through touch. The city’s essential infrastructure is audible in every room.

You may feel conspicuous, clumsy, and dependent. Accept that. A resident redirecting your foot or asking you to wait is not being fussy; they are helping you occupy a place with very little spare space.

Do not expect to find a silent corner in which to recover. Arrange somewhere safe to pause, and leave if the noise, cold, or confinement begins to overwhelm you.

A successful visit may consist of very little: following a guide through a few turns, waiting at a named trickle, watching a mason work, and standing well back from the drop cup.

Then departing with every object you brought.

The best evidence that you visited well will be that, afterward, the city continues exactly as before: the same water beneath the benches, the same tapping in the upper lane, the next bead forming above the roof.
