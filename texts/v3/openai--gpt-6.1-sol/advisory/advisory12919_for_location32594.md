Prepare for this place as you would prepare to enter a living seam, not a cave.

Its most important feature is not the darkness, the cold, or even the impossible distances. It is that **there is no unoccupied space**. Everything you might use as a foothold is already holding something else. Every passage is also a root’s route, a leaf’s folded interior, or a channel through which pressure travels.

An ordinary human cannot enter it at ordinary size. Shrinking would make entry possible, but it would not necessarily make you welcome—or leave enough room for you to turn around.

## Decide what “visiting” means

The safest visit is one in which your body remains elsewhere.

Establish yourself above the lake or at a stable site well away from the drowned tree, and send a very small, retrievable observer. Even then, do not assume that pushing a camera into the hollow is harmless. A probe smaller than a grain of rice could still displace a leaf that has held its position for several winters.

Begin with what can be learned from outside:

- The lake’s depth, temperature, and ordinary pressure.
- Whether the hollow’s tides produce any detectable signal outside it.
- Which openings already carry water, rather than which openings could be forced wider.
- How much illumination, warmth, or vibration the growth tolerates before changing position.

Do not clear away silt to improve access. The one recorded intrusion of gray light left a lasting mark on a leaf. Whatever that mark means, this is a place where a brief intervention may become part of the landscape.

If your travel method requires physical entry, use it only if it can place you inside **without excavating an arrival space**. Becoming small is not enough; you need somewhere your small body can exist.

## Bring your own conditions of life

There is no air pocket. There is no dry ledge. There is not even a roof with a dry upper surface.

You need a completely sealed life-support system, appropriate to the lake’s pressure and to whatever scale you adopt. Do not depend on finding breathable gas, extracting enough oxygen from the local water, or opening your faceplate for a moment.

Your system should contain its own:

- Breathing supply and carbon-dioxide removal.
- Thermal protection.
- Waste storage.
- Power.
- Means of departure.

The last item matters as much as the first.

Do not vent bubbles into the hollow. A bubble is a large foreign object in a space like this. It could separate surfaces that normally touch, lodge inside a folded leaf, or interrupt the wet contact between a root and its food.

Likewise, keep your heat to yourself. An ordinary human is a furnace compared with this environment. At reduced scale, maintaining your own warmth may become difficult, but warming the surrounding water is not a good solution. You could accelerate decay along one face of a splinter while the other remains hard, or provoke movement in tissue that usually takes a season to bend.

Bring insulation rather than an open heater.

## Make yourself smooth, but not slippery beyond control

There are fresh stone edges under the slime. The description specifically warns against expecting pebbles: this is fractured material, not rounded gravel.

Your outer covering should resist puncture and have no loose fibers, projecting buckles, dangling hoses, or exposed joints. A ridge capable of holding a filament through several seasons could hold a strand from your equipment just as effectively.

Avoid trailing fins and any propulsion that throws a jet of water. At this scale, a kick is not merely movement; it is a disturbance passing through many occupied surfaces.

You will also need to consider how water behaves at your chosen size. Motion may feel less like swimming through a lake and more like working through a resistant, clinging medium. Test your equipment at that scale before coming here. Human intuition about momentum, turning, and stopping may be unreliable.

A tether sounds reassuring, but it is not automatically safe. It can snag, compress growth, or take a route that ceases to correspond to yours. If you use one, treat it as a monitored piece of equipment—not as proof that the way home remains open.

## Secure an exit before looking inward

The back of the hollow does not have dependable geometry.

A root can enter one crack and be felt beneath the opposite wall. A filament can return through the ceiling without an observable turn. Two touching stones can also bound a depth that cannot be sounded.

Do not navigate by the reasoning that “this passage must lead behind that wall.”

For a bodily visit, the best safeguard would be a return mechanism that does not require retracing a physical path. It should be tested against spatial distortion before you trust it here, and an observer outside should be able to activate it if you cannot.

Set firm departure conditions:

- Loss of contact with your outside observer.
- Unexplained disagreement between your instruments.
- Growth beginning to compress your occupied space.
- A pressure change beyond the limits established during observation.
- Any failure of heat, breathing, or return systems.

Do not extend the visit because the exit is “only a short distance away.” Short distance is one of the least trustworthy promises this place offers.

## Learn its pace without adopting it

This hollow makes very small changes over very long periods.

That does not prove time itself runs differently. You may spend an hour watching something that simply does not move during that hour.

Prepare for that distinction. Bring instruments that can record slowly and compare images without requiring you to stare continuously. If you want to understand the creature’s route or the propagation of pressure through the vegetation, remote observation over months will tell you more than a longer personal stay.

Keep an independent clock. Set a visit duration before entry. Do not wait for a leaf to finish opening, a root to complete a bend, or the animal to reach you.

It is easy to imagine the psychological trap: everything appears almost ready to happen. The next movement seems imminent. Meanwhile, your supplies continue to run down at an ordinary human rate.

## Treat light as an intervention

Use the least illumination that lets you avoid damage. No flash photography, no sweeping floodlight, no prolonged beam trained on the gray-backed leaf.

That leaf is not a convenient landmark to manipulate. It is evidence of an event the hollow has retained.

Passive sensing should come first, but do not assume that infrared, ultrasound, or some exotic multiversal scanning method is harmless merely because you cannot see it. Establish its effects from outside.

Expect vision to provide fragments rather than a view: a pale edge, a sheen of slime, a black eye pressed against wood. There may never be enough open distance for you to see the hollow as a whole.

## Leave the arrangements alone

The root arch and leaf-rib enclosure deserve the caution you would give both a possible dwelling and a possible archaeological site.

Do not:

- Lift the white shell chips.
- Straighten the bent rootlets.
- Disturb the level grit.
- Widen the enclosure’s doorway.
- Send a probe inside merely to discover why its outflow is colder.

The uncertainty is the reason for restraint. These things may be constructions, growth patterns, or structures whose function you cannot recognize. There is no need to settle that question during a first visit.

In particular, do not regard the little enclosure as shelter. Its doorway is too narrow even for the resident animal, and colder water comes out of it. Neither fact recommends it to a human traveler.

## Be a poor stimulus for the animal

The pale creature is small, but it is not isolated. The leaves tighten before it moves, and a distant leaf may open long before it arrives.

You should therefore think of approaching it as approaching an interconnected habitat, not a lone animal.

Do not touch it, feed it, illuminate its eyes, or loosen the collars around its feet. Those collars may look restrictive to you; you do not know what removing them would do.

Nor should you excavate the second creature. Being covered does not establish that it is dead, and being motionless does not establish that it needs rescue. Record what you can without peeling back its surroundings.

If nearby leaves tighten, stop advancing. You may not know what the response means, but you know you are no longer observing an undisturbed scene.

## Plan around the tides

The lake’s surface will not necessarily warn you.

Observe several internal tides before entry, if possible, and keep pressure monitoring active throughout the visit. Do not count on predicting an event described as irregular.

When the vegetation lifts, the temporary gaps may look like opportunities. Resist that invitation. Those gaps are part of a rearrangement, and they close into changed positions. The tide that lets you enter a narrow interval may leave a root across your return path.

The buried second animal is a sufficient warning: arrival and continued existence are possible here without freedom of movement.

## What to expect emotionally

Expect closeness without comfort.

Water will touch every external surface of your equipment. Beyond it, cold lives in the wood and stone as well as in the lake. You will hear occasional small creaks and ticks, but probably nothing that gives you a reliable sense of distance. Much of what matters will happen too slowly to witness.

The folded leaves may release hints of rain, bark, or a season occurring somewhere else. Do not taste the water or open your suit to pursue those impressions. Let the strangeness remain outside your life-support boundary.

Above all, do not expect the hollow to become spacious when you understand it.

A successful visit would be brief, quiet, and almost disappointingly uneventful. You would leave with recordings, uncertainties, and no souvenir. Behind you, the shell chips would still hold the arch, the creature would still grip its two points, and the newest root tip would continue branching into the stone.

**Prepare to leave no vacancy, no wound, and nothing that the hollow must spend years growing around.**
