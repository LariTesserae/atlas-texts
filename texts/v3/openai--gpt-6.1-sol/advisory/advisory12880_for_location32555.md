Prepare for this place as an expedition into a deep ocean, not as a visit to mountains with unusual weather.

That distinction will be surprisingly hard to keep in mind. The daylight, the clean ridgelines, the visible machinery, and the enormous distances you can see will all encourage the wrong instincts. A summit will look like somewhere you could step out and walk. A pumping house will look like shelter. The space between two peaks will look empty.

None of those impressions should determine your preparations.

## Establish how you will arrive—and how you will leave

Your first requirement is a receiving environment that keeps you dry, warm, and at a breathable pressure. Arrive inside a verified pressure station or a suitable vehicle. Do not let a transit system deposit you “on the summit” unless it understands that the summit is submerged.

If this sea behaves approximately like an Earth ocean, the highest peaks already have about a kilometer of water above them: roughly a hundred atmospheres of pressure. The buried plains could be under about nine kilometers of water, approaching nine hundred atmospheres. The exact figures depend on local gravity and water density, but the practical conclusion is unchanged: ordinary diving equipment is not an option.

Confirm the local physics before trusting equipment brought from another universe. In particular, obtain measurements of:

- Gravity and hydrostatic pressure.
- Water temperature, salinity, and chemical composition.
- Maximum expected depth along your route.
- The vibration spectrum around active pumping stations.
- Whether your means of interuniversal travel functions from inside a pressure hull.

If departure requires a particular location, that location becomes the center of your entire itinerary. A spectacular ridge is not worth crossing if doing so puts your return point beyond your remaining power supply.

Leave a route plan with someone outside the expedition. Include overdue procedures that do not depend on receiving a distress call. Radio communication through seawater is generally poor, and this ocean’s acoustic background may make other communication methods unreliable.

## Choose a vehicle for pressure, cold, and continuous vibration

For a first visit, use a crewed submersible or small expedition vessel with a dry, surface-pressure interior. It should be rated for the deepest point you might actually reach, including an unintended descent—not merely for your planned cruising depth.

The depth below you matters. A vehicle operating comfortably near a summit may have an abyss beneath it that exceeds its structural rating. Plan routes that give you room to recover from a propulsion or buoyancy failure without entering unsurvivable pressure.

You want:

- Redundant life support, heating, power, navigation, and propulsion.
- An independently operable emergency ascent or recovery system appropriate to an ice-covered ocean.
- Cold-qualified seals, lubricants, batteries, and external mechanisms.
- Protected thrusters and retractable or shielded manipulators.
- Vibration monitoring on the hull and machinery.
- A warm interior with insulated observation windows and handholds.
- Enough reserves to wait out a missed rendezvous or a failed docking attempt.

“Emergency ascent” does not mean “go to the surface.” The surface here is sealed beneath ice. An ascent system is useful only if it takes you to a survivable refuge or a known recovery route. It must not merely deliver your vehicle to the underside of an inaccessible ceiling.

Equipment designed for ordinary deep water may still need modification. Cold can make seals stiff, reduce battery performance, and render exposed components brittle. Persistent vibration can loosen fittings and fatigue parts that would otherwise last for years.

Secure everything inside the cabin. The description of cutlery creeping across plates is a warning about your tools, medicine bottles, fasteners, and personal belongings. Use latching storage, restrained mugs, and padded mounts. Inspect for chafing throughout the trip.

Bring a small remotely operated vehicle for close investigation. Let it inspect intakes, ledges, and unfamiliar doors before you bring your occupied hull near them.

## Prepare for the sound before you prepare for the view

The roar is not an incidental nuisance. It is part of the environment your body will inhabit continuously.

Hearing protection may reduce what reaches your ears, but some of the vibration will arrive through the hull, the seat, your feet, and your bones. Noise-canceling headphones will not make this ocean quiet.

Have the vehicle tested under representative acoustic conditions if that is possible. What matters is not simply loudness but frequency, resonance, and exposure duration. A hull that amplifies a narrow band of vibration could be substantially worse to occupy than another vessel in the same water.

Bring fitted hearing protection, visual alarms, and communications equipment that remains intelligible inside the cabin. Safety-critical warnings should never be audio-only.

Plan short initial excursions. Do not commit yourself to a month because you tolerate the first hour. Continuous sound can interfere with sleep, concentration, mood, and the ability to notice that something has changed. The pumps may be monotonous; your response to them may not be.

For overnight stays, arrange vibration-isolated sleeping accommodation. A suspended bunk sounds appropriate, but its attachments and restraints must be engineered for the conditions. Improvised hanging beds can introduce their own motion and injury risks.

If you have tinnitus, vestibular problems, migraines triggered by vibration, or significant difficulty sleeping in noisy environments, discuss the expedition with a clinician beforehand.

## Treat visibility as a navigational trap

Thirty kilometers of clear water is beautiful, but it removes many of the cues by which humans judge distance.

You may see a ridge with such precision that it appears nearby. A translucent animal may look small because you have unconsciously placed it close to the window. A ledge may seem accessible while lying kilometers below your safe operating depth.

Use instruments for range, depth, attitude, and clearance. Agree on a simple crew rule: **no maneuver justified solely by how close something looks.**

Bring local charts if they exist, inertial navigation, pressure-based depth measurement, and independent position checks. Determine in advance whether acoustic ranging and sonar remain reliable amid the pump noise and basin echoes. Do not assume that equipment working well elsewhere will work well here.

Keep enough lighting to illuminate nearby obstacles and inspect machinery, even though daylight is abundant. Broad illumination is not the same as useful light inside a crevice, beneath an overhang, or below a terrace.

Expect vertigo. You are underwater, but the landscape will still tell your nervous system that you are suspended beside a mountain over an immense drop. Sit somewhere you can look away from the windows. Nobody should have to endure a panoramic view while operating safety-critical controls.

## Give the pumping stations generous clearance

A working station is an industrial hazard before it is a destination.

The absence of a visible discharge plume does not imply the absence of flow. Water can be moving strongly while remaining perfectly clear. Near an intake, a small object may accelerate into machinery before its motion looks alarming.

Approach only using known flow maps, current measurements, or cautious remote reconnaissance. Do not swim near the towers, dock to an intake structure, or trail a cable across their working area.

The basalt creates additional tether hazards. A sharp fin could damage or sever a line drawn across it. Loose cables can also snag in shell beds, grilles, gantries, and abandoned equipment. Any tethered operation needs deliberate routing and a recovery plan.

A stopped station is not necessarily safe. Its machinery might restart automatically; pressure differences may remain in its pipes; structural members may have degraded in ways that are invisible through the water.

Nor do burning lamps prove that people are inside.

Before entering a station, verify occupancy, internal pressure, atmosphere, structural condition, and the operation of its docking lock. A thick window is not evidence that the room behind it is habitable. A loading door is not an airlock.

Do not shut down, adjust, or dismantle a pump simply to get some quiet. The original purpose of the circulation system is uncertain, not necessarily obsolete.

## Keep your body inside a controlled environment

Pack warm layers for the cabin, spare dry clothing, insulated footwear, and gloves suitable for touching cold interior surfaces. Avoid leaning bare skin against windows or hull fittings.

Your provisions should assume that nothing outside is edible or drinkable without careful treatment. Clear water is not potable water. Familiar-looking shellfish are not familiar food.

Bring a medical kit appropriate to your party and expedition duration, personal medication with a reserve supply, and supplies for managing nausea, minor injuries, and sleep disruption under medical guidance. A minor condition becomes consequential when evacuation is slow.

For any planned work outside the vehicle, use equipment specifically certified for the local pressure and temperature, with trained support personnel. A conventional wetsuit, drysuit, or scuba set does not make this an ordinary dive site.

The safest first visit involves no exposure of your body to the sea at all.

## Assume life is present where the rock looks bare

Do not touch an apparent mineral patch until you have established that it is mineral.

The soft-bodied hunters make that rule obvious, but it applies more broadly. Seams, hinges, sheltered cracks, and intake collars are living habitat. A place that appears empty at landscape scale may be crowded at arm’s length.

Stay clear of resting animals and do not feed the transparent swimmers to bring them closer. Their teeth do not establish that they are dangerous to humans, but unfamiliar behavior and an uncertain sense of scale are enough reason not to experiment.

Remember that your vehicle is broadcasting through vibration. Thruster changes, manipulator contact, and impacts against rock may be more conspicuous to local animals than your lights.

Use slow, deliberate movements near habitat—but not because the fauna must be harmless or incapable of sudden action. Their ordinary thrift tells you little about their response to being trapped, struck, or approached.

Clean external equipment before arrival and after departure. Avoid moving organisms between pumping stations, and especially between universes. Do not take biological samples casually.

## Prepare to watch slowly

This is not a place where a longer checklist will make every hour feel comfortable.

The first impression may be astonishing freedom: mountains visible across vast, clear spaces, with no fog or weather to conceal them. Then you will notice that you cannot open a window, cannot step outside, cannot find silence, and cannot judge the drop beneath you with your eyes.

Build your itinerary around that contradiction.

Keep the first trip short, with a reliable refuge nearby. Travel with companions whose judgment you trust and with whom you can share a confined cabin without needing constant conversation. Allow unstructured observation time. Do not fill every day with crossings and inspections.

Some of the most important things here will only become visible after you stop expecting an empty landscape to remain empty: another swimmer arriving from the distance, a cliff patch loosening its hold, animals turning together into a current you cannot see.

The best preparation is a quiet competence inside the vehicle: warm hands, rested people, secured equipment, checked instruments, and a known way home.

Then you can sit beside a narrow summit and look outward without having to mistake beauty for safety.
