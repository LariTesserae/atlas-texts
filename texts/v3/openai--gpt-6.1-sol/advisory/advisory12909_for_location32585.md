Prepare for this place as you would for a high-altitude desert crossed with a slowly moving ice field—but do not let either comparison persuade you that you understand it.

The plateau’s greatest danger is that it looks walkable. There is daylight, breathable air, a mostly level surface, and occasional buildings. An ordinary traveler could mistake it for difficult but manageable country. In fact, the ground, the weather, and even the direction of weight near some structures cannot be taken for granted.

Your first visit should be short, close to a reliable means of departure, and finished well before evening.

## Before arrival

**Find out whether your exit depends on the ground staying where it is.** This matters more than almost any other detail.

A return gate placed on a mineral plate may move with it. A device that requires a flat surface could become unusable overnight. A route back to fixed coordinates might lead to an open seam rather than the arrival site.

Ideally, bring a tested means of extraction that works without local infrastructure and tolerates changes in elevation, orientation, and footing. Establish its limits before visiting. If you must return to a fixed point, keep the first excursion within easy reach of it and have an independent contingency. A painted mark on the stone is not enough.

Travel with a companion. Two people can check one another for altitude impairment, watch different parts of the ground, and assist after a fall. But the companion should not be your only backup: a shifting seam or dust front can incapacitate both people at once.

Do not start with a journey between shelters. Buildings separated by days of travel are destinations for an expedition, not convenient stopping points for a visitor.

## Dress for cold shade, strong sunlight, and abrasion

The air being breathable does not mean it will support your usual pace. Arrive rested, move slowly, and allow time to learn how your body responds. If acclimatization is possible, do it beforehand.

Bring supplemental oxygen with enough reserve for a delayed departure. A particle respirator protects against dust; it does not compensate for thin air. Headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, dizziness, or poor judgment should end the excursion rather than become something you try to walk off.

Your clothing needs to handle two different environments at once: solar warmth on exposed surfaces and persistent cold everywhere else.

Useful equipment includes:

- A wind-resistant, abrasion-resistant outer layer.
- Insulating layers that can be adjusted without exposing much skin.
- Close-fitting gloves suitable for handling equipment.
- Fully enclosed boots with strong soles, secure closures, and good traction on smooth stone.
- Sealed protective goggles, plus appropriate sun protection.
- A well-fitted particulate respirator with spare filters.
- A close-fitting hood or head covering that protects the neck and ears.

Tuck away every loose strap. Cover vulnerable buckles and closures where practical. Avoid garments with open pockets, broad cuffs, or trailing cords.

The near-ground air can be violent while the air around your face seems calm. Your ankles and boots may receive much more abrasion than your upper body. Inspect them during the visit, not just afterward.

If using oxygen equipment, make sure the dust protection and oxygen delivery are compatible. This is not a place to discover that two essential pieces of equipment cannot be worn together.

## Bring all your water

There is no reason to expect usable surface water here. No frost, no damp ground, and clouds far below you mean that familiar methods of finding water are unlikely to help.

Carry drinking water, a delay reserve, and protection against freezing where temperatures warrant it. Dry air and exertion can dehydrate you without much visible sweat. Pack food that you can eat with gloved hands and minimal preparation.

Do not plan to extract moisture from the plant colonies. Their ability to survive on traces of vapor says nothing about whether a traveler can do the same.

For a first visit, supplies should support an unexpected overnight delay, even though your intended stay is only a few hours.

## Learn to watch the ground, not just the horizon

On most worlds, approaching weather announces itself overhead. Here, much of it occurs below your knees.

Before walking, spend time observing from a known, checked position. Watch individual dust sheets, their edges, and places where they stop. Notice whether dust is collecting, stripping away, or beginning to turn.

Changes worth treating as warnings include:

- Dust moving toward a depression from several directions.
- A sharp dust boundary drifting across your intended route.
- Grains beginning to spiral or jump ahead of a moving sheet.
- A tremor felt through the soles.
- A seam exposing fresh dark material.
- Low ridges shedding grit.
- Several patches of dust lifting at once.

These observations will not let you predict everything. They can help you decide to retreat early rather than wait for certainty.

Do not assume that a groove is safe because an animal uses one. The animal can flatten its body against the rock and may sense changes you cannot. A human crouched in the same groove could be exposed to concentrated grit or movement at a plate boundary.

Likewise, do not lie down automatically when a current arrives. The most aggressive flow may be precisely where you would put your face. Prefer an already inspected refuge over an improvised hollow.

## Treat every seam as active

The plate movements are usually small, but a hand’s breadth is enough to crush fingers, trap a boot, or remove the support beneath a foot.

Never put your hand into a fissure to steady yourself. Do not sit with your legs across a boundary. Do not leave equipment straddling one. A seam that is closed now is not necessarily solid ground.

Favor routes across broad plate interiors where possible. When a crossing is unavoidable, inspect it, keep the crossing brief, and avoid having both travelers exposed simultaneously. A walking pole may help test reach and balance, but it should not be wedged into a gap or trusted to prove that the surrounding sheet is supported.

Keep well away from:

- The great escarpment.
- Openings that reveal suspended sheets beneath.
- Enormous shallow blisters.
- Broken shelters riding raised ground.
- Fresh displacement around a building’s threshold.

The rim deserves a much wider margin than its appearance suggests. A clear view downward offers no useful assurance about support beneath your feet. Neither does the absence of sound from falling stones.

Do not anchor a safety rope casually to a neighboring plate or ancient structure. An anchor point that moves independently can turn the rope itself into a hazard.

## Regard shelters as equipment of unknown condition

An intact ceramic shelter may be your best available refuge. It is still not a guarantee.

Approach in daylight and inspect the surrounding plates, threshold, walls, and roof. Look for displacement, cracks, raised ground, or openings near the foundation. Avoid buildings associated with obvious blistering or exposed cavities.

Inside, move slowly until you understand the local pull. A hanging thread or a controlled observation of a small object may reveal that weight behaves differently near a wall, but it will not tell you whether the anchor is healthy.

Secure loose objects. Keep essential gear close. Do not experiment by jumping, leaning into an anomalous pull, or reaching into a wall hollow. An apparently empty bowl may be part of a mechanism you cannot recognize.

Above all, **do not try to repair or activate an anchor**. It is acting on deep rock and supporting something larger than the room you can see. Familiar-looking architecture does not imply familiar engineering.

If you shelter overnight, reassess the doorway before leaving. Yesterday’s step may face a new seam. Yesterday’s approach may no longer exist.

## Plan around evening

The warm evening light is misleading. It does not mean warmer air, and it coincides with the onset of the largest disturbances.

Set your turnaround time generously. Reach your exit or inspected refuge while there is still ample daylight, before widespread cooling begins. Do not schedule “one more hour” because the route looks short.

If unexpectedly caught outside, stop making distance your primary goal. Look for previously inspected shelter, avoid seams and depressions, protect your eyes and breathing, and keep your departure equipment accessible. Do not chase a distant building across a field that has begun to shiver.

Sleep, if unavoidable, should not be directly on exposed ground. You need insulation, respiratory protection available at hand, and an exit plan that does not depend on yesterday’s arrangement of the plates.

## Leave the life alone

The gray cushions are not ordinary ground cover. A hand-sized patch may represent an immense span of growth, and its roots help hold a fissure together.

Do not step on colonies, peel them from the stone, or disturb the dust crescents around them. Give moving animals room to reach shelter. Their behavior may offer useful warnings, but they should not be used as test subjects or treated as infallible guides.

Photograph rather than collect. Keep foreign soil, seeds, food scraps, and wastewater out of the cracks.

## The right state of mind

You will need to resist three impressions: that level ground is stable, that silence means calm, and that a familiar route remains familiar.

Take frequent pauses. Keep a conservative walking pace. Maintain enough reserve that turning back is easy. Navigation should use your own tested instruments and multiple references, not tracks, dust patterns, or a single colored band in the stone.

A successful first visit might be no more than standing near your arrival point, watching one dust sheet cross another, examining a colony without touching it, and returning before the light changes.

That is not a lesser experience. It is the appropriate scale for an ordinary human entering a landscape where immense motion gives almost no warning—and almost no sound.
