Prepare for this hollow as you would for a hot volcanic chamber that is also entirely occupied by living things. The difficulty is not just that something dangerous might be hiding there. It is that **there may be nowhere inside that an ordinary human can safely stand, touch, or rest.**

The mountain outside looks empty; the chamber is anything but. Your preparations should let you remain independent of both.

## Plan a short visit, not an expedition camp

Establish your refuge well outside the hollow, on surveyed, stable ground away from the entrance, vents, and loose summit rock. Do not assume the old cut step is a safe place to wait. It is a threshold used by animals, exposed to hot wind, and possibly part of something whose original purpose you do not know.

Your refuge needs breathable air, cooling, water, communications, and a reliable route home. If multiverse travel permits a return portal or retrieval device, test it before approaching and keep its destination outside the chamber. A device that returns you to the entrance is not much comfort if the entrance has become impassable.

Have someone remain outside. Agree on a firm return time and an extraction procedure that does not require them to walk blindly into the same danger.

Bring everything you need to leave. There is no useful water here, and no reason to expect food, shelter, or breathable air merely because animals thrive.

## Protect yourself from the air and the heat

The hollow’s atmosphere is the first thing to investigate from a distance.

Volcanic gases may be poisonous, corrosive, or deficient in oxygen. They need not smell, and the description gives no basis for identifying them. Thin air adds another problem: an atmosphere can contain oxygen and still supply too little for a human.

For an initial visit, use:

- **An independent breathing supply**, rather than a filtering mask.
- **A sealed protective suit** compatible with the measured gases and mineral dust.
- **Active cooling**, with enough reserve for a delayed exit.
- **Pressure support if atmospheric measurements require it.**
- External sensors for temperature, pressure, oxygen, and hazardous gases.

Do not confuse heat-resistant clothing with cooling. Insulation can delay heat reaching you, but your body and equipment also generate heat. In a hot chamber, a suit can become a trap unless it has somewhere to put that heat.

Carry drinking water in a closed system you can use without opening your protection. Budget your visit by whichever resource runs out first—cooling, breathing supply, battery power, or your own endurance—not by how interesting the chamber becomes.

The animals’ presence is not evidence of human safety. They may live on substances that would disable you within minutes.

## Learn where the rock actually is

From the entrance, much of what looks like geology is alive. A dark projection may be a folded limb. Gravel may scatter. A wall base may open and swallow the debris beside it.

Before entry, map the chamber with passive thermal imaging and other low-disturbance instruments. These will help, but none should be treated as infallible: animals adapted to resting against hot stone may resemble it in temperature as well as appearance.

Avoid probing surfaces with your hands or a walking stick. A probe might identify an animal only by hurting it, provoking it, or pushing through a crust into a vent.

Your first choice should be **remote observation from outside**. If physical entry is necessary, make it conditional on finding a verified route that does not require crossing living surfaces. A remotely positioned walkway might be possible, but only if its supports can be placed on confirmed stable rock without crushing inhabitants or blocking their movement. Overhead suspension is not automatically safer; the ceiling is occupied, fractured, and subject to shedding stone.

If no suitable route exists, accept that the chamber is not walkable for you. That is a legitimate result of reconnaissance.

Ordinary boots will not solve this. They protect your feet while transferring your weight onto whatever lies beneath them.

## Keep your light to yourself

Much of this community withdraws from faint natural light. A powerful headlamp could clear a patch that appears convenient to a visitor while displacing thousands of animals into neighboring bodies, crevices, or hotter ground.

Start with passive observation. If illumination is necessary, use the lowest effective output, narrowly directed, for the shortest possible time. Do not assume red or infrared light is invisible or harmless to these animals; their sensory biology is unknown.

Avoid flash photography.

Do not deliberately use light to make a walking path. Apart from the disturbance, a suddenly bare surface may still be unsafe. It could expose a hot crack, fragile crust, or the resting place of something larger that has not moved.

Expect the darkness to remain difficult. Your equipment should help you navigate without requiring you to turn the whole hollow into a lit room.

## Treat the mountain’s knock as an exit signal

The most important warning is not a predator. It is the coordinated response to heat rising from below.

Watch for:

- Animals flattening near vents.
- Small beadlike creatures releasing their hold and climbing.
- Grazers lifting and following them.
- Movement spreading across several surfaces.
- A muted knock beneath the floor.
- Changes in measured gas output or temperature.

**Do not wait for the rearrangement to finish before deciding whether to leave.** The animals sense conditions through the mountain in ways you cannot. Their departure may be your earliest warning of a thermal or gas pulse.

Preselect an exit route that remains usable during animal movement. Do not follow creatures deeper inside on the assumption that they are leading you to safety; their refuge may be lethal to humans.

During a migration, avoid sudden movements and do not step into a briefly exposed patch simply because it has become empty. Empty here may mean intolerably hot.

If your way out becomes obstructed, your retrieval plan must work without requiring you to force a passage through the moving community.

## Keep away from the debris banks and old enclosure

The fine material along the walls is not a comfortable place to kneel. It contains animals, shed structures, mineral dust, and at least one enormous occupant beneath it. Its feeding is slow, but slowness is not safety: a sinking bank could take your footing before you understand what is happening.

Give those banks a generous margin. Do not test their depth.

Treat the old slab structure as both occupied habitat and a possible archaeological site. Do not crawl into it, move its roof, or use the large limb across its opening as a bridge. The smaller inhabitants can cross that limb because they belong to this traffic. You do not.

The cut steps establish that someone once shaped the rock. They do not establish that those builders were human-sized, breathed your atmosphere, or used the enclosure as shelter.

## Arrive clean, leave contained

There is no reason to assume alien life will infect a human. There is equally no reason to assume it cannot irritate, poison, adhere to, or damage human tissues and equipment.

Use smooth, sealed outer surfaces with few exposed joints or fabric folds. Small creatures could enter a boot seam or suit articulation without attacking you at all. Your equipment is another surface, and usable surfaces are scarce here.

Do not bring exposed food, shed fibers, leave water, or collect specimens casually. A spill could alter this exceptionally dry habitat. A bright lamp, warm battery housing, or dropped object might become a new gathering place.

After leaving, inspect and decontaminate equipment in a contained area. Do not brush dust and possible passengers into your refuge or carry them directly into another world. Build that time into the visit; it is part of getting home.

## What to expect emotionally

This place may feel calm enough to encourage mistakes.

There are no roars, warning calls, or dramatic rushes. Predation is almost silent. Geological change announces itself with one subdued knock. Even the largest animal may never look recognizably animal to you.

You will probably want to pause, lean against a wall, crouch beside the capsules, or put a hand on the old step to steady yourself. Prepare against those ordinary reflexes. Practice moving in your equipment without using the surroundings for balance.

The best first visit is likely a brief, quiet observation from the threshold, followed by a retreat while all your reserves are still comfortable. You need not reach the center to understand the hollow.

Go prepared to discover that the safest—and most respectful—way to visit is to remain just outside, watching a place where every apparent vacancy already belongs to something approaching it.
