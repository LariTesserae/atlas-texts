Prepare for this town as you would for a quiet, water-conscious industrial settlement in a cool climate—with one important adjustment: **the buildings, streets, and water infrastructure are living things, and almost every surface belongs to a carefully maintained system.**

The town does not sound immediately dangerous. It sounds habitable, patient, and short of spare capacity. Your main task would be to avoid mistaking something alive and functional for something inert and available.

## Before you arrive

Arrange lodging in an occupied house, preferably with someone accustomed to visitors. An empty building is not necessarily available shelter. It may be awaiting repair, being allowed to contract, or already scheduled to become a planting bed. The workers lowering a roof are not demolishing an ordinary abandoned property; they are withdrawing resources from a structure the town can no longer afford to sustain.

If you can contact a host beforehand, ask:

- Where do visitors obtain drinking water?
- Are there restrictions on washing or laundry?
- Which roads are undergoing growth correction or repairs?
- Are there local rules about bringing in seeds, soil, untreated wood, or plant fibers?
- Does your accommodation have heating, and how cold does it become overnight?

For an ordinary human arriving from another universe, confirm the basic things your transit service should establish: breathable air, tolerable gravity, safe food, and whether local organisms pose any unfamiliar health risks. Nothing in the description suggests that the town is hostile to human life, but familiar-looking vegetation is not proof of familiar biology.

## Pack for cold moisture, not heavy rain

The most persistent discomfort is likely to be **damp cold**.

The town sits where cold air gathers after sunset. You may arrive on a bright afternoon, see dry roofs and pale walls, and underestimate the night. By evening, exposed metal will be cold, condensation will be forming, and sheltered passages may already feel clammy.

Bring:

- Layered clothing, including a warm insulating layer.
- A light windproof, water-resistant outer layer.
- Spare socks.
- Walking shoes with good wet-surface traction.
- A small towel or cloth for drying railings, bicycle seats, and your belongings.
- A compact flashlight for early winter darkness.

You probably do not need expedition-grade rainwear. You do need clothing that remains comfortable through several hours of mist and condensation.

Keep luggage raised off damp floors or walls where possible. A modest waterproof bag liner would be more useful than a large umbrella.

## Learn how to walk on the streets

Rootwood paving will not behave exactly like stone, asphalt, or a wooden boardwalk.

It gives slightly in the morning and becomes firmer later. Its central walking surfaces are polished smooth. At intersections, the grain curls around drains and lamp posts, and local swelling can interfere with wheels.

Walk slowly at first, particularly:

- Early in the morning.
- Around drains.
- Beneath bridges and in shaded doorways.
- Where the paving changes direction.
- Where a joint has risen above its neighbors.

A little flexibility underfoot appears normal. You do not need to treat every yielding plate as a collapse hazard. But an unusually soft patch, especially near stored fibers or industrial waste, is worth reporting rather than investigating yourself.

Avoid narrow heels, hard slick soles, and dragging heavy luggage across the seams. A backpack or a bag you can carry is likely easier than a small-wheeled suitcase.

If you rent a bicycle, follow the locals’ slow pace. The cyclist passing carefully near a swollen drain is giving you useful information about the roads.

## Be conservative with water

Water is the town’s central constraint, even though moisture seems to be everywhere.

The wet railing, the droplets under the eaves, and the tapping inside a collector tree are signs of a working collection system—not signs of abundant disposable water. The town is already reducing the size of unused houses to lower demand.

Bring a refillable bottle and fill it at an approved source. Do not assume that canal water, collected roof moisture, or water stored in a hollow trunk is potable. Clear-looking water may still be untreated, and an accessible outlet may belong to irrigation or manufacturing rather than drinking supply.

Expect to ask before:

- Taking a long shower.
- Washing clothes.
- Emptying a basin.
- Using water to clean equipment.

Also ask where wastewater belongs. The streets contain shared roots and hollow conduits; pouring soap, oil, alcohol, or cleaning chemicals into a drain could affect much more than the patch beneath your feet.

Clean your footwear and equipment before arrival, ideally somewhere with proper disposal facilities. Removing foreign soil and seeds is a sensible precaution in a settlement whose infrastructure is biological.

## Treat the walls as infrastructure, not scenery

Do not peel bark, collect leaves, carve initials, drive in a hook, or pull at a visible root.

A fan of leaves along a roof edge may be helping support that building’s water and energy needs. A shallow groove in a wall may carry collected moisture. The carefully cut border around a junction box is probably the result of repeated maintenance.

Even apparently untidy details can have a purpose. Do not “fix” a cable groove, clear a channel, or scrape a growth away without being asked.

Inside your room, use existing hooks, shelves, and fittings. Ask before placing a heater against a wall or fastening anything to it. The walls can take screws, but that does not mean visitors are welcome to install them.

There is no indication that a house will close around you overnight or that the streets will seize your feet. Growth here is slow. The relevant danger is **ordinary damage accumulating in an extraordinary material**, not sudden botanical aggression.

## Be careful with wood and fiber purchases

The local boards and cordage would make appealing souvenirs. Buy them from someone who understands export and storage.

For pressed boards intended to remain inert, look for both required stamps, including the second mark: a small square without a center. An unstamped or incompletely rested board may still try to grow into whatever surrounds it.

Do not accept a fragrant fresh offcut as harmless scrap without asking whether it is fully inactive. Store purchases dry and separated from damp surfaces.

Your home universe may also have restrictions on importing living material. A piece that counts locally as “timber” might still count elsewhere as a biological specimen.

The same caution applies to loose fibers. Do not take material from the freight sheds or factory waste heaps simply because it appears discarded.

## Visit the factories by invitation

These factories are quiet, but they are still factories.

The cordage works contains combs, rollers, and winding machinery. Slow machinery can still catch clothing or fingers. Tie back long hair, secure loose scarves, and keep away from moving material.

Do not reach for branches entering through the wall slots. They may look like vegetation casually intruding into a building, but they are part of the production process.

Learn the local signals. A shift ends when roof vents open, not when a bell rings. Leaves fluttering over a roof can indicate machinery operating below. The quieter nighttime setting does not mean the equipment has stopped.

A guided visit would probably reveal more than wandering around the doors, and it would spare you the mistake of standing in a working space because it does not sound busy.

## Adjust to a quieter kind of company

The square is open, people are widely spaced, and work proceeds without much noise. That does not necessarily mean the inhabitants are unfriendly or the town is deserted.

You may simply be visiting a place where activity is distributed across slow processes and small maintenance tasks.

Offer a greeting. Ask before photographing people at work or entering the sheltered freight areas. Avoid broadcasting music into the square. You do not need to whisper, but let the local sound level guide yours.

If you want to help, ask for a task rather than inventing one. An outsider might see weeds around the municipal scale and decide to tidy them away. Those few weeds could be tolerated, valued, or merely none of your business.

## Allow more time than you think you need

This is not a destination best understood through a rapid circuit of landmarks.

Stay through at least one full evening and morning if you can. The afternoon town—with dry roofs, stiff paving, and sharp shadows—is only half the place. At dusk the cold settles, vents close, and collector leaves begin lifting. In the morning, surfaces are wet and the streets have a different feel underfoot.

Bring something to read, a notebook, or simply a willingness to sit without expecting a spectacle. Nothing grows fast enough to astonish you while you watch. What becomes striking is how much is happening without conspicuous motion.

**The best preparation is to arrive warm, travel lightly, use water sparingly, and ask before altering anything.** You are visiting a town that has learned to make living material hold still. Being a good guest means respecting the work required to keep it that way.
