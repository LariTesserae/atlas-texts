It lives beneath the lowest cushion of moss, where the bank meets the pool. At low water, most of it is out of sight. What shows is a strip of pale flesh following the edge of the moss, no thicker than the lip of a mushroom. There are little notches along it. Water collects in the notches and occasionally spills over.

You might take it for the underside of a leaf that has rotted without quite coming apart.

Then one of the notches closes.

The animal is broader than that first visible strip suggests. Its body extends beneath the moss and around two exposed roots, folded into spaces too shallow for a beetle. There is no good place from which to see all of it. Pulling up the moss would lift part of the animal with it, and leave other parts attached to the clay.

It has a skin thin enough to show the darker workings underneath. In the light nearest the entrance, these look like lengths of wet thread. Some shorten when it moves. Others carry a cloudy fluid that pauses, reverses, and travels on. Along the outer edge are small, firmer pieces, brown and translucent, overlapping like the scales on an unopened bud. They are not quite a shell. Each can move independently, and between them the flesh remains exposed.

Fine root hairs have grown into those gaps.

The animal tolerates this. It lives long enough in one position to become difficult to separate from what shelters it.

At the pool’s edge it puts out a number of narrow appendages. They are easily mistaken for dead moss stems until they bend against the movement of the water. Each ends in a shallow cup fringed with hairs. A cup can close around a grain of silt, hold it for several seconds, and discard it. Sometimes it retains something. Then a dark speck travels slowly up the appendage, disappearing beneath the bank.

It feeds on very little: the soft film on submerged leaves, damaged eggs, fragments loosened from a shrimp’s moult. A whole dead mite is enough to occupy one cup for a long time. Other appendages continue searching while it works.

The living shrimps do not seem afraid of it. They pick at its edge as they pick at the moss. When one steps onto a sensitive place, the animal draws that place inward, and the shrimp is left hovering over a small vacancy. It returns almost immediately.

There is a mouth, but it is not on the side facing the pool. It lies against the clay, sheltered in a fold, and can be moved to any part of the bank the body can reach. Most of what the cups gather is passed underneath. You see this only when the animal shifts: a brief rolling movement, a pale groove opening and closing, then the moss settling again.

It moves most often after the tide has covered it.

As the salt water rises, the body loosens its grip on the roots. The edge lifts first. Beneath it are rows of short, blunt feet, so close together that their movement resembles a slow shiver. They do not carry the animal forward so much as pass its weight from one point to another. A fold slides out from under a root. Another fold takes its place. Over an hour, it may move the length of a fern frond.

Its old position remains visible as a clean patch of clay.

Under water, it is less flat. Fluid enters chambers along its body, separating layers that had lain pressed together in the air. The cups lengthen. The overlapping brown pieces stand slightly apart. Small pockets of air, caught beneath the moss, lodge against its back.

It keeps some of these.

Along its upper surface are soft depressions that close around a bubble without crushing it. While the bank is submerged, the bubbles grow smaller. By the time the tide turns, they have become bright pinpoints. The animal carries them into the newly exposed moss and opens the depressions there.

It does not swim into the middle of the hollow. When a section of bank falls away beneath it, it can hang in the water for a few moments, spread broad and almost transparent. Its feet search without finding anything. The cups withdraw. Then the whole body contracts toward whatever still has a hold.

Once, this leaves it attached by a single strip wound around a root.

For several minutes the rest of it hangs below, turning slightly in the draining pool. It looks much larger like this. There are parts of it that ordinarily remain folded twice over, delicate seams that have never been exposed to the light at the entrance. One seam opens far enough to reveal a row of opaque beads.

These are its young.

They develop in pockets inside the folds, each already flattened, each with a few minute cups crowded along one edge. When released, they creep upwards rather than towards the water. Most disappear among the root hairs. Some spend their first days on the underside of a fern, occupying the wet interval between two veins.

A young one can live on the material collected in a single hanging drop. It holds the leaf with its feet and lowers its cups into the water. When the drop falls, it remains in place, its appendages drawn close against its body until another forms.

Many are lost when the leaves go under. Others are carried out through the buried passages with the ebb. There may be animals like it elsewhere along the estuary, in dark seams that have not yet opened.

This one was here before the roof broke.

Its oldest plates are blackened at the edges. Embedded in them are grains of clay from farther inside the shelf, finer and darker than the fresh fallen sediment. Until the collapse, it must have occupied a narrower cavity, fed by seepage and washed by the tide through channels scarcely wider than its extended cups. The room has opened around it. Light now reaches parts of its body that developed in darkness.

Those parts withdraw more slowly than the rest.

It can feel the machines through its feet. When the distant vibration begins, its cups pause, then resume. A change in pitch makes the body tighten against the bank. The firmer pieces along its edge are drawn flat. Beneath the moss, the feet move towards the roots.

Usually nothing falls.

Sometimes a plate of clay chatters overhead, and the animal is already holding in three places when it lets go.

It cannot leave the hollow quickly. It has no single movement that would take it away from a collapsing wall. What it can do is distribute its hold, keeping some feet on clay, some on moss, some curled around wood. It spends much of its life making these small adjustments. Even while feeding, one hidden portion of it is testing the bank.

When the tide drains and the sucking sound begins, it folds its cups tightly shut. The water draws at its edges. Loose leaves slide past. A shrimp is carried backwards, catches a grass blade, and clings.

The animal remains where it is.

Afterwards it opens slowly. First a cup near the entrance, then two beneath the fern. A thread of cloudy fluid travels through the pale rim. The moss above it settles by a fraction.

By the time the pool is still, it is again difficult to see.
