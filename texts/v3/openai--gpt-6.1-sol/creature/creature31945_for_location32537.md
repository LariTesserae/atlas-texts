The smallest inhabitants have to be distinguished from the surface they are feeding on.

A grayness gathers where a wet stone enters the water. Under a lens it resolves into overlapping bodies, each with a hard central grain and a transparent margin that spreads against the rock. There are no legs at this size. The margin loosens on one side and grips on the other, and the animal advances by something like the movement of a blister.

Where current passes, it stops. A faint branching mark appears inside it, persists for several seconds, and fades. The creature grows a little wider.

These animals do not eat the stone. They eat the brief difference between one part of the stone and another: the discharge where ice forms, the leakage along a metallic seam, the small surplus released when a loaded plate comes to rest. A good feeding place may be no larger than the head of a pin. Thousands crowd around it, overlapping until the lower animals can no longer move.

The lowest continue feeding. The uppermost begin to develop legs.

There is no clear division between the gray felt and the smallest of the rubble animals. A grain acquires a seam. The seam opens underneath. Several threads emerge and harden, and what had spread flat against the rock stands a little above it.

For a while it carries its transparent feeding margin draped around its legs. Then another animal eats the margin, or it wears away, leaving a dull, rounded body with an unexpectedly firm grip.

Some never stand. Some stand once, cross a few inches of stone, and flatten again. Others keep their legs.

Those that keep them are the animals that make the banks.

Their backs are not single shells. They consist of many thin layers, laid down at different rates and under different pressures, all worn flush at the edges. A fresh layer is almost white. It darkens within a few hours, except beneath another animal, where it can remain pale for years. When a colony opens, these sheltered patches show briefly in the gaps, like the inside of something broken.

The legs are crowded under the body. An animal may have six on one side and nine on the other. New ones grow wherever the underside has spent long enough bearing weight. Unused ones diminish, leaving shallow pits. A creature taken from the top of a bank and placed at its bottom will gradually become broader and more heavily jointed.

Their mouths are tucked among these changing supports. The mouthparts rotate, but not always in the same direction. They scrape electrical grazers from the rock, gather precipitated salts, and wind loose fibers out of the water. Sometimes two animals settle mouth to mouth and remain there through several floods.

When they separate, one may be visibly thinner.

There are no recognizable males or females. Parts pass between them. A set of mouthparts, a wet packet of undeveloped legs, a strip of tissue bearing the branching organs that receive current: any of these may be transferred. An injured animal can recover what it has lost by settling against its neighbors. A healthy one may give so much away that its shell drops onto the stone empty.

The empty shells remain useful. Small animals shelter beneath them. Larger ones wear them as additional layers, holding them with legs grown expressly for the purpose. In a crowded bank, a back that appears unusually high may belong to one creature carrying the remains of five others.

Their sociability is mostly a matter of load.

An individual displaced from a colony turns until it finds something to lean against. It will brace on a stone, a boot, another animal of any size. Left alone on level ground, it keeps changing its stance. Eventually it lowers itself and begins laying down a thicker shell.

Within the colony, every movement is answered. A raised body transfers weight to its neighbors; they rise or settle; the change travels outward. The ripple that lifts hundreds of animals is often begun by a single mouth finding a rich seam below them.

The deep colonies have inhabitants that never reach the surface.

They are pale, flattened, soft along their backs. Their legs reach upward as well as down. They feed in the water trapped between layers and hold the animals above them apart just far enough for a current to pass. If lifted into the open, they slowly curl around whatever last touched them.

They cannot tolerate rain. Each drop leaves a dark mark that spreads through their skin.

A colony disturbed too deeply closes over them at once.

The long swimmers hunt along the colony’s submerged edges. Their bodies are made of narrow segments, each slightly turned relative to the next, so that an animal stretched straight is also faintly twisted. They pass through shallow channels on their sides. Along one edge runs a fringe of sensory hairs; along the other, small hooked limbs work against the bottom.

Only the front end swims freely. The rest follows whatever path it has opened.

They can detect a feeding mouth through several layers of shell. Before striking, they lie with their hairs extended across the silt, waiting for the rotations to fall into a pattern. A colony under attack becomes irregular. Mouths stop and start. Legs tap out of sequence. The swimmers wait longer.

The sudden rigidity of the water is part of the capture.

It begins at the sensory fringe and passes outward in a clear, shallow lens. Within it, the prey cannot unfold its legs or close its shell. The hunter itself moves slowly, forcing each segment through the arrested water. Its hooked limbs fold the victim beneath its body.

This is costly. Afterward, the swimmer lies almost motionless while ice grows thick along its fringe. Small grazers gather on it. Occasionally they cover it completely, and by the next flood there is only a low gray patch where the predator had been.

The basin animals are less easily overwhelmed.

They are much narrower than their visible outlines suggest. Most of the bulk is a framework holding channels open: ribs, mineral partitions, hollow braces thickened by years of sediment. The living tissue lies in long strips inside this framework. Water runs past it continually.

Their legs end in spreading fans under the mud. During each feeding stroke the fans pull inward, raising a pulse through the basin floor. Then they open again, finding fresh purchase. An animal can move for decades without lifting a foot clear of the sediment.

The clean water leaving its rear vents carries tiny, soft versions of the same creature.

These young resemble scraps of soaked cloth. They drift until they catch on a submerged ridge, where they begin filtering. Most are eaten. Those that survive stiffen around the ridge and grow a set of channels, then another. For a long time their bodies belong partly to the ground.

Their first journey begins when the ridge wears through.

A young basin animal may drag a piece of rock behind it for several seasons. Others never free themselves. They remain fixed at the mouths of channels, growing vents upstream and downstream as the water changes direction. Around them, sediment accumulates in bands. Smaller creatures occupy the bands. Eventually the fixed animal is difficult to distinguish from one of the plain’s hydraulic beds, except that its openings close when a swimmer passes.

The traveling silent patches have inhabitants too, though they are seen poorly.

At a leading edge, the films across the water draw taut. A slight crease advances over a pool. Beneath it, something with a long, soft underside keeps contact with the surface, even where that surface is broken by stones.

Nothing protrudes above it. Rain enters small hollows and leaves again behind the crease.

A shell animal caught at the edge does not appear to be crushed. It loses its footing. Its legs make several uncoordinated movements, and then it is carried a little way, suspended beneath the advancing film. Most are released in the warm wake. A few come out without the thin membranes that line their joints.

For several days afterward, those animals move in complete silence, even on loose stone.

The basin creatures stop when a patch passes over them because their vents must remain open. Whatever moves in the film crosses their backs slowly, lingering at each opening. When it has gone, the filter-creatures expel a dark clot from their rear channels before they resume feeding.

In the wake, small animals gather so quickly that the warmer stone is seldom visible. They graze something deposited there: a colorless coating that draws into threads when touched.

Farther toward the white flats, the animals are harder to follow.

A track may continue beyond its maker. Shell flakes washed from a creature’s mouth may gather ahead of it, where they will only later be bitten free. A pale animal visible under one pool can also be found beneath another, half a mile away, making the same slow movement with the same missing limb.

The smooth bodies occasionally carried out of the flats have no obvious way to feed. Water does not cling to them. Mud slips off without leaving a stain. They remain among the banks for a little while, and the other animals shift around them with unusual care.

They do not seem to press down when they sink. The rock keeps its surface. For a moment the pale shape is visible inside it, shallow enough that it ought to be exposed.

Then it is gone.

Sometimes, hours later, a feeding seam brightens nearby. The smallest grazers gather along it. Their transparent margins overlap, their bodies widen, and the first of them begins to lift itself.

There are lives here lasting less than a flood, and lives extending through the wearing away of a basin. Between them are all the crowded creatures that can still be followed: a swimmer resting under its coat of grazers, a pale load-bearer opening a passage inside a bank, a filter animal pulling one buried foot free of a stone it has outgrown.

When the ground rises, they respond at different speeds.

The basin animals halt. The colonies brace. Deep beneath their neighbors, the pale ones spread their legs to keep the water passages open. Along the seams, the smallest bodies brighten internally.

One newly legged creature is lifted from the stone by the first running water. It turns twice, catches the edge of an empty shell, and holds there while the flood passes over it.

Afterward it loosens one leg at a time. It tests the wet surface beneath it, lowers its mouth, and begins to feed.
