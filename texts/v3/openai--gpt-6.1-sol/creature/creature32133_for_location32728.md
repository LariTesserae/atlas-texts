The largest inhabitants are easiest to find by looking for places where the dust is missing.

A pale sweep along the foot of a wall. A clean interval beneath a lintel. Two shallow grooves running through a room that otherwise seems untouched. These marks do not lead toward the brighter streets. They turn into passages whose roofs have come down too low for you to follow upright.

At first, the animal at the end of such a passage looks like another fallen slab.

Its back is broad and slightly domed, covered in overlapping plates the color of old limestone. Dust lies in the seams. There are darker patches where it has rubbed against a doorway, and a fine, irregular scoring where small claws have crossed it. A beetle walks over the whole surface without changing direction.

Then the slab draws a breath.

The plates separate almost imperceptibly along its sides. Under them is softer skin, gray and deeply folded. Its head rests flat on the floor, beyond the shelter of its back: a blunt head with a narrow mouth, two nostrils that close into creases, and eyes set beneath heavy rims. It has no visible ears. When the birds erupt somewhere overhead, however, it lifts its chin before the sound reaches you.

There are six limbs tucked beneath it. The rear four are short and powerful, with broad soles and blunt nails. The front pair are smaller. They can bear weight, but they also fold inward to hold something against the mouth.

An adult is long enough to occupy most of a doorstep. It can turn in surprisingly little space by remaining still at the center and moving its feet around itself. Going forward, it looks cumbersome until you notice that its underside never quite touches the ground. It passes over loose rubble with small adjustments, testing each stone before putting its weight there.

You may spend several minutes watching before it decides to move.

That is not because it has failed to notice you. One eye has been following you all along.

***

They live in the half-buried rooms.

Not the deepest chambers, where nothing grows and the bats hang densely from the ceilings, but the intermediate places: rooms that receive a little reflected light, passages beside rooted walls, courtyards that have become hollows in the sediment. They favor spaces with two exits, although the second may be no more than a gap beneath a displaced block.

A room occupied by them has a particular order.

The center is bare and firmly packed. Dry leaves are pushed against one wall. Larger stones lie near the entrances, often turned so that their flattest faces are uppermost. There may be a heap of empty seed cases, another of fibrous stems, and a few objects that seem to have been carried in for no immediate use: a smooth shell fragment, a piece of greenish stone, the curved neck of a broken vessel.

They do not make these objects. They find them as the city loosens.

Sometimes one becomes useful much later. A fragment placed near a threshold is worked under a rocking stone. A stem is drawn through a narrow opening to bring a fallen fruit within reach. Most remain where they were put, acquiring dust around them.

Their front feet have three thick digits and a smaller one that closes across the others. The nails are worn almost square. They can pick a seed from among gravel, though the effort takes time. They turn it, touch it to the edge of the mouth, turn it again.

Their mouths contain hard ridges rather than obvious teeth. When they eat, there is a slow grinding sound, easily lost beneath the birds.

They eat fallen leaves, swollen roots, dry fruits from the plants growing above the hollow, and the tough inner portions of the courtyard shrubs. They also eat beetles when they can catch them, especially the soft-bodied ones exposed beneath fallen material. Nothing is pursued very far.

At a feeding place, one may spend an hour stripping the dead outer leaves from a rosette. It leaves the central growth intact. Another comes behind it and searches what has been dropped.

There is rarely enough moisture in a single meal to seem sufficient. Nevertheless, their skin remains supple beneath the plates, and their eyes are clear. They waste little. At the rear of an occupied court are small, chalky deposits among the husks.

***

They are not solitary.

A room that appears to contain one may contain five. The others lie along the walls, where the curve of their backs completes the curve of the rubble. Younger animals shelter beneath the edges of adults’ plates or in recesses too small for the adults to enter.

The young are darker, their armor still thin and faintly translucent at its margins. Their feet look too large. They move in short, abrupt efforts, then flatten themselves wherever they happen to stop.

An adult lets a young one climb onto its back. It does not help, but it waits through the repeated slipping and scrabbling. Once the young one is settled, the adult begins walking again, carrying it through a doorway with so little clearance that the passenger must lower its head.

They touch often.

A forefoot rests against another animal’s neck. Two heads remain side by side on a threshold. When one returns to a room, those nearest the entrance lift their chins, and it touches each of them briefly with the underside of its mouth.

They make sounds, but these are quiet enough that you hear them chiefly through stone.

A resting animal sometimes presses its throat against the floor and produces a short pulse. Several rooms away, another raises its head. Along a buried terrace, the pulses pass slowly from chamber to chamber, with long silences between.

You cannot tell whether a returning pulse is an answer or simply another animal announcing that it is awake.

***

The birds use them.

Small ones descend onto their backs to search the seams for insects. The animals tolerate this, except near the eyes. A quick closing of the front foot sends an overbold bird back to the lintel.

Under the busiest colonies, they arrive after the birds have gone out to feed. They turn nest debris, crush empty eggshells, and nose through the darkened soil. Their passage opens little lanes among the vines. Seeds catch in the softer folds behind their legs and travel into adjoining courts.

Some plants grow chiefly along these routes.

The insects use them too. Moths rest against the sheltered sides of their plates. A particular small beetle lives among the dry material they accumulate in their rooms. When an adult rises after a long rest, several beetles may emerge from underneath it and hurry toward the warmth it has left in the floor.

A sleeping group is a small inhabited landscape.

***

They do not travel through the city at the speed of its noise.

A flock can cross three districts while one of them negotiates a stair. Yet their routes extend farther than you might expect. The same chipped plate, the same pale scar beside an eye, may appear near the descent and, much later, in a court deep beneath the rock.

They seem to remember openings that have not been used for years.

One will approach a wall, pause, and begin working at the dust beneath it. Another joins. They scrape backward with the front feet, pushing the loosened material aside with their chests. Eventually a black line appears under the lowest course of stone.

Sometimes it becomes a passage.

Sometimes they stop before it does and return to the room they already have.

After a collapse, they are among the last animals to approach. They wait until the smaller fragments have finished falling. Then they examine the edges, pressing their feet against the ground and holding still. If an occupied chamber has been closed, several may remain nearby for days, digging where the air still passes through.

Elsewhere, a newly opened room gradually becomes theirs. First there are tracks. Then the floor is cleared. Leaves accumulate along the wall, and one afternoon you find a young animal asleep in the angle between two stones.

***

Farther down, they grow larger.

Or perhaps the largest simply prefer to live farther down.

In the lowest visible district, there are polished gaps between buried walls wider than any you have seen above. The grooves in the dust are deeper. Once, beyond the broken arch, something withdraws its head from a patch of reflected light. You see the rim of one eye and a portion of the plated back, too broad to follow into the darkness.

It does not flee.

It settles, and the leaves around it become still.

After a while, a smaller animal comes out from beneath its side and crosses the enclosure. It pauses at a low wall, feels along the top with its forefeet, then climbs over.

The larger one remains where it is. Behind it, farther than the light reaches, another throat presses against the floor.

The stone under your feet gives a faint pulse.
