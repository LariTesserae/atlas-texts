At first, the only sign of them is a place where the fallen leaves have not quite settled.

The bed is deep there, caught between two roots on the sheltered side of a ridge. Its uppermost leaves lie silver-side up. Beneath them, something holds a narrow passage open—a dark seam running into the litter, too regular to be the work of the slow water. A strand of root crosses the entrance. The strand bends, although nothing has touched it.

Then a leaf lifts.

It does not lift far. Under it is a translucent fold, faintly amber at the edge, with a row of small white knots embedded in it. The fold draws back. The leaf returns almost to its former position, and the root crossing the entrance is carried a little farther into the dark.

There is an animal here, but finding its boundary takes time.

Part of it lies beneath the leaves. Part of it runs up the root as a thin, soft sleeve. Higher on the trunk, where the bark forks, it spreads into something like a loose collar, almost invisible except where sediment has settled along its upper edge. From that collar, narrow ribbons extend among the hanging roots. Some are attached. Others end freely in the water.

They are not tentacles in any hurried sense. One may remain extended for days. Another takes an hour to curl around a stem.

The animal is broad rather than bulky. Its living substance lies in sheets and folds, thicker where it grips the tree, so thin elsewhere that the silver leaves behind it remain plainly visible. Along certain seams runs a dark thread. This is what betrays it when the light changes: a fine branching line where the plants have no vein.

In the largest individuals, the line can be followed from the leaf bed almost to the crown.

They do not occupy a tree as a bird occupies a tree. They fit themselves to it.

A young one begins in a fork or beneath a ledge, scarcely longer than a finger. It has a narrow body, a dark central cord, and two transparent fins folded close along its sides. These are the animals occasionally seen passing between the trunks. They are searching for a place to stop.

They swim poorly but with great patience. A few folds carry them out of one patch of shade and into another. They may drift against a cliff and remain there, touching it with the front of the body. If the stone is too bare, they release it. If the roots are too crowded, they back away.

Sometimes one tests the same place repeatedly, approaching from below, then from the side, then settling with its whole length against it.

Once it stays, its shape begins to change.

The fins widen. Their edges adhere to bark or stone. The body flattens between them, and the dark cord divides. Months later, there is no longer a clear front or back. There are only folds reaching toward food, thicker seams carrying what has been gathered, and small muscular pads holding the whole animal in place.

It can still leave. It almost never does.

What feeds it is largely what the grove lets fall: pollen, fragments of soft green coating, the delicate skins shed by grazers, tissue from a broken bladder. Along the free edges of its body are fringes too fine to distinguish without bringing one’s face very close. These draw particles into shallow channels. The channels converge beneath the leaves, where the animal is thickest and least transparent.

A whole hillside’s slow circulation may deliver less food in an hour than a single snapped stem delivers in a moment.

When that happens, the animal becomes visibly attentive.

A ribbon that has hung motionless for a week turns upstream. Several folds open along the trunk. The leaf bed rises in a low ridge as the concealed body shifts beneath it. Nothing darts. Nothing strikes. But the drifting tissue is intercepted, passed from one fringe to another, and brought into the dark.

Afterward, the folds close. The leaves settle.

On old groves there may be several of these animals, their edges meeting without overlapping. Their territories are difficult to map because each follows a different part of the tree. One occupies the lower roots and the leaf hollow beneath them. Another spreads through the middle shelves of foliage. A third hangs near the surface, where the light is strongest and the rain can sometimes be felt through the water.

At their boundaries, two translucent margins lie beside each other. They occasionally contract in sequence, a slow movement traveling along one seam and then the other.

Whether this is warning, recognition, or merely the consequence of contact is hard to know. They make no wound. They do not attempt to push each other away.

Their disputes, if they have them, consist of waiting.

The plants benefit from their presence, though not evenly. The animal clears some roots of coating and leaves others untouched. It catches loose leaves before they can press against new shoots. When a branch bends too far beneath the weight of a crown, a broad fold may draw taut between it and the trunk.

This is not enough to hold up a broken limb. It is enough to change how an unbroken one settles.

Over years, the inhabited trees acquire a slightly different shape. Their lower branches are less tangled. Their hanging roots spread in curtains rather than ropes. Around each animal’s hidden center, young stems grow especially thickly from the litter.

There are also bare places.

Near its larger folds, the animal keeps small patches of stone free from growth. These smooth areas are not entrances or feeding grounds. It rests portions of its body directly against them, with nothing between flesh and mountain.

When a warm seam opens deeper in the rock, it feels the change there first.

The response spreads slowly upward. A fold tightens against the trunk. A free ribbon turns toward the stone. On the other side of the tree, a fringe that has been feeding closes and remains closed.

Hours may pass before the surrounding water becomes perceptibly warmer.

The animal has already moved its youngest growth away from the crack.

It knows the mountain at the scale of contact: where a plate is loosening, where a root presses into a fracture, where warmth has begun to travel along an edge. Occasionally it abandons a ledge shortly before that ledge falls. More often, nothing falls. The fresh stone simply warms, cools, and holds.

The animal does not hurry back.

Some live beneath the pockets of trapped air.

There, their upper folds become thicker and pale, and small chambers form within them. These open against the bubble under the rock. Moisture gathers around the openings. White roots pass among them, their tips shining.

The animal is still submerged everywhere else. Its fringes feed in the water; its broad lower body rests on wet stone. But a portion of it lies against that little dryness, sharing the enclosed air with the roots.

If the bubble shrinks, it withdraws. If a rising bladder catches beneath the ledge and breaks, enlarging the pocket, it extends into the newly available space.

In these sheltered places, it bears its young.

They appear at first as dark strokes inside clear pouches, each folded around itself. For a long time they scarcely move. Then one straightens, and the pouch opens along a seam.

The little animal does not immediately swim away. It remains beside the parent, caught among the fringes, turning its narrow body against the same slow water. Eventually a fold releases it.

Most leave singly. A few remain close enough to settle on another root of the same tree.

Thus a grove can hold several generations without ever looking crowded.

An old individual may be very large. Its lower body can occupy an entire hollow, while its thinnest extensions reach through the roots of neighboring trees. Parts of it die where shade becomes too deep or a branch falls away. Other parts continue growing toward light, warmth, and the routes along which pollen drifts.

Its age cannot be read from its size. A small one beneath a cold ledge may be older than the wide, translucent animal spread across a sunlit slope.

Nor is it always possible to tell whether one has died. The dark lines fade. The fringes stop gathering. Leaves collect on the loosened folds, and stems rise through them. Yet somewhere higher on the trunk, a sleeve may still tighten when touched.

The grove keeps both the living tissue and what has ceased to live.

From a summit, looking down through the clear water, you would seldom distinguish any of this. You would see the shelves of foliage, the pale trunks, the silver turning of leaves. Perhaps one crown would seem oddly well supported. Perhaps a strip of open stone would interrupt the green.

Only by remaining beside a tree would you begin to notice how many things were being held.

A loose leaf prevented from covering a shoot.

A root kept a little apart from its neighbor.

A fold of living tissue pressed against a fracture that had warmed during the night.

And, beneath it all, the animal slowly changing its grip on the mountain, while the mountain was still becoming itself.
