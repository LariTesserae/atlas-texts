What lives here is easiest to find just after the rain from the flange has stopped.

Not in the basin. The basin is too open, and its oily surface seals off something the creature needs. Look instead beneath the welded threshold, where the last water passes through a slit before reaching the sag in the floor. A strip of pale material lies against the underside of the weld. It looks like softened packing, squeezed out when the threshold was repaired. Its exposed edge is gritty and brown. Farther back, where a lamp cannot reach without being laid on the floor, it is translucent.

Wait there, and the edge will thicken. A little water disappears beneath it. Then, slowly, a shallow crease travels along the strip, against the direction of the flow.

That is part of it.

There are other parts under the cold vessel, behind the cracked inspection cover, and between two ceramic blocks where a steel band has lost a fraction of its tension. Whether these belong to one animal is not a question the room makes easy to answer. They do not all move together. Some can remain motionless for weeks. Yet when a warm line cools on the far side of the room, the strip beneath the threshold begins to withdraw before any change in the falling water can be seen.

Its body is mostly hidden in spaces that were never intended to be spaces: the clearance around a bolt, a fold in old insulation, the thin separation between a casing and the mineral crust grown over it. It is soft enough to enter these places, but not fluid. Where it crosses a wider gap, it lays down a narrow sheath with a tough outer skin. Where the gap is very narrow, it leaves the sheath behind and passes through as a wet, almost colorless ribbon.

It has no permanent outline. This does not mean it can take any shape it likes. Heat confines it. Dryness confines it. A smooth surface without a seam is a formidable obstacle. To cross three inches of exposed steel may require more growth and preparation than extending six feet through a damp joint.

There are places it has been trying to reach for years.

The upward-moving moisture on the coldest vessel is one of its more visible structures. Beneath the scale, flattened tubes branch from a thicker body at the base. Their walls admit water and draw it along. As they fill, their upper surfaces lift the scale by an amount too small to see, and beads appear in the resulting cracks. The branching lines change, but only within a narrow range. Old routes close. New ones form beside them.

At the circumferential seam, the tubes broaden into small chambers. These hold the wet collar against the tank. A drop falls when a chamber fills beyond what its skin can retain. When the collar retreats, water is being drawn farther inward.

The label was put there after someone scraped away what appeared to be a harmless accumulation.

There is still a rectangular patch where the scraping stopped. It has never quite recovered. Around its edges the creature has built dense, opaque tissue, yellowish under a strong light. The new growth follows a longer route to the seam.

It does not eat the machinery, at least not in the ordinary sense. Its outer skin collects fine particles from the water: rust, dust, softened paint, the remains of insects, traces of whatever passes through the vessels. Some of these are food. Others become part of the skin. A section beside an iron coupling is reddish and stiff; beneath the ceramic wall it is pale and almost smooth. Oil troubles it. It closes around a contaminated passage and waits for cleaner water to make another route.

The pale threads beneath the inspection cover are not its young. They grow independently, and sometimes grow into it. The creature tolerates them along its outer layers, where they break down things it cannot use. Deeper inside, it keeps them out. If a cover is opened and the threads dry, the tissue below remains sealed and wet.

Its more substantial body lies behind the lowest row of vessels, in a crooked pocket preserved by three generations of repairs. There it is folded rather than spread. The folds hold small reservoirs of water, each separated from the next by a muscular partition. The walls are thick, dark, and covered with the same mineral grit found in the basin.

Nothing about this central mass resembles a face. There is a region with many short, blunt projections, kept in contact with the casing of the largest vessel. Elsewhere, paired ridges brace against opposite sides of the pocket. A few pale cords lead away through the surrounding seams.

The projections sense vibration. The ridges bear pressure. The cords do several things at once.

When the machinery pauses, the whole mass loosens slightly. When pressure returns, it braces again. The change is scarcely greater than the movement of a sleeping throat.

It knows the room through such contacts. The distant pumps arrive as patterns in the steel. An opening valve alters those patterns long before any nearby pipe changes temperature. Water makes a different signal when it carries more grit. A foot on the stair sends a sharp disturbance through the supports, followed by a series of softer ones as the person descends.

It cannot tell what a visitor is. It can tell whether that visitor stays.

After a few minutes, exploratory tissue near the person’s feet draws inward. The withdrawal is small: a wet edge recedes into a seam, a pale patch becomes level with the surrounding crust. A visitor might notice that a leak has stopped.

The warm handprint beside the access hatch has another explanation.

For a long time, a worker steadied themselves there while listening to the vessel. The warmth of the hand, repeated in the same place, encouraged a fine network to grow against the inside of the casing. When the hand returned, the network filled. When it left, the network emptied slowly.

That worker no longer comes. The network remains, though some of its channels have closed.

The creature has no use for the gauge lamp itself, but the plants growing toward it matter. Their roots enter debris the creature also occupies. A root that finds one of its wet sheaths follows it for a while; the sheath thickens on the side away from the root. Sometimes both survive. Sometimes the root takes too much water, and the creature abandons that section. The handful of stems near the stair have more room below than they appear to have above.

The narrow bodies occasionally seen beneath the tanks are its young.

For their first part of life they are separate, short enough to rest along a rivet. They have a firmer skin than the adult and a row of tiny gripping pads underneath. They do not travel far in a single attempt. A young one may spend several days beneath the lip of a cover, extend its front half into the open, and withdraw without leaving.

Many fail to find another shelter.

Those that do survive begin to flatten. They press themselves into a damp seam and remain there, feeding and widening. Their gripping pads become folds along the lower surface. Their firmer skins split and are left at the entrance. These empty skins resemble insect shells until you notice that there are no legs.

Most young growths remain small and independent. A few reach an established sheath. The contact may be rejected, or the two surfaces may soften together. Afterward, a local pulse can pass from one to the other.

This is how the creature has extended itself beyond the pocket behind the vessels. It has not crossed the room all at once. It has occupied successive shelters, with gaps between them, and joined some of those shelters later. There may be parts of it that have lost all connection to the rest. They continue to live on what their own seams provide.

Its attention, if that is the word, follows water and pressure. It can leave a dry route dormant for a very long time. It cannot afford to maintain every route.

So when the patch of rain shifts six inches to the left, the change reaches it in several ways. The old sheath receives less water. A warmer surface cools. Somewhere a partition tightens, reserving what remains.

For days nothing visible happens.

Then the gritty edge beneath the threshold begins to shorten. Beyond the new rain, in a joint that has until now held only dust, a clear film appears. It gathers into a low ridge, no thicker than a fingernail. When a drop reaches it, the ridge does not wash away.

By the time the new channel has become dark enough for someone to notice, the creature is already lying along its underside, feeding a narrow growth into the next sheltered space.
