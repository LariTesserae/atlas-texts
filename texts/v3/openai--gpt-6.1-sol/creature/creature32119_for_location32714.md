The most numerous inhabitant is small enough to disappear behind a grain of lime.

Seen without magnification, it is a pale interruption in the dust. Seen closely, it has a broad head, six short legs, and an abdomen that seems slightly too large for the rest of it. Its body is not white but translucent cream, with darker seams where the plates overlap. Two fine feelers test the ground ahead. At the end of each foot is a little fan of hairs, usually folded together. On clean stone the hairs open; on a greasy surface they close, and a hooked claw takes their place.

It can walk upside down beneath the retaining clip. It cannot cross a fresh bead of oil.

The adults know this, or behave as though they know it. They approach the bead until their feelers touch its margin, then work sideways along the edge. The youngest sometimes go straight on. Their feet enter the liquid, their bodies tilt, and the more they struggle the more of them becomes attached. Later, when the bead has flattened and its surface has gathered dust, other animals reach them.

There is no ceremony in this. The dead are food before they are recognizable as dead.

The pale animals carry amber globules inside their abdomens. These are clearest in individuals that have recently fed: two or three bright chambers surrounded by a faint, granular cloud. Under the grille they look as if they contain pieces of the light. In fact, they contain oil, altered by a population of organisms smaller than any of the chamber’s visible inhabitants.

Those organisms live in folds of the gut. The pale animal provides warmth, movement, and a steady supply of difficult food; they make some of that food usable. Neither partner does especially well alone. A newly hatched animal spends its first hours among the droppings and cast skins of older ones, taking in what it needs.

This is why the nursery is dirty.

It occupies the underside of a limestone flake near the rear wall, where the cold comes through most steadily. Eggs lie in patches rather than neat clutches, each fixed by a spot of dull adhesive. Empty shells remain after hatching. Dust sticks to them, then scraps of skin, then the powdered remains of things eaten nearby. From above, the flake looks unoccupied. Beneath it are hundreds of tiny bodies, some still soft from emergence, some waiting motionless while their new coverings harden.

An adult entering this crowd is enormous. The young part around its feet and close behind it.

The adults do not tend them. Nevertheless, the young survive better where adults have been.

Across the chamber, the same animal looks different. Near the entrance it is graphite-colored with belt dust. Beneath the oil stain its abdomen is yellow and its limbs shine. In the cold sockets of vanished pebbles, older individuals wear a chalky coat thick enough to obscure their joints. They pause often to clean themselves, drawing a feeler through their mouthparts, scraping one hind leg with another. The coating comes off in rolls. Almost immediately, something smaller begins to feed on it.

Their lives are largely spent crossing distances a human finger could bridge.

One follows the nested hollows along the wall, climbs the threaded stud, descends by a groove packed with dust, and reaches the same patch of stone from which it started. During that journey it encounters two fresh skins, a predator, a moving shadow, and a smear of food that was not there on its previous circuit. There is no reason for it to regard the journey as repetitive.

The small hunters take advantage of these routes.

They are narrow, brown animals with forelegs that remain lifted while the other legs hold them against the stone. Some station themselves beside quartz grains. Others wait where the retaining clip meets the floor, at the point where travelers must slow and change their footing. A hunter may stay there through several revolutions of the shaft. When the press strikes, it flattens with everything else but keeps its place.

Its prey usually returns to motion sooner than it does.

The black beetles are less dependent on concealment. They push into gatherings with their heads lowered, their polished backs carrying lime in the fine scratches. Their jaws can open a carcass that the pale animals cannot penetrate. For a while, their feeding excludes the smaller bodies. Then a beetle shifts its grip, or carries away the largest piece, and a hundred mouths find the exposed interior.

Even the centipede leaves something behind. Its meals shed legs, feelers, fragments of gut. It eats beneath a brick, bracing its many feet against the whitewashed underside. When disturbed, it folds itself into a crevice that appears much too narrow to receive it. The pale animals hesitate at the opening, then resume their passage over the legs still protruding.

There are creatures here that almost never touch the bare limestone. They live in the dust caught among screw threads, in the fibrous undersides of old skins, in a hollow inside a dead beetle’s head. Their whole habitat can be carried away by another animal. A body feeding beside the stud may therefore contain passengers that feed too, and smaller passengers feeding on those.

When the engines stop, this crowded life loosens.

Air enters from the grille instead of leaving through it. The change reaches the inhabitants before the chamber becomes quiet. Feelers lift. Animals that have spent the day beneath flakes of stone climb onto their upper surfaces. The pale ones gather along the copper tube, standing far enough apart that their legs do not touch. In the diminishing light, their amber interiors are briefly visible together.

They are not watching anything. Some feed on a film too thin to see. Some clean their feet. Others remain still until another body touches them.

By morning, the tube is almost bare again. A fresh skin hangs from its lower edge. Beneath the staircase, the oil bead has grown heavy, but has not fallen. The routes around its expected landing place are already busy.
