At first you find only the places where it has been resting.

They are shallow clearings beneath the lowest tiers of leaves, no wider than the reach of two arms. The silt there is combed into fine parallel lines. Nothing has been eaten down to the stone; nothing is broken. The plants have simply grown to either side, leaving room for a body that is no longer present.

Some of these clearings are old. Roots have crossed them, but each root makes a small detour before continuing. Even after the space fills with growth, its shape remains legible.

Then, in a grove where the light has almost failed, you see what looks like a pale branch laid across several others.

It has too many joints for a branch. Along its underside, a row of small, blunt extremities holds the translucent stalks without bending them. One releases. Farther along, another takes hold. The whole length advances a little, with no visible effort and almost no displacement of water.

You may follow it for some time before finding either end.

Its body is folded back upon itself. What seemed to be one branch is an assembly of long limbs, narrow plates, and soft connecting tissue, arranged so that nearly all of it can lie in the same plane. In the tight groves it spreads horizontally among the leaves. On bare stone it gathers into a low, many-jointed mound. In open water it lengthens, letting the current draw its limbs apart.

There is a head, though it is not always foremost.

It is small and rounded, protected by two overlapping shells the color of soaked ivory. Between them is a seam of dark skin. When the shells separate, the skin unfolds into a fan of delicate ridges. These face neither consistently forward nor upward. They turn toward the nearest stone.

Its eyes are elsewhere.

Several of the blunt extremities carry a clear bead just behind the gripping surface. Each bead is covered by a thin lid that opens sideways. While some limbs hold the body in place, others reach into the water with their eyes exposed. It can look over a ledge without bringing its head near it. It can inspect the underside of its own resting place. On the hanging shoals, one eye may watch the forest while another looks down through the silver skin into the empty air.

The beads are not very good at distinguishing shape. A passing white animal may be no more visible to them than a loose leaf. But they see differences in light that persist after the light itself has gone. A slope illuminated yesterday remains faintly legible. A stone recently turned over is bright on one side and dark on the other, even in a basin where there has been no brightness for weeks.

The animal places its limbs carefully around such stones.

Its skin, where it is not plated, is brown-green and slightly translucent. There are grains embedded in it: pale sand, copper-colored fragments, small pieces of shell. These are not ornaments. Fine grooves in the skin admit water, and suspended grit lodges there. Over time the grooves close around it.

A young one is almost transparent. An old one has the appearance of something assembled from the floor beneath it.

Only at the joints does the flesh remain pale. When it unfolds, narrow white intervals appear all along its body. Then the intervals close again.

It eats in the sheltered places where currents have been divided by leaves.

There, among the leaf tiers, tiny growths collect that never establish themselves as plants. Some have begun to make a root. Others are little knots of tissue surrounded by a cloud of milky threads. Most will dissolve or be covered by silt. The animal gathers them with the soft edges of its limbs and passes them inward, from joint to joint, toward a mouth concealed beneath the shell.

It feeds for a long time in one grove, taking very little.

A plant whose lowest leaves have become clogged will sometimes tilt toward it. The animal moves beneath the leaf and draws its body along the underside, collecting the soft accumulation at the edges. Afterward, the leaf rises more easily.

Not every plant permits this. The pale ribbons of the deep troughs tighten when touched. Some exude a bitter substance that makes the animal close all its eyes at once. It withdraws, feeling its way with the empty gripping surfaces, and waits beneath a stone lip until the lids open again.

Its movements are usually so slow that they seem to belong to the forest. But a cold pocket changes it.

Before the pearly boundary arrives, the head turns. The fan between its shells opens to its full width. Every gripping limb releases except one.

For a moment it hangs by that single hold.

Then the pocket passes over it.

The embedded grains stand out sharply beneath its skin. Its joints contract. Along the margins of the plates, serrations appear where there had been smoothness. The body becomes smaller, more definite, visibly capable of hurting something.

It does not follow the cold. It endures it.

When warmth returns, the serrations subside. The limbs unfold one after another. Sometimes the last limb remains closed for days, and the animal carries it against its underside as though protecting an injury.

The young do not survive every such passage. In a cold pocket’s wake you may find a delicate arrangement of clear limbs, caught among the leaves. For a little while it resembles a discarded piece of the plant. Then the living parts of the grove begin to lean toward it.

The adults are scarce, but they are not always solitary.

There are resting places large enough for several bodies. These lie on the sheltered sides of ridges, where a current returns gently upon itself. Here the combed lines in the silt cross at different angles. A limb has left a groove; another has laid grains across it. The marks are precise enough to remain distinct through several changes of current.

An animal arriving at such a place passes its head close to the ground. It opens the dark fan and holds it almost against the silt.

Then it adds a mark.

Sometimes it moves one grain from the end of a line to the beginning. Sometimes it presses a shallow hollow beside the others. It may spend an entire brightening of the basin on this work, and leave without resting.

Others come much later.

They are not identical. One has a broad, chipped plate over its central body. Another carries a closed limb that has become overgrown with coppery filaments. A third is so heavily grained that its soft parts are visible only when it moves.

They inspect the changes and make changes of their own.

At certain times, several remain together. Their outer limbs grip the surrounding stalks while their inner ones touch. A pale extremity passes slowly along a row of joints. Another turns, exposing an old break in its plate.

There is no struggle for position. If one needs to unfold, the others loosen their holds to make room.

It is during these gatherings that the clearings enlarge.

Silt is carried to the margins. Crowded leaves are gently turned aside, held there until their growth has changed direction. Loose stones are lifted from beneath the roots and set upright.

One stone is difficult work. Several bodies brace themselves against the mountain while another draws the block upward. They pause often. When the distant knock enters the water and all motion ceases, they remain with their limbs stretched taut around the load.

After the pause, they test every hold before continuing.

Not all the arrangements you find are theirs. An animal may occupy the space between three upright stones without showing any interest in moving them. It may examine a cut step for days, tracing the edge with its fan, then leave a single grain on the unbroken center.

They spend more time near the solitary doorways.

Usually they approach sideways, one eye extended ahead. The head remains well back. A limb touches the inner edge, withdraws, and touches again at a different height.

Occasionally one stays there through a whole season.

Its limbs anchor among the plants outside the opening, but one rests within it. On that limb the skin grows differently. Fresh pale tissue appears along the joints. The embedded grains loosen and fall. Meanwhile, the rest of the animal continues to darken, collecting grit from the passing water.

When it finally withdraws, it spends a long time cleaning the altered limb. It rubs it against its oldest plates, slowly, until fragments from the worn shell have lodged in the new skin.

You can distinguish its descendants by those fragments.

The young begin beneath the plates of an adult, in spaces that look at first like ordinary joints. They are little folded bodies, each surrounding a bead of clear fluid. The adult carries several, though they are seldom the same size.

As they grow, their limbs emerge and take hold of the parent.

For a while the parent has more eyes than usual.

Some are small and perfectly clear. Others are yellowed, with faint mineral clouds caught beneath the lids. When the body crosses a ridge, the young look outward while the adult’s eyes remain against the stone.

Separation is gradual. A small one transfers two limbs to a leaf. Days later it transfers a third. The adult continues feeding nearby, sometimes within reach, sometimes just beyond it. At last only one narrow grip joins them.

The adult does not pull away.

It waits until the young releases.

Afterward, the small animal remains in the groove left by the larger body. It opens its fan against the ground. It touches the grains there, placing some in the channels of its own almost transparent skin.

During the golden rain, these young creatures are easier to find. New growth overwhelms their resting places. They climb from tier to tier, awkwardly, carrying tufts of vegetation caught at their joints. An adult may return and clear a space beneath them, or hold down a leaf until they have passed.

The enormous shadows bring a different response.

Long before one covers the ridge, the animals lower themselves. Eyes close. Plates overlap. Limbs that seemed thin become broad as their soft margins unfurl against the stone.

A body that could occupy half a grove disappears into a patch of uneven mineral color.

The young remain visible longest. Sometimes an adult settles over one, leaving just enough space beneath its plates for the smaller limbs to fold.

They do not rise when the light returns. They wait until the floating meadows begin to open.

An old animal eventually becomes difficult to separate from its resting place even when no shadow is passing.

Its plates thicken. Roots enter the channels where sand once lodged. It can still loosen them, and sometimes does, but increasingly it leaves them alone. The grove grows around its held limbs. Leaves turn toward the small movements of its mouth.

It may remain capable of travel for years after it has ceased to travel.

Others visit it. They clear silt from the beads of its eyes and place soft food within reach. A young one may settle along its back, fastening itself between the plates.

At last the head’s shell stays closed.

Water continues through the skin’s grooves. Plants occupy them. The limbs retain their holds until the soft tissue is gone; afterward, roots keep the pale joints in much the same arrangement.

These remains make good shelter. Young ones rest beneath them, especially during the inland storms.

After the water clears, one comes out and grips the edge of an old plate. It tests the current with an open eye. For some time it holds there, its other limbs still folded beneath the shelter.

Then it reaches toward the nearest leaf.

The leaf gives slightly under its weight. A few grains slide down the tilted surface, and the young animal catches one against the pale groove of its wrist.
