The first inhabitants you are likely to touch are too small to feel separately.

They live in the moisture on a handrail, in the warm film inside a boot, between the layers of a reed. On dry material they gather into a substance like softened wax. In water they spread until a finger drawn through them meets no resistance at all. They favor the shallow grooves on the gauges. An operator cleans these with a wooden pick, then waits before taking a reading, because the creatures withdraw slowly and can make a groove seem deeper than it is.

They are not especially troublesome to people. They collect beneath a fingernail but do not enter a cut. Around a wound they form a narrow raised border, apparently attracted by the difference between the pressure inside the body and the pressure outside it. Workers wash them off with water from the same station where they first acquired them. Water brought from farther away sometimes makes them cling.

What looks like a crust on the mud is a larger, related sort of life. Its upper surface incorporates whatever settles there: clay, shell dust, filings, shed skin. The living part lies underneath. Turn up an edge and you can feel it trying to flatten itself against your fingers.

A patch may extend for hundreds of yards without having any part thicker than a thumb.

It contracts when stepped on, but not necessarily where the foot lands. A boot pressed into one end may cause the other end to wrinkle around a reed bed. Animals crossing these patches hesitate, shift their weight, and proceed. People learn to do the same. A crust carrying too much weight becomes taut. It is possible to feel, through the soles of broad boots, the difference between ground that is firm and ground that is merely holding itself still.

The fleshy reeds grow through it. Their roots are short and blunt, with no fine branching that anyone has found. Instead, each root fits into a soft socket in the mat. A reed pulled free leaves a depression which closes over several hours. The uprooted plant stays bent in whatever position it was carried, and is seldom successfully planted elsewhere.

Small animals shelter in the wet space between leaf and stem. These are narrow, muscular things with mouths at both ends. When disturbed they tighten into rings. A worker who brushes against a stand of reeds may find several around a sleeve, warm and stubborn as bands of rubber. They can be removed by resting them against another reed and waiting for them to open.

Most of the swimmers have mouths underneath.

Their segmented backs protect long, uninterrupted bodies that fold and lengthen without much regard for the divisions in the skin. The smaller ones travel in the narrow streams descending from ridge tops. Sometimes the stream is scarcely wider than the animal; water bulges around its flanks as it goes uphill.

The larger ones lie across several channels at once. Part of an animal can be feeding in one basin while another part remains caught beneath a mineral shelf a considerable distance away. They do not pull themselves free abruptly. Over a shift, the trapped portion becomes thinner, the rest thicker, until the shelf no longer holds them.

Operators call these animals drawstrings. The name appears in maintenance reports: *Three drawstrings removed from south intake. Fourth remains beyond reach.*

A drawstring lifted clear of the water is unexpectedly heavy. Its weight changes as it is held. Two people carrying one may have to stop and exchange ends, although the animal has neither struggled nor altered its position. They put it down at the nearest channel and wait until all of it has entered. Leaving a length on the ground can obstruct drainage across an entire shelf.

The fish are more conventional in the hand. They have jaws, fins, bones, and eyes that feel like small hard seeds beneath the skin. Their bodies are pale, according to people who have taken them away from the plain.

Here they feed by pressing their mouths against one another’s flanks. This looks, when reconstructed from touch, like crowding rather than predation. One fish holds still while another works its mouth along a strip of skin; then they separate, both apparently unharmed. They also press against boots, pilings, and submerged pipes. Older pipes acquire bands polished smooth at the depth where the fish gather.

A blocked intake does not always contain more fish than usual. Sometimes the same number have simply ceased to yield.

The flat crabs live beneath mineral plates that they can lift but cannot easily leave. A large plate shelters several generations. The oldest animals occupy its center, where the mud is deepest; younger ones crowd along the edges and are the first to be found when a plate is overturned.

Their claws are poorly suited to cutting. One is broad and hollow underneath. They use it against anything that carries a vibration well.

There are evenings when every crab around a station taps the pipework once, with long intervals between taps. The sound is faint. An operator leaning against a valve feels it travel through the stem and into the bones of the wrist. Animals in the basin gradually move away from the screens. Sometimes a bank settles afterward. Sometimes nothing happens that the workers can recognize.

Nobody deliberately answers the tapping. A dropped wrench, however, can keep a station’s animals moving for hours.

The larger walkers are known by their feet.

There are padded animals whose tracks resemble places where someone has set down a heavy sack. They lift each sole from the mud gradually, peeling it loose from one edge. Small fish follow them, entering the hollow as the foot leaves and dispersing before it comes down again.

Others walk on bundles of narrow supports. These leave punctures in rows, though the arrangement changes from step to step. A person touching one of these animals’ legs feels the separate shafts sliding beneath a common sleeve of skin. They can spread over doubtful ground, or gather closely enough to pass between two rails.

Both kinds wait at established crossings.

They may have learned this from people. They may instead be waiting for the chain buried under the crossing to become still. When several stand together, workers sometimes find that the ceramic route studs have sunk flush with the mud. There is no sign of trampling. The whole crossing has lowered evenly under their combined weight.

The very large animals have fewer identifiable habits.

They stand beside pump houses where warm leakage runs down the pilings. Their legs can be embraced only partway. The skin is thick, with shallow folds that remain wet inside even above the reach of splashing water. Scars are useful for identification because they are harder than the surrounding tissue. One animal near the western valve yard has a long scar with three branches; another has a round depression large enough to receive a worker’s hand.

A station crew may know the same individual for years.

They rub accumulated grit from its scars. They lean ladders against its legs, although this is discouraged. One crew hangs a spare coat from a fold in the skin, retrieving it at the end of each shift. The animal has occasionally moved during the day. The coat is then found hanging farther out over the basin, requiring a boat.

Nothing about these animals is easily established above the height a person can reach. A pole pushed upward meets flesh in one place, empty warm air in another. Platforms built to examine them have been abandoned after their stairs ceased to lead to the expected part of the animal.

They breathe, or do something close enough to breathing that people use the word. A slow pressure passes down the body. Water lifts around the feet. For a moment the little creatures on nearby surfaces draw themselves flat.

Then the pressure eases.

The people who live on the plain are usually attached to a group of stations rather than a settlement.

Their rooms stand on pilings, with low beds and hooks for clothing. Furniture has broad feet. Cups are kept upside down, since an upright cup is liable to acquire inhabitants before it is needed. A bed unused for several weeks develops a fine, yielding layer underneath the mattress. This can be peeled away and carried outside, but someone has to return afterward and check the joints of the frame.

Households maintain their own routes. A child learns the sequence of textures between bed, door, washing basin, and kitchen before being allowed onto a station path. Adults check these short routes by hand each morning. Most changes are slight: a sill farther from a step, a wall with room behind it where there was none before.

Children also learn to stand still when water rises unexpectedly.

They are not told that every unseen animal is dangerous. They are taught to leave space for a foot to land, to keep fingers out of a narrowing seam, and to let go of a tool if something beneath the water is holding the other end.

People keep small possessions by their feel. A cup may have a dent made on purpose. Spouses know the repair stitches in one another’s coats. Names are spoken close by, where a voice is plainly a voice and not a sound returning after a long absence.

There is no reliable account of the oldest residents. Employment records are kept in station houses, but some list shifts at installations no one now knows how to reach. A few operators remember servicing pumps before their present foundations were built. They do not make much of it. If a familiar bearing is still in use, they grease it.

They know where the great animals prefer to stand. They know which basin will return a dropped tool, and which will return only the grip with something soft still holding it.

When the broad movement comes beneath the mud, these workers sometimes put a hand on the nearest pipe and wait.

The swimmers fold themselves into the channels. The crabs hold their claws against stone. Reeds remain bent in air that has stopped moving. Around the stations, the great animals take a little more of their weight onto one foot.

There is time to finish what is in your hands.

Then water begins to travel along an edge that was dry before. A mat loosens from the mud. Somewhere beyond the routes, a heavy sole lifts carefully, and remains lifted until there is a place to put it down.
