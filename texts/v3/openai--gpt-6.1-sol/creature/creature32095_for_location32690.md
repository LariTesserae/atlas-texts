The most numerous inhabitants are difficult to separate from the fastenings.

They have flat, square backs the color of old solder, with a shallow depression at each corner. A screw head beside one looks almost exactly like another animal seen from above. The difference becomes apparent when the depression fills with water. The animal contracts beneath it, drawing the water through a seam in its shell; the screw does not.

Under the shell is a soft body folded around a hollow steel pin. Six legs extend from it, although only four bear weight. The other two reach into adjacent sockets and stay there, feeling the pressure of the ceramic against their tips. They do not need to find their way. Each occupies the only place that fits it.

Their eyes are underneath.

There is a little light down there, between the body and the ground. In that blue clearance they watch a feeding needle advance and retreat. It advances less than the width of an eye. Something transparent gathers on its end. The mouth rises to meet it.

They look blind from above because nothing above them has ever needed to be seen.

Among these square-backed animals live creatures so thin that their thickness is more important than their length. One fits between a mounting bracket and the ridge the bracket holds. Its body is a transparent ribbon, edged with short legs that fold forward rather than sideways. Seen through water, the legs resemble the teeth of a zipper.

Its digestive tract makes three neat turns around the bracket’s central bolt.

It cannot straighten. It was assembled in this posture, with the bolt already passing through the loop of its body. Yet its muscles continually attempt a small extension. The extension draws fresh water along one side of it and presses used water out along the other. What appears to be imprisonment is also the shape of its breathing.

At the deepest point of the loop is its face: two pale sensory pits and a mouth like a puncture. A grain of food approaches along the film. The face shifts toward it. For an instant the whole animal seems about to come free.

Then the mouth closes, and the body returns to its former tension.

The hanging drinkers are less easy to mistake for hardware. Their abdomens are full of cloudy fluid, and something red travels through each one in a circuit too small to call a pulse. They hang with their heads down, their legs locked into the drilled sockets under the ledges. Between their forelegs they hold the filaments from which the water drops depend.

They do not bite through the filaments. They comb them.

Each combing motion draws a film of nourishment toward the mouth. The drop beneath them trembles, distorting their faces into broad, solemn masks. The faces themselves are narrow and nearly featureless. What looks in the drop like a pair of large eyes is the reflection of two joints.

Some have wings. There is nowhere to extend them, but along the folded wing’s lower edge are breathing openings that must remain unobstructed. A neighbor lifts its own wing case whenever the first animal flexes. The movements fit together closely enough that neither touches the other’s soft parts.

This accommodation is everywhere. The chamber is crowded, but the crowd has an intricate courtesy of clearance. A jaw opens beneath a lifted foot. A gill spreads into the space vacated by a contracting belly. One animal’s necessary movement supplies another with its necessary interval.

They need not understand the arrangement. They feel it through the surfaces against which they live.

The black swimmers have broad heads and bodies that taper into threads. Each occupies a current emerging from a hole in the pool wall. Their tails beat continuously, holding their mouths a fixed distance from the opening.

Their mouths contain white, inward-pointing bristles.

Caught between the bristles, food remains visible for a while: a pale sphere, a fleck of oil, a translucent scrap manufactured somewhere beneath the ceramic. The swimmer works it backward with movements of the throat. Its eyes, set close beside the mouth, look directly into the source of its food.

Behind each swimmer is a space just long enough to hold its tail.

They never see the pool as a pool. They experience it as pressure in front, effort behind, and the steady arrival of things they can swallow.

There are fleshier animals in the blind channels. Their skins have no shells to protect them, so the channels protect them instead. Their bodies fill the cavities without quite sealing them. Water passes through grooves along their flanks.

Inside one, a little brass ring can be seen whenever its heart contracts away from the skin. The ring bears a number. The number faces outward, although no eye in the chamber is positioned to read it.

This animal has a soft frill around its mouth. The frill brushes the channel wall and folds back upon itself. Beneath it, a harder mouth takes hold of a pellet. The animal eats slowly, with the concentrated movements of something nursing. At the other end of its body, a pore opens against a black hole in the ceramic.

Its waste leaves without entering the water.

A faint blemish on its flank continually disappears as the rubbing wall continually makes it. The creature’s tenderness is visible there, in a spot that remains neither injured nor healed.

Near the green cushion live the small white ones that can be mistaken for frost. Their limbs are long compared with their bodies, bent sharply at the joints, and crowded with fine sensory hairs. They stand rigid through most of each mechanical pulse. Between pulses, they relax enough to bring their mouths against the root sockets.

They take no part of the plant into themselves. They remove the salts forming where root and socket meet, gathering the crystals before they can obstruct the contact.

Their mouths work with an almost delicate fussiness. One foreleg braces against the root; the other turns a crystal until its narrow end can pass between the jaws. Beneath the translucent head, a dark speck moves downward and vanishes.

The cushion’s lower cells respond to their touch. Small changes in charge pass through the conductive particles within the green tissue, then into the roots, then into the ceramic bed. The animals feel these changes in their feet.

Around the cushion, therefore, the roar has a second texture. Under the blows and scraping is a continual exchange too slight to hear: pressure answered by contraction, contact answered by a change in current. The animals linger there because their bodies are fitted to these answers. None could live in the place of another.

There is one creature behind the amber inspection lamp that is larger than it first appears.

Its visible portion is a pale knuckle wedged between two fins. Below that, its body branches. One branch descends into a refrigerant passage; another runs beneath a pool; a third lies along the back of a forming die. There are small mouths at the ends, and little muscular collars spaced along the branches. Food moves inward from several directions.

It has no central face.

When the delayed shock of a blow from the building below finally arrives, the collars tighten in sequence. The knuckle presses against the lamp housing. For a moment the amber light is obscured, and then it returns.

No other animal responds so visibly to these distant blows. Most receive them as one more disturbance among the thousands entering through their feet. The branched creature receives the same disturbance at several separated points, with tiny differences in delay. Its contractions follow those differences. Somewhere inside its pale body, the tower’s long transmission becomes a movement passing from part to part.

It remains touching the unfinished metal in the die.

Along that metal’s emerging edge, several smaller animals feed from pores that would be covered if the ridge advanced. Their mouthparts are braced against the fresh surface. Beyond their heads, the metal is still inside the machine; behind their bodies, it is already a mountain.

They live at this exact exposure, with their legs on one and their mouths against the other.

Close to the cover plate, their movements seem frantic. Farther back, through the suspended drops, they become slow and difficult to distinguish. A raised leg appears in several places at once. A mouth is magnified until it seems large enough to bite, then shrinks as the drop trembles.

There is no vantage from which all the inhabitants can be counted. Some are behind others; some are inside the thickness of the scenery; some disclose themselves only when a surface thought to be solid draws inward to breathe.

With your face against the clear inner film, you can follow a single square-backed animal for a little while. Its feeding needle moves. Its lower eyes turn. One foot lifts to admit the edge of its neighbor’s wing.

It sets the foot down in the same socket.

Behind it, out of sight, another mouth opens.
