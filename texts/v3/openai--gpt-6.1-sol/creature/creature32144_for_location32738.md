The space they leave is not quite the shape of the falling drop.

It is longer, with a narrow place at one end and three shallow recesses along the other. The animals nearest it back away before the drop reaches the floor. A pale swimmer trapped in a diminishing puddle throws its tail over the rim and drags itself clear. One of the red wall-creatures remains where it is, holding all its feet against the glass except a single limb extended into the empty space.

The drop lands without splashing.

For a while there is only water, taller than water should remain, trembling on a small patch of yellow stone. Then the extended red limb touches it. Something inside the drop takes hold.

The wall-creature pulls. A white tip comes off its limb.

With this first small piece of hardness, the animal inside the water begins to make a mouth.

Its body is difficult to distinguish from the water around it. A slight cloudiness travels from one side to the other. Two folds meet, part, and meet again. The stolen limb-tip turns crosswise between them. Other fragments rise from the floor: sulfur dust, a transparent splinter, the empty case of an egg. Some are taken into the cloudiness. Some are pushed out so deliberately that they travel farther than they would have if the drop had simply broken.

The red animal withdraws. Its injured limb is already whitening farther up.

There are several of these clear inhabitants in the fissure, though never many in the same part of it. Most spend their lives flattened beneath pools or spread so thinly against the ceiling that they are mistaken for condensation. A mature one may cover an entire hollow. At rest, it has no visible limbs, no outline other than the edge of the water, and no separate color.

The small animals know where its edges are. They graze up to them and stop.

When it moves, it gathers.

The thinness peels away from the stone in successive strips. What seemed to be a film becomes a body with a thick middle and many loose margins. Its underside carries rows of short, blind tubes. These press against the ground, fill, empty, and pull the rest forward. A full-grown animal can cross dry glass this way, leaving beads behind it that diminish but do not evaporate until it has passed out of sight.

Inside, grit circulates slowly. There is usually a curved piece of black glass near the front, although which end is the front may change. The animal moves this piece to the side it intends to use. Around it, other fragments settle into place: a broken mineral scale, two or three needles, the jaw of a swimmer.

The mouth takes a long time to assemble.

The newborn in the drop has only the red limb-tip. Its folds close around it, testing. The tip slips. The folds open again.

A six-limbed grazer approaches carrying a shard longer than its body. It has been using the shard in a hot crack, and the point is coated in mineral skin. At the edge of the cleared space, the grazer lowers it.

The newborn draws the skin off the point. The grazer holds on until the bare glass becomes visible, then lifts its tool and leaves.

For the rest of that day the young animal stays where it fell. Drops strike it and are admitted. When the floor beneath it heats, it rises on its blind tubes. When the heat subsides, it spreads. By afternoon its body has acquired a faint amber cast, and the scraps inside it cast tiny shadows in the water.

One of those shadows does not belong to a scrap.

It has six legs. It crouches under the unfinished mouth, where there is nothing for it to crouch beneath. Near the lip of the hollow, a grazer feeds with no darkness under its clear feet.

The shadow remains with the newborn until the light at the entrance reddens. Then it slips out, crosses a dry ridge, and rejoins the feeding animal. The grazer goes still. All six feet tighten against the stone.

Afterward, the newborn is able to crawl.

It moves less than the width of an egg-case, but the movement is its own.

The older clear animals carry many shadows. In bright light, these may be seen through their bodies, lying at different depths. Some retain the shape of swimmers or wall-climbers. Others belong to animals with more joints than there are room for beneath the ledges. There are shadows as narrow as needles, and one that appears in a particular hollow every few days: a broad, branching darkness, far larger than anything living in the seam.

The clear animal carrying it is no larger than the others. It avoids the hottest stone and spends much of its time under a ledge crowded with eggs.

Its mouths are small. It feeds chiefly on the translucent skin, pulling it loose in ribbons and enclosing it between the folds of its body. Sometimes it takes an egg whose case has opened but whose occupant has not moved. Sometimes a dead swimmer enters with the water. The hard parts remain inside it for a while and are eventually set down in a damp line.

Living swimmers pass through it too.

They enter at the ragged rear margin, swim among the suspended grit, and emerge near the mouth. The passage can be brief. It can also last through several changes of daylight. An animal that disappeared into a clear body no larger than a shallow pool may return from it cold, coated with the unfamiliar dust found on creatures that have fallen between close ledges.

During the passage, a swimmer sometimes rests beside a jaw indistinguishable from its own. It turns away from that place when it can.

The clear inhabitants are not safe shelters. Their folds occasionally close. A small animal can be drawn inward with the water and never reappear. The obsidian hunters leave them alone, but the red wall-creatures probe their margins and steal bits of assembled mouth. A clear animal deprived of its black glass cannot feed until it finds another suitable edge.

Nor do they prevent the stone from changing. Pools drain beneath them. New mineral ridges split their bodies apart. The separated portions pull toward one another across the floor, dragging their contents with them. Most join again. Some do not, and the smaller portion thins over several hours until there is only an ordinary wet patch and a scatter of grit.

Yet when a passage opens suddenly, the other inhabitants gather near them.

The grazers arrive first, touching the clear skin with their forefeet. Swimmers follow whatever threads of water remain. The wall-creatures descend, carrying their scorched limbs carefully. Even a folded hunter may loosen its hold beneath an overhang and come down.

A clear animal swollen with so many bodies becomes milky. Its tubes shorten. Its borrowed mouth falls apart inside it. It begins to move toward a cooler hollow, slowly enough that the smallest grazers can walk alongside.

Sometimes it reaches the hollow before the water does.

It flattens there and lets its passengers out. The swimmers lie close together in its wet margins. The grazers begin scraping the floor. A red animal tests the wall, withdraws a foot, and tries farther along. When the next drops arrive, everyone is already in place.

Near the back, the newborn has reached its first ridge. It pushes its front folds upward but cannot lift the rest of its body. The stolen white limb-tip presses against the yellow mineral and slides down.

It tries again.

Above it, among the crowded eggs, the older animal with the branching shadow slowly gathers one of its margins. A clear lobe lengthens down the face of the ridge. It stops short of the floor.

The newborn touches it.

For a moment the two bodies remain separate, each holding its own small store of water. Then the young one rises along the hanging lobe, grit turning inside it, its unfinished mouth pointed toward the dark beneath the ledge.
