Something has kept one lane open.

It is not the lane from the shelter to the pump. That has been lost. This one runs along the inner edge of the second shelf, bends behind the brick enclosure, and descends almost to the pool. Leaves overlap above it, concealing the space from anyone standing upright. Beneath them, the red grit bears a pair of shallow tracks.

The tracks are close together. Here and there, one stops while the other continues.

Whatever passes through is narrow enough not to brush the leaves, or knows how to turn before brushing them. It steps over some roots. Others it has polished by stepping on them in precisely the same place for a very long time.

You might see it first beside the pressure gauge.

There is a shape there which seems to belong to the cabinet: a vertical strip of dark material, a pale joint, something bent beneath the dial. Then the ceiling tile above it brightens, and the shape withdraws from the light.

Not quickly. Its withdrawal takes most of the time the tile remains yellow. A long upper part tilts; two folded appendages settle closer to its sides. The light catches a wet seam and is gone.

It stands about as high as the tolerable band of air, though “stands” gives the wrong impression. Most of its weight hangs between two supports, neither straight nor quite alike. One ends in a broad, dark pad. The other has three short prongs, worn pale underneath. When it moves, the pad slides and the prongs lift. The resulting gait is uneven but never hesitant.

Its body is flattened from front to back. Passing sideways between the plants, it occupies less room than a person’s open hand.

What appears to be its back is layered with hard, overlapping plates. Some are brown and fibrous at their edges. Others have the faintly granular surface of old cast iron. There are places where a plate has cracked and the crack has filled with a green material, glossy near the opening, almost black deeper in. None of these repairs look recent.

Below the plates, its body is pale.

Not white: the color of a root exposed briefly to air. Narrow bands circle it at irregular intervals. In the hollows between them, moisture remains undisturbed by the heat. When it bends, these hollows close, and a little dark fluid wells along the seams before being drawn back inside.

Its uppermost part is easy to mistake for an unopened leaf.

Two broad surfaces fold together along a central line. Their outside faces are tough and dull; their inner faces, glimpsed when they separate, have a soft gray sheen. They open only a little, usually toward a cooler leaf or a patch of fading light. There are no eyes that you can identify. Along one edge is a row of small depressions, each holding a bead of clear liquid.

It does not turn this part toward you when you enter.

One of its feet turns instead.

The prongs settle against the stone. For a while nothing else moves. Beneath the roar of the pump, it appears to be listening through the floor.

Then it resumes whatever it was doing.

Its arms—if they are arms—are carried folded across the lower body. There are four, although two are so short that you may not notice them until it works. The longer pair divide near their ends into strips of different widths. A strip can stiffen enough to push aside a heavy leaf, or loosen and follow the underside of that leaf to its base. The short pair have blunt tips with a pale crescent embedded in each.

It uses those crescents on the roots.

A root has swollen across one of the water grooves. The creature lowers itself beside it, resting its broad foot on the hottest stone. Its upper folds remain among the cool leaves. One long hand holds the root; a short one presses underneath.

There is no cutting sound. You see a shallow notch appear, then a second beside it. A thin curl of root comes free.

The creature holds the curl for some time. Eventually a seam in its lower body opens, and the piece disappears into it.

It does not clear the groove completely. It makes just enough room for water to pass on the next rise of the pool.

When that rise comes, it is still there.

Water reaches the obstruction, hesitates, then threads through. The creature lowers the narrowest strip of one hand into the flow. It leaves it there until the pool subsides. Afterward it touches the wet strip to several places beneath its plates.

There are other marks of its work, once you know to look: a leaf held away from the flywheel by a loop of dried stem; grit banked around a seep in the wall; a young shoot bent so that its copper thread does not cross the turning shaft. A broken conduit has been packed with something dark and porous. Water drips from it onto a root rather than vanishing into a crack.

The repairs are small. It does not restore things to their original condition.

Sometimes it loosens a fastening. Sometimes it spends a whole revolution of the wheel worrying a flake of metal from a housing, then carries the flake into the vegetation. It selects a soft seam in a trunk and presses the metal inside. The seam closes slowly around it.

At other times it removes a shoot that has grown too close to a bearing.

There is no consistent preference for plant or machine. There is only the next movement, the next place where water will fail to arrive, the next surface beginning to rub.

It rests beneath the metal ledge where condensation gathers.

There, its long supports fold until the body lies nearly horizontal in the narrow cool layer. The upper folds open farther. Droplets collect on their gray surfaces and travel toward the central seam.

At rest it is almost indistinguishable from the surrounding growth. A small stem rises between two plates on its back. Three leaves hang from it. They turn toward the intermittent tiles while the body beneath them remains still.

One of those leaves is dead. It has dried to a curled brown blade, but the creature has not shed it. The blade covers an opening through which you can occasionally see something turning.

The turning is very slow.

It stops when the piston reaches the bottom of its stroke, then begins again.

Perhaps the creature sleeps there. Perhaps that is simply another kind of work.

Its journeys take hours. It may travel from the gauge to the pool and back between two changes in the weather above. It has no reason to hurry while the pump keeps moving. Once, though, the loose plate in the wall catches instead of rattling. The continuous noise develops a brief gap.

The creature unfolds at once.

Not fast enough to startle you with speed, but with a decisiveness absent from its other movements. Its pronged foot grips a root. The long hands rise. It crosses the shelf without pausing at the light and reaches into a space too narrow for your fingers.

The rattle returns.

It remains there for another full stroke, holding something you cannot see.

Near the entrance, its tracks end before the outside air reaches the floor. It approaches that boundary occasionally. The folded upper part lifts into the draft, and the clear beads along its edge shrink.

It waits until they have almost disappeared, then backs down into the hollow.

There is nothing to suggest it has ever gone farther.

Much later, you may find it in the little metal shelter. It has eased itself through the foliage that blocks the old threshold and stands facing the shelf.

One hand rests on the ceramic cup.

It does not lift it. A narrow strip enters the red dust, follows the inner rim, and withdraws. The surface is left almost level, with a fine circular groove close to the edge.

Then the hand folds away.

Whether this is necessary, you cannot tell. The cup remains dry. Nothing beneath it turns or trembles. No water is diverted by the small new groove.

The creature stays for a while nevertheless, fitted into the space where the stool used to be.

Then the pool rises, and it leaves.
