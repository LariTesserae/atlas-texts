The woman at the turn has heard you waiting for the echo to end.

You do not know she is there until she sets two fingers against the head of your staff.

She has approached along the wall, placing her feet where the gray plates overlap the floor. Her lamp is covered. When she uncovers it, you see a narrow face, a mouth held slightly open, and a strip of polished shell tied across her forehead. There is nothing ceremonial about the shell. It keeps her hair from touching the hanging animals.

She looks at your boots first.

“Too thick,” she says.

Then she considers the load on your back.

“Perhaps not.”

She will not ask your name until she has brought you off the glass.

***

The inhabitants are human, though travelers sometimes doubt this before seeing them well. In the darkness, a person feeling along a wall can look like another jointed creature attached to it. People resting in the floor current lie so still, and so far apart, that a lamp may disclose a hand without revealing the body to which it belongs.

They have no single appearance. Some families are broad and heavy; some produce children whose wrists seem scarcely thicker than their tools. Skin ranges from pale brown to almost black. Light eyes occur, but are neither valued nor distrusted. Good vision is useful at a workbench. Beyond the lamp, everyone must learn other means.

What distinguishes them is mostly habit.

They lift a foot only as high as necessary. They turn their shoulders before their hips when passing a sharp mineral edge. They seldom lean backward without first reaching behind themselves. Even in familiar rooms, their hands remain occupied with knowing where things are.

Children are taught the floor before they are taught distances.

A child sits with one palm on glass and the other on a fitted tile. An adult taps each surface farther away. Later, the child must say which hand felt the tap. Later still, someone walks beyond a partition, and the child points to the passage being used.

There are games in this. There is cheating, too: siblings scrape a nail instead of tapping, or send a little seam-dweller across the tiles to confuse the listener. Adults pretend not to notice until the animal disappears beneath something valuable.

Children who cannot distinguish the vibrations are not left behind. They learn routes through temperature, slope, and touch. A settlement usually has several people whose knowledge of one narrow stretch exceeds everyone else’s.

The woman at the turn knows thin glass by the feeling in her knees. She cannot tell you precisely what changes. She sets her staff down, shifts her weight, and declines to go farther.

Her judgment is respected. It is also checked.

***

Their clothes are made from the inhabitants around them.

Certain wall animals grow, beneath their rigid plates, a loose fibrous layer that can be lifted after shedding. Washed in very little water, combed with crystal, and worked for a long time between the palms, it becomes a soft gray fabric. It does not stretch much. Garments are therefore assembled from small pieces, with generous openings at the joints.

There are families known by the shape of their patches.

The fabric catches falling scales. This makes a coat useful as a collecting surface and troublesome as clothing. People shake themselves out over feeding beds before entering a dwelling. Guests who forget are forgiven once.

Shoes are more complicated. A thick sole protects against crystal blades but conceals the warning of a weak floor. Most people own several pairs of separate sole-pieces, strapped on according to the route. At home they go barefoot. On old glass they wear something little thicker than skin.

Travelers carry soles on their belts, where other people might carry keys.

Water is kept in small, tightly stopped vessels. Much of it comes from the soft tissues of animals harvested for food; more is recovered from cooking and breath. At night, several sleepers may breathe through folds of absorbent membrane laid within a shared frame. In the morning these are pressed out.

No one finds this unpleasant. What troubles them is a stopper left loose.

A wet handprint on a wall brings everyone’s attention to the hand.

***

The wall colonies provide most of what sustains a settlement, but not every mound can be harvested.

Some are watched for decades. A plate is marked with a tiny scratch; the scratch moves outward as the animal grows. The keeper knows which individuals are preparing to shed, which have ceased feeding, and which young ones are pressed too tightly between their neighbors.

Food is taken from those that can be removed without opening a large gap.

This requires patience and an ugly-looking collection of tools. A keeper inserts curved bone beneath a plate, warms a soft joint with the heel of the hand, and waits. The animal’s feet gradually release. If it holds more firmly, the keeper stops.

An impatient harvester can strip a wall in a day. Restoring it may occupy that harvester’s grandchildren.

The animals’ flesh is dense, faintly sweet, and full of dark threads that must be pulled out before cooking. The threads are saved for binding. The fat yields lamp wax. Plates become dishes, shutters, scoops, and the smooth surfaces on which children practice cutting.

There are meals that consist almost entirely of scraped paste, warmed on a slab and folded into sheets. There are rarer meals with distinct pieces to bite.

A whole animal on the table means visitors, a birth, or a dispute that has finally ended.

***

Not everything kept near a house is food.

The little creatures admitted through gaps in the foundations are troublesome tenants. They clean deposits from stone but also rasp the handles of tools if these are left low enough. Each household has its own method of discouraging them. One hangs everything. Another rubs handles with an acrid secretion. A third insists that only poorly made tools are eaten, and replaces rather more handles than its neighbors.

Children keep seam-dwellers in shallow boxes, feeding them selected scales and stroking the smooth side that has no joints. The creatures learn a person’s approach. They will turn toward a familiar footfall and withdraw from another.

An adult may deny that these animals feel affection while carrying one carefully inside a sleeve.

The hanging discs are treated with more reserve.

Near a dwelling, their cords are moved aside with a forked rod. No one cuts them unless they must. A disc deprived of its suspension lies flat on the floor, still alive, slowly turning its pale underside toward passing warmth. People find this distressing.

They lift it back toward the roof on a warmed stone. Sometimes it fastens a new cord. Sometimes it does not.

The task can keep an entire household standing quietly for an evening.

***

For people who live in such quiet, they are not especially solemn.

Their humor is exact and local. A man who once mistook a feeding mound for his sleeping wife will hear about it for the rest of his life. Someone who gives elaborate directions to a place within arm’s reach acquires a reputation difficult to lose.

They speak softly because a normal voice carries all the necessary information. In large chambers they sometimes speak louder for pleasure, then listen to what comes back.

Children delight in echoes stripped so thoroughly of words that a familiar voice returns as a stranger’s.

There is music. It is usually felt through a shared slab: alternating taps, a heel pressed and released, a scraper drawn across grooves. Several people sitting against the same stone can follow a performance that someone standing nearby scarcely notices.

Some pieces require two rooms.

Some require an animal colony along the intervening wall. Its shifting plates alter the rhythm in ways no player entirely controls.

The oldest musicians are valued for knowing when to wait.

***

A household may include people who are not related, and exclude close relatives who sleep only a passage away. Membership follows practical arrangements: who tends the lamps, who recovers water, who can wake the others if the floor becomes unsafe.

Sleeping places are assigned with care. Infants lie beside an adult but never against the hottest wall. The old choose warmth or airflow according to the pains of that particular night.

Privacy is achieved by leaving people alone rather than putting them far away.

A person turns a lamp toward the wall. Others announce themselves before approaching. Lovers have favored corners, known to almost everyone and visited by no one without reason.

Quarrels are difficult to hide. A sharp movement travels through the floor. Neighbors may feel an argument long before they hear it, and will sometimes move their own lamps farther from the partition as a courtesy.

***

They know that the chambers continue below them.

Their stories disagree about how far.

There are accounts of people descending through broken glass and finding another warm floor, another colony, another current moving in its appointed course. There are also accounts of empty spaces so deep that a lamp burns out while still falling.

The inhabitants do not reconcile these stories. Both may be useful at different edges.

Some spend their lives searching for routes downward. They travel lightly, with extra cord and little food, and acquire the distracted manner of people continually testing something underfoot. Settlements welcome them because they bring news. Settlements also count the cords they take away.

Occasionally one returns with a mineral no one recognizes, or an animal whose plates overlap in the wrong direction.

For years afterward, people touch the object while discussing quite ordinary matters.

***

The woman who met you at the turn has made journeys of her own.

In her dwelling is a pale plate larger than her hand. Branching black lines run through it, almost but not quite like those visible inside the transparent animals along the green veins.

She found it in a passage where her lamp would not stay lit.

She does not say the plate is precious. It has served as a lid, a work surface, and once as a splint. Yet when she moves house, she carries it herself.

Tonight she sets food on it for you.

While you eat, a child comes in carrying two sole-pieces and asks permission to visit someone beyond the narrow glass. The woman feels the soles, checks the straps, and asks which way the child intends to go.

The child answers impatiently.

She makes him answer again.

Outside, gray scales are settling into the collecting cloth. Something beneath the sleeping platform advances, joint by joint, and stops against a foot. The woman nudges it away without looking.

Then she lowers her lamp so the child can light his own.

He shields the little flame with his hand, steps across the threshold, and disappears. For a while you can still see the light, moving at the height of his knees. When that is gone, the woman keeps her bare heel against the floor.

She waits until she feels him reach the safer stone.
