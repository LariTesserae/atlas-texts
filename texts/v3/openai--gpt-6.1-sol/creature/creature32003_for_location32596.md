The resident leaves her shoes at the upper rim, beneath an overturned basin weighted with a brick. They are good shoes, with stiff backs and recently mended soles. She wears them when she goes to the village. Below the basin hangs a pair of thin sandals, each made from a different section of old drive belt. Their straps have been lengthened several times.

You find her first by her work. A rag has been folded over the yellow railing where a fresh cut leaks onto it. Someone has wedged a spoon beneath the lip of a leaf so that its dripping falls into the channel rather than behind a bearing. Near the clearing, three white root tips have been tied back with red thread. The knots are small and easy to undo.

Then she comes around the shed carrying a bowl of washers.

She is shorter than the open locker door, narrow across the shoulders, with a heavy braid wound up and pinned against the back of her head. Loose hairs stick to her temples. Her shirt has no cuffs; she has cut them off rather than keep them wet. The hem of her trousers ends well above her ankles. Neither garment is green. The shirt was blue once, and the trousers are brown, and both have pale, stiff patches where plant sap has dried through repeated washing.

Her age is difficult to estimate. Her hands look older than her face. The knuckles are enlarged, the nails short and discolored along their edges. Across the palm of her left hand runs a smooth scar that opens slightly whenever she grips a tool. She turns things with her right hand and steadies them with her left, unless they are very small. With very small things she is unexpectedly delicate.

She puts the bowl on the table, lifts the glass lid of the ledger, and waits for the condensation to clear.

Her name is Mara. There is a surname in the ledger, but it appears chiefly on the earlier pages, beside formal statements of expenditure. On the current pages she signs with a single hooked stroke. She has a pencil tied to the table with a length of fishing line. Before writing, she dries its point against her shirt.

She records more than the gauge measures. Beside the pump strokes are notes about the smell of the water, the firmness of the clearing, which roof leaf has begun to curl. Some entries concern a noise heard only once. Others describe a change so slight that you cannot find it even with her finger resting beside it.

A pale swelling beneath a clamp, for example.

“It wasn’t there on Tuesday,” she says.

She does not tell you immediately whether this is good or bad. She presses it, feels along its underside, then checks the neighboring stem.

Her home is under the lowest of the three roofs. You enter sideways through an opening she cuts back every other morning. Inside there is a bed raised on four iron feet, a stove, two shelves, and a chair with one shorter leg. The floor has lifted beneath that leg until the chair stands level.

The bed is the only piece of furniture entirely clear of growth. Its feet sit in shallow bowls containing something bitter and gray. Mara changes this mixture weekly. A small broom hangs beside the bed, and every evening she sweeps beneath it, although there is almost nothing to sweep away. Sometimes a translucent root has crossed the floor during the day. She lifts it with the broom handle and sends it elsewhere.

She likes plants near her. She does not like them in her bedding.

There are dry biscuits in a sealed tin, tea in another, and a jar of plum preserve on the highest shelf. The labels on the tins have vanished. She distinguishes them by two dents and a soldered seam. Her books are wrapped individually in waxed cloth. She owns a photograph of five people standing beside a road, but keeps it face down because the glass reflects the wet walls. When she turns it over for you, she holds it by the frame and studies it herself for a while.

She has lived here long enough to remember a visible stretch of brick beside the pump shed. Long enough that the steel bridge was once a bridge you crossed in sunlight. She remembers her father lowering the first sleeved stems into position, and the trouble they had keeping the sleeves from slipping.

Several of his arrangements are still in use. Several she has dismantled. One she has been meaning to dismantle for eleven years.

“That bend,” she says, pointing beneath the flywheel. “It costs us pressure.”

But removing it would disturb a root crown that now supports the higher pulley. The crown also bears part of her kitchen roof. For the moment, she oils the bend’s worn joint and enters its losses in the ledger.

She is not the mistress of the hollow. There are places she cannot reach, motions she cannot prevent, things she has learned to leave alone. Nor does she treat its growth as a single creature with a single preference. One stem thickens readily against iron; another bruises under the same pressure. The great leaf of the clearing becomes brittle if the evening valve opens too far. The roof over her bed needs more water than the roof over the machine, though they were once shoots of the same stock.

She remembers these differences as she remembers people’s habits.

When the metallic squeal comes, she stops speaking. She listens through it, her head turned slightly, and then either resumes her sentence or goes at once to the shed. You cannot hear the distinction. She says it is clearest at the end of the sound.

Her work occupies much of the day, but seldom all of her attention. She eats while watching the upper belt. She washes clothes in water drawn before the stems have warmed it. While a cut drains, she sits with her feet in the channel and reads a few pages from a book. Some afternoons she does very little. The tools stay in their places, the pump turns, and she sleeps in the chair with her mouth slightly open.

The hollow has fitted itself around these habits. Beside the clearing is a low fork worn smooth where she rests her elbow. Above the stove, the leaves have been kept parted for years; even the new shoots tend to turn along the edges of the opening. A root has swollen beneath the step into her room, making the climb easier for a knee that troubles her.

Other accommodations are less comfortable. The handle of a valve she once opened daily has disappeared since she began opening it only once a week. She has marked its position with a strip of cloth. To use it now, she must cut down through a collar of pale tissue, and afterward both her hands smell sharply sweet.

There are mornings when she goes up to the village and stays until late. On returning, she pauses at the rim before descending. From there she can hear whether the pump is working, but cannot tell which of the smaller channels has blocked, or whether the leaf beneath the table has softened. She takes off her good shoes, puts them under the basin, and carries whatever she has bought down against her chest.

Once, while she is away, you see the pale snail on the underside of a leaf. Later you mention it.

She asks where.

You show her, and she bends carefully to look. There is a faint clean line behind its shell.

“It’s moved,” she says.

That evening she leaves a piece of cucumber wedged beside it. By morning the cucumber has fallen into the water. She retrieves it with the pruning shears and puts it back.

Before bed, she makes a last circuit with a lamp. She checks the exposed shafts, the channel entrance, the small folded leaves at the rim. At the clearing she rests one bare foot in the shallow puddle while she waits for the evening valve.

The groan rises underneath. The water lowers around her toes. She shifts her weight onto the leaf as it firms, then reaches up and hangs the lamp on its hook.

For a few moments she stands there listening. When she is satisfied, she goes inside. The pump keeps turning, and under the lowest roof a narrow rectangle of light remains until she has finished reading.
