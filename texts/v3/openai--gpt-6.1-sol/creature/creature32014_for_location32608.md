The first part of it anyone notices is usually mistaken for a repair.

There is a length of pale material beneath the pump-bay lip, pressed into the angle where the plinth meets the floor. It looks like sealant laid badly: thick in some places, flattened in others, with grit worked into its surface. At one end it has taken the impression of a washer. At the other it disappears behind a pipe.

The workers do not remember putting it there. This is not unusual. Much of the court was installed by people whose names are no longer on the maintenance rota.

When the pump runs, the pale strip tightens. Its lower edge lifts a little clear of the concrete. Someone looking directly at it might suppose the vibration had loosened it, except that it settles back before the pump stops.

That is only one part of the animal.

Most of it lies beneath the plinth, in a space too shallow for a rat and too wet for the cockroaches to use comfortably. Its body is spread rather than coiled. Several broad lobes occupy the hollows left by the shuttering, joined by narrower lengths that pass around bolts and through gaps in the aggregate. There is no central mass that could be pointed to as the body proper. It has grown into the available room.

Along its underside are small, densely set folds. Water enters between them and leaves through other folds farther along. Under a good light, their edges are almost transparent. Silt collects in them, but never enough to stop them moving.

The upper surface is tougher. It is gray-white when clean, though very little of it is clean. Most carries a coating of iron stain, grease, and fine sand. Near the warm leak, mineral crust has formed directly upon it. The animal sheds these patches occasionally. A plate comes free, exposing wet skin beneath, and remains beside the body like a fragment of broken plaster.

It does not seem troubled by the crust until it becomes heavy enough to impede a particular fold. Then, over several nights, that part of the body withdraws from beneath it.

Its growing ends are softer and more distinct. They are narrow, blunt, and faintly pink, with a dark line visible down the middle. One may extend across a brick overnight, stop at the far edge, and remain there for a week. Another feels along the underside of a cable bracket without ever coming fully into view. When touched, an end contracts slowly. When cut, it contracts very quickly.

There are no eyes. It is sensitive to light, but light means little to it except at the shaft, where a growing end once crossed into the daylight patch and stayed for three days beneath the leaning grass. It then withdrew, leaving a wet groove in the grit.

Heat matters more. So does the depth of water, and the way water changes before anything visible arrives in it.

A dropped piece of fruit upstream will cause the folds nearest the overflow to open. Diluted detergent closes them. The residue of washed blood from the market brings out several narrow ends at once, not hurriedly, but with a persistence that can be followed from one inspection to the next.

It takes small things. Larvae disappear into the folds. Grease adheres to the softer surfaces and is gradually worked inward. A dead fly resting against an exposed lobe becomes difficult to distinguish from it, then loses its shape. The hard parts remain and are eventually released.

A living rat is too large and too active to interest it. Rats step over its exposed lengths, sometimes placing a foot directly upon them. The body gives slightly and holds still. There is one place where their regular route crosses a lobe that has grown around a pipe elbow. The surface there is darker and smoother than the rest.

The cockroaches keep a narrower distance. A nymph can be held by the wet folds if it blunders too close, though this happens less often than might be expected. Most learn the margin. Under the plinth, their hiding spaces begin just above the animal’s upper edge.

It has occupied that plinth for a long time.

A little of it is present behind the abandoned slab, and another length lies under the bottom stair. Whether these are separate animals is difficult to establish. Between them are stretches of wet floor across which nothing can be seen. But beneath the concrete there are old drains, joints filled with clay, gaps around conduits. Once, when a screen was pulled sharply from the sump, the strip beneath the stair contracted at the same moment as the one beside the pump.

The workers have occasionally tried to remove it.

A scraper cuts the exposed material readily enough. What comes away is unexpectedly heavy and curls on the blade. It has a mild smell, closer to wet flour than to rot. Left in a bucket, it slowly flattens against the bottom. If there is water in the bucket, its edges begin to spread.

For this reason, the older workers do not put scraps down the drain.

They scrape it only where it interferes with access. Around one inspection cover they maintain a bare band a hand’s width across. The animal repeatedly grows into that band, but less quickly now than it did several years ago. Its thickest part follows the outer edge of the cleared space. When the cover is lifted, the fringe beneath it closes before the worker’s torch reaches the chamber.

It is not uniformly cautious.

There is a bolt hole near the unfinished wall into which it sends the same soft projection every spring. The hole dries out during prolonged dry weather. The projection shrivels, darkens, and has to be abandoned. A new one enters when the water returns.

Elsewhere it turns readily from an unsuitable gap. Here it continues into the hole, year after year.

Perhaps there is something below it: a seam of damp clay, a slow leak, a pocket reached during a wetter season. The visible part gives no indication. It rests with its tip deep in the threaded opening, while water gathers along its lower edge.

Its movements are easiest to see during maintenance.

When the pumps are switched off, some of the exposed lobes become thicker. Narrow passages relax, and the body shifts water into places usually held flat by vibration. An end hidden behind the lagging may lower itself several centimeters. The worker hears a soft wet contact beneath the larger drip sounds and looks down too late to locate it.

When the pumps start again, the animal settles gradually into its usual shape. A raised edge flattens. A passage that has sagged across a gap draws taut. The movement continues through the first few strokes, then becomes too small to follow.

During heavy rain, it draws back from the overflow and gathers beneath the plinth. The fringe closes. It can remain that way for days, surrounded by water that would seem ideal for it. Only after the runoff has lost its street smell do the folds reopen.

In the driest weeks, it crosses farther into the court. Not far enough to be conspicuous: a narrow length beneath a loose brick, a soft patch beside an old fixing plate. Its exposed skin darkens. Drops carried along the underside fall at intervals into the clay.

One summer, a worker moved a brick island to improve access to a valve. Beneath the brick lay a broad, thin lobe, fitted closely to its underside. The worker lifted it with the brick, and for a moment the animal hung free: translucent at the edges, threaded with dark internal channels, fringed with folds that closed one after another.

Then its weight drew it loose.

It landed without a splash, spread unevenly, and began to pull toward the plinth. The movement took most of the afternoon. By the time the tools were packed away, only a pale connecting strip remained in the open.

The brick was set beside the walkway.

Nothing occupied the space for several weeks. Then a small end emerged from a crack at the base of the valve cabinet. It crossed the exposed concrete and stopped where the brick had been. It lay there through two maintenance visits, gathering grit along its wet edge, before withdrawing.

The next spring, another end followed the same route.
