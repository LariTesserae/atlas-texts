The animal carrying the dark, round thing is slower than the others. It has to stop where the slope changes, even when the change is too slight to see. Its front feet search the air immediately above the ground. Once they have found the same surface as its hind feet, it proceeds.

It is pale, but not transparent. Its body has the color of a root exposed for the first time, and the same slight tenderness at the joints. There are eight legs, arranged in four pairs, though the rear pair is usually folded beneath it. A narrow collar of fine hairs surrounds its head. When it pauses, these hairs spread until each stands separately in the bright air.

The dark object is held against its underside.

It cannot be an egg. The animal has carried it through too many changes of weather, and sometimes leaves it unattended in the doorway of the flake house. It cannot be food, either, although young grazers approach it with their mouths open. The animal gently turns them away. It does this with a leg laid across their backs, waiting until they choose another direction.

When it sets the object down, the ground underneath stops filling in its footprints.

For a while, a shallow mark remains.

Other animals find these marks. A seam-creature puts its head into one and lies still, its sideways joints slowly settling into alignment. A winged creature lands at the edge, folds its furred wings, and rubs the place where its body met its reflection. The marks last longer near the house. Farther out, they close before anything can reach them.

The pale animal sometimes returns to a mark that has already vanished. It lowers its head and touches the smooth ground with the longest hair of its collar. Then it lifts the dark object again.

There are several animals like this, although it is difficult to count them. One is always near the unfinished walls. Another appears at the western edge after the mist. A third can be recognized by a missing foreleg and by the careful sideways motion with which it approaches water. They are rarely visible together. When two do meet, they rest their collars against one another.

The hairs interlace.

They remain like that for a long time, while traffic passes around their legs. Eventually they separate, and each goes away carrying the dark thing the other brought.

Their mouths are very small, on the underside of the head. They feed where a damp surface is beginning to harden, drawing something out of it that leaves no scrape or hollow. A fed animal develops a faint yellow line down its back. This line moves slowly toward the abdomen over the course of an afternoon. By evening—if the interval after the last water click can be called evening—it has divided into two pale spots.

The spots become young.

At first they are no more substantial than the magnification beneath a grazer’s transparent back. They slip down the adult’s sides and remain between its feet, overlapping one another. A whole brood can shelter under a single parent without changing its outline.

The parent takes them to the ring of posts.

There it unfolds the legs it has kept beneath its body and stands high enough for the young to move freely below. Its collar hangs down around them. The young explore the enclosed patch of air, falling occasionally through the ground to the depth of a feeler. Each time, they climb out more slowly.

As the surface firms, their feet become distinct. First the tips, then the little pale hinges. Their bodies follow. A parent with six young may leave the ring with four. The other two can sometimes be seen through the earth, still moving in the softer country immediately beneath it.

The adult waits for them at the edge until its yellow line has faded.

The dome belongs to these animals, though belonging is not quite a matter of occupation. Its overlapping flakes grow from the same material as their collars. A young animal resting for long enough against its wall will leave a new scale there, thin and damp at first, then curled over the scale below.

Within the dome are several hollows. They are different sizes, and none corresponds exactly to the size of the animal that sleeps in it. The sleepers curl around the empty portion. Their collars spread against the floor, and the fine hairs lie pointing toward the doorway.

The bead of water is kept in a depression beside the entrance. An arriving animal touches it before going in. The bead shivers. On some visits it diminishes; on others it grows.

When the house walks, its occupants stay asleep. The delicate feet beneath the threshold lift first. The rear edge drags briefly, sweeping away the tracks. Usually it travels only far enough to put its doorway opposite a newly formed hollow. Once, during a long mist, it climbed the escarpment and settled with one side of its roof still below the level of the plain.

The animals used the tilted doorway without hesitation. By the next weather cycle, the ground had risen around the house.

They have enemies. The nearly invisible hunters at the bowls take the young, especially those that have only recently acquired definite feet. A young animal approaching water may stop and draw its collar tightly around its head. If it backs away, it leaves the hairs behind, standing in a little circle.

The hunter sometimes closes upon that circle.

For a few moments, the hairs hold the shape of an animal. Then they bend in different directions, and the hunter becomes visible around an empty space.

Older animals seldom shed their collars. They approach a bowl with their rear legs unfolded and their bodies lifted clear of the ground. Each foot is set down separately. Drinking takes a long time. The one with the missing foreleg never goes to the nearest bowl, where a pale crack runs down into the basin. It passes that water and crosses the open apron to a drop beneath a ledge.

During the mist, all of them become anxious about the dark objects.

They bring them indoors if they can. If a house is too far away, an animal crouches over its burden, pressing all its feet into the soil. Moisture gathers along the collar hairs. The object remains dry beneath it.

Afterward, the animal has to pull itself free. Sometimes one foot stays behind, held in ground that has hardened during the weather. The animal stands beside it for a little while. It touches the abandoned foot, then the place on its leg where the foot had been.

A new tip appears there several days later. It is soft and passes through the ground. The animal must return to the posts.

The dark objects are different sizes. Some are no bigger than a grazer’s grain. The largest nearly fills the underside of its bearer and has an uneven surface, with a pale fleck caught in one side.

They do not roll. Set on a slope, they stay where they are put.

Occasionally an animal takes one to the shallow doorway at the boundary. It waits with its head inside and its body outside. Something moves beyond it. The collar hairs draw forward.

Then it enters.

There may be no visible change for hours. Grazers pass over the mound. A shadow arrives and feeds at its damp edge. The suspended drop near the center of the country grows heavier.

Eventually a pale animal comes out. Its collar is short, its legs uncertain. It carries a dark object close against its underside.

The ground at the doorway is briefly marked by two sets of feet: one deep and narrow, the other shallow, with small slips between the prints.

The shallow tracks begin filling at once.

The deeper ones remain until the next mist.
