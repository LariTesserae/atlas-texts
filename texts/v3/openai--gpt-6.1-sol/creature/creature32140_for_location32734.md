The largest animal in the clearing is lying along the inside of the basin’s rim.

You may have rested your hand on it.

Its back has the same cream color as the mineral surface, and the seam beneath it is no deeper than the seams between plates of earth. A little violet shows where its body meets the stone. Only the regularity of its outline distinguishes it: a shallow curve, then a short straight edge, then another shallow curve. The pattern continues beneath the leaning stems.

When it lifts its head, the part you mistook for the rim remains in place. There is more animal beneath the leaves than the visible length of its back would accommodate.

It is about the height of your shin, standing. Its body is broad and low, supported by eight limbs arranged in unequal pairs. The foremost pair are short, with flattened ends; the next are longer and fold close against its sides. The last four rest on the ground like the legs of a small table. All have joints, but the joints are difficult to locate. A limb bends along one line when it supports weight and along another when it reaches.

Its surface is neither furred nor scaled. Small fields of faintly different texture fit together over it, each smooth in one direction and resistant in another. Stroking its back toward the head feels like stroking a dry leaf. In the opposite direction, your fingertips catch without finding anything sharp.

There is a head, though not a neck. The front of the body narrows into a blunt wedge that can lift or turn independently. Two dark depressions lie high on either side. They look like eyes until sunlight reaches them. Then you see that each contains several small, clear surfaces, tilted at different angles.

Most reflect the clearing.

One reflects the underside of a leaf.

Another remains dark even when you lean close enough to obscure the sky.

The animal attends to you with all of them. Its head shifts slightly, and a reflection of your hand appears in a surface that faces away from it.

It does not seem alarmed. Your presence gives it something to investigate.

The two short forelimbs rise and settle near your fingers, carefully enough that you feel the touch before you see it. Their ends divide into three pale lobes. One rests against your skin; another touches the stone; the third stays suspended just above the seam between them.

For a moment the animal holds this position.

Its touch is warm, with a dry softness unlike the ground. You can feel a small tension within the lobe, changing as you move your finger. It does not grip. When you pull away, it lets you.

Then it turns its head toward the grove beyond the clearing.

The turn is slow and entirely visible. There is none of the little six-legged creatures’ abrupt replacement of one posture with another. Its head moves through every intervening angle. Yet when it finishes, the body seems to be lying farther around the basin, although its feet have not changed their places.

A thin strip of violet has appeared beneath your palm.

It lives here, but “here” includes more than the basin.

Parts of its resting place lie under the copper-fringed trees. Others occupy the dry hollow beneath a broad-leaved plant you passed on the way into the clearing. The animal can lie against the basin’s rim while one of its hind feet rests in that hollow. From either place, its posture appears ordinary. You would need to watch both at once to discover that there is only one foot.

It does not stretch between them. Nothing slender passes through the roots. Its body is compact and continuous wherever it can be touched.

The leaves leaning over the basin give it access to these adjoining places. It uses some openings often enough that the foliage has settled around them. A low arch between two stems accommodates its head exactly. A narrower gap admits a forelimb. Others are available only when it presses a leaf aside.

The animal knows these passages by touch. Before entering one, it lays a flattened foot against the nearest stem and waits. Sometimes it withdraws. Sometimes the stem bends a little, and the animal passes beneath a gap that looked too small for it.

It moves with great economy. A step need not carry it very far to bring it somewhere else.

Under the trees it is less easy to mistake for stone. Its pale body stands out against the shade, and its outline has a loose, irregular fullness. The fields on its back become faintly translucent. Violet lines show within them, branching and rejoining without quite forming a symmetrical pattern.

A copper filament adheres to its flank. The animal stops, bends one of its longer limbs backward, and removes it with a delicate pinching motion. It holds the filament briefly before setting it on the ground.

This attention to its own surface occupies much of its waking activity. It smooths one patch, tests another, passes a foot over the underside of its head. Sometimes it folds two limbs against its belly and remains that way for several minutes.

It has a mouth: a short transverse line low on the head. When open, the line reveals two soft ridges and a small, bright cavity between them. There are no teeth.

It feeds where a plant has begun to fit its surroundings imperfectly.

Such places are almost invisible. A leaf might show a gold line that stops just short of its stem. A root junction might sound its clear note twice, the second sound so faint that you would doubt it. The animal pauses beside these places and touches them with its mouth.

A feeding takes a long time.

The soft ridges brace against the stem. Something inside the mouth changes position, catching the light. The animal’s back grows a little fuller on one side. Beneath its skin, two violet lines that previously met now pass alongside each other.

When it releases the plant, no piece is missing. The gold line travels the full length of the leaf.

The animal rests afterward. Its limbs spread, its head sinks, and its dark eye surfaces turn downward. In that posture, the asymmetry of its back is more pronounced. It looks as though it has swallowed something broad and flat.

Later, at a patch of bare ground, it lowers its belly until it touches the mineral plates. The fullness gradually disappears. A seam opens beneath it, no wider than a hair, revealing violet. When the animal rises, the seam closes along a slightly different course.

A pebble that lay beside its foot is now on the other side of the plate.

This is enough to sustain it. There are long intervals between feedings, and nothing in its behavior suggests urgency. It will abandon a stem if one of the small pale creatures is already attending to it. The smaller animal turns without moving, and the larger one waits, its head lowered to the same height.

Sometimes they touch.

The six-legged creature places one fine foot against the broad animal’s mouth. Both remain perfectly steady. Then the smaller one is elsewhere among the rosettes, and the larger resumes its slow examination of the stem.

You cannot readily count how many of the broad animals live on the plain.

Walking between two groves, you may find three resting in separate patches of shade. Each has a copper filament lying near its forefoot. Each has the same shallow notch in the left edge of its head.

If you approach one, the other two places can no longer be seen through the leaves.

There are also individuals with different markings. One has a narrow dark field extending all the way across its back. Another holds a rear limb clear of the ground, although the limb appears sound. They sometimes rest together, touching along their flanks. Their shared outline is difficult to follow, but their heads remain distinct.

They make no calls. At close quarters there is an occasional dry brushing sound as a foot passes over skin.

The basin animal will allow you to sit beside it. It has no particular affection for you, but your changing posture interests it. A bent knee, a cupped hand, the space beneath your lifted heel: it tests these with the same patient attention it gives the stems.

If you sit still long enough, it settles against your leg.

The contact is light. Its warmth matches the stone beneath you. One forelimb lies across the back of your hand, the three soft lobes loosely spread.

Across the clearing, the distant grove remains visible through the copper-fringed crown. Beside your knee, the same trunks show between two leaves.

The animal lifts its head toward that nearer opening.

You feel the forelimb withdraw. Its body leans, compresses slightly, and begins to pass beneath the leaves. For several seconds you can follow the movement: the broad back, the folded middle limbs, the hind feet lifting one at a time from the rim.

Then it is lying in the distant grove.

Its head rests on the pale ground between two trunks. One forefoot is still raised.

You look down. The last of its soft lobes slips across your knuckle and disappears beneath the leaf.
