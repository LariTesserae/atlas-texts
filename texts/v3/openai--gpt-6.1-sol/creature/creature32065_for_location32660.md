The living things are easiest to recognize at their edges.

There, a leaf has to hold itself apart from the stone. Its weight passes backward through a pleated surface, into a thickened rib, down through a joint whose outer sheath is almost black. Along the underside run threads fine enough to disappear when viewed straight on. They carry a little more current when the leaf bends.

The leaf does bend. Its movement is too small to notice without watching the narrow shadow beneath its tip. Over several hours the shadow changes width by less than a grain of sand. The leaf is adjusting its load, keeping an internal spacing open, preserving the thin separation between layers through which its charge moves.

That much effort goes into remaining where it is.

Within a grove, every apparent stillness contains such work. A fan holds its blades at slightly different angles to prevent one surface from starving another of light. A collar around a branching stem redistributes strain. Deep inside a trunk, a conductive thread becomes brittle; neighboring threads take its current while its atoms are released and set back into place. The repair leaves no scar. There is no wounded surface from which something must regrow. The damaged part is kept in service until its replacement occupies the same dimensions.

The oldest leaves are not necessarily the ones that have lived longest. Their matte surfaces are a condition the plant maintains. A dark edge sheds heat differently from a pale one. A thick blade serves as a reserve through which current can be routed when another part of the crown is under repair. What looks like age can be a task.

Still, the plants have histories.

Far inside their stems are small irregularities that maintenance does not remove: a conduit dividing a little sooner than its neighbors, a buried fold around a fleck of mineral, a junction with more branches than it now requires. Such features record the period when the groves were growing. The growth was neither slow nor hurried. Each new surface appeared as soon as the structures beneath it could bear and supply it.

Then the supply and the surface came into balance.

Some crowns still contain the beginnings of leaves made for conditions that never arrived. Their pale folds are packed tightly together, protected by outer layers that have darkened around them. Within, the conducting paths are complete. A little current passes through them. They remain viable.

They may stay enclosed for as long as the plateau stays warm.

The roots are less still than the crowns, although their movement is even harder to observe. Their tips occupy minute cavities in the stone, fitting so closely that there is scarcely a boundary between root and wall. Across that contact, individual atoms change their attachments. Carbon passes into a root. Another substance passes out. A root tip can advance through the mineral without opening a larger hole behind it; the material it relinquishes fills the space its movement leaves.

Farther down, the roots branch along seams too narrow to see from the surface. They encounter other roots there.

Some contacts remain brief. Current crosses a small gap, the local balance changes, and the two ends turn away. Others thicken into enduring junctions. Roots from plants on opposite sides of a grove may share such a junction. So may roots whose crowns stand kilometers apart.

There are no underground cables running cleanly from one stand to the next. The connections spread through branching seams, through dense knots of dark matter, through the thin walls of the buried chambers. Many routes overlap. A change at one root can reach another by several paths and arrive at slightly different strengths.

This gives each grove a kind of awareness of the others, though nothing in it resembles watching.

If one crown receives less light, the condition travels downward as a change in current. Nearby reserves discharge. Elsewhere, a surface that had been retaining heat begins to release it. Most of the adjustment takes place beneath the bare plain. Above, the leaves continue to look immaculate.

The broad spaces between the groves are therefore inhabited too.

Under a smooth cream-colored stretch of ground, a chamber wall may be lined with a tissue as thin as a stain. It has no green surface and obtains nothing directly from sunlight. It takes charge from one seam, holds it, and passes it into another when the surrounding conditions permit. Elsewhere, dense cellular nodules separate useful material from the mineral lattice. Their interiors are crowded with partitions. Each partition sustains a difference in charge small enough to be lost if the walls were only slightly thicker.

These tissues have few reasons to change their shapes. They are already positioned where their work can be done.

In places, they meet the underside of the plateau’s outer skin. There the distinction between living tissue and ground becomes difficult to follow. The cream mineral contains fine structures that recur at the same intervals as those in a root. A fissure cuts through them, exposing clean edges. Along its depth, some portions carry current and some do not. The unlit portions may be structural. They may also be dormant.

The visible plants are the parts whose work requires an elaborate shape in the open air. Beneath them lies a much greater extent of life that can do its work in darkness, pressed against stone.

Whether all of it is one organism is uncertain. The groves preserve local differences. One makes broad leaves with bronze undersides; another develops narrow, densely pleated banks. A deep junction can connect both without making either grow like the other. Yet a grove has no clear limit belowground, and cutting the connections would not reveal a single place where its body ended.

Its individuality is strongest in its habits.

At the terrace edge, a stand routes most of its reserve through the lowest tiers of leaves. Another holds it in the branching collars. The nearest grove keeps an excess of conducting tissue in the roots beneath its eastern side. These arrangements persist through countless internal replacements. Matter passes through them; the pattern remains.

There may be a form of memory in that persistence. A plant keeps capacity where capacity was once needed. A buried network preserves a route long after the conditions that established it have ceased. The plateau holds the consequences of events whose visible traces have been repaired away.

It has no use for the ordinary activity of an inhabited landscape. Nothing must leave a shelter to feed. Nothing must pursue, escape, court, or carry a seed to a better place. The living surfaces gather what comes to them. Below, the mineral supplies the rest.

What remains uncertain is whether this balance is an ending or a long interval.

The enclosed folds have not lost the means to open. At certain root junctions, small packets of organized material are held apart from the active tissue. Their layers are arranged like the beginnings of new nodes. They receive enough energy to remain intact, although none is being sent toward the surface.

The place retains more possibilities than it uses.

For now, its inhabitants spend almost everything on continuity: the flex of a leaf, the exchange at a root tip, the rebuilding of a wall deep beneath kilometers of bare stone. Their work passes into the plain as an even warmth.

At the edge of the grove, the broad leaf holds its tip above the shallow depression. The narrow shadow stays clear. Somewhere within the leaf, a failing thread is relieved of its current. Another takes the load.

The tip does not touch the ground.
