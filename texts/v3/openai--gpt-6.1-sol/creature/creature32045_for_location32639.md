It lives mostly inside the filter bank.

What can be seen of it looks like a little extra thickness beneath the moss: a wet, ivory-coloured edge, no wider than the rim of a fingernail. Roots cross it. A fern has grown through it. In the work lamp’s white light it has the dull translucence of something that has spent its whole life covered.

It is easy to mistake for old sealant.

Follow that edge, however, and it divides. One part turns into a pore in the block; another continues beneath the leaves; a third runs down the ceramic to the waterline, where it ends in a small, flattened lip. Water passes over the lip without disturbing it. Every few minutes the margin rolls inward, so slowly that the movement is difficult to distinguish from a change in the light. When it opens again, a faint brown thread has vanished from the lining.

There are dozens of these mouths. Most face into the bank, where dead roots soften and the filter cloth continues its long decay. A few open into the current. They take what the water has not yet carried away.

Behind them is a body that has never had to preserve a particular outline.

It fills some of the manufactured pores and leaves others open. Its flesh is thin where the roots are thick, thicker in an old recess where sediment gathers. Long portions of it lie against the backs of the blocks, beyond the reach of the lamp. The exposed fringe is only its most recent growth. Older tissue has worked into the joints and spread between the ceramic lining and the concrete, following places where water can remain without quite flowing.

It does not occupy much volume. Taken out and gathered together, it might scarcely fill two hands. But there is no way to gather it without tearing it apart, and no part of the bank through which it could be pulled whole.

The plants are rooted through it. That is how both have come to fit.

At first a root passing into its flesh must have been an injury. Now the flesh makes a sleeve around each root and tightens when the root dies. Decay collects there. New roots find the same passage, entering a moist channel already lined with what they need. Beneath the green, the bank is full of these repeated accommodations.

The snails graze its exposed surfaces as well as the ceramic. Their tracks close behind them. Occasionally one settles into a shallow fold and remains there for several days, eating the film that grows along the fold’s edge. The creature does not close around it. A snail is something to grow beside.

Its sensitivity is spread through the whole body.

A pressure on one part produces a tightening elsewhere. The tightening is usually too small to see: a pore narrowed, a lip drawn flush, a strand shortened by less than the width of a hair. There are fine cords in the wet joints that serve almost entirely to feel the tremor of the wall.

The pump gives them a continuous load.

Through years of bearing it, the creature has developed around that particular violence. Its cords lie taut at the working speed of the machinery. Its soft chambers remain slightly compressed. The deeper pulse from the delivery main moves through it in a different direction, crossing the first vibration wherever the lining joins the floor.

Within that crossing, there are differences it can feel.

It feels a root tip entering a sleeve. It feels a snail lifting the front of its foot. It feels the slow increase in weight before a ceiling drop releases, and then the brief pressure of the drop arriving in the shallow water. Events unavailable to a person crouched inside the roar reach it through their separate paths.

The broad pedestal is nearly outside its body, but not outside its reach. A strand has followed the floor seam to one of the ground-down bolts. There it has spread into a transparent disc over the dark metal. The disc is almost always closed. It has little to eat, and growing farther would bring no advantage. Nevertheless, it remains.

Perhaps that strand began when the pedestal still held machinery. Perhaps it found a trace of oil, or a deposit that is no longer there. Its persistence is one of the few things in the room that cannot be explained by present conditions alone.

When a pump upstairs is replaced, the creature loses its arrangement of pressure.

For a while its mouths remain shut. Fluid moves into chambers that usually stay flat. The visible edge beneath the moss becomes fractionally paler. Then the new machine starts, and a different vibration loads the wall.

Over the following weeks, cords loosen or thicken. Some tissue withdraws from the ceramic; some extends into a joint it previously left untouched. It adjusts with the same slowness by which the pedestal has lost its corners, though it is adjusting to something quite precise.

A hand laid on the filter bank is a much more local disturbance.

Under the fingers, the ivory margin becomes firm. Farther along the bank, two or three little mouths close. Nothing lunges or retreats. The roots remain held in their sleeves. After the hand is removed, the pressed tissue retains a shallow impression for a minute before water fills it out again.

It has no use for the open air above the floor. No part of it will unfold into that space when the hatch closes. It has already reached nearly everywhere worth reaching.

The lamp keeps the leaves growing. The leaves keep making roots, and eventually release them. The reservoir supplies a narrow current carrying small, usable losses from elsewhere. The machinery keeps the wall in motion.

Within the bank, the creature opens another mouth against a softening piece of cloth. Around it, the old pores remain open enough for water to pass.
