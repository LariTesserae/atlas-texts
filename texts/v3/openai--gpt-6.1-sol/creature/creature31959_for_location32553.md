What lives there has no use for the doors.

It comes up under them, through the fine separation between a threshold and the stone beneath it. At first it is a pale thread, flattened where the weight of the building presses down. In the open air it thickens and divides. One branch lies along the lane; another turns back into the house, following a crack that passes beneath the bench. Years later, the two growths may look like different kinds of plant. One has made a low bank of overlapping scales. The other has climbed the inner wall and hung its folded leaves above the bowls. Beneath the threshold they are still joined.

There are many such connections. There are also many that have broken.

The town is occupied by growths whose boundaries are difficult to find.

Their roots are less like buried cords than like seams of dark solder. They spread along the undersides of stones, widening where gas escapes and thinning where the supply fails. A root may run a considerable distance without putting up a stem. Then, at a joint no wider than a scored line, it will make a whole crowded stand of foliage. The bare lane beside that stand is not necessarily empty underneath.

They do not drink. They retain very little that could be called moisture. When a stem breaks, the fracture is clean and pale, with several fine layers visible around a darker core. For a while its edges remain flexible. Then they stiffen, and the broken end closes under a small cap of scales.

Their nourishment arrives warm, mixed with substances that would poison most things living above the doorstep. They take what they need from it slowly. Along the roots, thin deposits accumulate: black at first, then green where exposed, with an occasional copper brightness. Some become part of the root. Others are left behind as crusts, marking a route the growth has abandoned.

The leaves are working surfaces. A newly opened leaf is almost transparent, delicate enough that the scant light shows the ribs within it. As it matures, layer after layer gathers on those ribs. Its color deepens. Its folds become more pronounced. Eventually it is a small, tough apparatus, capable of opening to the warm current and closing again without tearing.

A leaf can remain in service for many cycles. When it ceases to respond, younger leaves shade it out. It curls, loosens at its attachment, and drops.

Much of the glitter in the streets was once alive.

There are differences among the growths, though they are easiest to see during an exhalation. The cushions lift their upper scales only a little; the movement travels across them like a hand smoothing cloth in the wrong direction. The black-stemmed thickets open more abruptly. Their leaves stand away from the stems, exposing dull undersides to the gas. The climbers straighten joint by joint, each hook keeping its hold while the length between hooks changes.

Nothing moves toward anything. Nevertheless, over time, the growth arrives precisely where it can live.

A branch that enters a sealed room remains thin. Its last few leaves may persist long after extension has stopped. A branch that reaches an active fissure thickens behind its tip, puts out side growth, and crowds the opening until the current is diverted around it. Then its own margins become the favorable places. Fresh stems rise there while the old center gradually hardens.

The luxuriant growth in the court contains patches of dead structure. Living shoots pass through them. Some stems use the remains of others as supports, hooking into a lattice built by generations that had access to a slightly different course of gas. From above, all of it would look continuous. Close to the paving, there are small vacant spaces among the roots, and stems that no longer lift when the stone below breathes.

The yellow combs are brief expenditures.

They form on growth that has received enough heat and nourishment to spare. Before opening, they are tight bundles tucked among the leaves. A tremor loosens them. Pressure spreads their branches, and powder gathers along the edges until the rising current takes it.

Most of that powder accomplishes nothing. It settles on roofs, fills bowls, disappears into the dust. A little reaches a warm fissure with the right exposed surface. There it adheres. Through many exhalations, a speck may darken and develop a rim too small to see. Its first root follows the crack sideways. Its first leaf appears only when the root has found a supply sufficient to sustain it.

There is no seedling waiting for rain.

Near the entrance, the long runner grows between infrequent opportunities. The cooler air slows it; the light seems to determine how its newest leaves fold, but cannot feed them. Its older length is dark and firm. At the tip, the pale leaves are still being assembled, their supports not yet fully hardened. Each breath opens them a fraction. Each still interval closes them.

Perhaps the runner will reach the seam. Perhaps the supply beneath it will shift, and it will remain beside the fallen tile until its tip becomes brittle. Either outcome will leave almost the same mark in the dust.

The inhabitants have altered very little in any single season. A stone rises by a thickness smaller than a leaf. A drain acquires a crust. A fallen wall shelters enough warmth for one new shoot. Yet the workshops are becoming thickets, and the court’s paving can no longer be seen.

The tiny bench remains a bench. The bowls remain where they were placed. Growth has passed beneath them without using them, and powder has filled them without being stored.

During the next breath, leaves brush the doorway. A root takes nourishment from a crack below the floor. The bowls receive another fine settling of yellow dust.
