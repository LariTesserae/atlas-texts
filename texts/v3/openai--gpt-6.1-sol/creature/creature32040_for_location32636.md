The inhabitant is easiest to see when something has gone slightly wrong.

A cup overfills. A shelf leans farther than its cords will allow. The stitched vine draws its next length of thread and finds a knot.

Then, among all the movements of water and vegetation, there is a movement that hesitates.

Two fingers emerge beside the knot. They are small enough to pass together through the handle of a jar, broad at the tips, and stained red along their creases. One holds the thread while the other works beneath it. For a moment there is a wrist, with a cuff rolled back from it. Then the knot loosens, the vine lengthens, and a leaf moves across the place where the fingers were.

You might suppose there is a little person working behind the leaves.

There is, sometimes.

Seen across one of the deep shafts, the inhabitant has a narrow back, two bent knees, and a head bowed over something held in its lap. Its clothes are the brown of soaked basketry. One heel rests on a rung; the other foot searches below for a rung that has not yet been made. It wears a cap with a soft, uneven brim, wet enough to adhere to its neck.

It turns at the sound of your breathing.

The face is not blank or monstrous. There are simply too many things between you and it: a hanging root, the handle of a pot, a drop swelling on a cord. Through them you see a cheek, a dark eye, the brief tightening of a mouth around a strand of fiber. By the time the drop falls, the inhabitant has descended.

Its foot leaves a print on the new rung. The print fills with water.

Elsewhere it is less easy to separate from the place.

What looked like a folded sleeve becomes the underside of a moss mat. A bent knee proves to be a cup with the lip still being pressed out. Near the ceiling, a hand closes around a peg, and when it opens there is a pale root gripping the wood in precisely the same way.

Yet it is not everywhere. It cannot attend to every difficulty at once. Sometimes a gutter leaks for several long moments before anyone comes to pinch it shut. A fern leaflet grows crooked and remains crooked. Beneath an overburdened shelf, the clicking insect waits with its splinter raised until a hand reaches out to take the far end.

They work together without greeting. The insect braces; the hand ties. Afterward the insect backs away, dragging one leg over the other to clean off the clay.

The inhabitant keeps things.

Under the largest terrace is a cupboard whose door opens inward into more room than the cupboard occupies. Inside are lengths of thread wound around dry stalks, three smooth stones, a bent thorn, and a little heap of green scraps. These are old leaves that have ceased to grow. It turns them over before choosing one, moistens the edge in its mouth, and lays it against a gap in the foliage.

Not every scrap takes. Some must be lifted away and folded again.

There is also a broken handle which it has kept for a long time. You can tell by the surface: it is dull, without the shine of fresh work, and its fracture has been touched so often that the clay there is darker. Occasionally the inhabitant brings it out and fits it against a jar. None of the jars is the right one. It puts the handle back carefully.

It eats during the flowering.

While the yellow mouths are open, it crouches beside a shallow cup and gathers the pollen floating on the water. It uses a spoon made from half a seed husk. Each mouthful is followed by a pause in which its shoulders settle. The steam makes little wet patches on its cap.

Once, the snail comes over the lip of the cup.

The inhabitant moves the spoon aside and waits. The snail’s repaired jar knocks softly against the seed husk. Neither is in a hurry, although behind them a whole row of terraces is rising through the mud.

When the snail has passed, the inhabitant wipes the rim with its thumb and finishes eating.

Its work continues while it rests, but less neatly.

It sleeps sitting up, somewhere close to running water. The cap is drawn over its eyes. Its fingers remain curved, each holding a little of the position of the last task. In sleep, one hand occasionally presses against the other palm. Below it, a tile pushes upward with a slight hollow in the middle.

This is how some of the deeper pools begin.

During a shower it wakes immediately. Before opening its eyes, it reaches to steady whatever is nearest. Sometimes that is a jar. Sometimes it is a stem. Once it catches a falling drop between both palms and holds it while the floor beneath it takes shape.

Then it lowers the water into its new place.

It does not seem to understand an empty space as something that needs filling. It leaves the shafts open. It leaves the steep passage behind the hanging pots unlit. If a root approaches one of these depths, the inhabitant guides it along the edge rather than across.

There are paths down there that it uses.

You can hear its steps becoming farther away, though no more than a fingernail’s width separates the opening from the back of the stair. A light appears well below. For a while something is being hammered. Later it returns carrying a wet bundle against its chest, and the bundle opens into leaves before it can be set down.

What it thinks of the mountain is difficult to tell.

At the distant thump of a load arriving below, it stops. Its head lifts. One hand rests flat on the clay, listening through the palm. It stays that way until the vibration has passed, then examines the cups nearest the door.

It knows about weight. It knows about work arriving from elsewhere.

It may know about the backs on which the slabs are carried. When your hand rests too long in the opening, the first thing it makes beneath you is not a wall or a trap. It is a support: a shallow rest fitted to the side of your knuckle, with a channel to carry the sweat away.

Only afterward do the three taps come.

They are patient, practical taps. The inhabitant is feeling for the thickness of what it has been given.

If you withdraw, it lets you. The tendril loosens. The small support remains for a time, awkwardly shaped, useful to nothing yet. Eventually a new stem bends into it, or the snail passes over its edge, or it becomes the landing place for a gutter.

Behind the reeds, the inhabitant bends close to examine the fit.

Its cap brushes a leaf. Water runs off the brim. There is just enough room for its elbow when it raises its hand to begin again.
