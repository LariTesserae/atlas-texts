At first you would take it for something left hanging to dry.

It occupies the underside of the western staircase, where a length of dark material lies across three braces. One end trails into the channel. The other is gathered against the wall in folds so close to the mineral glaze that there seems to be no boundary between them. Water runs over it without beading.

Then the hanging end shortens.

Not quickly. It rises by less than the width of a finger, holds there, and rises again. A pale line appears along its edge. What looked like folded cloth has a thickness to it, and the thickness changes as it moves.

It is withdrawing from the cold current.

The thing beneath the staircase is broad and very flat, though neither condition is permanent. At rest it can spread itself across a ledge, scarcely thicker than the moss beneath it. When it travels, its sides gather inward and its back rises into a low ridge. There are shallow transverse folds along that ridge, unevenly spaced. Near the front, four of them deepen into openings.

The openings close when water passes over them.

Its upper surface is the color of soaked roofing felt. Embedded in it are grains of sand, flakes of tile, a little mica. These are not a shell. Some work loose as it bends; others disappear slowly into the surface. A piece of blue glaze, caught near the middle of the back, has been carried long enough for the flesh around it to take its shape.

Underneath, it is nearly white.

You see this only when it crosses a handrail. It lays its front portion over the metal, adheres, and draws the rest of itself up from the water. The underside has no feet in the ordinary sense. There are rows of soft knobs, each able to lengthen, flatten, or divide at its end. On a broad surface they make little sucking movements. Around a bolt, three or four close together and form something remarkably like a hand.

The resemblance lasts until they let go.

It has a front, but not a face. Two short grooves converge toward a blunt margin. Between them is a small, dense pad that it presses against whatever lies ahead: brick, water, the warm jacket of a pipe. There are no eyes to catch the lamp light. When you stand nearby, the pad turns toward your shoes.

It seems to be attending to the pressure of your weight on the paving.

If you move onto the raised stones, it turns farther. If you remain still, it eventually resumes its crossing. The scrape of the fan does not disturb it, nor does the knock beneath the square. But when a loose tread shifts under your foot, its whole underside tightens. It stays fastened to the rail for several minutes.

There are others.

Once you know what to look for, some of the dark seams prove too regular. A strip beneath the lip of the central basin is not mortar. The rounded accumulation behind a drain grate has drawn away from the bars since you last passed it. High in the pattern shop, something rests among the flattened leaves, its pale underside exposed to the warm glass.

They vary greatly in size. The smallest you find is the length of a thumbnail and almost transparent, with a black thread showing through its middle. It lies on the lower side of a fern frond, beside the central rib. The largest occupies most of a disused doorway. Its back is divided by old injuries into lobes that move with a slight delay between them.

That one cannot pass through the doorway anymore. It reaches into the channel with a narrow part of itself and waits there for the water to rise.

Their bodies take the shape of their resting places. An individual that spends its days beneath a pipe has a long depression down its back. Those in the tiled hopper are compact, thick at the center, with edges worn pale by the passing sediment. One on the wall beside the generating wheel has grown around an iron bracket. It can leave the bracket, but the hole remains open through its body, closing to a slit when it draws itself along.

They feed where the water hesitates.

At an outlet, one spreads its front edge into a thin ruffle and holds it against the current. Fine black matter accumulates there. From time to time the ruffle rolls inward, enclosing what it has caught. Others press themselves over the amber-lit patches of algae. After they move on, the green remains, but it is shorter and evenly grazed.

Their feeding leaves a faint sour smell on the stone, soon lost among the oil and wet roots.

They gather in warmer places before the channels reverse. Under the service grilles, they overlap in loose piles, the edges of one resting in the folds of another. A small one can disappear entirely beneath a larger body. Occasionally a pale knob emerges from the pile and presses against the brick, as though checking whether the warmth is still coming.

They do not all arrive together. There is a long period of movement: one descending a pipe, two crossing the shallow film on the pavement, another easing out from behind a ceramic stud. By the time the basin reaches its brass mark, most have settled above the level of the returning water.

A few remain below.

These fasten themselves at the mouths of cellar grates, their bodies stretched across some of the gaps. Water passes around them more quickly. Fibers and loose leaves collect against their backs. They hold until the current slackens, then pull free and feed on what has gathered.

One grate has a bent bar. Whenever the water reverses, a large individual pushes a lobe into the widened opening. Its flesh bulges through on the cellar side. During the next rise it withdraws, leaving the bar unobstructed.

There is a polished hollow in the iron where it braces itself.

Much of what they do resembles maintenance, although not quite the maintenance the machinery originally required. They keep certain drains open and allow others to narrow. They lift mats of roots away from a moving shaft, but let the roots settle across an abandoned gear. A strip of loose pipe wrapping is repeatedly pushed into the same leaking joint. On the other side of that joint, where the small leak feeds a bed of liverworts, the wrapping has been pulled back.

Their choices are local and persistent. A blockage may remain for months in one channel while an adjacent outlet is kept clear of every leaf.

The water travels through the paths that are left.

When two meet, they stop with their front margins touching. Small ridges pass along their undersides. Sometimes one turns aside. Sometimes they remain together through an entire change of current, becoming difficult to count. They separate without haste, and neither necessarily goes in the direction it had been traveling.

The young stay in such contact more often. They ride under the edges of larger bodies, sheltered from the scrape of the metal walkways. At the warm grilles they venture onto the wall, returning when the engine misses a stroke and the vibration changes.

There are very few dead ones.

In a blind recess behind the boiler, you might find a thin, stiffened shape adhering to the brick. Its dark outer surface is intact, but the thickness beneath has gone. Small pale bodies cluster at its edges. Farther down, a loosened portion has fallen into the water and swollen until it resembles a scrap of wet paper.

The large one in the doorway has several such scraps caught along its back. Living tissue has grown over their margins.

It is difficult to tell how long any individual has been here. Their surfaces acquire the materials of the district; their undersides remain soft. An old one may carry brick dust from a wall that has since been rebuilt, or a curved green chip from a lamp no longer hanging above the square. The marks travel with them, slowly changing position.

They do not come out to inspect a visitor. There is no gathering, no withdrawal into hidden rooms. Your presence makes small alterations. A body that would have crossed the paving waits beneath a step. Another uses the wake around your boot to reach a warmer current. If you stand too long beside the basin, one may begin to climb the leather, testing it with a row of pale knobs.

It feels cool at first, then unexpectedly heavy.

When you lift your foot, it lets go. It folds into the shallow water and lies still until the disturbed sediment settles.

Later, from the western arcade, you can see it moving again. It has reached the two sunken paving stones. A slender edge rises from the water and touches the wall beneath the white ceramic markers. The rest of its body follows, spreading against the wet brick.

Above it, a drop swells under a projecting ledge.

The creature waits while the drop grows. When it falls, the front margin opens a little, catches the water, and closes. Then it continues upward, toward the pipes.
