The people who live here can tell when a pump has stopped before they notice the silence.

A cup ceases to tremble against its saucer. The fine hairs on a forearm settle. Someone asleep beside a wall turns over, having lost a vibration that has accompanied them since childhood. In the workshops, people pause with a tool still in their hands. They wait through the interval in which a machine might simply be changing its load.

Then, if the stillness continues, somebody goes to look.

Most of them could not explain the whole mountain. They know portions of it intimately: a run of pipes, three terraces, the chambers beneath a market, the different sounds a bearing makes before and after rain. Their knowledge overlaps. A woman who maintains the orchard lifts can tell you whose kitchen gets hot when the east boilers are working; the person in that kitchen knows which hatch admits the mechanic without requiring her to carry her tools up six flights of stairs.

There are maps, carefully kept. There are also places where the maps say a passage is clear and an old man says it has been full of roots for forty years.

The old man is usually right about the roots. The map may still be right about what lies beyond them.

Children learn the district in fragments joined by journeys. Home to the bread stall. Bread stall to school. School to the shaded steps where someone will be waiting if the upper bridge is closed. A place that appears close across a shaft can be half an hour away on foot, and a room directly overhead may belong to people they have never met.

They learn early not to chase a dropped thing.

A ball, a spoon, a sandal shaken loose by a monkey: whatever goes over the edge is allowed to go. Small children are taught this with games played well away from the railings. An adult rolls a wooden ring beyond a chalk line, and everyone holds still while it passes. Later, the rule becomes harder. There are letters that escape a pocket. There are tools a person cannot readily afford to replace.

Under certain bridges, nets have been installed to catch falling objects. These nets have to be cleared before they become too heavy. The workers who clear them return identifiable possessions when they can, and sell the rest from a table near the maintenance stairs.

Many households own a cup that has fallen farther than any member of the household has ever travelled.

Their homes are fitted into the structure rather than set apart from it. A thick internal wall makes one room warm throughout the year; another has a narrow window opening onto leaves and is cold whenever the wind comes from the wrong direction. In some apartments the floors rise gently toward a corner because they were once part of a drainage slope. People wedge furniture, hang curtains around inconvenient columns, and put shelves into old recesses whose original purpose has been forgotten.

They pay attention to sounds from next door, though not always from curiosity. A scraping bedstead may be merely a scraping bedstead. Water moving behind plaster at an unfamiliar hour is worth mentioning.

There are arguments about heat. About whether someone has diverted a little too much water to a private vine. About the proper day to prune the tree that shades one household’s bedroom and deprives another of morning sun. A flowering branch can pass through three properties without the trunk leaving its basket.

The district has officials for such matters, but people prefer, at first, to lean from a window and speak to whoever is nearest the disputed branch.

The gardeners are recognizable less by their clothes than by what they carry. A narrow blade, a bent hook for lifting screens, a coil of soft wire, spare washers in a tin. Their pockets contain seeds and bits of machinery, sometimes in the same envelope. They work kneeling, reaching, leaning into openings. The older ones develop a practiced economy of movement: they position the bucket before opening the valve, test the edge of a planting cage before putting weight on it, and keep a free hand even when nothing appears likely to go wrong.

An apprentice may spend a morning carrying water that could have been sent through a channel. The channel is being repaired, or a bed has been isolated because of disease, or nobody yet trusts a new joint above a bedroom ceiling. Much of the work consists of providing, by hand, what normally arrives unnoticed.

They know the plants individually. One tree sheds its leaves after a change in pressure. Another has lived for decades with half its roots dead. A jasmine beside a bakery grows lavishly in summer and looks exhausted by winter, when the ovens work longest. Cuttings from it do better elsewhere, but people keep asking for them because they remember its scent on the bakery steps.

There are plants grown for food, plants grown for shade, and plants grown because someone would miss them. The last category occupies more space than the planners intended.

The animals make their own use of these arrangements.

A monkey may learn the hour when a particular shutter is opened and wait above it with an appearance of complete indifference. Doves recognize the carts that carry spilled grain back from the elevators. They follow those carts even when the sacks are empty, walking indignantly around the wheels at stops.

The goats are returned to their pens often enough to understand the journey. What they do not accept is that a closed gate settles the matter. They nose at latches, climb against hinges, and stand on one another’s resting places to reach leaves beyond the wire. Some are fitted with bells of distinct tones so that their keepers can hear which animal has got out.

A missing bell is consequently more troubling than a ringing one.

The smallest creatures occupy the parts people only intermittently uncover. Beetles move through the damp undersides of planting beds. Ants establish routes inside handrails. Geckos spend the day behind signs and emerge after the lamps warm. When a notice is taken down, a pale body sometimes drops from behind it and lies motionless for a moment on the wall, surprised by daylight.

Repair crews have habits for dealing with such inhabitants. They tap housings before opening them. They leave an old piece of flashing nearby when replacing a roof where swifts nest. This does not always work. Animals return to the exact gap that has vanished and spend days searching the new smooth surface.

Some crews take more trouble than others. People notice that too.

The district’s elderly residents spend a good deal of time in places that younger people pass through quickly. There are benches positioned above warm ducts, stools outside small shops, a sheltered landing from which five levels of traffic can be observed. Sitting there, they watch baskets hauled upward, laundry drawn in, gardeners crossing with empty pails.

They remember different routes. They still give directions by a lift that no longer operates or a tree removed before their listener was born. Occasionally this is useful: the old name leads someone to a service door everyone else has overlooked. More often it causes confusion, followed by patient correction.

They do not all love the place. Some complain about the stairs every day. Some keep pictures of flat country on their walls. A few have moved away and returned, unable to sleep where the floor was completely quiet.

There are people who never grow comfortable at the bridges. They cross looking straight ahead, or talk steadily to a companion, or choose the longer enclosed route. No one who has lived here long mistakes familiarity for immunity. A gust can still arrive underneath an awning. A wet leaf can still turn beneath a shoe.

By evening, much of the population is moving something indoors. Chairs, drying clothes, cages of seedlings, tools left to cool. Shopkeepers lower shutters while continuing conversations through the narrowing openings. The last goats come home reluctantly. Someone calls a child whose answer rises from a terrace below rather than the one expected.

In the apartments, windows remain open to the machinery and the birds. Meals are carried into the cooler rooms. A person washing bowls holds each one briefly under the tap, shuts it, wipes the bowl clean.

Later, when the evening watering has finished, a gardener goes back to a bed that seemed slow to drain. She takes off the screen and reaches into the channel. There is a wad of roots there, fine and surprisingly strong, and caught among them a blue thread, two feathers, and the little foil seal from a bottle.

She clears it with her fingers. Water begins to move.

On the terrace beneath her, a man pauses while taking in his washing. A few drops strike the railing beside his hand. He looks up, sees her shoulder through the leaves, and asks whether it is fixed.

“For now,” she says.

He nods and gathers the rest of the cloth against his chest. She replaces the screen, tests it once with the hook, and goes home.
