The woman beneath the tree has a small notch in the rim of one ear. When she turns her head, the light passes through it.

It is not an injury. The edge is smooth, and she sometimes rests the end of her thread there while both hands are occupied. Other people have similar accommodations: a groove along a forefinger, a shallow hollow in the wrist where a bead will stay without rolling. The man who fits the boundary tiles has a broad, flat thumbnail. He uses it to test whether a peg has been driven far enough.

Their hands are otherwise ordinary. They become tired. A person carrying too many bowls will stop and set one down. Someone who has been working with a fine knife all morning may spend the afternoon holding nothing at all.

When the woman rests, she puts her palms against her thighs. Green powder remains in the creases. She does not brush it away until she needs to touch something pale.

There are perhaps thirty people here. Counting them is difficult, not because they hide, but because a count made from the court does not agree with a count made by visiting the houses. A woman seated in a doorway may be the woman you saw carrying a basket behind the wall. She has the same notch in her ear, but the basket is still visible through a gap in the foliage, and someone is carrying it.

The inhabitants seldom trouble themselves over this. If you need a particular person, you bring the thing you need them to work on. The leaf-maker will come to a damaged leaf. The man at the wall will eventually be beside an unfitted tile.

They do have names. You hear them called across rooms, softly, with the expectation that the hearer is close. A name may have several syllables when spoken in a passage and only one beneath a low ceiling. No one corrects either version.

They speak most often about fit.

“Lift that corner.”

“It has warmed enough.”

“Leave room for the foot.”

Their voices are dry but not hoarse. Each seems to begin a little way behind the speaker’s mouth. Sometimes a person pauses to let the last part of a sentence arrive.

They laugh. The sound is brief and quite recognizable. One afternoon a pleated bird tries to land on a carved bird that has been left on a balcony rail. Its feet close on ridges that are not yet there. It slips, flutters indignantly, and settles on the maker’s head. The maker holds still until it has found a secure place.

For several days afterward, people mention this when they pass him.

There are children, though few are very small. They spend much of their time sorting: long splinters from short ones, usable husks from husks that have opened badly, animal parts according to the direction in which they bend. Their work is often interrupted by discoveries. A cup-shaped leaf can be worn over an elbow. Two empty seed cases, pressed together, make a noise that brings the six-legged grazers out from under the shelves.

The adults ask the children not to do this indoors.

A child may sit for an hour with a single grazer on the back of her hand, watching its mouth cut rectangles from a green shaving. She turns her hand as it walks. When it reaches the edge, she offers the other hand.

Its feet leave six faint dents. These remain until evening.

The people eat at low tables or wherever there is room to set a plate. They take the pale fruit apart flake by flake, checking each hollow center before they break it. A tiny chair is set aside. A sleeping creature is left in half the casing and carried to a shelf.

There is no ceremony around these findings, but there is care. The smallest chairs are put where a person might otherwise place a thumb while lifting a bowl. This saves them from being crushed.

At meals, someone usually gathers the flakes that have fallen into the table’s joints. These are fed to the animals in the walls. A flat head emerges slowly between two bricks. Its mouth opens sideways, revealing a pale, ridged surface against which the food is pressed.

The person feeding it must wait until the mouth closes before withdrawing a finger.

Nobody drinks. Nevertheless, there are cups in every house. They hold powder, pegs, scraps of cord. At night a cup is sometimes placed beside a bed with nothing in it. In the morning its owner takes it up and turns it once between the palms before rising.

The beds are narrow frames woven with strips that give beneath a body. Several have become warmer and softer in the places most often occupied. One has developed a joint near its foot. Its owner props it straight with a small block before lying down.

He is fond of this bed. When other people suggest replacing the troublesome strip, he says it has only just begun to settle.

The large animals are treated with a familiarity that takes little notice of their size. People lean tools against them. Children climb onto the pale-backed creature in the courtyard to retrieve fallen seed cases from among its saplings. Before climbing, they touch its flank and wait.

Usually nothing happens.

Occasionally the whole stand of trees inclines a fraction, and the child waits longer.

The animal has a name, though nobody agrees which part of the courtyard it belongs to. Its head rests beside one household’s door; its back shelters another household’s upper room. When a tree growing from it needs pruning, both households come out, and someone from a third brings the knife.

It has never been seen eating. There are old plates fitted into the paving near its mouth, worn smooth in their centers.

The smaller animals are less patient. Some return repeatedly to the same shelf, bringing pieces of themselves that have worked loose. An ear cup appears beside a bowl. A jointed tail is dragged across the threshold by its former owner.

Repairs are made when they can be made. The creature must be persuaded to lie on its side, and it may refuse. One long-bodied animal has gone without a forefoot for so long that the inhabitants no longer remember whether the foot waiting in a tray is meant for it.

It moves well enough. The wall above it has a slight lean.

Making a new animal involves more hesitation than making a leaf. A leaf has somewhere to go. An animal, once completed, may dislike its intended ledge or food or place in the household. It may push into a narrow gap and remain there.

The makers therefore leave certain choices unresolved. An eyelid is fitted loosely. A jaw is given a little extra play. A foot is finished only after the creature has used it.

You can recognize a practiced animal-maker by the number of incomplete things near her chair and by how little she minds them being moved.

One old woman keeps a small, four-footed body in her lap while she works. It has no head. She rests a hand on its back whenever she stops to consider a piece.

Sometimes it presses upward into her palm.

No one asks whether it is finished.

The inhabitants also mend one another, but privately. A curtain is drawn across the doorway, tools are taken from a high shelf, and whoever is helping washes their hands by rubbing them with fine white powder until it falls away clean.

Afterward, the person who has been attended to sits near the door. A new strip at the throat or wrist catches the light differently from the surrounding skin. They are given something easy to eat. For a few days, a neighbor does the work that requires reaching.

Old age is visible chiefly in the number of careful accommodations a body needs. The oldest man cannot turn his left palm upward. He has a table with a notch cut into its edge so he can steady things from the side. His neighbors bring him intricate repairs, especially objects that have already been repaired many times. He knows where to hold them without disturbing the earlier work.

He remembers making the woman’s board.

She does not remember a time before it.

At the end of the day, they put away dangerous tools and leave the others where they will be wanted. Loose animal parts go into trays. Green scraps are placed beneath the balconies. Someone walks through the houses checking that no growing ribbon has tightened around a shutter.

There is usually a little work that continues after the lamps are covered. A cord must be held under tension. A new joint must cool in the right position. Two people sit together in the dark, talking intermittently, each waiting for the other to say that it is time to let go.

Beyond their hands, the house makes small adjustments.

By morning a beam may rest more comfortably against a trunk. A sleeping animal has turned toward the warm plaster. The woman’s board has acquired another green stain, shallow as the print of a leaf.

She carries it into the court and sets it across her knees. The grazers are already waiting beneath her stool. One has brought a pellet; another carries nothing and follows it anyway.

She lowers the bowl so they can reach.

Then she looks up into the tree for a place where the light is still coming through.
