At first it is another joint in the floor.

It lies where the white terrace ends, its body pressed into the pale seam above the basin. There is just enough of it showing to suggest that the sealant was smoothed badly in one place. A rounded swelling, no longer than the last segment of a finger. Two fine lines across it. Something translucent tucked beneath the edge.

Then one of the lines opens.

The animal withdraws a foot from under its body and sets it on the ceramic. It does this very slowly. The foot is broad for the leg that carries it, divided into four blunt pads, each of which makes contact separately. Once all four are down, the leg stiffens. A second foot appears farther along the seam.

There are six altogether, though it seldom uses them all to stand. Two are usually folded against its belly, where their pads touch a shallow row of pits. In that position they look less like spare limbs than parts of a fastening.

Its body has the color of the roots one might imagine beneath the seven plants: pale, almost gray, with yellow showing through at the joints. It is not armored. The skin is smooth and firm, and very thin in a few places. Beneath those thinner places, little bundles run lengthwise. They do not all tighten together.

There is no recognizable head. One end is slightly narrower and carries a transverse slit. Above the slit are two low domes, milky rather than clear. Neither turns toward the lamps.

What seems to be its back is not quite symmetrical. A narrow keel runs off-center along it, then divides near the broad end into three flattened lobes. The middle lobe is the smallest. When the animal is still, the lobes lie against the surface beneath it. When it moves, they lift enough to expose a dark, moist-looking interior that does not dry in the chamber’s air.

It leaves no mark.

Not on the white ceramic, where a mark would be obvious; not on the carbon, whose sheen remains unbroken. Whatever passes between its pads and the floor is recovered as the pads lift. Each step ends with a slight gathering-in, a tightening at the sole, before the foot comes away.

It takes several minutes to cross the width of the sealant.

During that crossing it stops more often than it advances. A foot is raised and held just above the surface. The body inclines. One of the dorsal lobes opens a fraction farther than the others. Then the foot comes down, sometimes almost where it began.

Its hesitation is not weakness. On the steep face of a ceramic ridge, it can hold its entire body by a single forefoot. The other limbs unfold into the air. For a while it hangs there, broad end outward, in a position that looks precarious only if one assumes that down is the direction most important to it.

The roar passes through the attached foot.

Inside the leg, the long bundles shorten in sequence. The keel flexes. The three lobes shift against one another.

There are differences in the sound too fine and too rapid for a person to separate from the overwhelming whole. The animal separates them. Its body is small enough for one end to occupy a pressure that the other end does not. The distances between its feet matter. So does the angle of a lobe, the depth of a fold, the particular ridge to which it has attached itself.

The place is not uniform to it.

The gray basin has passages through it. They cannot be seen, and they do not correspond precisely to the spaces between the welded granules. A passage may run above a rounded lump, descend almost to the metal, and turn sharply where there is no obstruction. The animal follows such routes with the deliberate ease of something moving through undergrowth.

Elsewhere it refuses apparently open ground. On the black terrace, there is a square hole it will approach from three sides but never cross. It reaches a foreleg over the opening, feels nothing a person could identify as contact, and takes the leg back.

Near the plants, its movements become less cautious.

It visits them individually. Not in a fixed order, and not with equal frequency. The plant with the thickened joint receives the longest visits. The animal climbs its socket, braces four feet on the circular rim, and brings the slit at its narrow end against the stem.

A small fold comes out of the slit. It is soft, finely divided, and pale blue at the edges. The fold spreads around part of the stem without enclosing it completely.

For a long time nothing happens.

Then a minute quantity of fluid travels along one of the fold’s divisions. The animal contracts once, not abruptly, and settles lower between its legs.

The plant does not bow under its weight.

There are places on all seven stems where the ivory surface is interrupted by an oval patch. These are easy to mistake for ordinary differences in growth. The animal finds them without searching. At some visits it takes fluid; at others the movement goes the opposite way. Its body becomes fractionally flatter, and the plant’s patch gains a faint translucence that fades before the animal leaves.

The roots and their sealed reservoirs sustain more than the plants alone.

Whether this exchange was intended is not evident. The sockets have no attachments shaped for the animal. There is no little shelter manufactured for its body, no dedicated port at the height of its mouth. Yet the stems carry those patches, and beneath the animal’s belly the folded feet fit into pits with the precision of parts made together.

When it rests, it returns to the seam.

It places four feet on three different materials: two on ceramic, one on metal, one on sealant. The remaining pair fold inward. The lobes settle. Its narrow end is tucked beneath the small overhang, leaving the fuller part of its body exposed.

This position is not quite the same every time.

Sometimes it moves one pad by less than its own thickness. Sometimes it lies farther toward the rear wall. It may remain in one arrangement for so long that a watcher ceases to expect another. Then, without any visible alteration in the chamber, it releases a foot and begins again.

There is no reason for its life to divide into days. It has periods of feeding, movement, and rest, but none answers to the lamps. While it rests, the bundles beneath its skin continue their small separate contractions. The roar holds them in motion.

Occasionally its mouth opens away from a plant.

The blue fold appears, but only at the center. It trembles too rapidly to retain a visible edge. The animal stands very low on its legs while this happens, its pads spread to their full width.

Nothing can be heard.

There may be an answer elsewhere in the tower. There may once have been one. The animal waits with the fold extended, closes it, and resumes whatever route it was following.

It does not seem lonely. That would require knowing what an unanswered act is to it.

Nor does it seem entirely without expectation. At the inspection window it sometimes pauses with its narrow end against the frame. Here its legs straighten to their greatest length. The three lobes lift apart.

Through the frame, the shaft gives it something the floor does not: a long, attenuated vibration from below, altered by every connection along the structure. The animal holds this posture until its limbs begin to tremble. Then it lowers itself and goes back across the black terrace.

A person standing in the chamber changes its behavior at once.

It does not flee from the shoe. It withdraws from the area around the shoe, which is much larger. The new weight has changed the surfaces before the animal reaches them.

It stops. Sets down a lifted foot. Opens one lobe, closes another. For the first time its smooth progress breaks into short, unrepeated motions.

If the person puts a hand against the wall, the animal turns.

Not toward the hand exactly. Toward the altered route through the floor and brackets by which that contact reaches it. It advances, corrects itself, advances again. What has entered its country has no familiar outline. It bears on widely separated surfaces. It moves parts of the country without moving others. Its smallest adjustments are enormous.

Eventually the animal reaches the edge of the person’s sole.

One forefoot rises.

The pads touch the rubber separately, with the same care they give the ceramic. The animal holds there while the leg stiffens and the bundles beneath its skin begin to work.

For a few moments it has a connection to something that is neither floor nor plant, something warm that shifts under its own weight.

Then the person moves, and it lets go.
