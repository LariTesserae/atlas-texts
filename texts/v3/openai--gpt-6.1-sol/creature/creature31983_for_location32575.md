What lives here is broad enough to be mistaken for a stone, but not quite still enough.

On an open slope, its back has the dull gray of old snow worked into ash. Beside a pool, the same back looks nearly black. This is partly the light and partly the coat: close, coarse hairs whose pale tips darken when wet. Grit lodges among them. An animal resting through a windy afternoon acquires the color of the ground immediately around it.

The head is usually hidden. It lies tucked between the forelegs, below the highest curve of the shoulders. Only when a bird lands nearby does a muzzle appear, blunt and wet, with two narrow nostrils that open separately. The eyes are small, dark, and set far enough apart to watch the sky without much movement.

Then it stands, and the resemblance to a stone becomes less convincing.

Its legs are short but not weak. They lift a long body a little clear of the ground. The feet spread under its weight, four thick toes splaying around a smaller inner one. On wet slabs, the pads hold almost without sound. In loose ash, the toes close and the claws do the work. It moves slowly until it comes to a gap; then the body lengthens unexpectedly, crosses, and gathers itself again.

There are usually others nearby.

Not beside it, necessarily. One may be beneath the lip of a lava plate, another in the steam, a third several hundred paces away where a strip of moss marks a seep. Their spacing makes them look solitary. But when the first animal raises its head, the others often do the same.

They listen through the ground as well as through the air.

A resting animal keeps the underside of its jaw against the rock. Beneath the fur there, the skin is bare and thickly folded. The forefeet remain spread. A loose stone clicking downslope, a sheep stepping on a hollow crust, water beginning to knock inside a fissure: each produces a different response. For some sounds, the animal merely opens an eye. For others, it gets up before anything visible has changed.

Their shelters occupy the spaces left inside the lava.

They do not excavate much. The rock is too hard, and the ash is unreliable. Instead they enter through low openings and follow the cavities beneath cooled flows, using chambers where a little underground warmth persists. A good chamber is neither hot nor cold. It has two exits, a dry shelf above the seepage, and a floor that does not grow warmer when rain begins.

Those requirements exclude most of the plateau.

A hollow may be comfortable for a season and then fill with vapor. A crack may admit cold air after frost widens it. The animals abandon such places early, sometimes carrying bedding out of a chamber that still seems perfectly habitable.

Their bedding is made of sedge stems, shed feathers, and the dry inner parts of cushion plants. They bring it in small mouthfuls from a wide area. Inside a shelter it becomes a dense, greasy mat, flattened by several bodies. The youngest sleep in its middle. Adults lie around the edges, with their heads facing different openings.

The young are born dark.

For their first weeks they have almost no pale hair at all. Their skin shows at the ears and feet, reddish against the black coat. They cling to the bedding and to one another with an urgency that makes separating them difficult. When an adult leaves, a young one sometimes retains its grip for several steps and is carried out hanging beneath the belly.

Outside, it releases at once.

Cold stone is one of the first things it learns.

The adults feed chiefly where warm water becomes cool.

They work along the narrow boundary, turning submerged fragments with their forefeet and licking larvae from the undersides. The tongue is short and muscular. The muzzle can remain underwater for a surprisingly long time, while the nostrils close into two pale seams. When feeding is good, the animals advance with their faces beneath the surface and only their backs showing, like stones being slowly pushed along the bottom.

They also eat the roots of sedges, soft shoots, stranded insects, and eggs.

A nest beneath a lava lip is safe from the wind but not from them. They find it by smell, sometimes reaching in with one forefoot while keeping the rest of the body concealed. Birds mob them. The animal lowers its head, closes its eyes, and waits out the blows. If the nest is too deep, it may leave. It does not spend warmth freely.

Yet feeding birds often tolerate one at the margin of a pool. Its feet disturb larvae, and its passage rolls small prey into the shallows. Shorebirds follow at a cautious distance. When the animal stops, they spread out again.

Much of its day is spent choosing where to stop.

A patch of ground warmed from below can spare hours of feeding. Too much warmth dries the coat and injures the skin. The animal tests a resting place with its muzzle, then one forefoot, then its belly. It may settle for a minute and rise again. Along a single fissure there can be several abandoned impressions in the ash before the suitable place is found.

The bare skin beneath the chest is crossed by deep creases. In cold weather the animal draws its limbs inward and folds this surface away. Over a warm slab it spreads itself flat, exposing as much as it can. A group resting like this appears unusually shapeless, all broad backs and short limbs, until a sudden noise makes every body contract.

They make few audible calls.

At close quarters there is a dry churring, especially between an adult and its young. Alarm is a sharp exhalation. Disputes at a feeding place involve open mouths and a low sound felt more readily than heard. But across a basin they communicate by striking the ground.

An animal lifts one forefoot and brings the heel down on a slab. It waits, then does it again.

The sound in the air is slight. Through continuous rock it travels farther. Others raise their jaws from the ground, turn, and sometimes answer. Broken flows interfere with it; loose ash nearly stops it. Around the oldest shelters, where many routes meet on the same body of lava, these exchanges are frequent at dusk.

The animals also move stones.

Usually the stones are small enough to carry in the mouth. Sometimes two adults push a larger fragment with their shoulders, working in short efforts and resting between them. They place the fragments along the edges of shallow runoff, behind existing obstructions, or across a channel no wider than a foot.

The result may be a pool, a wetter feeding margin, or merely a longer route for the water. Many of these works fail. A night of hard frost lifts a stone out of position. Heavy rain sends the whole arrangement downslope. A new fracture empties the pool from below.

The animals return and nose through the remains. Sometimes they rebuild. Sometimes they carry the stones elsewhere.

At the best places, their work accumulates over years. Fine ash settles behind the little barriers. Moss holds it. Sedges root in it. What began as a bare trickle becomes a narrow green shelf, with larvae in the water and cover beside it. Sheep come to graze there. Birds nest. The animals that made it spend part of each evening moving displaced stones back into place.

The older ones know routes that the young cannot yet see.

They cross empty slopes with no apparent hesitation, passing one dark block, then another, and arriving at a warm opening hidden under snow. A young animal follows closely enough to put its feet in the same prints. When fog closes around them, the line shortens. Muzzles touch flanks. The smallest are allowed between the adults.

Occasionally a familiar route ends at a crack that was not there before.

Then the group waits.

One adult advances, belly low, and tests the edges. Another turns back toward the last shelter. The rest remain pressed together, their coats collecting moisture. They may wait a long time before a decision spreads through the group and they begin to move.

Toward evening they leave the feeding margins. For a few minutes there are bodies everywhere that seemed empty: emerging from beneath shelves, lifting from warm stone, wading out of pools.

Their fur holds the water in small shining points. They pause on the approaches to their shelters, jaws lowered, listening.

The last young one is often reluctant to enter. It stands in the low doorway while an adult waits behind it, nudging once, then waiting again. Beyond them the cloud thickens. A flock lifts somewhere in the basin, and both animals turn toward the sound.

Then the adult presses its muzzle against the young one’s flank. They disappear beneath the slab.

A little water runs past the entrance. Inside, something gives a quiet churr. For a while there is the sound of bedding being pulled into place, and then the shelter is still.
