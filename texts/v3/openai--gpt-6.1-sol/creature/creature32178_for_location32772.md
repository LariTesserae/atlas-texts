The first part of it you notice is a small obstruction in one of the old footholds.

Not the crimson tuft. Below that, in the next hollow down, something pale lies across the stone. It looks like a splinter from a shell, caught crosswise. When you put your boot there, it withdraws.

There is no hurry in the withdrawal. One end lifts, the other shortens, and the whole thing slides into a seam too fine to admit the edge of a coin. You feel a faint click through your sole.

On the sheltered ledge, there are more of these splinters.

Some occupy cracks. Others lie along the boundaries between sun and shade. One projects from a finger-sized hole in the wall, curved downward like the remnant of a hook. They differ in color, but not quite as separate objects would: the palest have the same yellow cast, and each dark one carries a thin red line along one edge.

If you sit without moving, a piece of the ledge rises beside your hip.

It is no thicker than a fingernail. Underneath is a row of folded joints, then another row facing the opposite way. The raised piece turns toward you. For a moment it holds that position, its blunt end poised a little above the stone. Then it lowers itself.

Farther down the wall, another piece lifts.

What lives here is mostly fitted into the mountain.

Its body follows a fracture behind the ledge, branching where the fracture branches, widening where the rock permits it. There may be several arm-lengths of it beneath your seat. There may be much more. No exposed part suggests a center. The little plates and splinters are its extremities, each connected to the others by a pale cord lying deep in the stone.

In strong sunlight, the cord is faintly translucent. Dark beads move inside it, slowly and at irregular intervals. They pass through narrow places singly, then gather in wider chambers where a plate meets its stalk.

The creature does not have blood that flows continuously. It sends what it needs to where it is needed.

For long periods this amounts to almost nothing.

It can wait with one limb raised for an entire afternoon. It can spend several days easing a broken grazer shell out of a crack. Meanwhile, elsewhere along its body, a dozen other limbs are occupied. One scrapes salt from the underside of the ledge. One holds a filament taut. One presses against the wall and remains there, receiving the noise of the garden.

Its senses are distributed through these contacts.

The deep notes from the summit cavities travel through its buried cords. The grazers’ shell-strikes arrive as small, distinct shocks. It knows when roots are working into a nearby seam, when the mineral oil rises, when something heavy settles on the ledge. Airborne sound reaches it too, through thin exposed plates that tremble differently in different winds, but the stone carries the finer information.

Your breathing is probably less apparent to it than the small adjustments of your weight.

At first, it withdraws whenever you shift. Later, it stops withdrawing. A pale limb may stay beneath the edge of your boot, touching the leather without bearing against it.

It is measuring something.

Or it has learned how far your foot moves.

***

When the wind slackens, it brings out its broadest parts.

They emerge from under the ledge in a series of folds: six or seven triangular membranes supported by branching ribs. Each membrane is cloudy near its base and almost invisible at the tip. Opened fully, they reach farther than a person’s outstretched arms, although their roots remain crowded into a crack scarcely wider than a thumb.

They have no single orientation. One turns upward, another hangs below the ledge, another bends around the warm face of the cabinet. Their edges continually adjust.

Mineral dust adheres to them.

So do scraps of leaf, shed limbs, empty eggs, the translucent mouthparts of dead grazers. Larger fragments make the membranes dip. The creature folds a portion inward and passes the burden down through several smaller joints, until it reaches an opening beneath a lifted plate.

A whole wing takes hours to consume.

A grazer that walks onto the feeding surface is not consumed at all. The membrane sags beneath it, making a shallow channel back toward the rock. If the animal persists, a little rib rises behind it and pushes.

This distinction is not perfectly reliable. Newly hatched grazers are sometimes small enough to be taken with the dust. Injured flyers occasionally escape because their broken wings still twitch. The creature tests doubtful things with the red-edged tips of its limbs, touching them more than once before deciding.

Around its feeding folds, a few small animals have learned to wait. They take the crystals it dislodges and the fragments it rejects. Their shells are stained the same dull yellow as its oldest plates.

When the wind strengthens, the membranes fold away.

There is no scrambling. Each fold closes over the next, starting at the farthest tip. The last visible surface settles flush against the rock. A shower of dust travels over it, and it is once again part of the ledge.

***

It prepares for the ladder’s release before you can see the rungs move.

The first sign is activity around the cabinet.

Three limbs emerge from a seam beside the coldest depression. They stretch across the face of the stone, searching with their tips. A fourth arrives carrying a collapsed bladder, the remains of one of the coughing scavengers. The bladder is placed in the second depression and held there.

It slowly rounds out.

There is still a sealed pocket inside it. Warmth expands the trapped gas until the bladder strains against the limb holding it down. The creature pierces it, waits, then moves it to a cooler hollow.

It does this with other materials: egg cases, strips of membrane, tightly coiled root fiber. Some soften against the cabinet. Some curl. Some become brittle enough to break neatly.

The resulting pieces are carried back to the ledge and fastened between its ribs.

By the time the lower rung begins turning, the creature has made a temporary shelter beneath itself. The old folds form the roof; scavenged membranes close the gaps. Narrow entrances face away from the strongest wind.

Grazers go into it.

So do several animals that usually hunt grazers. Inside, they find separate pockets, divided by walls too closely fitted for them to see one another. The creature keeps touching those walls as the occupants settle.

When the release comes, its exposed plates press hard against the stone.

The new leaves rush overhead. Flying predators turn through the basin. A tendril lashes across the ledge and catches one of its limbs. The limb gives way at a joint.

For a while the severed part hangs from the tendril, opening and closing its tip. Then a flock passes through and strips it apart.

The creature does not pursue it. It remains spread across the shelter, braced at more points than you can count. Every impact reaches the wall behind your back. Some of the impacts are sharp enough to distinguish from the continuous vibration of the mountain.

Under its body, the grazers keep striking their shells.

It answers them.

You feel the response through the ledge: a short succession of taps, repeated after each violent gust. The rhythm is uneven but recognizable. After a few repetitions, the animals inside the shelter fall quiet.

When the upper rung stops, the creature waits.

It waits through the stopping of the next rung, and the next. Only when the suspended column has begun to collect loose fragments again does it open the first entrance.

A grazer comes out carrying an empty shell caught on one foot.

Then another.

The hunters leave last, by a different opening, lower down the wall.

***

There are marks beneath the creature’s oldest plates.

You see them when it lifts to clear grit from a seam: little parallel scratches cut into the sheltered stone. Some are shallow and bright. Others have widened with age. They occur in groups, with long bare intervals between them.

The limb that makes them is dark and short. Its tip has been worn flat.

After a storm in which much of the new growth is stripped away, it adds several scratches. After a quieter release, it makes one. Sometimes it touches old marks without adding anything.

There are too few to record every storm.

Perhaps they record losses. Perhaps unusually abundant gatherings. Perhaps the groups belong to parts of its life that cannot be recognized from outside. Once, after you have remained on the ledge until sunset, the dark limb emerges and rests against the rock near an untouched space.

It stays there for a long time.

No mark is made.

As the light leaves the basin, its broad feeding folds open again. One rises between you and the wind. Dust catches along the ribs. Through the clear tip you can see the far mountains and a few suspended eggs beneath the ladder.

You shift to leave, and the fold turns aside.

The pale limb in the foothold withdraws before your boot reaches it. Higher up, another rests across a shallow place where the stone has crumbled. It presses twice against the rock, firmly enough for you to feel through your hand.

When you have climbed past, it begins picking grit from the hollow your boot has left.

Below you, the feeding surface turns back into the wind. The ledge is occupied again by something that looks, from only a few steps away, like weathered stone.
