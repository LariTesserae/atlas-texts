The people who live here have a way of turning sideways before they need to.

They do it at doorways, between a goat and a wall, beneath a basket suspended in a passage. Even on the open stretches of the ledge, they carry their elbows close. Someone newly arrived can be recognized by the little disturbances following them: a trembling branch, a scraped knuckle, a pot shifted on its shelf. The villagers seldom notice these accommodations in themselves. They notice when a familiar person no longer makes them—when an old man begins catching his sleeve on the same thorn every morning, or a child grows tall enough to strike her forehead on a lintel she once ran beneath.

They are not all small. There are broad women who carry stone, long-legged men who fold themselves beside low hearths, children whose feet seem to grow faster than the rest of them. Their bodies bear the consequences of the place in different ways. Knees ache from the steps. One shoulder sits higher after years beneath a basket strap. The people who work on the exposed plateau have wind-reddened faces; those who spend their days weaving in the inner rooms may be almost pale.

Hands are easier to recognize than faces in the dimness. There are the green-black fingertips of someone who gathers juniper berries, the glossy callus left by a rope, the thumbnail split by a chisel years ago and never grown quite straight. A person may pass a bowl through a curtain and know who takes it without hearing a word.

Their clothes are wool, chiefly, worn soft where they bend and stiff where they have been repeatedly mended. Sleeves are narrow at the wrist. Loose cloth is troublesome here. It catches on branches, drags through hearth ash, frightens a goat at the wrong moment. A working apron may be made from a grain sack opened along one seam. Children inherit garments with so many altered hems that the lower edge resembles a record of their older cousins’ heights.

They own brighter things as well. A strip of red cloth kept in a jar. Beads traded up from the lower valleys. A little copper clasp that has outlasted the names of its first owners. These come out for weddings, departures, the return of someone thought lost, and occasionally because a woman has decided she is tired of dressing as though every day were a day for carrying dung.

The children know the ledge as a collection of particular places rather than a shape suspended above a drop.

This is the stone that rocks underfoot. This is where the baker’s cat will tolerate being stroked. This is the wall you must not climb because the top course is loose, though the next wall, apparently identical, is allowed. This branch holds your weight. That one only looks as though it will.

They are taught the outer edge early, without ceremony. A small child is brought to the parapet in an adult’s grip and allowed to look down. Some lean forward; some turn away so violently they have to be held against a chest. Nobody laughs.

Afterward, they are given tasks that keep them on the inner side of the paths. Older children earn the exposed routes, first carrying something light, then leading a goat, then going alone. Their games include elaborate prohibitions against touching the ground, which exasperate adults who have spent the morning making that ground safe to walk on.

There is a girl who can identify every household by the sound of its latch. There is a boy who throws pebbles at the choughs and insists he is not trying to hit them. There are two sisters who have made a secret room beneath a juniper, though everyone can see their feet.

Water occupies more of their thoughts than a visitor would guess from looking at the dry lanes.

Above the settlement, reached by the steps through the crack, are hollows where snow lingers after it has vanished from the sunward slopes. Farther back, a seep darkens the floor of a stone cleft. Its flow is poor and sometimes stops. They have enlarged the hollows with low walls, covered what they can against wind and evaporation, and laid slabs over storage chambers in the rock. In the cold season they carry down snow packed into hide bags. In warmer months they bring water in stoppered vessels that knock softly against one another on a carrier’s back.

The descent with a full load is harder than the climb. Nobody makes it casually.

Water arriving in a house has destinations already assigned to it. Drinking jars are narrow-necked and kept in the coolest recesses. A basin stands below washed hands. Vegetable peelings are rinsed over a second vessel. The final grey water goes to selected roots, not simply to whatever plant is nearest.

There are arguments about this. A shrub whose roots pass beneath three houses can belong, in practice, to three people with incompatible accounts of what it needs. Someone finds a new shoot cut away and knows exactly who has done it. Someone’s child spills a cup and is scolded too sharply. At the next meal, the adult who shouted pushes an extra piece of flatbread across the mat without mentioning why.

They are careful people, but they are not careful in every moment. They forget lids. They fall asleep while grain is roasting. They lend a good knife to someone who returns a poorer one. Their habits save them from some mistakes and make others harder to forgive.

Most households keep goats, though keeping is not quite the same as possessing. An animal may sleep in one pen, receive scraps at another door, and be milked by a third person under an arrangement nobody has written down. The goats know these arrangements less well than they know the location of food.

Their milk is sharp and rich. It becomes small cheeses dried hard enough to travel. Their wool is combed out in tangled handfuls, full of seeds and dust. Their dung warms the hearths after drying; their browsing threatens the planting beds that help keep the people fed. Every goat is therefore both useful and an ongoing grievance.

The oldest doe has learned to work a particular gate with her horn. Several people have repaired it. None has quite outwitted her.

Food tastes strongly here: bitter fruit, sour milk, toasted grain, herbs whose oils cling to the tongue. The planting beds provide leaves, pulses, roots, little onions, things grown for medicine, and things grown because somebody likes them. Grain comes partly from small plots beyond the shelter and partly in loads brought up from below. So do salt, metal, and news.

The villagers trade wool, woven bands, dried herbs, seed selected over generations, and sometimes their own labor. A few leave for entire seasons. They return with unfamiliar ways of tying a bundle, with songs that do not fit the local work rhythms, with stories that are listened to eagerly and then disputed.

Those who remain are not uniformly content with remaining. A young man may stand at the parapet watching a route he cannot see. A woman who once lived in a river town may still dream of water moving without anyone carrying it. Another person goes down the mountain once, dislikes the crowds and the damp bedding, and speaks of the journey for forty years.

Their speech travels oddly beneath the overhang. A whisper may carry into the next courtyard while an ordinary sentence is lost behind the knock of a loom. They have learned where to stand to call someone, and where to stand when they would rather not be heard. A name spoken toward one patch of ceiling reaches the upper houses clearly.

Privacy depends on courtesy as much as walls. Everyone knows when a couple quarrels. Not everyone admits it.

The shrines are small because everything is small. A niche holds a dark stone, a twist of wool, a shallow cup rarely filled. Another contains a carved wooden figure whose face has been touched smooth. Different families tell different stories about them. The rituals are easier to agree upon: first seeds set aside, a hand laid against the rock before a long descent, a thread tied when someone is ill.

People who doubt these things still sometimes make the gestures.

The dead leave difficulties as well as grief. Someone else must take the goats. A half-finished length of cloth must be completed or unwoven. A room that seemed too cramped for a living person can become painfully empty after that person is gone. For months, a daughter continues to turn her head at the hour when her mother used to call from the roof.

Old people retain work as long as they can: sorting seed, watching a pot, teasing fibers apart. Some are patient with children. Others were never patient and see no reason to begin. They remember walls before repairs and trees before pruning, but disagree about both. There is an old woman who claims the passage beside her house was once wide enough for two loaded animals. Her brother says she has confused it with somewhere else.

In the evening, when light reaches under the stone, they come toward the parapet with work in their laps. Faces emerge from the dim lanes carrying expressions that were hard to read indoors. A mother finds a burr behind her son’s ear. A man holds a needle farther from his eyes than he did last year. Someone begins telling a story and is interrupted before reaching the part he most enjoys.

Below them, the clouds cover the routes to everywhere else.

They keep talking while they can still see to work. Then one by one they gather their tools, lift the sleeping children, collect the bowls and loose scraps of wool. A goat blocks a doorway and has to be persuaded aside. Someone calls back to ask whether the water jar has been covered.

By full dark, they are inside.
