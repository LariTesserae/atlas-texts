What lives here is difficult to count.

At first there seems to be one plant, spread into an entire country. Then there seem to be thousands: one for every pillar, every basin, every separate plate of tissue pushing its pale edge into the rain. Neither impression survives close examination. A single column may contain several bodies that arrived at different times. A leaf may be fed from two columns. Along an old seam, the vessels of one plant pass into another so thoroughly that no boundary remains except a buried difference in the grain.

There are individuals here, but their outlines do not always coincide with the shapes they make.

A young plant is much easier to recognize.

It begins as a narrow green strip in a spillway, caught across the current by two or three hooked roots. The strip is translucent. Water lifts it, folds it, lays it flat again. At its center is a little thickening, no larger than a closed hand, divided into chambers around a pocket of pale fluid.

Most are washed away before they do anything else.

One that catches against a transverse rib puts out more hooks. Grit accumulates behind it, though the grit is all vegetable: broken vessel walls, fragments of tough leaf skin, pollen clotted by rain. The added weight presses the strip against the floor. Beneath that pressure its lower surface begins to change. Smooth tissue becomes ridged. The roots cease to be separate threads and broaden into pads.

It is not trying to climb yet. It is trying to become difficult to move.

When the next flood comes, the strip may be buried beneath debris many times its own weight. This is often what establishes it. A living pad spreads under the pile, entering wet cracks and joining whatever still has sap. Its little central chamber lengthens. Two folds rise on either side of it. Water pools between them.

By the time the debris has softened beyond recognition, the seedling has made its first reservoir.

The great builders grow from such beginnings. For years they can remain low, adding folds around water, enlarging the area that presses down on their roots. Then a loaded patch begins to rise. What looks like a stalk is formed beneath it: not a shoot stretching into the air, but a succession of chambers inserted under something already heavy.

The basin rises with it.

A mature builder has no permanent crown. Its newest leaves may be miles from the roots that first anchored it. Its oldest leaves may have disappeared into the black strata below, still conducting water through a few surviving cords. Growth is distributed among places where weight, moisture, and living contact occur together.

On the broad upper surfaces, the builder’s life is most visible as changes in thickness. A pool deepens, and a pale swelling forms beneath its lowest point. A seam begins to bear sideways tension, and fresh bands appear across it. A bridge sags; its lower fibers lengthen while shorter, thicker fibers grow along its back.

These changes are not always sufficient. The plants leave many failed answers behind.

There are columns with an extra wall inside them that never reached the chamber roof. There are ridges braced on the wrong side of an old fracture. There are thick ropes hanging from a bridge whose far end was lost before the ropes could reach anything. Some remain alive for years, conducting water into a shape that no longer has a use.

The builders do not clear these away. Another plant may eventually use them.

Along the wet flanks of the columns live smaller kinds, rooted in bark rather than joined deeply into the support. Their leaves are thin, dark, and pressed close to the surface. From a distance they resemble stains. Up close, each stain resolves into overlapping blades, with clear droplets held between them.

They flourish where warm water seeps through a wall. Their roots follow the seep inward only a little way. A growing column can overwhelm them, sealing their leaves beneath new bark; a drying one leaves them curled into brittle brown patches. Consequently, even the largest colonies seldom keep the same margins for more than a season. One edge greens while another dies.

Other plants occupy the broad water.

They are shallow cups with their rims just above the surface, each balanced by a curtain of roots below. Most are small enough to lift in two hands. Older ones may be wide enough to stand in, though their bottoms will slowly yield beneath a foot.

They turn in changing currents by releasing water from one side and taking it into the other. The movement is gradual until a flood catches them. Then the cups collapse into long folds, shielding their delicate upper skins inside. They can travel down several terraces in that shape.

Afterward they open wherever the water rests.

Some root into the floor. Some remain afloat. A cup carried against a reservoir wall may attach there and lose its floating shape altogether, becoming a lip, then a shelf. In sheltered basins these plants can gather into pale, loosely joined rafts. Their leaves rub together with a faint leathery squeaking that is usually lost beneath the water noise.

At certain times they flower.

The flowers are low and unobtrusive, held inside the cups: rings of narrow cream-colored tissue around a wet central opening. They do not need to attract anything. Pollen arriving on the surface water is drawn inward through grooves in the leaf. A ring closes once enough has collected, and the cup sits a little lower as its seeds develop.

Their seeds are the hooked strips that catch in spillways.

Not all of those strips become great builders. Some make another cup. Others thicken into a patch that never rises above the ground. What they become depends partly on what first holds them down, partly on the channels already present in their small bodies. Two seedlings under the same fallen sheet can grow quite differently.

There are inhabitants of the lower tiers as well.

In openings where a little sunlight reaches the old terraces, narrow leaves hang edge-on to the dimness. They scarcely resemble the immense sheets above. Their surfaces are dark red, almost black, with green showing only where a vein enters them. A leaf may spend many years extending a few inches into a shaft of light.

Deeper down, leaves are rarer. Pale roots occupy the cracks between compressed layers, feeding on tissue that has begun to come apart. Some belong to living builders far overhead. Others belong to plants that have lost their illuminated parts but retained their connection to a neighbor’s vessels.

A root crossing one of these dark chambers can look dead for most of its length. Scrape its surface, and the inner tissue is wet. Cut across it, and fluid rises slowly into the cut.

Some such roots still supply an upper terrace. Some draw nourishment from it. In others the flow reverses when reservoirs fill or empty.

The country’s joins make this possible, but not every meeting produces one. Two living edges can remain pressed together for months without fusing. Between them forms a slick layer that keeps their vessels apart. Rain enters, warms, and leaves.

Elsewhere a fresh root penetrates another plant almost immediately. The receiving tissue swells around it. Within days the join begins carrying water.

Even on a long-established seam, portions can close. A band of dense tissue forms across the vessels, leaving each side to survive on its own supply. One side may wither. Sometimes the seam opens enough to admit daylight, and the edges grow past each other rather than meeting again.

Thus the high country contains neighbors as well as continuations. There is no reliable way to tell them apart by looking at the landscape.

Their responses are easier to see.

Before a flood reaches a basin, some root fringes draw close against the floor. Floating cups tighten. The hollow stalks on the banks lean downstream. They have felt the pressure change in connected water, or the strain transmitted through the terrace beneath them.

Plants farther from those connections do nothing until the flood arrives.

When a plateau shudders, fresh growth does not appear everywhere at once. It begins where the shift has concentrated weight. A hollow wall lays down an inward ridge. Several yards away, a previously active seam becomes still. The adjustment moves through the tissue unevenly, interrupted by scars and closed vessels. For days afterward, small pools may continue to tilt.

There is no single pace to the inhabitants. A root tip can turn during one afternoon. A support can keep thickening around a wound long after the terrace that made the wound has fallen. Beneath the new white fringe on a torn wall, old fibers may remain alive in the position the wall held before it broke.

The youngest growth must work around them.

On the highest terrace, the unfinished column has already accumulated a few such differences. One corner receives more runoff than the others. Its wall is thicker and rises sooner. Another corner has a shallow split that opens in the afternoon heat. Water escapes there, keeping the outside wet.

The fold at the rim is still almost transparent. Its veins divide near the edge, then end in tiny rounded swellings. Some will extend. Others will stop and become the tough border of the leaf.

For now the fold cannot hold much. Rain gathers in it and bends it downward. Part drains into the column; part spills over the unfinished edge. Beneath the heaviest hollow, the tissue becomes a little less transparent.

By the next morning there is a faint rib there.

The fold settles onto it, holding a little more water than it held before.
