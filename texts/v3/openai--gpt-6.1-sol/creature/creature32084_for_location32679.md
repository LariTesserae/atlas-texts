At first, it is easy to mistake them for places where the ice has failed to meet the stone.

They are clear at the edges and faintly grey toward the middle. Some are no larger than a screw head. The largest would cover the nail of a little finger. They lie flat beneath the frozen shelves, each with one rounded end and one end divided into several fine points. Water passing over them lifts the points in sequence.

There are usually seven or eight in the hollow. There may be many more beneath the metal plate.

To see one properly, you have to wait until the afternoon light enters the basin. Then the grey centre resolves into grains: black, white, occasionally a fleck with the blue shine of fresh metal. Around these is a clear body, thicker than a film of water but not much thicker. Light catches its upper surface. Its underside remains pressed against the granite.

The grains are not loose. Each occupies a separate pocket. Between the pockets, narrow channels open and close.

One of the creatures can take half an hour to move its own length. It releases the stone at its divided end, extends those points into the current, and lays them down again farther away. The rest of the body follows in increments too small to watch. Where it has been, the rock stays wet after the surrounding surface has begun to dry.

They live at the boundary where ice, water, and stone touch. Deep water gives them nothing to hold against. Dry stone draws their edges inward until they resemble tiny, cloudy beads. In a flowing film, however, they spread almost invisibly, fitting themselves into the shallow irregularities left by the cut.

The court’s sharpness suits them. A drill groove provides shelter along its entire length. A fractured crystal face gives a firm anchorage. Beneath an edge of ceramic coating, two can lie folded together with their points protruding into the trickle.

They are seldom found on the rounded head of a bolt.

Whatever they eat arrives in quantities too small to cloud the water. Their fringes intercept it. At intervals, a point curls underneath, carrying a speck toward the grey centre. Some of the specks disappear. Others enter the pockets and remain there, becoming part of the creature’s weight.

The oldest ones are darkest. They have accumulated enough mineral to be difficult to dislodge, even when the reservoir release strengthens. Young ones are almost entirely transparent. A few black grains arranged in a crescent may be all that distinguishes one from the water around it.

They did not come with the exposed granite. The newly cut seam was empty.

Perhaps they arrived through the reservoir pipe, folded against a fleck of scale. Perhaps something descended behind the outer wall with the first season’s meltwater. Their beginnings would have been easy to miss: one clear patch adhering to stone, then two patches, with a thread between them that broke during the evening release.

They divide slowly. A broad individual develops a pale crease across its middle. For several days it moves awkwardly, the two halves attaching at different times. Eventually the crease opens. The halves remain joined by a narrow strip, through which the dark grains pass back and forth.

When that strip parts, neither half moves for a while.

The bird sometimes takes one while drinking. Its beak enters the water and comes away with a small translucent fold stuck to the lower edge. Most such visits leave the colony unchanged. Once, three of the youngest disappear in a single morning.

The others continue opening and closing their channels.

Cold does not stop them all at once. As ice grows inward from the basin’s rim, they gather along its advancing edge. Their bodies fit into the shallow spaces underneath, where a little water persists against the warmer stone. There they become thicker and shorter. The points fold inward, sealing the grey centre.

A hard freeze can hold them in this shape for weeks.

The first water of a thaw passes over them without effect. Later, one develops a clear margin. Another opens a single point. By evening, the survivors have begun to flatten again. Those that do not wake remain as dull spots beneath the shelf until the ice loosens and carries them away.

Their most rapid movement happens before a shift in the foundations.

A fringe lifts. Several bodies contract at once. The young detach completely and travel a few millimetres with the water; the heavier ones creep toward a groove or the sheltered side of the thumb-shaped ridge. Then the puddle changes shape, and a grain of crystal slides uphill.

Whether they sense a vibration or a change in pressure is difficult to tell. They do not always respond. Sometimes the building transfers its weight and a creature is left stretched across a closing hairline crack. Its clear edge pinches off. Over the next few days, if water continues to reach it, the edge grows back unevenly.

The drain’s reversal is another matter. They assemble near it well before the bead rises.

The smallest occupy the smooth ceramic ring. Larger ones wait on the stone beyond. When the bead swells and breaks, they flatten against the surface, their fringes laid downstream. For a minute, fresh water passes through every open channel.

Afterward, several are gone from the ring. Some return from beneath the plate hours later. Others are never seen there again.

There is no single place in the court that belongs to them in every season. In winter they keep close to the hollow’s open centre. During a mild spell they spread up the branching wet lines, sometimes reaching the lowest half-round drill groove. In dry weather, the whole visible population may retreat beneath the plate.

A person kneeling beside it would hear the drops falling into the space below and see nothing living.

Late in the day, while the basin glows, their bodies become easiest to distinguish. The mineral pockets cast minute shadows onto the pale stone. The fringes show as wavering lines. Two may meet beneath an ice shelf and remain with their clear edges overlapping until the light has moved away.

Above them, the new water turns a loose shelf slowly against another.

One creature is lifted with it. For a moment it hangs on the underside, grey centre suspended over the hollow, its points reaching into open water. When the shelf catches and stops, it extends along the ice toward the rim. It will take most of the night to reach stone.

The shelves freeze together. The maintenance door stays shut. Behind the wall, the freight lift passes upward.

Under the ice, a clear point finds the granite and holds.
