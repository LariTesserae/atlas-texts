The largest inhabitant is easiest to find by its warmth.

Not much warmth. Put your hand into one of the narrow passages beneath the moss and the cold diminishes. There is a surface down there that feels like skin through a layer of wet velvet. It moves against your palm with the shallow, muscular pressure of an animal breathing.

The passage is too small for any animal of that size to have entered it. The moss above is undisturbed.

In another basin, several shelves away, you can feel the same warmth beneath a root. Here the surface is bare and finely creased. Something hard passes underneath it, slowly enough to be mistaken for a bone shifting with the breath. It passes again. There is no perceptible return.

You might assume that these are two animals, each buried in its own patch of growth. But if you keep one hand in the moss and reach the other across to the root basin, the movements fit together. The pressure beneath your left palm eases exactly as the hard ridge comes beneath your right. Between your hands lies a strip of open black rock. Nothing moves across it.

There are many places where the animal can be touched. Very few where it can be seen.

One is behind the split crystal.

Looking through the fern roots, you find a portion of its face. This takes some time to recognize. There is a low, broad cheek, dark as a soaked seed husk, and a closed eye set almost horizontally beneath a fold of pale skin. Fine hairs lie flat around the eye. Each holds a little water.

The eye is larger than your hand.

Its lid moves continually, but it does not blink. A narrow line opens along the lower edge, showing a brown, wet brightness; the line becomes narrower farther along, where the lid is closing. Opening and closing occupy different parts of the same eye. Neither finishes.

It is not looking through the crystal at you. Its gaze is directed down toward something close to its mouth.

You cannot see the mouth from here.

At a pool near the opposite wall, however, there is a jaw.

It rests just below the surface. The fish with square snouts keep their stations above it, their fins nearly touching its skin. What first looks like a pale mineral ridge is a row of teeth: small, blunt, unevenly spaced. Some are translucent. One is dark at the root.

The jaw is in the act of closing.

Water presses out between the teeth, making a thin clear bulge that does not break. A strand of vegetation lies across the lower lip. It is green where it enters the mouth and green where it leaves it. The lips have the softness of a grazing animal’s, though the whole exposed portion is as long as a person lying down.

There is no head connecting this jaw to the eye behind the crystal. No throat passes beneath the intervening shelf. Through the clefts you can see water, turning mineral, and the open dark below.

Nevertheless, once you have seen both parts, their relation is difficult to doubt. The cheek is lifted slightly by the movement of the jaw. A crease beside the eye deepens with its pressure. The face is not scattered at random. It is occupied with one small, continuous act.

Other portions become recognizable after that.

A thick forelimb lies among the roots of the largest fern. Its fur is short and colorless, showing the dark skin beneath. The limb ends in a broad pad, divided by three grooves. One edge presses against the base of the fern; another curls around a stone that remains visible through it in certain lights.

On a high ledge is the curve of a shoulder. Birds stand along it with their feet sunk into the fur. They do not shift when the shoulder moves. The movement is present in the bunching of muscle, in the tension of skin, in the way a tuft leans against another tuft. There is no displacement you can measure against the wall.

Deep in a narrow cleft, something like a tail has a pale underside. It is bent sharply around one of the rotating columns. At first this looks dangerous. Then you see the exact clearance between fur and mineral. A long hair extends into the gap and shivers under the passage of water.

The animal is large, but its size cannot be calculated by adding these portions together. There are places where two pieces ought to meet and do not. There are others where the same stretch of flank seems available from more than one direction. A fold of skin under the moss can also be found beneath a pool, recognizable by three little scars arranged like the points of a narrow triangle.

The scars are old in shape and fresh in substance. Their edges are pale, smooth, and completely healed. Inside the lowest one, a bead of clear fluid stands against the skin.

Nothing here suggests that the animal has ever lain down, although much of it is resting. Nothing suggests that it could get up.

It has the bearing of an animal easing its weight into a comfortable place. The shoulder lowers; the forelimb takes the pressure; the jaw works on the strand between its lips. In an ordinary creature these actions would occur together for a moment and then give way to others. Here you can spend an afternoon with them.

The smaller animals are intimate with it.

The pale six-limbed creatures climb through its fur wherever the fur emerges among roots. They part the hairs with their narrow feet and put their mouths against the skin. There are no wounds. A small patch puckers beneath each mouth, as though responding to suction.

The transparent-backed creatures gather along the creases of its pads. Their bright internal specks travel close to its skin before taking the returning route through their bodies.

Occasionally one of the birds on its shoulder gives a harsh cry. Under your hand, far below the ledge, the animal’s muscle tightens. The cry and the tightening belong together as plainly as a sound and a flinch. You cannot establish which comes first.

It does not appear to attend to these creatures. It tolerates them with the complete ease of a large sleeper tolerating the small movements of its own coat.

Its attention is elsewhere.

Beneath the jaw, in a hollow no wider than a cup, grows a single plant with three thick leaves. The animal’s exposed eye is directed toward it. The forelimb shelters it. The strand held between the teeth comes from its center.

The plant is being drawn gently upward.

Its roots remain embedded. A little of the material around them has lifted into an irregular mound. Pale root hairs are exposed along one side, each tipped with moisture. The central stem bends toward the mouth, neither taut nor slack.

It is impossible to tell whether the animal is eating the plant, helping it out of the ground, or merely holding it. The care is visible; the intention is not. There is a delicacy in that enormous jaw which becomes more striking the longer you watch.

If you touch the plant, the animal notices.

The eye does not turn. The limb does not tighten around your wrist. Instead, you feel a pressure against the underside of your fingers, as if a soft muzzle has been laid there. No muzzle is visible. Its warmth is unmistakable, and its breathing wets the spaces between your fingers.

You can withdraw your hand.

You can also leave it there. The pressure accommodates the shape of your palm. After a while you become aware of the minute roughness of whisker roots, of a lip resting against your smallest finger. The animal seems neither welcoming nor warning you. It has made contact with the thing that is touching the plant.

Beyond that, it asks nothing.

When you move away, there is no moment of release that you can identify. You find yourself standing beside the pool with an empty hand.

Below the water, the jaw continues closing around the green strand. Behind the crystal, the great eye continues its incomplete blink. The forelimb has the weight of a body settling upon it, and the shoulder carries its scattered birds.

For a long time afterward, walking outside the hollow, you may remember it as a whole animal. You remember the breadth of its head, the placement of its feet, the slight bend in its back as it lowers itself toward that small plant.

You did not see those things.

But your hand remembers where its mouth was.
