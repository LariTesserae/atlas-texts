The first creature you meet may be several hours away from the place where you hear it.

A dry clicking rises from a crack beside your foot: five quick strokes, a pause, then a slow scrape. Something answers beneath the ridge. You crouch, expecting to find a small animal in the fissure, but there is only cool air moving over a wall of white fibers. The clicking stops when your shadow crosses the opening.

Far down the shelf, a narrow head withdraws into the ground.

These animals are called seam-runners. Above the surface they look unfinished: a blunt head, two grasping forelimbs, and a body so long and low that much of it remains concealed even while they feed. Below, they unfold six further pairs of legs. Their joints bend close against the body, allowing them to pass through spaces little wider than a wrist.

Their eyes distinguish light from darkness. Their forefeet distinguish things for which people have no names: the tension of a root before it lifts, the difference between sap rising and sap retreating, the tremor of a grazer that has not yet appeared above the horizon.

They eat the soft membranes separating old chambers from new growth. A healthy seam-runner leaves its passage narrower than it found it. Its waste adheres to the walls and hardens into a thin, flexible lining, so that the oldest tunnels can remain open after the tissue around them has thickened.

A runner that encounters one of these ancient passages will sometimes travel in it for days. It calls as it goes. The sound emerges through openings scattered over a mile of ground, and people listening at different fissures disagree about which way the animal is moving.

During the breeding season the runners become particular about echoes. A female tests a chamber repeatedly, shifting her position between calls. If its answering note pleases her, she lays a chain of translucent eggs along the roof. The young hatch suspended over empty air. Their first task is to climb into a crack before the hard-winged insects find them.

Sometimes an entire clutch falls.

For several minutes the chamber rings with the small impacts, and everything nearby that eats flesh becomes very still.

The immense plated grazers have no common name across the plateau. In one settlement they are called broadmouths; in another, road-makers. People who live far from their habitual routes may have a word for their tracks but none for the animals.

A mature one is longer than a house. Its armor consists of overlapping, dull brown plates, each scuffed white along the rear edge. Dust gathers in the joints. Small plants sometimes root there after rain, survive for a season, and disappear when the animal passes beneath a low fold.

Its mouth is not quite a trunk. It hangs in three muscular lobes that can spread into a shovel, pinch together around a loose scale, or flatten against the ground. The inner surfaces are covered with hard ridges. With these it works a lifted edge back and forth until a strip tears free.

It feeds slowly. Across an open shelf you may watch one from breakfast until late afternoon and see it advance no farther than a person could walk in a few minutes. Most of that time it is feeling the ground.

The oldest broadmouths avoid certain healthy-looking expanses. They circle them, sometimes adding a day to their journey. Water keepers examine these places when they can. Usually the rind is sound. Occasionally they find a chamber beneath it so large that a heavy foot might break through.

A broadmouth sleeps standing on six short legs, its belly barely clear of the surface. It wakes to movement underneath it more readily than to a shout.

Its followers know this. The little tissue-eaters that crowd into a fresh trail scatter before the grazer lifts a foot. They are pale, quick animals with moist noses and blunt scraping teeth. In daylight they shelter under the curled fragments it leaves behind. At dusk they spread across the exposed strip, licking sap and chewing the tender fibers.

Behind them come the stitchbacks.

A stitchback’s outline is difficult to hold in the eye. It has a furred belly, a row of narrow dorsal plates, and hind limbs that fold above its back when it crouches. At rest it resembles debris caught against a scale of rind. In motion it opens to twice its apparent height.

It does not chase for long. It listens through its lower jaw, which it presses flat against the trail. When a tissue-eater pauses to gnaw, the stitchback advances. When the prey moves, it stops.

The final leap is quick enough that a watcher often notices only the falling dust.

Young stitchbacks make repeated mistakes. They stalk droplets, loose fibers, the vibrations of their own tails. A litter raised along a busy grazing road loses half its number before learning which tremors can be caught.

Adults sometimes follow the same broadmouth for years. They know where it rests and where it turns. When it dies, they remain beside the body until the larger scavengers arrive, then hunt among the crowds gathering beneath its lifted plates.

The water hollows have their own inhabitants, most of which never experience an unbroken sky.

In a pool beneath a folded root, little round grazers move over the submerged fibers. Their mouths make clear circles in the green growth. They carry air under their shells and release it one bubble at a time as they descend.

Others hang at the surface, supported by fringes of waterproof hair. Their bodies are transparent except for a dark gut and two silvery breathing sacs. When frightened, they dive together, and the pool seems to acquire a second, trembling surface halfway down.

The larger hollows hold animals that resemble lengths of loose root. They grip the bottom with a ring of hooks while a delicate feeding crown opens above them. A crown may be as broad as a person’s hand, divided into hundreds of finer branches. Anything drifting into it is examined, folded inward, and swallowed.

These animals are harmless while anchored. During a failing dry season they detach.

People checking a diminished pool stir it with a bone rod before reaching in. A detached feeder can fasten to warm skin, and the hooks are difficult to remove without leaving pieces behind.

Some pools also contain blind, flattened hunters. They move beneath the other animals, pressing themselves into the lowest folds. Their backs take on the color of whatever tissue they rest against. They can survive a nearly dry hollow by enclosing themselves in a film of mucus and waiting.

A water keeper may remember one particular hunter for most of a lifetime: a wide female with a nick in her rim, living in the same bowl through wet years and bad ones. Children are shown where she lies. They are allowed to touch the water above her, but not to put their fingers against the bottom.

***

At the great trees, an animal can spend its whole life moving and remain within a single limb.

The pale climbers are social, quarrelsome creatures with four hands and a pair of smaller grasping feet. Their fur grows densely on the back and sparsely on the belly. When they hang beneath a branch, they look almost naked.

They eat the swollen buds that form along the edges of the foliage pads. They also eat eggs, resin-softened bark, and the young of anything they can reach without descending too far.

Each troop occupies a succession of feeding places rather than one continuous territory. Its members hurry through stretches of unproductive bark, then spend days around a knot of buds. An old female usually travels first. She tests each handhold before putting her weight on it.

The animals following her do not always use the same route. Young climbers take exposed shortcuts, swing from drying fibers, and sometimes fall.

The lower branches contain their bones.

Flower-feeders arrive when the tree blooms. They are large enough to darken a doorway, with six narrow limbs, a folded drinking organ, and wings supported by long ribs. At close range their bodies smell faintly of heated resin.

They hang beneath the foliage during the brightest part of the day. Their wings continue to move, slowly drawing air over the breathing slits along their sides. Small birds gather around them to catch the insects displaced by each stroke.

At evening the flower-feeders crawl toward the newly opened cups. They drink while clinging upside down. Pollen catches in the dense brush beneath their throats, making yellow or rust-red patches that can be seen from the ground.

A single tree cannot keep them indefinitely. Once the richest flowers have emptied, the feeders launch themselves from the crown and travel to the next rise. They descend gradually through the hot air, then climb in the updraft along the trunk.

The shelled trunk-grazers make the same journey much more slowly.

They begin near the base, no larger than a thumbnail, and ascend while rasping films of growth from the bark. Their shells broaden with age, developing thick lips around the opening. A full-grown animal can obstruct a path used by climbers.

Females leave eggs in protected seams. The young crawl upward past their mothers, who may have stopped moving altogether. Along some branches there are colonies descended from animals that hatched before the oldest living people were born.

A loosened shell can fall for a long time.

People working beneath the crowns listen for the first strike against a branch and get under cover before the next.

***

The people of the high country are mostly seen at work.

Someone kneels beside a pale patch in the rind, a shell blade held loosely between finger and thumb. Someone else stands several paces away with one heel raised, feeling a sound through the other foot. A child sits in the shade of a roof plate, picking grit from a bundle of fibers.

Their clothes are light and close-fitting, made from softened strands twisted into open fabric. Loose ends catch on scales and give small biting animals somewhere to hide. Outer wraps are heavier, useful against the sun by day and against the cooling wind after dark.

Travelers carry a drinking vessel against the chest, under the wrap. They carry spare soles as well. Foot coverings wear through quickly on some shelves, and bare skin receives too much information from a crowded passage underneath: enough to make an inexperienced traveler stumble.

Most people can tell a broadmouth’s approach from a swarm beginning to rise. Specialists hear much more. There are hunters who can follow a stitchback through a chambered ridge without seeing it, and builders who can judge whether a hollow root will carry a signal to the neighboring settlement.

A good builder tries each striking place at several times of day. Heat alters the note. So does water moving underneath. A summons that carries clearly in the morning may become confused with an insect call by afternoon.

People grow irritated when visitors ask why they do not simply shout.

Some do shout. Children call across the open ground, couples quarrel at full volume, and a person finding a lost relative may make more noise than any bird. Ordinary conversation is quiet because much of it would otherwise be missed.

At home, touch supplements speech. Two fingers on a wrist ask someone to wait. A palm resting briefly against the back indicates that a load is passing behind. Family gestures become so particular that an outsider can watch a meal being prepared and fail to notice how many instructions have been exchanged.

The settlements keep stores of dried bud flesh, edible nodules, insect meal, and strips of tissue harvested from fresh grazing roads. Not every part of the ground can be eaten. Some cuts yield bitter fibers; others darken rapidly and bring on a fever. The safest patches are remembered and revisited.

Children learn them alongside the water places.

A child may know how to recognize a cupful beneath a root long before being trusted to open it. The adult presses the child’s hands to the surface, asks what feels different, and waits. On a difficult patch, they return the next morning and try again.

Water keepers bear old scars across their fingers. They also make mistakes. A poorly judged cut can empty a useful hollow into a deeper chamber, leaving a family dependent on another settlement until the pocket recovers. Such accidents are argued over long after the seam has closed.

People move house when a root lifts too far, when a dependable pool fails, or when a broadmouth abandons a road. They take the fittings, the water vessels, the seasoned roof plates. Woven walls are often left because their lower edges have already grown into the substrate.

Years afterward, a traveler may find a seam-runner entering through the old doorway.

***

No one knows how many kinds of living things the rind supports.

A plate lifted for repairs reveals white colonies no one in the household has seen before. A storm brings winged animals from a distant shelf. A dead branch falls from a great tree carrying creatures that cannot endure the heat at ground level; for an hour they creep about in the branch’s dwindling shade.

Even familiar animals change with distance. Broadmouths on one shelf have narrow mouths and dark plates. Those beyond a deep valley may be larger, with brushes of stiff hair around their feet. People compare descriptions when travelers arrive, and sometimes spend an evening deciding whether two names refer to the same creature.

After sunset, this uncertainty comes close to the houses.

Things emerge through cracks too small to have looked like entrances. A glossy head rises beside a doorstep. Long antennae feel along a vessel. Feet hurry beneath the roof plates, then stop when someone turns.

The people bank their cooking heat, cover the water, and check the seams around sleeping places. Outside, the first drinkers are already traveling toward the folds. They come singly at first, then in crossing lines: runners, tissue-eaters, heavy beetles, animals with soft bodies carried high on jointed legs.

There are so many that their feet begin to sound like rain.

Beneath an overhang, a water keeper waits with a shallow bowl. Clear beads swell along the green underside. She catches what she needs, covers it, and withdraws.

A broad-mouthed little animal takes the place of her knee before she has fully stood. Others crowd beside it. By the time she reaches home, the whole underside is busy with tongues.
