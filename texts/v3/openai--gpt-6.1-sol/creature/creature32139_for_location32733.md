The scraping comes from an animal no larger than a house cat, though for a while only its hands are visible.

They emerge beneath a shelf of clay: dark, almost hairless, with long middle fingers and blunt nails. One hand holds the edge of the opening while the other works at a seam. The movements are careful. A few grains fall, then a flake, then a lump that the animal catches before it can strike the ground.

It puts the lump aside.

Behind the hands, two eyes catch what little light reaches the bank. They are widely spaced, amber-brown, and partly sheltered by folds of skin. The muzzle below them is broad rather than pointed. Fine whiskers project forward into the opening, touching its upper and lower edges before the head follows.

The animal pauses with its nose outside.

Its ears turn separately. One follows the movement of a bat overhead; the other remains directed into the wall. There is activity behind the clay, a faint succession of clicks and scratches. The animal listens until these settle into a steady rhythm. Then it draws itself out.

It has a low body, strong shoulders, and hindquarters narrower than its chest. Its coat is brown at the roots and gray at the tips, but the fur along its underside is stained almost orange. Clay adheres to the elbows. Small pale scars interrupt the hair on its muzzle. The tail is thick at the base and thin toward the end, with a bare strip underneath that grips when it curls around a root.

For a moment it hangs halfway out of the bank, supported by one hand and its tail. Then its hind feet find the wall.

It does not jump down. It descends sideways, spreading its weight across every purchase it can reach.

This is how it travels through the hillside. On a crest it can walk briskly, but near a hollow it slows, lowering its head and testing the surface with its fingers. It knows something about the ground that a heavier animal does not: the slight give of a covered passage, the hollow sound beneath a thin roof, the difference between old clay and clay recently laid over an opening.

Occasionally it chooses a route that looks impossible. It presses beneath a root, turns one shoulder downward, and slides into a slit scarcely wider than its head. Its ribs yield. The fur lies flat. The tail follows last, drawing a loose leaf into the entrance.

Several moments later it appears farther along the bank.

There are many of these animals, but they seldom gather. Each uses a network of sleeping chambers, feeding places, and sheltered routes that overlaps with the networks of its neighbors. They recognize one another by smell. At certain roots the bark is polished by repeated rubbing, and the clay below is dark with oil from their cheeks.

A newcomer stops at these places for a long time.

An established animal may only touch its nose to the mark, add its own scent, and continue. Even a close neighbor’s scent can change its route. A female with young leaves a stronger trace than usual; for several nights, others will go around her sleeping bank.

The animal at the opening is such a female.

Two young occupy a chamber behind her, among dry leaf fibers and the soft, bitten stems of creepers. They are already furred, with disproportionately large feet. One lies across the other’s back. Their eyes are closed, but their fingers keep opening and closing against the bedding.

Their mother does not sleep in the deepest part of the mound. Those chambers are warmer, but the air can become poor after rain. She has chosen a cavity with two narrow entrances at different heights. Air moves between them slowly enough to leave the bedding warm and quickly enough to carry away its dampness.

The insects have sealed a third entrance twice.

Twice she has reopened it.

She uses this one when she returns carrying food, because it enters above the nest and lets her lower herself beside the young without stepping on them. Each night she removes just enough of the new wall to pass. The builders begin again after she leaves.

There are places where she feeds on the builders themselves. She opens a small section of gallery, waits for the workers to crowd toward the breach, and takes them with quick movements of her lips and tongue. Soldiers bite her muzzle. She shakes them off or crushes them against the clay with one hand.

But most of her food comes from the larger occupants of the chambers: grubs in damp wood, beetles sheltering beneath loose roofs, soft-bodied creatures exposed when she turns over a piece of litter. Fallen fruit draws her onto the paths. She eats it slowly, rotating each piece in her hands and dropping the skin.

She will spend nearly an hour on a fruit too small to fill a person’s palm.

Tonight she goes first to the pool.

She approaches along a root, keeping out of the mud until she reaches the water. A frog leaves the rim with a tiny splash. The animal draws back, waits, and lowers her muzzle. After drinking, she licks one wrist and rubs the wet fur across her face. A biting insect crawls from beneath her ear; she catches it with two fingers and eats it.

The rain begins while she is still there.

At first she stays under the fern. When water starts running down its stem, she moves uphill, choosing a route beneath overlapping leaves. Her coat sheds the scattered drops, but the thin fur on her hands darkens. At each exposed crossing she pauses to listen.

Her call is among the smaller sounds of the hillside: three low, throaty notes, closely spaced. Heard nearby, it seems too resonant for her size. Farther away it is difficult to distinguish from a frog calling in a hollow.

An answer comes from above.

She stops beneath the bank and answers once more. A young head appears at the nest entrance, ears flattened by the low roof. She reaches up, places a hand against its chest, and pushes it gently backward before climbing in.

Inside, both young nose through her wet fur. One finds the piece of fruit held against her belly and seizes it with its hands. The other follows her toward the bedding.

She turns twice in the confined space, pulls a dry stem out from beneath her ribs, and settles facing the entrance. Her tail lies along the edge of the nest. The youngsters crowd against her, still chewing.

Outside, insects are already gathering where she has broken their wall. A worker explores the rough edge. Another arrives with a dark grain between its jaws.

The female watches them briefly. Then her eyes close.

By morning they will have narrowed the doorway again.
