At first you would take it for something left by the maintenance crew: a length of folded hose on a landing, a sodden coat hung over a rail, the dark backing of a sheet of insulation. It is usually still when exposed. Its upper surface takes the color of whatever it rests against, though imperfectly. On ceramic it is charcoal gray. Against the blue-black leaves it acquires a faint green sheen. On the washhouse steps it remains too dark, and anyone who uses those steps regularly has learned to look before putting a hand down.

The underside is pale and closely pleated. Water remains between the pleats even when it has crossed several meters of dry ground. If surprised, it contracts, gathering its loose edges under itself until it resembles a small, heavily worn bag.

It has no settled outline. In a channel it lengthens, letting the current draw it thin. To climb a railing it becomes short and thick, and a succession of blunt grips appears along its underside. These grips are not quite fingers. They have no nails or distinct joints, but they close securely around a rung and release it with care.

It can pass beneath most doors.

There are places it cannot go: across the brighter road beyond the gate, over an open stretch of roof, into any room where the ventilation has kept the walls dry. It has tried some of these places. There are faint, complicated stains on the threshold of the control room, where it reached inward and then withdrew. The desk fan used to turn. While it did, the creature never came farther than the door.

Its largest parts live below the paving.

How much of it is down there cannot be established by watching what comes up. Sometimes a narrow fold is visible in three courts at once. Sometimes an entire week passes with nothing seen but a pale grip holding the underside of a platform. The older maintenance workers disagree about whether these are separate animals. One says there are seven. Another says there is one, and that it has become thin in the middle.

Neither has seen it completely.

They know which noises it responds to. The deep running of the pumps leaves it undisturbed; the ordinary valve rush may even draw it nearer. But a bearing beginning to fail will bring a portion of it out of the nearest drain. It lays itself along the motor housing, flattening until the tremor passes through most of its body. If the housing is too hot, it holds away from the metal on its grips, leaving a narrow space underneath.

During repairs it remains nearby. It does not interfere with tools in use. Set a wrench down, however, and it may rest an edge over the handle. The worker has to touch it before picking the wrench up again.

It gives way immediately.

Its awareness seems to travel by contact. A footfall on a stair, a change in pressure behind a wall, the weight of a branch lowering into water: these reach it. A person speaking from several paces away does not. Workers who want it to move tap the surface beside it twice and wait.

No one knows why twice works. Tapping once can hold its attention for a very long time.

It feeds where warm water meets something old.

At the outlets, a fringe develops along its edges. The fringe spreads against deposits on the channel walls and stays there through several pump cycles. Later the surface beneath it is clean, though not scraped clean; the brick retains its soft skin of water. It also spends days wrapped around roots, especially roots that have reached through mortar into the buried passages. The plants show no damage afterward. Often they put out a new leaf.

In the deepest parts of the district, where obsolete pipes have been left full, it grows heavy enough to settle against the bottom. Above ground, these periods are marked by an absence. The landings are empty. Nothing touches the motor housings. Then, after a reversal of the pumps, small folds begin to appear at the grilles.

Some are translucent, scarcely longer than a hand. Their grips form and vanish without finding anything to hold.

The mature creature gathers these folds beneath itself. For a while they move together. Eventually one may remain on a wall after the larger body has gone.

This has been observed too rarely to have a name.

The wall at the western edge is one of its regular places. It enters the brick low down, where the surface is always soaked, and can sometimes be seen through missing mortar: a pale thickness pressing slowly into a space that looks much too small.

The upward pulses begin afterward.

There is no visible body traveling up the face of the wall. Instead, the damp deepens in successive bands. Leaves turn. A dark line reaches the lowest stone cup, then the next. When the cups overflow, something fine and nearly transparent occasionally trails over their rims with the water.

On these afternoons, the creature below the wall becomes very still. A hand placed against it would feel a slow tightening and release. No worker does this anymore. It seems discourteous, though nobody could say when that feeling began.

It is not always occupied with feeding or with the ascent.

There are stretches of an hour in which it lies under the gantry, partly submerged, letting the square fill around it. The first water lifts its edges. The stronger flow pulls those edges straight. It holds on until the sluice is nearly closed, then lets go and travels a short distance with the last rush.

It does this repeatedly, even when the water carries nothing useful.

Afterward it searches the seams in the paving.

The finds on the washhouse steps are partly its work. People put things there, certainly. But an object sometimes arrives during a night when no one has entered the district: a porcelain switch cover, a length of braided cable, a brass fitting still packed with black silt.

It carries them beneath a fold of its body and deposits them above the waterline.

It favors things with an opening. Rings, pipe collars, broken handles. The softened chair leg was kept because a deep socket remained at one end. Before leaving an object, it settles around it for several minutes, pressing its pale underside into hollows that have been inaccessible for years.

It does not make a tidy collection. Some pieces are moved again and again. Others remain where they were first placed until roots grow through them. A newly discovered fitting may be laid beside an older one of the same size, although their metals and purposes are different.

The workers leave the arrangement alone.

There is no evident hostility in it. Nor does it offer the easy reassurance of a tame animal. It will withdraw from a bare hand and then, on another day, rest its full weight against someone’s boot. Once it followed a worker as far as the gate, keeping to the wet gutter beside him.

When he stopped, it stopped.

When he opened the gate, it drew back from the light.

He waited with his hand on the latch until a pump started beneath the street. Then it turned—not quickly, but with a loosening of one end and a gathering of the other—and went back into the district.

It spends much of its life returning: to a warm outlet, to a root bed, to the wall where the water rises, to an old pipe that still fills although nothing requires it to. Its paths change whenever a repair is made. It takes several days to become accustomed to a new joint. During that time a fold may remain pressed beside the replacement, lifting whenever water passes through.

By the end of the week it has usually found somewhere else to grip.

And when the bell sounds twice, waits, and sounds again, the portions visible above ground begin withdrawing. They slip down the steps or gather themselves through the gaps beneath the workshops. A few pale grips remain at the drain mouths.

The chain moves overhead.

By the time water wells along the paving seams, almost all of it is below, spread through the passages where the pressure is about to change.
