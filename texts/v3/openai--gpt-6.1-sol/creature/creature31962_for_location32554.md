At first it resembles something the garden has shed.

It lies beneath the thick-rooted plant, in the space between the ceramic plate and the wall: a gray-brown fold with a dry edge, almost the color of the old filter material. One end has caught on a screw. The other rests against a root. Warm air moves the loose fringe along its side.

Then the fringe draws inward.

What seemed to be a torn sheet is skin folded along a flank. What seemed to be a twig is a foreleg, thin below the elbow and ending in a wide, divided foot. There is a head, but it is held so close to the chest that the face remains hidden. Only one eye is visible, a glossy bead with the pale opening of the chamber reflected in it.

The animal is asleep.

It has chosen a spot where a little warmth passes continuously through the ceramic. Its back is pressed into the curve of the root, and its feet touch four separate surfaces: root, plate, wall, the underside of a pipe. Even in sleep it does not hang from any single thing.

It is about the length of a work glove, without its tail. The tail nearly doubles that length, though most of it is hidden behind the plant. Its body is narrow when viewed from the side and unexpectedly broad when viewed from above. Loose skin joins wrist to ankle on either side. Folded, the skin makes it look heavier than it is; extended, it gives the body the shape of a leaf with a head at one end.

It has fur, but not everywhere. The back carries a short, dark pile that collects dust until individual hairs can scarcely be distinguished. The belly is pale and almost bare. So are the inner surfaces of the feet. Those surfaces are covered in fine creases, soft enough to conform to a screw head or the raised lettering on a pipe.

Its claws are small. On bark they would be unremarkable. Here they find the edges of welds, pits in corroded metal, the narrow space beneath a washer. On clean enamel they find nothing.

This limits where it can go more strictly than distance does.

The animal wakes before the daylight has quite left the opening. For a while, waking changes only its eye. Then the nose emerges from the fold of its chest. It is broad, divided by a groove, and moist at the edges. The whiskers are short except for two long ones that curve downward past the jaw.

It touches the plate with those whiskers.

Then it puts down a forefoot and waits.

The tower’s pounding enters through its toes, through the heel of the foot, through its breast where the breast still rests on the root. Most of that movement is familiar. It can separate some disturbances from the continuous tremor: a beetle moving beneath dry fibers, a bird landing heavily on the pipe above, the irregular scraping of another animal on the far side of the rack.

It cannot sense everything in the chamber. A moth crossing the open air leaves no trace it can follow. A bird can pass behind it unnoticed. Its awareness is acute along the surfaces it touches and abruptly poor beyond them.

When it lifts its body, a second shape remains in the hollow.

This one is smaller, and cleaner. It has been tucked against the adult’s bare belly with its face buried in the fur beneath the foreleg. Its tail is no longer than a root tip. It grips with all four feet.

The adult lowers itself again.

There is no sound between them that a visitor could hear. The young animal shifts its mouth, finds what it was seeking, and becomes still. The adult waits with its head raised. Its eyes are dark, but in this light the edges show amber.

For another twenty minutes, it does not leave.

***

Its first journey is only a few body lengths.

It moves around the lip of the plate, under two leaves, and into the filter mat. The young one rides beneath it, held between the belly and the folded side membranes. From above, neither is visible now. A leaf tips. Something dry settles. One reddish root moves and returns to its place.

The adult’s jaws are stronger than its narrow face suggests. It lifts a crust of compacted fibers, bites through the pale underside, and exposes a moist layer full of beetle larvae. It takes several, working slowly. A piece of dead leaf adheres to its nose.

It also eats the soft bases of fallen flowers, the dark growth under a dripping pipe, and the sweet film that appears on certain stems after rain. A mature leaf is too tough. A living root is useful where it is. Much of its food is found beneath things rather than growing visibly on them.

After feeding, it rubs its cheek against the root beside its sleeping place.

The mark is invisible. Another of its kind would stop there, put its nose against the same patch, and either move on or wait. Several live in the hanging garden, but each uses a different resting hollow. They meet on the thicker pipes. Sometimes one has to reverse for a considerable distance before the other can pass.

Their disputes are slow, with heads lowered and feet planted wide. A bite would be dangerous on a narrow support. More often one animal presses forward until the other yields, each pausing whenever the structure carries an unfamiliar jolt.

The oldest has a missing toe and a pale strip across one membrane. It sleeps near the upper cable tray, where the heat is strongest. It rarely comes down to the ceramic basin.

The mother belongs to the lower roots.

She knows the route to the bridge.

***

There is a place near its unfinished end where insects gather after the lights outside have faded.

The metal there stays warm, and a faint outward current meets a stronger current rising through the chamber. Moths caught between them drift sideways, recover, and drift again. The mother approaches along the underside of the rail, belly close to the metal, her tail trailing through the yellow-flowered vine.

She stops before the last socket.

The nest in it is occupied. A soot-colored bird stands in the mouth with its beak open. Its wings lift once, sharply. The animal takes a different route, lowering herself beneath the rail and reaching for the edge of the bridge.

Here she leaves the young one.

It clings to the underside of a broad flange, pressed into the angle where metal meets metal. She touches it with her nose before moving away.

At the end of the bridge she becomes a different shape.

Her limbs spread. The folds along her sides open into thin, dark membranes, furred at their margins. Her tail straightens, revealing a flattened underside. For a moment she crouches with her whole body exposed above the emptiness.

Then she drops.

There is no leap toward the opposite wall. It is too smooth and too far away. She falls beneath the bridge, catches the rising air, and turns toward a lower pipe crossing the garden. The movement takes less time than the descent of a leaf. Halfway through it, her head snaps sideways.

She reaches the pipe badly.

One forefoot strikes first; the other scrabbles against a seam. Her body swings below it, membranes collapsing around her. For an instant she hangs by one hand.

Then the hind feet find purchase.

A moth protrudes from her mouth. One wing continues to move.

She eats it while gripping the pipe with three feet. Afterward she climbs back to the bridge by the long route, through a cable tray and over a cluster of swollen stems. The young one is still beneath the flange. It reaches toward her before she touches it.

There will be other crossings, when it is older. Its first will probably be between two roots only a little farther apart than its body is long.

***

Rain changes her behavior more than darkness does.

As water begins to enter, she comes out onto the exposed pipes and lifts her face. Drops collect in the fur above her eyes. She wipes them away with the backs of her wrists, then licks the wrists. When the shallow basin fills, she drinks from its edge, stopping often to look upward.

Birds land nearby. Beetles move openly over the darkened floor. For a short time there is food in places where she seldom finds it, and she leaves the young one in the root hollow while she makes repeated trips.

The cooling valve’s water is less predictable. She has waited beneath its seam on nights when nothing fell. On other nights the first drops have struck while she was asleep, and she has emerged to find the plant’s leaves already wet.

She drinks what remains in their folds.

Her world contains many such limits. A seam may be warm enough to sleep beside and hot enough, farther along, to burn a foot. An old route disappears when a root pulls loose. An insect on the opposite enamel wall may remain plainly visible for an hour, beyond her reach.

Sometimes she watches that wall.

Her eyes follow the thin-legged hunters as they take their stations. Her nose moves. She lifts one foot, sets it down, and eventually turns back into the plants.

By morning she is beneath the ceramic lip again. The young one has crawled halfway onto her back. A hind foot protrudes from the fur, its pale toes spread against the root.

The opening brightens. Birds begin to move among the pipe elbows. Somewhere below, the engines change pitch, and the loose edge of a nearby plate starts to tremble.

Without opening her eyes, she moves one foot from the plate to the wall.

The young one tightens its grip.

Both settle again.
