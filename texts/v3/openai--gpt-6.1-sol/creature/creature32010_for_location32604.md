The smallest inhabitants live inside the thickness of a drop.

You see them when a bead of water catches the ceiling light and, for an instant, becomes transparent all the way through. There are three or four pale bodies in it, each bent around a darker center. They move without troubling the surface. When the bead lengthens, they lengthen with it; when it breaks free, they gather toward the round end, away from the narrowing neck.

Some fall into the pools. Others remain behind in the wetness on the rail. There is no visible struggle over which happens. Both places contain what they need.

Under magnification they would have faces, though not faces you would recognize immediately: a ring of soft points, a shallow depression that closes, two spots that darken when light passes over them. At ordinary distance they are part of the water’s clarity. A pool can be crowded with them and still look clean.

The animals above them are not simply larger versions of the same thing. There are many kinds of flesh here.

The snails on the conduits have a dense, almost rubbery foot that extends slightly beyond the shell. Its edge is divided into short pads. Each pad can hold independently, so a snail crossing a coupling never has to loosen its entire grip. The shell is neither metal nor stone. It has the grain of shell, visible where light enters the thin margin, but its ridges are so regular that your fingers expect it to turn.

If you touch one, it does not withdraw at once. First the pads nearest your hand tighten. Then a small head appears on the other side of the shell, as though the animal is looking for the source of a change that has reached it through its whole body.

Its eyes sit low, almost against the conduit.

A moving snail leaves no obvious trail. Behind its foot, however, the surface holds water differently for a few moments. Condensation gathers in a narrow stripe, making the route visible after the animal has passed. Nearby snails sometimes turn into that stripe. Sometimes they cross it and continue on.

On long pipes these routes form pale, temporary braids. They fade before they can become a map.

The creatures around the bolt heads are quicker. Their six legs fold sharply upward at the middle, giving them the compact stance of an animal ready to climb in any direction. What seems to be a single little creature often proves to be two, one lying along the back of the other with its legs tucked into the spaces between the lower animal’s joints.

They separate to feed.

One descends into the groove. The other waits on the head of the bolt, facing outward. After a while they exchange positions. There is no signal you can hear, but immediately before the change, both become still.

Their mouths are fine brushes. They work along the groove with short, deliberate strokes, pausing at points that look no different from the metal around them. They carry a little water beneath their bodies, held between the legs. Even on a dry-looking fastener, their brushes remain wet.

The crabs are less orderly than their fit in the drainage slots suggests.

Seen from above, a crab is nearly rectangular, with rounded corners and a back the color of smoke under glass. But when it emerges, its body opens sideways. Legs unfold from beneath the flat shell; the front edge lifts; two very small eyes come forward on broad, fleshy bases. The claws are unequal. One is a blunt wedge, useful for holding a lip or testing a gap. The other is long and delicate, ending in several comb-like points.

At rest, the comb is kept under the body.

A crab uses it to gather material from the flowing water, and sometimes to tend another crab. Two will face each other at the mouth of a slot, one braced with its wedge claw while the other draws the fine points across the soft places around its eyes. The animal being tended leans forward until its shell almost touches its companion’s.

They can remain like that long after the cleaning seems finished.

When a foot approaches, they part without haste. One goes into the slot. The other steps beneath the nearest raised plate. Once the foot has passed, both return, though not always to each other.

The fish are easier to watch and harder to remember individually.

Their colors change slowly, following the surfaces beneath them. A fish crossing from nickel to ceramic carries a dark patch for several body lengths before it pales. When it passes over a seam, the seam remains visible as a stripe along its belly, displaced slightly from the one below.

From certain angles the fish appear to be small errors in the continuity of the floor.

Their eyes undo the illusion. They are clear, round, and set high enough to watch the water above while the mouth remains close to the plate. Some have a second, much smaller pair of eyes near the jaw. These look downward.

They are not motionless, even when they lie in place. Water passes through soft folds behind their heads. A fin lifts and settles. The mouth touches the floor, releases it, touches again farther along. Occasionally a fish raises its whole body by less than an inch and glides sideways, allowing another to occupy the warm patch beneath it.

There is no visible hierarchy in these exchanges. A small fish may displace a large one. The large one waits nearby and returns when the smaller has gone.

Beneath the railing, certain fish spend much of their time looking up.

They do not take every drop. A bead may fall untouched between several raised mouths. Another, apparently identical, brings one fish from several feet away. It arrives without a splash and is back on the bottom almost before the ring has widened.

The broad-winged animals live where air and machinery exchange warmth.

Up close, their wings are surprisingly thick. Each perforation passes through a soft rim that can narrow or widen. When the animal is asleep, most of the holes are small, holding bright points of water. When it wakes, the rims relax in patches. The moisture spreads through the sheet, and its pale color deepens.

Its body is tucked between the wings, a short, furred weight with four grasping limbs. The face is broad, with nostrils above the mouth and eyes sheltered beneath folds of skin. There is a little fur on the cheeks, always damp at the ends.

They can fly, but do so rarely.

More often they climb along the exchanger fins, unfolding one wing to cross a space and folding it again when their feet find purchase. A wing can serve briefly as a bridge for the body. It bends under the animal’s weight without creasing anywhere except along the old lines.

Those hanging in rows touch one another at the wing tips. A movement at one end passes slowly along the bank: an opening, a settling, a series of small adjustments. Sometimes the movement reaches a sleeping animal and stops there. Its neighbor waits, one wing partly extended, until it wakes.

The ivory animals in the basins are the largest you can see in their entirety.

Their feet are broad despite the narrowness of their legs. Each foot has three spreading toes enclosed in a continuous soft sole. On a raised lip, the toes separate enough to grip either side. In water, they lie together.

The paired ridges on their backs are firmer than the skin around them, but not hard. When an animal bends, each ridge divides into shallow segments. Warmth rises from the trough between them. Tiny insects with nearly transparent bodies rest there, aligned head to tail, moving aside when droplets run down the back.

An ivory animal’s head hangs low because much of its sensing takes place near the water. Its bristles spread when it drinks. The skin around its mouth puckers into fine channels, and the bristles gather these channels into a dark, wet brush.

Yet it can lift its head.

When it does, you see the full face: a blunt muzzle, wide-set eyes, and small ears folded backward against the skull. The eyes have horizontal pupils that contract almost to threads in the ceiling light. The animal looks at you for a long time. Its jaw may continue moving while it watches.

Some bear faint marks. A crescent beside an ear. A darker seam along a shoulder. One has a ridge that curves outward instead of running straight. You begin to distinguish them only after you have stopped expecting distinction to announce itself.

They sleep standing, but they also lie down.

First the hind legs fold. Then the body lowers with extraordinary care, displacing water evenly around its sides. Other animals shift as it settles. The basin briefly acquires a slow current, and fish move with it.

The black creatures sheltering beneath them are not their young.

Their feet are different: narrow, divided, capable of closing around a leg or pipe. Their heads are more pointed, and their polished bodies have a fine covering of hair that lies so flat it is visible only against the light.

They feed along the larger animals’ soles.

An ivory animal sometimes lifts one foot and holds it above the water while a black one works beneath it. The posture looks awkward. It may be maintained for several minutes. When the foot lowers, the smaller creature presses its forehead against the leg, then moves on to another.

They sleep against the warm undersides, gripping lightly even in sleep. If the larger animal walks, they wake before its first step and travel with it.

There are young animals here, though they are easy to miss.

A snail no wider than a pellet moves along the sheltered side of a coupling. A crab with a translucent shell occupies the corner of a slot beside a much larger one. Beneath an ivory animal’s neck, another ivory body stands with its back scarcely above the water. Its ridges are only soft folds. It puts its mouth against the bristles of the adult and remains there while the adult drinks.

Growth is taking place at a scale the landscape does not reveal.

So is absence. After several visits to one conduit, you may notice that a particular snail is no longer there. Nothing has closed over its place. Other snails pass across it, and the water continues to bead on the metal.

Deeper down, life becomes difficult to separate from the warmth of the structure.

In the clear tubes, the worms turn slowly against one another. Each has a dark thread running through its body. At junctions they gather into loose knots, then unlace as the flow changes. Some are much longer than the visible section of tube. You can follow one through a bend, lose it behind a support, and find what may be the same body several yards away.

At an inspection shaft, something occasionally passes across the lowest visible light.

You see a curved flank, pale and finely wrinkled. A row of short limbs makes contact with the wall. Then it moves beyond the square, leaving the light unchanged. Whatever lives there has passages broad enough for its body. It need not come up to the plain.

The animals near you accept your presence in smaller increments.

A fish learns the shape of your shadow and no longer shifts when it crosses. A crab keeps its fine claw extended as you step beside the slot. If you sit on the edge of a warm plate, a black creature may settle beneath your knee, where your body has made a patch of shade.

It does not mistake you for anything else. When you move, it watches. When you leave, it chooses another shelter.

For a while after standing, you can see where you were: the displaced animals returning, a little water spreading into the space your heel kept dry. A snail reaches the damp print of your hand and pauses at its edge. Its pads tighten; its low eyes turn toward the empty place above it. Then it crosses.
