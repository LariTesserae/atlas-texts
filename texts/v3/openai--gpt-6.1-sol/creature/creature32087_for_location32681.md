The largest animal in the hollow is easy to mistake for another healed place in the wall.

It rests just above the damp strip, in a depression broad enough to hold its body without enclosing it. Its back is the gray-green of a leaf seen through water. Along its sides, the color thins until the slow movement of its blood is visible. It is about the length of a hand, with a broad head and four short legs held so close beneath it that, at rest, it seems to have none.

Only its eyes clearly separate it from the root. They are dark, low-set, and open.

It does not watch the entrance continuously. One eye faces the basin; the other faces a short stretch of floor where the beetles pass. When an insect crosses that stretch, the nearer eye moves a little. Usually nothing else follows.

Its skin has a fine covering of soft projections, too blunt to be hairs. Water gathers between them. Along its belly they are longer and fit into the living nap of the floor, so that when it settles, the two surfaces interlock loosely. It can sleep on a slope without bracing its feet. To get up, it first lifts its head, then peels the rest of itself free.

This makes a small sound, like separating two wet leaves.

There are three of these animals in the hollow, though you would rarely see all three at once. One occupies the low recess beside the basin. Another lies farther back, where a fold in the wall casts a permanent shadow. The smallest has a resting place above the entrance, reached by a path so narrow that it appears to be a pale crease.

They do not sleep together. If one finds another in its recess, it waits on the floor below. Eventually the occupant leaves, or the waiting animal loses interest. There is no display, no calling. Sometimes their bodies touch as they pass, and both pause until the contact is over.

When they walk, their feet explain the polished paths.

Each foot has three broad toes with a soft pad beneath. The toes spread before taking weight, then close slightly as the body moves forward. Their claws are tiny and almost buried, used only when climbing. On the level floor, they walk with an unhurried rolling motion, belly just clear of the fibers. A journey from a recess to the stalks may take several minutes, interrupted by long pauses that do not seem to have any purpose beyond pausing.

They eat the white pellets, but not directly from the stalk. A pellet must reach the floor first. The animal lowers its mouth over it, presses once, and lifts its head with the food tucked into a pocket beneath its jaw. That pocket fills gradually. Later, back in its recess, the animal swallows with a series of slow movements visible through the thin skin of its throat.

It also eats certain mites, fallen eggs, and the soft portion of leaf fragments. It leaves the veins behind. A beetle may walk between its forefeet without being taken.

At the basin, it puts its whole face underwater.

For a while, nothing moves except the loose edges of its cheeks. Then those edges open into delicate fringes, folded so neatly against the head that they were invisible in air. Water passes through them. A shrimp sometimes climbs onto a fringe and grazes there, picking among the folds while the animal remains still.

When it raises its head, the fringes close from back to front. A few drops remain caught along the jaw. It wipes these away against its foreleg and then licks the leg, taking back almost all the water.

The youngest is nearly transparent. Through its abdomen you can see a pale knot of organs and, after it has fed, the separate white pellets softening inside. It makes its way down from the entrance by the same route every time. Where its toes miss the polished track, the root fibers catch against its pads. It stops, frees one foot, and continues.

An older animal sometimes waits below that difficult place. It does not lift the young one or lead it. It merely stays until the little body reaches the floor, then moves aside.

None of them is the source of the humming.

That belongs to the body in which they live: a branching, water-filled organism whose exposed portions resemble roots because they have spent so long doing the work of roots. Much of it lies under the bank. Some of it spreads through the marsh as submerged cords. Its leaves rise among ordinary plants, broad and unremarkable except for their unusually thick veins.

The hollow is one of its permanent spaces. The organism holds it open as steadily as it holds a channel open for water. Around it, tissue is fed, drained, renewed. Worn paths grow more slowly than unworn surfaces. A resting animal’s belly leaves a slight depression that remains warm after it moves away.

The walls respond, but not quickly. If a stalk is damaged, the slit closes over several hours and another opens nearby. If too much water gathers on the floor, the fibers lean together until a narrow runnel forms. When an animal stays in one recess for days, fresh growth softens around the places where its feet and ribs press.

The animals, in turn, attend to small failures. One noses a translucent scrap away from a valve. Another spends a long time licking a bitter deposit from a seam. Beetles carry pellets into crevices where pale larvae wait. The shrimp keep the basin’s bottom bare. These activities overlap without anyone directing them.

Occasionally the animal nearest the water wakes during a change in the pumping rhythm. It lifts its head before the basin trembles, opens its mouth slightly, and waits. The pressure passes. Its mouth closes. It settles back into the wall.

There is no obvious moment when the great body notices its inhabitants, or when they notice it. Their contact is more continuous than that. They know its warmth through their bellies, its changing moisture through their feet, the working of its chambers through the bones of their jaws when they rest their heads.

Late in the day, the light through the ceiling loses its brightness. The flies leave the syrup stalk one by one. A beetle carries the last fallen pellet under the edge of a root.

The gray-green animal comes down to drink. It crosses the open floor, placing each broad foot in the old path, and lowers its face into the trembling water. Behind it, the nap slowly rises where its body has brushed it flat.
