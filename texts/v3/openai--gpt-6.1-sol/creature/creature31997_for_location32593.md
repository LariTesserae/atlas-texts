What lives here is difficult to count.

A stem rises beside the stairway, as slender as a cord, with leaves spaced along it in opposite pairs. Each leaf is narrow at its attachment and widens toward a blunt, translucent tip. In the weak light, the veins show more clearly than the flesh around them. There is no flower, no conspicuous seed vessel. Halfway down the stem, below the living leaves, a small white root has emerged from a joint.

It reaches sideways into the mat.

Follow that root and it becomes a thicker strand, buried under brown leaves. Follow the strand and it passes beneath the stair, through a seam in the masonry, and into the next court. There it gives rise to other stems. Some resemble the first exactly. Others have broader leaves, or longer bare intervals between them, having grown in darker water.

What appears to be a crowd of plants may be one plant. What appears to be one continuous bed may contain several, fitted so closely together that their boundaries cannot be found without lifting the whole growth from the floor.

No one lifts it.

The oldest portions lie beneath the younger ones, alive in some places and hollow in others. Their outer skins persist after their centers have softened. New roots travel through these empty sleeves. A strand can lose its connection behind it and continue growing ahead; two ends that once belonged to the same body can come to occupy separate rooms.

There is no fixed shape to the inhabitant. There are only the shapes it is currently able to maintain.

On the roofs, it grows low and dense. The leaves crowd one another, dividing the little light among so many surfaces that the lower layers remain brown. Along a broken roof edge, it hangs in long curtains. In a chamber reached by a single bright crack, all the new stems lean toward that crack, and the older ones gradually disappear beneath them.

Its newest leaves are tightly folded. They look like dark needles until they open. The opening is so slow that a leaf watched for an hour seems unchanged. Yet after a long interval, the needle has become a blade, and the blade has turned enough to receive the gray light coming through the water.

This is the most visible life here. There is smaller life on it.

The leaves are coated in films too thin to alter their outlines. Some films feed where light still reaches; others live on substances seeping from the plant, or on the first damage along an aging edge. Under an overhang, where a dead stem rests against stone, pale growth spreads from the point of contact. It has no leaves. Its fine threads enter softened tissue and extend between the fibers.

A fallen leaf is occupied from both sides.

For a long time it keeps its shape. Then its surface becomes fragile, and a current takes a little of it away. The threads remain briefly in the shape of what they consumed: a pale blade attached to a pale stalk. Eventually these, too, settle or disperse.

Deeper in the mud, life proceeds without light. Thin bands of it follow buried leaves, old wood, and the dark contents of broken jars. Where a wall has recently slipped, exposing a fresh face of sediment, these bands can be seen as slight changes of color. Cream beneath brown. Gray beneath cream. Here and there, a nearly black line.

They have been growing at depths the green stems never reach.

A little gas gathers in their work. It collects under a paving slab or in the upper curve of a buried vessel. Much later, a bubble escapes through the roots and rises past a living leaf. The leaf moves aside and returns. Nothing pursues the bubble. Nothing notices its arrival at the ceiling.

The green growth is not entirely unmarked, despite the absence of mouths.

Some leaves bear round dead patches. Some stems narrow unexpectedly and break at the narrow place. A court that was dense with growth can become sparse while the court beside it remains full. Something small has multiplied in the roots, or the sediment has changed, or too many layers of old growth have accumulated over the buried joints.

The survivors grow around the failing patch.

At its edge, roots branch into the darkened mass. They take up what decay releases. New leaves stand above the collapsed ones, and after enough time the difference between the two courts becomes difficult to see.

There are no seasons of hurried abundance. The cold permits only a little growth at once, and the cover overhead makes even that uncertain. A gap may open far above, admitting a faint increase of light for several days. Leaves begin to form near the growing tips. Then floating stems close the gap, and some of those leaves never finish opening. They remain folded until they soften.

Others persist.

A single blade may outlast several neighboring shoots. Silt settles on it; a change in the current clears it. Its tip wears thin. Its base thickens. Eventually a root emerges from the joint below it, and another stem begins.

Near the broad opening beside the stair, a strand has crossed the threshold.

It entered along the floor, where the water carries a little sediment into the adjoining court. Its first roots found almost nothing between the slabs. Farther in, a broken tile held a pocket of mud. The strand branched there.

Now there are six upright shoots beyond the doorway. Three carry open leaves. One is still folded. Two have failed, though their stems remain attached. The connecting root is pale where it crosses clean stone and brown where sediment covers it.

It has not yet reached the colonnade.

Much of that court is empty. The paving continues through dim water, with old leaves resting in its joints. Beyond the nearest column lies a shallow deposit rich enough to support more growth, but the strand has to reach it first.

At its end is a small, firm bud.

Around it the stones are settling, the dead leaves are being consumed, and water passes through the opening with scarcely enough force to bend a stem. The bud adds a little length. Behind it, roots hold to the uneven rim of the broken tile.
