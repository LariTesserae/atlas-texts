The pale animal on the stair has no face that can be seen from above.

What looks like its back is a shallow bowl of cartilage, translucent at the edges, with grains of ceramic embedded in the skin. Underneath, a ring of soft feet holds it to the stone. It loosens the upstream feet when a boat passes and tightens the others, letting the wave lift it without peeling it away. In the pocket between its body and the stair, it keeps a small quantity of water almost still.

Things settle there.

Flour from a floating kitchen. Scales. Fibers from clothing. The delicate bodies of animals too small to hold their place in the current. A fringe beneath the pale rim sorts these, taking some inward and pushing others away. Once every few minutes, a pellet rolls out from under it and drops to the next step.

There are hundreds along the market stair, although most look like worn patches in the stone. Older ones have acquired a darker crust. Some have grown around the heads of bolts.

The woman who cleans the stair leaves them alone unless they obstruct a drain.

She comes down after the evening surge with a short-handled scraper, a basket clipped to her hip, and a lamp that throws light sideways across the risers. Her name is Iven. When she was younger, she could clean the whole flight before the kitchens closed. Now she does the upper half while her nephew does the lower. They meet where the water reaches his thighs.

Iven has broad feet, blunt fingers, and a thick fold of skin that closes the outer opening of each ear. These are ordinary features here. In the baths, where people wash the grit from those folds, nobody looks twice. People visiting from the cooler outer districts sometimes stare at the feet.

Her soles spread when she puts weight on them. On dry ceramic, they make a faint peeling sound. On wet stone, they hold well enough that she can stand on the sloping lip of a drain and pull a clogged screen toward herself with both hands.

They do not hold on grease. Her brother died that way.

There is a small brass mark beside the drain where it happened. It carries his name and the water level at the time. Iven polishes it when she cleans the stair, partly because she wants it legible and partly because tarnish under the fastenings will eventually lift it off.

She is sixty-three gate years old. This means she was born sixty-three annual inspection cycles ago at the gate called Long Mother. Elsewhere in the city, people count by other gates, by weather records, by the replacement dates of particular roofs. Official documents convert these into a common calendar. In conversation, a person’s age tells you where they began.

Iven has lived in four apartments, all within sight of the same pumping house.

Her present rooms are beneath a freight approach. The ceiling is low at one end and high at the other because the structure above has been reinforced twice. A cold-water pipe passes through the bedroom. She has wrapped most of it to prevent dripping, but left a short section bare over a basin. In hot weather she rests her wrists against it.

Her possessions are secured with more care than they are arranged. Cups sit in shallow sockets. The bedding hangs from a rail when not in use. A photograph of her brother is clipped at all four corners to the wall. Beside it is a pressure diagram her nephew brought home from school, beautifully colored and incorrect in two places.

She has kept it for eleven years.

***

The inhabitants of the city vary considerably in shape.

Some of this variation is familial. Some comes from work, age, diet, and the interventions available at different clinics. A person who spends thirty years clearing submerged valves may have a chest broad enough to seem disproportionate on land, and a throat fitted with a soft auxiliary opening that must be rinsed every night. A pressure-passage technician may have reinforced joints and poor distance vision. People whose grandparents lived in the hottest lower districts tend to have large, finely folded surfaces behind their jaws, which darken as blood moves through them.

No single body is adequate to the city.

Children are taught this before they are allowed onto public walkways alone. They learn where their grip fails. They learn how long they can remain in cold water, how much heat they can tolerate, and whether their eyelids seal properly against suspended grit. A child who can swim still has to learn how to escape a current beneath a deck. A child who can hold their breath still has to carry an air marker.

The markers are little buoyant capsules worn on the shoulder. When submerged, they rise on a cord and flash.

On rainy days, the school corridors are full of these flashes as children demonstrate, interfere with one another’s equipment, and are made to begin again.

People speak with their voices when they can. Close together, in the noise, they also use their mouths without sound. At greater distances they use hands, lamps, and reflective patches sewn inside their cuffs.

The hand language is quick and economical. It has precise forms for *stay where you are*, *the surface will move*, *do not take that route*, and *I have understood*. Its vocabulary for affection is less public. A parent can rest two fingers against the inside of a child’s wrist and say something that would be difficult to express across a room.

At meals, families often sit with their knees touching beneath the table. Conversation moves between voice and gesture according to the machinery outside. Someone begins a sentence aloud, finishes it with one hand, and uses the other to stop a bowl from sliding.

***

Most households keep something alive that they intend neither to eat nor to sell.

In Iven’s rooms it is a translucent animal in a shallow stone tray. It has a central stalk and twelve soft lobes that unfold when food enters the water. At rest, it resembles a collapsed glove.

Her nephew bought it from a bath attendant who raises them in the warm overflow. He expected it to glow. It does not.

It does, however, turn slowly toward Iven when she sits beside the tray. This is probably a response to vibration. She knows that. She taps the edge once before feeding it, and the lobes begin opening before the minced scraps touch the water.

Down the corridor, a household keeps three vent birds. Their wings are narrow, their feet surprisingly strong. They sleep clinging to a heated grille and become agitated when the heat cycle changes. One has learned to reproduce the descending whistle that precedes a freight lift’s arrival. It does this just before dawn.

There have been complaints.

The building’s least welcome residents are small jointed scavengers that enter through the drainage sleeves. They flatten themselves almost perfectly and can travel behind a wall coating thinner than a fingernail. Their young feed on skin flakes, glue, and the fat that collects around cooking vents. Adults will eat wire insulation.

Residents put smooth bowls under furniture legs, repair cracks, and borrow hunting animals from the maintenance depot.

The hunters are slow, many-legged things with stiff sensory hairs along their flanks. Released into a room, they stand motionless for so long that borrowers often think they have been given a defective one. Then a leg slips into a seam, followed by another, and the entire animal draws itself into a space that seemed too small to contain it.

There is usually a soft clicking afterward.

***

The great canal animals graze chiefly on the black colonies coating submerged structures.

Up close, their gray backs are scarred by propellers, falling cargo, old mooring cables, and encounters with one another. Their mouths are underneath, broad and ridged. They can strip a piling clean without damaging it, but they also take unsecured screens and occasionally remove a door.

Each carries a moving community. Little fish shelter in the folds around its limbs. Hard-shelled animals cluster along the scars. A white, blind scavenger lives only in the warm crease behind the jaw, where it picks fragments from the grazer’s meals.

Some canal crews recognize individuals across decades. They identify them by damaged ridges, feeding habits, the shape of the wake. Names pass from pilot to apprentice. Ledger, with the split left shoulder, waits below a closed gate until the final warning horn. Six Windows moves away from inspection lamps but permits workboats to rest against its side.

Their young are born in quiet retention basins. For the first season they lie beneath floating mats of debris, breathing through short tubes raised just above the surface. Workers clearing those basins use long-handled probes. A calf disturbed at close range can overturn the cleaning platform without ever becoming fully visible.

There are fines for interfering with a nursery. There are also fines for failing to report one beside a flood outlet. The clerk administering these rules keeps a list of births, deaths, and sightings in the same room as the records of damaged vessels.

***

After the storm, Iven’s nephew works down to the lowest accessible step.

His lamp shows three old mooring rings, a broken cup, and a fish nosing through the sediment inside a flooded doorway. He reaches beneath the landing and pulls out a wad of fabric caught on a bolt. Something has laid eggs in it: a cluster of clear beads with dark curls suspended inside.

He shakes the fabric once, then stops. There is a sheltered crack beside the stair. He tucks it there and weighs one corner down with the broken cup.

Above him, Iven scrapes grease from the stone.

A kitchen lowers its evening prices. The colored lamps change twice. Across the canal, a mechanic opens the side of a maintenance animal and washes grit from its joints. It flexes each limb as she finishes, then settles over a fresh bead of water rising through the pavement.

When the stair is clean, Iven counts the scraper heads in her basket and waits for her nephew to climb out. He checks his shoulder marker, although she has already checked it. They go home along the suspended walk while the canal falls beneath them.

On the submerged face of the lowest riser, the pale animal loosens two feet. A little water enters the pocket under its body. Its fringe stirs, finds something edible, and draws it in.
