The animal beneath the drop has eight feet, though only six are used for holding on. The front pair have become cups. It raises them beside its mouth, not quite touching, and waits with the patience of a creature for which a missed drop might mean another day of waiting.

Its body is pale enough that the last meal shows through: a brown thread folded several times inside it. Across its back lie three loose shields, overlapping like pieces of broken nail. When the drop falls, the shields lift. Water travels beneath them, darkening a network of channels, and the animal becomes briefly transparent.

It does not drink all it catches. Once swollen, it backs into a crevice where four smaller bodies press their mouths against the edges of its shields. They have been waiting too.

These are not its young. They are older animals whose cups have worn through.

The hollow supports many such arrangements. What looks like a gathering is often a means of surviving some small bodily failure. An animal with a damaged foot occupies the middle of a group, where it can brace itself against its neighbors. One that can no longer scrape food from stone follows a grazer and eats what comes loose. These associations last while they are useful. When a foot regrows, its owner leaves. When a grazer dies, its follower eats the soft attachments beneath the shell before moving on.

There is little haste in any of this. Most of the inhabitants have a quick movement available to them, but use it sparingly. A leap may escape a mouth and land the animal somewhere from which it cannot climb back.

The commonest grazers are narrow, jointed creatures, dark along the back and white underneath. Their heads can fold almost entirely between their forelegs. They work a patch of leathery growth by rocking from side to side, drawing paired blades across it. After each few strokes they stop and clean the blades with a small brush concealed inside the mouth.

They leave more than feeding marks. Along the edges of the pale arcs are specks of waste, each wrapped in a film that adheres to wet stone. The growth returns first around these specks. A grazer eventually comes back to its own old feeding ground and finds a row of dark, raised dots where there was once a smooth surface.

Some spend their entire lives on a patch smaller than a palm.

Others travel. They have longer feet and a harder ridge down the back, useful for forcing a passage between packed bodies. A traveling grazer taps before entering a crevice: two quick blows, a pause, then a third. Usually the occupants shift enough to admit it. Sometimes an answering tap sends it elsewhere. Sometimes there is no answer, and it enters a space where the animals inside have died, feeds, and stays.

The taps are made with different parts of the body. Feet produce the high, dry notes; a ridge struck against stone gives a deeper sound. The animals distinguish these through the pads of their legs. Those pads remain in contact with the wall even during sleep.

The young learn the hollow by following vibrations. Before their eyes darken, they move toward scraping and away from anything that shakes more than one foot at once. They make mistakes. A drop landing on a loose flake can draw a whole brood toward an empty shelf. The scratching of a predator’s claws may sound enough like feeding to bring them within reach.

Several predators seem to exploit this. One lies beneath a stone with only the tips of its forelegs exposed. It rubs the tips together in uneven bursts, stops, and rubs again. When a small grazer touches the stone above it, the forelegs open.

The winged inhabitants are poor walkers. They move sideways, testing each foothold with hooked toes. Their folded wings are stiffened by narrow ribs; opened suddenly, they can slow a fall or catch against a projecting edge. A torn wing is seldom fatal. A creature may descend for the rest of its life using one sound fan and the ragged remnant of the other.

They carry eggs beneath their bodies in strings that look like droplets. When a suitable seam is found, the parent presses the string into it and holds there until the outer coating grips. Then it moves away, lighter and more agile.

The larvae hatch facing inward.

For a long time they do not leave the seam. Their heads work at the thin growth inside it, while their bodies push against one another. Those farther from the moisture pass food backward in pale pellets. Those nearest the seep swell with water and are slowly squeezed toward the drier end. A crowded seam is always rearranging itself, though no larva appears to travel far.

When one is ready to emerge, the others make room by contracting. It comes out soft, with its wings still lying in wet pleats along its sides, and hangs beneath the crack until they harden. This is the most dangerous interval of its life. Grazers that would ignore a mature animal will stop to eat an unhardened joint.

The luminous creatures belong to several kinds. Some reveal their lights only when jostled. Some show a steady point beneath the abdomen. In a few, the glow lives in a removable casing near the last pair of legs.

These casings occasionally come loose.

An abandoned light draws attention for hours. Animals approach, touch it, retreat, and approach again. A small hunter may wait beside it until something edible arrives. Sometimes a grazer carries the casing away and wedges it near its resting place. The light fades regardless, but while it lasts the grazer is more likely to find its way back.

The forearm-length inhabitant at the deepest end carries no light.

Up close, its resemblance to stone is less perfect than it appears from a distance. There are pores in the hide, each surrounded by a low rim. Grains of mineral lodge in them and remain through successive molts. Older parts of the body are coarser and darker; newly exposed skin shows as a thin gray border beneath the jaw.

It has more limbs than can easily be counted. Most remain folded into grooves along its flanks. Two brace against the back of the crack. Others reach farther in, where their claws grip rock beyond sight. The visible body is only what rests near the entrance.

Small animals cross its face because its closed mouth offers a warm, slightly damp surface. They scrape the hide and clean debris from the pores. It tolerates them until a larger animal comes close.

Then the lower part of its face separates along a line that seemed to be a seam in the stone. The mouth opens to either side. Two pale folds turn outward and close around the visitor with so little movement that the animals on its brow sometimes keep feeding.

Afterward it remains with its eyelid raised for several days.

There are scars on the stone beside it: short, deep grooves, crowded together near the entrance and more widely spaced farther in. It has occupied the crack long enough to grow, though not long enough for the grooves to lose their sharpness. When warmth increases in the deeper rock, its folded limbs begin to move. It may shift inward by the breadth of a finger, carrying its face away from the creatures grazing there. They follow until the crack becomes too narrow.

The low knock from within the mountain reaches it before the smaller animals stop.

Its eye closes. The limbs tighten. Along its flanks, ridges rise and press against both walls.

Elsewhere, the little inhabitants flatten themselves into whatever shelter they possess. The water-catchers empty their cups. Winged animals clamp their fans shut. Even those feeding inside a fallen moth withdraw their heads and become still beneath its fur.

Usually nothing follows.

Sometimes dust trickles from a hanging plate. Sometimes a stack on the floor settles by a distance too small to see, and every route across it must be tried again. A stone that has supported three generations rolls half a turn and places their crevice underneath.

Then there is work. Animals emerge around the altered edges. Some recover eggs. Some search for companions by tapping. Others begin scraping the fresh undersides of stones, where a little moisture has already gathered.

At the deepest end, the large animal loosens its hold.

The one beneath the drop returns to its place. It sets six feet against the rock and raises the other two. Behind it, in the shelter of the fracture, the older animals wait with their mouths against its empty shields.
