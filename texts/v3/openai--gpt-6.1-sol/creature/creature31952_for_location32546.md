The caretaker lives in the eastern service house, in a room separated from the pulping machine by two walls and a cupboard full of folded sacking. The room is long enough for a bed if the bed stands beneath the window. To open the cupboard, she has to close the door.

Her name is Mara. It appears on the maintenance roster, on a tin of tea, and on the handle of a pruning knife that has been sharpened until the blade is noticeably narrower than its sheath. On most of the labels she makes, she writes only the date.

She is neither very old nor young enough to climb the six flights without stopping. At the third landing she rests while pretending to inspect a coupling. She knows the coupling is sound. She installed it herself.

Down among the machines, she wears boots. In the garden she generally goes barefoot, carrying the boots by their laces until she reaches the service house. This is not a fondness for nature. The ceramic plates tell her more through the soles of her feet. A plate that stays warm through the quiet part of a pump cycle may have a blocked return beneath it. One that rocks diagonally has probably acquired a root under its corner. Sometimes she stops in the middle of a conversation and shifts her weight from heel to toe.

“Go on,” she says.

But she is listening elsewhere.

Her work has made her slightly uneven. One shoulder sits higher from carrying baskets through paths too narrow to change hands. The nails of her left hand are clean and short; those of her right retain a pale rim of mineral paste. There is a small burn on the inside of her wrist, renewed often enough that it has never quite become a scar. She catches that wrist against the exhaust casing when reaching for the shrub behind it.

She could move the shrub. There is a note about this in the service book.

The note is four years old.

Mara does not speak to the plants as a rule. She speaks to valves, especially those with handles polished smooth by earlier caretakers. She says, “There,” when one finally turns, and “Don’t start,” when a pressure line knocks inside the wall. With plants she is more physical. She lifts a leaf with the back of her hand, parts two stems, feels beneath a root collar. When she passes the banana plant, she sometimes lays a palm against its trunk without stopping.

She knows which leaves can take her weight in water and which will fold. She knows the difference between the sourness of a healthy fermentation vat and the sweeter smell that means the mixture has gone wrong. She can find the western path in the dark, though she would not advise anyone else to try. A ledge has grown inward there since the paths were last mapped.

The first thing she does each morning is look for the frog.

Usually she finds only evidence: rings in the pool, a displaced film of pollen, two hind feet withdrawing beneath a mat of roots. She records it as present.

When she sees it clearly, it is smaller than her thumb, dark brown rather than black, with one pale fleck below the eye. She has never given it a name. Naming it would make the replacement procedure unpleasant to read.

Nevertheless, she checks the intake grille every evening. She once spent nearly an hour loosening a strand of root caught across it, although the flow was still within tolerance. The frog was sitting three inches away throughout, its throat moving, offering no assistance.

There are things she removes at once. Mold beneath a membrane. A root entering the wrong side of a pump seal. Any shoot that finds the emergency drain.

Other things receive a mark and are allowed to continue.

The fern in the gauge housing has three such marks. The first records its appearance. The second records the day it began interfering with the needle. The third indicates that Mara moved the gauge.

Its old housing remains in place. Through the cloudy glass, the fern looks larger than it is.

The engineers sometimes ask her why a component has been displaced when the original drawing shows ample clearance. She sends measurements and photographs. In the photographs she holds a ruler beside the obstruction, making the problem look orderly and available for decision.

She does not photograph the hour she spent deciding where the new bracket could go.

Mara has been here long enough to remember the garden when its boundaries were visible from almost every path. The little trees stood separately in their beds. Their numbered root sleeves could still be reached without cutting anything away. Water ran in bright, shallow lines over bare ceramic.

For the first few months, she had trouble sleeping. A sound upstairs meant a fault. A drip was a leak; a scrape was a loose fixing. Now the roof makes hundreds of sounds that require nothing from her.

She sleeps through most of them.

What wakes her is an absence: the missing tremor of the return pump, or a gutter that should be ticking after rain and is silent instead. She sits up before she knows what has changed. Then she waits in the dark, letting the other noises separate.

Her room contains very little brought from below. A blue cup. A wool coat too warm for the garden and useful only on the open maintenance platforms. A photograph of three people standing in shallow water, their trousers rolled above their knees. The photograph is faded enough that visitors assume it is older than it is.

Under the bed are two boxes of letters and a pair of shoes she wears when she descends to the inhabited floors.

She goes down more often than people suppose. She dislikes the lift, buys things she does not need, and sometimes stays away for two nights. During her absence, another caretaker follows the written schedule precisely. Mara returns to a cleaner path and a stack of completed forms.

She reads every form.

Then she walks the garden, slowly, finding out what happened between the entries.

In the afternoons she sits on a low ceramic stool near the western bank and cleans her tools. It is one of the few places where she can see the southern rail without standing. Beyond it, clouds move over distances she cannot judge. Their scale makes her momentarily uncertain of the size of things close at hand: the knife across her knees, the stool, her own foot resting on a damp plate.

Then a fly settles on her wrist, and everything becomes local again.

She brushes it away.

When the bank cracks, she rises without haste. She tests the new surface first with a finger, then with the flat of her hand. Fresh material may hold its shape while remaining soft underneath. She knows where to touch without leaving a print.

Today a small lip has formed beside a channel. It was not there yesterday. Water hangs from its edge in three clear drops.

Mara takes the grease pencil from behind her ear and marks the stone beside it. Then she crouches, looking beneath the lip.

There is room underneath for something: moss, perhaps, once the surface has hardened. A root might reach it from the bed above. She moves her head to see where the water falls.

Before going inside, she shifts one path plate a little to the east.

Not far. Just enough that she will not knock the new edge with her heel when she comes through in the morning.
