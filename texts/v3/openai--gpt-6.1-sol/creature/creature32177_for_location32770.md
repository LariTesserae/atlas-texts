The inhabitant is in the passage with you before you recognize that there is room for two.

You have taken the dark strip beside a wall for a seam in the masonry. Then its upper end draws back from the lip of a pipe. Farther down, a narrow joint bends. Something resting along the floor gathers itself upward, without scraping, and the strip becomes the edge of a body.

It has been standing sideways.

Turned toward you, it is surprisingly broad. Turned away, it almost disappears. Its chest is a flattened case, its pelvis another, joined by several short lengths that can bend independently. There is no single waist. It can put the upper half of itself around a corner while the lower half remains in the passage behind. Its legs fold at more places than yours, but the folds are shallow, suitable for stepping over pipes rather than climbing. Its feet are long and soft-edged. They take the shape of whatever small purchase the floor provides.

Its surface is the color of the court, though not quite its substance. Where light falls across it, there is a faint, dull translucence, like a shaving from a pale horn. In sheltered creases it is reddish. Fine mineral dust lies along its shoulders and in the depressions of its joints. It carries no clothing, but several parts of it have been bound with strips of fibrous material, pressed flat and sealed under clay. One of these bindings has the same rounded, ancient appearance as the repairs in the pavement.

It does not look strong enough to have carried the blocks.

Then you see its hands.

Each has two heavy opposing fingers and a spread of smaller ones between them. The heavy fingers are blunt and flattened on their inner faces. They could grip a wet coil without cutting it, or hold a block firmly while leaving most of its surface exposed. The smaller fingers fit into holes. Some are scarcely thicker than reeds. Their tips are dark, polished by contact.

One hand remains inside the wall.

Not caught there. Resting.

Its head is a low continuation of the upper case, with no neck to speak of. There are two shallow pits on the front, protected by overhanging rims. They do not turn toward your face. Between them is a vertical opening whose edges stay pressed together. Nothing about it suggests that the inhabitant is trying to hear you.

When you speak, it lays its free hand against the pillar.

It waits until your throat stops moving.

Then it places two fingers on your wrist.

The touch is warm, no warmer than the slabs. Its pressure changes several times, very slightly. You feel those changes more readily than you would feel them in a quiet place, because everything else is vibrating. One finger holds steady; the other moves across your tendons, finding something.

It is not taking your pulse. When you draw your arm back, it lets go before you exert any force.

For a while it attends to the place where you stood.

***

It lives chiefly by contact.

The pits in its head distinguish an opening from a wall, perhaps a body from its surroundings. They are useful when a passage has been altered. For the rest, it has the court under its hands and feet. The immense sound that makes the place almost intolerable to you reaches it as innumerable small differences: the resistance of a slab, a tremor arriving through a joint, the altered pressure within a pipe when something has been laid across its mouth.

It can find a loose block without seeing it move.

You watch it stop beside a wall that appears no different from any other. It puts one heavy finger on the wall’s top and slips three narrow fingers into separate holes. For a long time nothing happens. Then it withdraws one finger, presses with another, and settles its shoulder against the clay.

A little dust falls.

That is all.

The roar does not change for you. The inhabitant remains there until whatever it was attending to has passed.

There are no tools laid out for it. Most of what it needs is stored in places you have mistaken for defects. A polished stone sits deep in a rounded pocket. A short stamping stick lies behind a pipe. Under a ledge is a pad of clay enclosed between two plates, still workable when it separates them. It takes a quantity smaller than the end of your thumb and rolls it between its heavy fingers.

The clay receives a new impression.

For the first time, one of the marks in the court has an age you can understand.

It presses the little coil around a pipe mouth, leaving most of the opening clear. The motion is careful but not ceremonial. It rubs the added material with the stone, wipes the stone against the underside of the ledge, and returns everything to its place.

The repair is almost invisible. The inhabitant’s narrow fingers stay inside the pipe for some time afterward.

***

There may be others.

Once you have seen its body, certain shapes become difficult to dismiss. Beneath the shelf of sealed jars, a pale length curves where no pipe seems necessary. In a shallow chamber, something resembling a hand lies flat against the floor, its fingers spread into five vents. Beyond a doorway blocked by a later wall, two reddish joint-lines interrupt the flour-colored clay.

None moves while you watch.

The inhabitant touches one of these shapes as it passes. It does not pause. You cannot tell whether the gesture is an acknowledgment, an examination, or merely another point of contact with the construction.

Its own body bears places where clay has accumulated. Along one side of the chest, a broad patch covers the translucent surface. The patch is scored with the grain of a wooden tool. At the hip, a projecting lip has been built up until it resembles part of a seat. If it stood still in a recess with that side outward, you would take it for a fitted piece of masonry.

Perhaps you have already done so.

There is a shallow chamber scarcely long enough for it to occupy. It enters backward, folding its legs beneath the lower case, slipping its feet into slots you thought were drains. The chamber’s seat receives its hip exactly. Its chest rests against the back wall. Both hands disappear into openings at different heights.

Nothing closes over it. It is still plainly there.

Yet the chamber now seems complete in a way it did not before.

This is where it rests. It does not become limp or withdraw from the sound. Small tensions pass through its exposed joints. Sometimes a finger moves within the wall. The vertical opening in its face parts a little, revealing several narrow ridges, then closes.

You cannot hear whether it makes a sound.

***

Its relation to the court is not that of a keeper to a machine left in its charge.

There is wear on the floor where its feet pass, and wear on its feet corresponding to particular slabs. The court has shaped the inhabitant as precisely as the inhabitant has shaped the court. A swelling behind its shoulder fits a hollow beside the pillar. A finger bends at an angle that allows it to reach around an obstruction inside a pipe. One leg can no longer straighten fully; the route never requires it to.

It has no use for an open square.

When it crosses the meeting of the passages, it touches the squat pillar with its chest, transfers its weight, and turns around it in one continuous movement. Without the pillar, that movement would have nothing to bear against. The space that constricts you supports it.

Still, it sometimes encounters difficulty.

At the low pipe you stepped over, it stops. One foot goes across; the other remains behind. It tries the passage at a slightly different angle, then returns to the first position. Its hand settles on an old mortar ridge.

You expect it to break something away.

Instead it stays there, considering through its fingers. At last it takes the longer route through two shallow chambers and reaches the parapet from the other side.

It has known this inconvenience before. There is a faint polish on the alternate route.

***

Beside the parapet it unfolds as far as its joints permit.

The broad chest faces the drop. Its fingers settle on the rounded wall top, widely spaced. One extends over the outer face and searches down between the upper courses. The movement has an unhurried familiarity.

It does not look toward the brown haze.

Whatever it attends to is closer: within reach of its hand, within the thickness of the wall, perhaps traveling upward through all those tightly laid blocks.

You stand beside it for as long as you can bear. Your jaw aches. Your knees feel loose. You want to tell it that you are leaving, but the words disappear against your own teeth.

When you turn, it removes one hand from the parapet.

For a moment its fingers rest across the passage, not barring it, only occupying the space. Then they fold back. It presses its body close to the wall and leaves you enough room.

You pass sideways.

Behind you, it returns its hand to the masonry. Nothing else in the court changes.
