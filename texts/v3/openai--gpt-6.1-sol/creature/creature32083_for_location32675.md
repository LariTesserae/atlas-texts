The first one you see is sitting in a recess that appeared empty a moment before.

It has not arrived. It has unfolded.

What you took for the curved back of the recess was its spine. What you took for a darker patch on the floor was the underside of its feet. Now it leans forward, slowly, bringing two long hands together between its knees. A scatter of beetles drops from its chest and hurries into the wall.

Seated, it would come up to your waist. Standing, it might be taller than you. There is a great deal of it tucked away.

Its skin is the gray-green of a stem kept out of the sun. Fine creases run lengthwise along its limbs, opening at the joints to reveal a pale, damp membrane. Its head has no neck to speak of, and its face is crowded low on the front: a narrow mouth, two glossy eyes beneath heavy folds, several small pits that open and close independently. Above these, the skull rises into a smooth ridge.

It wears nothing. A few strands hang from one wrist, but these are growing there. Something small and amber-colored moves up one strand, turns, and descends into a fold of skin.

The creature watches you without turning its head.

Then it places one hand against the wall.

The wall answers with three dry knocks.

***

There are more of them than the empty streets suggest.

One lies along the top of an arcade, thin as a gutter, collecting dew on the ridges of its back. Another has fitted itself behind a handrail, its fingers threaded among the tough supporting fibers. Two occupy opposite sides of a doorway. Their eyes are closed, but when a procession of frogs comes through, both draw their feet out of the way.

They spend much of their lives pressed into the district’s edges. Their bodies flatten under sustained pressure. Skin against bark becomes barklike; skin against the damp bottoms of basins softens and turns dark. Left undisturbed, they become extraordinarily difficult to distinguish from the places where they rest.

But they do move.

One rises from a shaded ledge and crosses the market with an uneven, deliberate gait. Its limbs straighten in stages. For the first few steps it uses its hands; then its hips lift, its shoulders settle, and it walks upright beneath the leaf-roof.

It stops at an empty fruit bowl and reaches inside.

You hear a furious rattling. The creature withdraws its hand with a small lizard clamped to one finger. It waits until the lizard lets go, then reaches in again.

This time it brings out a wad of wet fibers, smells it through the pits in its face, and eats.

There is a dark, nearly circular stain on the finger where it was bitten. By evening, you cannot find it.

***

The little animals live on them as readily as they live in the walls.

Mites graze the folds behind their knees. Transparent larvae inhabit channels under their skin. A particular black cricket shelters in the hollow beneath the breastbone, where its calls are amplified by a thin plate of tissue. Almost every adult carries one. When the cricket falls silent, its host taps the plate until it resumes.

They are not indifferent to all this occupancy. They groom themselves patiently, combing narrow places with a split fingernail. Some animals they return to their bodies. Others they eat. A worm pulled from an elbow may be inspected, stretched between two fingers, and placed carefully in a wall seam; the next may go straight into the mouth.

Their decisions seem to depend on things you cannot see.

They also groom one another. In the afternoon, three gather beneath a low arch. One bends its head while another opens the folds along its skull. The third holds a palm below to catch what falls.

For a while they resemble people helping someone untangle their hair.

Then the bent one spreads the plates of its head, exposing a small chamber full of pale eggs, and the resemblance becomes harder to hold.

The eggs belong to a moth. All three watch them closely.

***

They hear through their hands, feet, ribs, and the long ridge of the skull.

The deafening air does not trouble them. They seem, in fact, uneasy in the quieter rooms. There they remain standing, with several points of contact against the walls, as though a floor alone provides too little information.

Their conversations enter the district.

A heel pressed down. Fingertips dragged across bark. A rapid trembling in the chest that sends the resident cricket into a different pitch. The replies may come from far away, conducted through roots, or from a neighbor who has not visibly moved.

Sometimes an entire gallery takes part. Frogs deepen their calls. Beetles begin striking a hollow stem in a slower rhythm. A door narrows, then releases. Two inhabitants leave their recesses and go to the same basin.

You cannot tell whether the animals are carrying a message or merely responding to the changes that carry it.

The distinction may not matter here.

Once, the creature you first saw lays its hand beside yours on a warm wall. It taps twice, waits, and taps again.

Something answers under your palm.

It repeats the sequence. When you fail to repeat it, it stays beside you for a considerable time, apparently willing to begin again.

***

The young are harder to find because they do not keep still.

They are broad-footed, soft, and a little translucent. Light through their hands reveals bundles of fibers instead of bones. Their heads are rounder than those of the adults, their faces less deeply folded. They climb the public arches, fall from low heights, and immediately climb again.

Their shoulders are surprisingly wide.

You understand some of the old doorways when you see one turn sideways to pass through. The openings were made for bodies at this age, before the limbs lengthened and the torso learned to compress.

A young one carries a mouthful of water across a court. It moves with exaggerated care, cheeks swollen, hands held out for balance. At the far side it spits the water into a dry crack.

An adult nearby touches the crack, then the youngster’s forehead.

Later the same youngster performs the journey again, although the crack is already damp. On the third trip it is distracted by a stream of insects and loses most of the water laughing.

The laugh is a low, percussive chatter. Several creatures inside the wall chatter back.

***

They eat fruit, softened bark, insects, fungal sheets peeled from shaded corners. They drink from the basins with their mouths close to the green lining, taking so little at once that the surface scarcely changes.

Much of their work consists of noticing.

They test a swollen threshold with their weight. They open a seam where the animals have begun to crowd. They rub a dry leaf-joint until moisture rises through it. One spends an hour coaxing a root away from a drainage groove, moving it no farther than the width of a finger.

They possess no separate implements, but their bodies make many.

A nail divides into flexible teeth. A wrist stiffens into a wedge. Saliva softens certain fibers and hardens others. When two inhabitants must move a heavy piece of dead roof, they join their hands beneath it, and the skin of their palms adheres until the work is done.

They leave the dead roof beside a sunny wall.

By the next day, something has laid eggs under it.

***

Age does not make them larger in any simple way.

An old inhabitant may be very small, pared down to a head, folded limbs, and a thin body almost entirely occupied by other life. It may cross the district in the early morning, pausing often to listen.

Another may occupy the whole side of a room.

This one still has a face. You find it near the floor, turned toward a doorway, with eyes half covered by growth. Far above it, fingers remain visible in the ceiling. Its torso has spread between them, making ledges and enclosed spaces.

A younger inhabitant comes each evening to clean the eyes.

The old one follows its movements. Once, when the visitor turns to leave, a long crease opens in the wall and gently catches its wrist.

They remain that way until dark.

Not every resting inhabitant becomes part of the district. Some rise after years. They separate themselves by degrees, leaving shallow hollows that retain their shape. Their first movements are painful to watch. Roots cling to their backs; small chambers rupture; animals flee from spaces that have abruptly become joints.

Others never rise again.

There is no obvious point at which you could say they have ceased to be inhabitants and become architecture.

***

Beneath the central square lives one that can no longer be seen whole.

The chewing is its ordinary occupation.

Through a narrow opening beside the square, you glimpse a pale surface advancing and withdrawing. It is scored with deep grooves, and each stroke brings a smell of crushed stems into the air. Younger inhabitants sometimes lower bundles of dead fiber into the opening. Sometimes they retrieve them, softened and threaded with new roots.

When the four entrances contract at midday, an old creature resting nearby lifts its face from the paving.

It listens.

Then it lowers its head again.

Whatever lies beneath the square is familiar to them. They let the young play over it. They bring it the parts of the district that have become tough, obstructive, or useless. Its steady labor continues through drought, rain, and the long settling of the rooms above.

You never discover where its body ends.

***

When the brown seed splits in the market, several inhabitants are already waiting.

One clears loose husks from the floor. Another settles beside the bowl and supports the seed while the pale root searches downward. The young crowd close until an adult pushes them back with an extended foot.

There is something moving inside the split.

For a moment it looks like a hand. Then it opens into a cluster of folded leaves. Deeper in the seed, a small glossy eye turns toward the light.

The inhabitants wait through the evening.

By morning the root has entered the paving. The seed’s upper half remains intact, tilted against the bowl. Beneath it is a sheltered space scarcely large enough for a child.

One of the adults reaches into that space.

Something takes hold of its finger.

It leaves its hand there while the market wakes around them, while moisture travels down the gutters, while the animals begin their enormous morning noise.
