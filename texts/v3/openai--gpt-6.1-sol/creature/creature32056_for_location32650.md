The animal beside the pool is not quite an otter. Its body is too short, its head too broad, and the fur along its spine grows in overlapping ridges, like moss that has been combed by water. Its forefeet are narrow and almost hairless. Each finger ends in a pale nail, and between the fingers the skin is thin enough to show the branching vessels within.

It holds one hand in the pool.

Not deeply: just to the knuckles, where the surface meets the fur without climbing it. Small aquatic animals occupy the spaces beneath its palm. A snail adheres to the inside of one finger. The animal does not withdraw from this contact. Its hand belongs among the submerged roots as naturally as its body belongs on the bank.

The stone flake rests beside its other hand. There is a shallow groove in it, fitted to the pad of a thumb. Perhaps it is used to open shells. Perhaps it is held for the particular coolness of stone against skin. There are no discarded shells nearby, no chips, no evidence of a task completed. Still, the fit is unmistakable. This is an object known by a hand.

Its eyes are dark, with a narrow amber margin. They are directed toward the water, though not toward anything that swims. Beneath the short muzzle, its whiskers spread across the pool’s surface. Each carries a bead at its tip.

Farther uphill, the horned animal rests with its cheek against a cushion of liverwort.

It is smaller than its heavy coat first suggests. The horns emerge close behind the ears and curve backward, nearly disappearing into the fur. Their exposed surfaces are dull and finely ridged. A little moss grows in the deepest grooves, not enough to conceal them.

Its nostrils are broad. Their wet rims hold a darkness different from the darkness of the coat. One ear lies folded beneath a fern; the other is upright, its interior lined with fur so fine that it resembles the pale underside of a leaf.

There is room beneath its chest for several smaller bodies. A mouse-shaped animal rests there, flattened comfortably against the stone, its naked tail laid along a root. Two moths occupy the longer hair behind the foreleg. A slug has pressed itself into the crease where horn meets skull.

None of these seems to be a burden. The larger animal has the substantial ease of a creature lying alone.

The birds are harder to separate from one another.

Some have blunt bills and heavy throats. Others have fine curved bills tucked partly into the feathers of their breasts. Their feet are large for their bodies, gripping branches that appear too slight to support them. But the branches do not tremble. Moss runs over the toes, leaving the claws exposed.

One bird holds the sharpened twig beneath its foot. The twig’s point extends past the branch into a fold of bark. Its breast feathers are parted around a patch of bare skin, and against that patch rests a tiny bird, fully feathered, with an open eye and a bill no longer than a thorn.

There is no visible nest.

Elsewhere, eggs sit directly in the dense foliage. Some are white, some faintly green, some so dark they can be found only by the difference between their smoothness and the leaves around them. An adult’s feather touches each one. The contact is slight but exact.

In the pool, the salamanders have faces like unfinished expressions.

Their mouths are broad, their eyes small and clear. The amber beneath their jaws gives them an appearance of holding a little light inside the throat. Their translucent feet spread over the stone with exquisite delicacy. You can see each joint, each pale tendon, the tiny blunt ends of the toes.

Some are very young. Their gills stand out in fine red branches. Others carry the traces of gills as soft seams behind the head. There is no division between nursery and dwelling place. Eggs adhere beside the feet of adults; larvae lie beneath their bellies.

A larger salamander rests at the bottom of the deepest bowl. Its back is almost black. Along its sides, minute pale animals occupy the folds of skin. Its throat has the same soft fullness as the throats above it.

The little mammal in the moss has wrapped its tail across its face.

Its fur grows thickly around the shoulders and thins toward the feet, which are tucked together beneath its chin. At first there seems to be no opening through which it could see. Then an eye becomes apparent between two strands of fur: brown, moist, unclosed.

It is not hiding from you. The concealed face is simply where its face belongs.

This is true of the others as well. A spider is tucked behind a swollen web-junction with only the joints of its legs visible. A beetle occupies a pocket of moss scarcely wider than its shell. Beneath the fallen limb, something long-bodied has fitted itself around the curve of the hollow wood. Its smooth flank can be seen through a hole left by decay. The wood has softened against it without collapsing.

Every creature has more of its body concealed than shown.

Yet none looks trapped. No claw digs for purchase. No folded wing strains against a branch. Even the small creature between the larva’s jaws retains its own composure: legs gathered, antennae extended into the water. The jaw fits around it with terrible precision.

What lives here is not peaceful in the sense of having ceased to need anything. There are mouths, gripping feet, eggs, tender undersides. There are sharp parts concealed in soft bodies. But need has become as closely fitted to the hollow as flesh is fitted to bone.

You could put your hand near the otterlike animal and find no space for it beside the worn stone. You could lean close to the horned animal and discover another small face beneath its jaw.

They do not make room.

They have room.
