What lives here is easiest to find by leaving a hand in the warm water.

Not searching with it. Searching makes the little eyeless animal withdraw between the ribs, and whatever else is there becomes difficult to distinguish from the floor. The hand must be left open, palm upward, until the warmth of the water and the warmth of the skin seem equal.

Eventually there is a weight against one finger.

It is not much heavier than a fold of wet cloth. It rests there, withdraws, then returns against the side of the thumb. It does not seem to be testing whether the hand is dangerous. Rather, it is finding how the hand is put together. It follows the crease where the thumb joins the palm. At the wrist, where a sleeve begins, it stops.

There is no light by which to see it, and bringing a lamp into the hollow changes its behavior. It goes beneath the ledge behind the deepest pool. A very weak light may reveal part of its back before it withdraws: a low, pale arch, with several darker places within it. The surface has the dull translucence of a soaked seed.

Its body is about as large as two hands held together.

This measurement is approximate because it does not always hold the same shape. At rest it settles broad and low. When moving between the partitions, it becomes narrower, gathering its underside into several short supports. They are not jointed quite like legs. Each can bend in more than one place, and each ends in a small area that spreads against the stone.

There are more of these supports toward one end.

That is the end it usually puts forward, though it can reverse direction without turning around. Beneath it is a small opening surrounded by a firmer rim. This opening closes upon the submerged mineral with such delicacy that the surface afterward appears untouched. Whatever it takes from there is less than a visible scraping.

The eyeless animal sometimes rests against its side. It tolerates this. If the animal approaches the opening underneath, however, one of the short supports intervenes and moves it away.

Its back is firmer than its underside. The dark places within are firmest of all. A finger laid very gently upon one can feel a resistance like a tiny stone beneath leather. Between them, the body yields. It does not feel fragile, but it gives the impression that its parts have not agreed upon a single degree of hardness.

One edge bears a notch.

The notch has the smooth, complete surface of an old injury, although nothing suggests how it was made. When the creature passes through a narrow place, it turns that edge inward. It is capable of remembering an inconvenience without repairing it.

It spends much of its time beneath the floor.

The little depression by the wall is one entrance. There are others under the ledges, accessible only to a body that can make itself very thin. It does not dissolve into the mineral or pass through solid stone. There are spaces underneath, connected by narrow passages. Its body comes back from them with fine grains adhering to the soft portions. It removes these in the shallow pool, using its foremost supports one after another.

It is careful about this washing. It attends to places a person would not know were dirty.

Afterward it may remain in the pool, settled against a rib, with the opening underneath held clear of the floor. In this position it appears to be resting. A touch will not always wake it. Sustained pressure will. Then the supports gather beneath it, and it moves away without hurry.

It has no eyes. There are no recognizable ears or nostrils. Along the softer edge of its body are minute folds which open when it crosses a dry surface and close when submerged. These may be organs of some kind. They may simply permit the body to change its width.

It knows where the cup is.

The three wooden pegs are too high for it to reach from the floor, but one broad rib runs almost to the shelf. It climbs this rib slowly, spreading two supports against the wet surface before releasing another. At the top, it has to extend itself across a short gap.

This is the least easy part of its day, if day is a useful word here.

Sometimes it remains stretched between rib and shelf for a long while. Its underside trembles faintly. Then it draws the rest of itself across.

It does not enter the cup. It goes around it.

The separately tied handle receives particular attention. The creature presses each turn of the binding, then reaches beneath the shell. If moisture has gathered there, it takes the water onto its underside and carries it to the edge of the shelf. There it touches a wooden peg, allowing the moisture to pass into the wood.

A bead forming on the ceiling above the cup is dealt with in the same way. The creature climbs as far as it can, extends one of its supports, and draws the bead aside until it joins the water on the wall.

It cannot keep the whole chamber dry. It does not attempt to.

Only the inside of the cup remains empty.

Someone may have taught it this. Someone may have discovered that it already did something similar and placed the cup where it could reach. The cup offers no answer. Its chipped rim is old, and the binding of the handle has tightened into the shell.

The creature sometimes rests beneath the shelf after its work, touching the bottom of the cup with one support.

Of the things brought into the chamber, the flat stone interests it differently. It travels along the worn face, following the slight hollow made by use elsewhere. The groove at the edge can occupy it for a considerable time. It places one support in the groove, draws it out, and places another there.

It does not do this to the chamber’s own stone.

A person sitting on the flat stone will eventually feel it near a heel. It examines footwear closely. Bare skin holds its attention longer. If the person shifts suddenly, it flattens itself against the floor, but it seldom flees all the way beneath it.

The heel impressions are not its work. Neither is the handprint. It sometimes settles inside the deeper impression, occupying the warm water there, its back just below the surface.

It has acquired a place to rest from the weight of someone who came before.

Whether it waits for that person is impossible to tell. It does not go to the entrance when sounds come through the seam. The dark filaments on the shelf are disturbed more often by entering sleeves than by its passage. It lives farther in.

Yet a hand left quietly in the water will be found.

Once it has examined the palm, it may stay there. Its weight spreads gradually across the fingers. The firmer pieces inside its back can be felt, separated from one another by softer tissue. Now and then one of its supports folds against the wrist.

It remains until the hand is moved or taken from the water. Then it releases its hold and settles to the floor. A little water clings between its body and the departing fingers before separating.

Later it will climb to the shelf. It will pass around the shell cup, press the binding, and remove whatever moisture it finds beneath it.

There is room in the hollow for this much movement. There is water enough to cover its body, a passage beneath the floor, and a worn place where it can rest. The cup is a difficult climb, but it can reach it.
