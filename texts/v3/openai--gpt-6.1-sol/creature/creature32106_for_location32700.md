The largest inhabitants cannot be seen whole. You can stand inside one, in a sense, without seeing more than a pale wall and the underside of an arch.

Their flesh lies below the passages, pressed into the pillar’s irregularities. It is dense, finely folded flesh, the color of a cut root. Thick portions extend upward inside the shell walls; thinner ones spread beneath the floors. At the lowest points, little muscular cuffs surround the plants’ translucent cords. They do not close tightly enough to injure them. A root can thicken there for decades, and the cuff will widen.

The shell animals feed where root and body touch. What passes between them is scarcely a meal: a little of the amber substance, altered by the plant, taken through a surface that has never been exposed to air. In return, the animals leave substances the roots can use. The exchanges are slow enough that a root may remain embraced by the same fold of flesh for its entire life.

The visible entrances are not mouths. They are spaces the animal has left while growing.

A young one makes a low hood, barely large enough to shelter a beetle. Its first chamber is occupied almost immediately. Feet smooth the sill; small bodies bring dust into the corners. As the animal grows, it lays down shell around these interruptions. A place repeatedly crossed tends to remain open. An unused opening slowly acquires a lip, then a shallow bowl of wall, and finally closes.

They are not aware of a visitor as a shape. They know pressure, warmth, the rhythm of feet. A grazer resting against a wall is a broad, familiar warmth. A beetle is a sequence of tiny points. The pressure of a hand is strange chiefly because it remains so still.

Deep in each animal, beneath its rounded upper chamber, is a narrow cavity lined with moist tissue. This is where it breathes. Air enters through pores too small to see and moves across surfaces that fold and unfold very slowly. Almost all the moisture remains inside. A shell animal may spend an afternoon making one full adjustment of these folds.

Sometimes a patch of wall feels warmer than the rest. Sometimes it gives, almost imperceptibly, beneath a sustained touch.

The grazers are more readily seen, although at first they resemble things left in the rooms: rolled mats, pale bags, lengths of folded cloth. Their backs are broad and low, covered in short fibers that lie flat in one direction and stand up in the other. Dust settles into them without reaching the skin. Their bellies are nearly bare.

Each has four legs, but little of any leg is visible when it is resting. The feet spread under its weight into soft, many-lobed pads. On level shell they make no sound. At a steep threshold the pads gather themselves, exposing a ring of small hard hooks. An old grazer climbing a wall does so with the patient concentration of an animal crossing something fragile.

Their heads are narrow because there is little reason here for a broad head. The mouth must fit between overlapping leaves. The eyes sit well back, protected by ridges of pale skin. Along the muzzle are two rows of blunt, sensitive papillae. These move separately. A feeding grazer feels the margin of a leaf before it bites, testing along it until it finds a suitable thickness.

It takes a piece smaller than one might expect.

The cut closes behind its mouth with a faint dull sheen. The grazer waits, jaw moving slowly, while its muzzle rests against the remaining leaf. Some leaves yield three or four bites. Others are left untouched after the first examination. The animals remember these differences by position and smell, though there is very little smell for anything else to detect.

Their bodies retain nearly everything they take in. Waste emerges as small, dry crescents that collect beside favored resting places. Beetles carry away the freshest ones. Older pieces become brittle and are worked into the joints of the shell, where roots eventually find them.

A grazer rarely drinks. A damaged leaf sometimes exposes a soft inner layer, and then several may stand beside it, taking turns at the wound. They do not tear it wider. Once it has sealed, they go elsewhere.

Touch serves them for much that calls would otherwise do.

A returning animal presses its muzzle beneath the jaw of one already resting. The other lifts its head, or does not. A shoulder laid firmly across a neck can ask for space. A foot held against another animal’s flank can keep two bodies together while they sleep. Their fibers retain the faint traces of these meetings. An individual coming into a crowded chamber works its muzzle across the backs nearest the door before choosing where to lie.

They are affectionate without being indiscriminate. Some will sleep beside almost anyone. Others pass through several occupied rooms to reach a particular old companion.

The young have disproportionately large feet and almost no hooks. For their first season they follow the worn, shallow routes. They learn the town partly by leaning against adults and feeling what those bodies do at a turn. Before a difficult step, an adult may pause with one hind foot lifted. The young animal noses the exposed pad, then places its own foot on the same patch of stone.

A passage can change during such a lesson. Growth beneath a floor may have raised a ridge since the last crossing. The adult tests it, settles its weight, and proceeds. Behind it, the young tests it too.

There are places where adults can no longer go.

In the narrower chambers live small grazers whose bodies never broaden very much. They are not young, though they are often taken for young by the larger animals. They accept the customary muzzle touch and then slip away through an opening the other cannot use. Their feeding grounds are inside the thickest growth, where leaves rise close together and even noon arrives as a green dimness.

Some carry old scars along their sides from squeezing past a shell lip that has since become impassable. When their customary entrance closes, they establish another route. For a time their new passage can be recognized by the brightness of the rubbed leaf undersides.

The beetles travel everywhere.

They are narrow enough to walk between a root and the rock it grips. Their wing cases are dull, with pale seams that disappear against the shell. In a file, each beetle touches the one ahead with its antennae before moving into its place. A broken line joins again without hurry.

They carry things under their bodies: a pellet, a flake of dead leaf, a fragment of shed skin folded several times. Much of this goes into warm cracks where larvae lie. The larvae have soft ridges along their backs and a single dark point at the head. A larva may take days to turn around. The adults feed it at whichever end of the crack it currently presents.

When a crack narrows, the beetles begin removing the larvae. Two or three brace themselves against the walls and pull one pale body into the open. It stretches astonishingly far, then contracts upon itself. They carry it away in a slow procession, its head held clear of the floor.

The flat animals beneath the leaves are hunters. Most wait with all six feet widely spread, their bodies sealed against the copper surface. A beetle can pass within a finger’s width and be left alone. A soft-winged flyer crawling past is seized so quickly that the leaf scarcely shifts.

Afterward the hunter resumes its old outline. Only the position of a foot has changed.

They mate without leaving the leaf. Two approach along its underside and rest edge to edge, joined by a small pale swelling between their bodies. For several days they look like one unusually broad blemish. Their young hatch along the central rib, smaller than the dust caught there, and spread out only when the leaf flexes.

The flyers are seldom airborne within the town. Their clear wings remain folded along their backs while they crawl over roots and grazer necks. They use flight chiefly to cross a bright court or escape a hunter. In the glare at the rim, wings open and close abruptly. Most turn back.

A few do not.

There is no visible distinction between one that will return and one that will pass beyond the last leaf. For a moment both hang in the same warm air. Then one lands on a root, and the other becomes too small to follow.

The animals within the town have little occasion to notice the desert. Their distances are measured in thresholds and bodies: the length of a companion’s back, the height of a familiar sill, the several chambers between feeding and sleep.

But they notice the stone.

Before a fracture opens, the shell animals become firm along one side. Grazers wake and stand. Beetle files divide around a place that appears unchanged. A court may empty during the cool part of the morning, its occupants shifting into neighboring rooms without any disturbance audible above their feet.

When the stone finally snaps, it is usually already alone.

Afterward there is a clean pink line in the floor. A grazer examines it with its muzzle. Beetles cross, turn, and cross again. The shell animal beneath begins adding a new margin, grain by grain.

At the outer ledges, where there is no room for another chamber, young shell animals grow close to the roots. Their hoods are small and exposed. A curled leaf may be all the shade they have.

One has settled beside the fresh crack. Its shell is scarcely more than a raised rim, still translucent at the edge. Beneath it, the soft body has spread into the narrow space the root has found.

A beetle enters, carrying a dry crescent under its belly.

For now it must back out the way it came.
