The people who live here can usually tell whether someone has come up from the valley by the way they put down a bag.

A visitor sets it on the floor. A resident looks first for a hook, a shelf, the back of a chair. Even in a dry room, even in summer, there may be water underfoot before morning. Children learn this before they learn to tie the fastenings on their coats. A dropped mitten is not necessarily lost, but it must be found soon.

Their clothes are made in layers that can be separated without undressing: short capes over sleeveless felt jackets, cuffs that unbutton from shirts, removable linings in boots. The outer layers hang beside the door. The inner ones go farther into the house, sometimes all the way to the bed. In a household of six, the drying hooks are as carefully assigned as places at the table.

They are not all patient people.

There are quick-tempered gutter owners, hurried factory supervisors, children who cannot bear to wait for a tram whose driver they can see eating something at the far end of the street. There are people who let a tap run while searching for a cup, and people who hear this through a wall and have to restrain themselves from knocking. Slowness is an expense imposed on them, not a virtue they uniformly possess.

What they have acquired is a habit of checking.

A woman going out to buy flour touches the stone beside her door. A machinist, crossing a bridge, glances at the underside of the next span. Someone looking out a window will pause over a leaf that yesterday was turned the other way. These observations rarely produce an immediate action. They are held until something else joins them.

One western household keeps a little slate on the kitchen wall. It records the first drip from the ceiling, the date the cupboard door began sticking, and the day a patch of moss outside changed color. Between these entries are ordinary reminders: return a borrowed saw, pay for the tram pass, ask a cousin why he has stopped visiting. The same hand writes all of them.

The town’s maintenance workers are recognizable by their pockets. They carry wedges, scraps of fine mesh, chalk, short lengths of cord, and blunt knives with blades worn into unfamiliar shapes. Some carry a spoon. A spoon is useful for clearing a narrow catchment without scratching the lining, and for lifting a whole pad of young moss when it is wanted elsewhere.

The oldest workers use very few instruments, which sometimes makes the younger ones distrust them. They say that a channel is *tired*, or that a wall has *begun taking*, or that a basin is *holding back*. Apprentices translate these expressions into depth, flow, temperature, and surface tension. After several years, the apprentices begin using the expressions themselves.

One crew leader has a bad knee and a notebook full of exact measurements. She refuses the idea that intuition is something a person either has or lacks.

“You remember badly until you’ve had to mend what you forgot,” she tells new workers.

She has twice been responsible for flooding the same cellar.

Most residents work at more than one thing. A press operator may also maintain the water screens for her building. A school caretaker tends the public propagation beds. An accountant has a turn at the washhouse boilers every sixth evening, and complains about it with sufficient regularity that everyone knows his schedule.

This is partly necessity and partly inheritance. Responsibilities pass down awkwardly. Someone dies, and along with furniture and savings leaves an obligation to inspect a culvert shared by three houses. The heir may live on the other side of town. There are arguments over whether such duties can be sold, exchanged, or abandoned. The water offices have shelves of rulings concerning people who no longer live at the addresses named in them.

Some families are proud of having watched the same trickle for two hundred years. Others consider this a ridiculous reason to remain in a damp apartment.

In the eastern gardens lives a man who came from the valley to repair electric motors and stayed because he liked a woman who grew medicinal roots. He still finds the vegetation oppressive. He cuts back the stems around their windows farther than she thinks necessary. Each spring they repeat the disagreement, now so gently that a stranger might mistake it for a discussion about pruning.

He has installed a small lamp over his workbench that imitates the strong downward light of lower country. His wife dislikes sitting beneath it. Their daughter, born here, uses it to examine beetle specimens sent by a school in the valley. She has never seen a living beetle and imagines their arrival in such numbers that the town would have to make provision for them.

Children are much less solemn about the plants than adults expect.

They make small boats from the broad leaves and send them through unauthorized channels. They discover that the moss behind a certain shed can be jumped upon three times before it releases enough water to soak a person’s stockings. They strip the pale interiors from dead stems to build rooms for imaginary animals. On warm pipes they arrange seedpods and wait for the sudden split, arguing over whether touching one invalidates the result.

The records of animal visits are serious work, however. Schools keep them in bound volumes. A drawing may be corrected by witnesses, but not erased. Beside one depiction of a shaggy visitor, a child has written, years later and in a different hand: *I made the legs too long because I was looking down from the bridge.*

Several adults still consult that page when discussing the animal’s size.

When the gray birds arrive, people try not to crowd them. This restraint is difficult. Faces collect behind windows. A tram keeper may delay opening a grate until the birds have finished probing it, causing complaints from passengers who would also like to see them but cannot from where they are sitting.

The birds possess no corresponding interest in the town. They leave scraps of wet root on clean sills. They quarrel. They depart during the finest weather, just when everyone expects them to stay.

The residents who live nearest the exposed western edge are thought to be independent, though this may only mean they are inconvenient to visit. Their rooms receive cloudlight from below, and many arrange their furniture to face the lower halves of the windows. A person can spend an afternoon mending clothes with the valley somewhere beneath their feet and never once see it.

Some become uneasy in houses farther inland, where light comes only from above.

There is an elderly woman at the end of the railing street who has lived in four apartments in the same building. As her sight failed, she moved down one floor at a time, following the light. Her current room was once a shop. The old counter holds her stove and a tray of cuttings. She knows who is passing by the sound their shoes make on the last dry stones before the railing.

She does not remember every voice as well.

People occasionally tell her that she should move nearer the heated routes. She asks whether they have found her a room with better windows. Usually they have not.

Illness here involves a great deal of moving things. Beds are brought into kitchens. Plants are removed from sleeping rooms, or brought into them, according to the physician and the ailment. Someone must carry the invalid’s washing to a drying house, then collect it before it cools. During a long fever, relatives can grow angry over a blanket that has been left damp. Years afterward, this may be what they remember most sharply: not the fear, but the blanket, and who failed to fetch it.

The warm seats at tram stops are occupied by people recovering from illness, workers waiting between shifts, and adolescents who have nowhere private to meet. Their purposes conflict. An old man will ask two young lovers to move so he can sit directly above the steam inlet. Sometimes they move. Sometimes they tell him the whole bench is warm.

The factories employ people who like repeatable things. They take satisfaction in a hundred valve housings with the same clean lip, in a belt properly tensioned, in sheets rolled to an even thickness. Outside work, these people may find the spreading shrubs and uncertain gutters exhausting.

A few maintain exceptionally bare homes. They scrape their thresholds, refuse window boxes, and keep their possessions in closed cabinets. Their neighbors consider them severe. Yet when a new seam appears in a wall, it is often noticed first in one of these houses.

Other residents deliberately make room for growth. They train a branch across a kitchen ceiling or allow a fern to occupy the unused end of a landing. One family eats beneath a low tree rooted in the courtyard outside; its limbs enter through an opening that can no longer be closed. In winter they fit felt collars around the branches. The youngest child has never known a dining room without bark in it.

Neither kind of household is entirely secure. Roots can split a well-kept drain. A flourishing indoor shrub can die over a single cold night, leaving its owners surprised by the amount of empty space it had occupied.

At the abandoned square, people waiting for the seventeenth-day water bring chairs, knitting, buckets with repaired handles. Those who need the earliest flow arrive before dawn. Those who come chiefly for company arrive later and claim they miscounted.

A retired surveyor visits with a folding rule. He has been measuring the seam for thirty-one years. At first he recorded its width. Later he began measuring differences in the height of the paving on either side. Now a younger colleague accompanies him, and they disagree about where a reference mark ought to be placed.

People waiting with buckets listen to this argument with interest. Their water will cross the seam if it opens much farther.

The surveyor’s grandson, who works in the south yards, has asked him whether he believes the stone is doing something.

“Of course it is,” the old man says.

He will not say more, because he has no measurements for more.

There are residents who intend to leave. They keep advertisements from the valley, practice trades that will be useful elsewhere, and complain about wet sleeves and the absence of animals. Some go. For a while they send back accounts of the noise at night: insects striking windows, dogs barking in different streets, water rushing openly over stones. Their families read these letters aloud.

Others return with new tools, unsuitable shoes, or a spouse who sets a bag on the floor.

And there are people who never think seriously about leaving, though they may dislike their work, their house, and most of their neighbors. They know a place where the first warmth reaches the wall in winter. They know which turn in a stair brings the smell of bread, which tram driver will wait for someone carrying two full buckets, which upstairs room is empty now.

At dusk they come home through the leaves. Before taking off their coats, they look at the catchment bowl, feel the drying cloth, listen for water beneath the grate.

Then they attend to whatever the evening has brought: a stopped channel, a child’s drawing of a bird, soup kept warm for a late shift. Someone opens the door to a neighbor. Damp boots are left on the rack. A bag is hung on its hook.
