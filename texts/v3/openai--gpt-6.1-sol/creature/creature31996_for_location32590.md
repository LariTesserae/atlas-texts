What lives here is easiest to find when the basin has begun to freeze but its center is still open.

It comes to the water by a passage beneath the shelter, too low for a person’s shoulder. First there is a faint scraping, easily mistaken for grit sliding under a slab. Then something pale rests on the basin’s lip. It has several points of contact with the stone, though at first they do not look like feet. They look like the ends of thin plates that have been set down carefully.

The rest follows slowly. Folded, it is about the size of a bundle held against the chest. Unfolded, it occupies considerably more space.

Its back is covered with overlapping leaves of a hard, translucent substance. Some are broad and clouded; others are narrow enough to admit light through their whole thickness. Their edges are worn, but their undersides have a faint lustre that ordinary stone lacks. Between them lie strips of dark material, visible only when it stretches. In those seams its body is supple.

It lowers one end of itself to the water.

There is a head, although no neck separates it clearly from the body. The head fits under the foremost plates when withdrawn. Extended, it is narrow and rather blunt, with two depressions beneath a projecting ridge. They may be eyes. Nothing in them catches the light.

Its mouth is easier to recognize: a short opening that divides when it touches the surface. It does not lap or draw water in visibly. It remains there while a wet line creeps upward along the underside of its head. Eventually the line reaches the first dark seam behind it.

By then ice has joined across part of the basin. The inhabitant lifts away before the opening closes.

It knows the basin in all its depths: full after a morning of ascending cloud, reduced to a few wet hollows beneath old ice, empty except for moisture between the pebbles. When water is scarce it goes to the carved vessel. The cut in the vessel’s side is too clean and deep for it to mend, but a little water remains below the channel. It can reach that water by placing its head almost upside down.

It is not the maker of the vessel. Nor did it build the shelter. Its body cannot lift the broad roof slabs, and it has no tool that would explain the chipped footholds near the notch.

It has, however, altered the shelter.

Small flakes have been fitted along the bottom of one wall, closing gaps that would matter little to a person but admit a draught at the height of its body. The depression in the floor contains no bedding. Instead there is a layer of selected fragments, laid with their flattest surfaces uppermost. They are not all the same kind of stone. Several retain warmth longer than the surrounding rock. Others take up moisture readily.

In full daylight these differences are almost invisible. After sunset, the inhabitant feels its way over them before settling.

Its limbs emerge from beneath the plates in pairs. There are at least six, perhaps more farther back. Each bends in several places and ends in a broad, divided pad. The pads can spread against a flat surface or close around an edge. On powder they leave shallow impressions like the marks of a hand with its fingers held together.

It moves with an economy that is not quite slowness. One limb tests a stone. Another braces. A plate shifts to give room. Then the body passes forward with surprising ease.

It seldom commits its full weight to a loose surface. Where a person would cross a heap of flakes in three noisy steps, it goes around, under, or between. Its routes are worn on the inward faces of the slabs, where no boot could comfortably reach.

In the morning it stays in shadow.

As sunlight spreads through the hollow, it approaches the boundary between bright stone and blue shade. It places part of its back in the sun, keeping its head and underside concealed. The pale plates separate slightly. Within each widening interval, the dark tissue turns toward the light.

For a time it resembles an object laid out to dry.

Then the small adjustments begin. A forward plate rises. A limb pulls the body sideways by less than its own width. A seam closes as another opens. It follows the moving edge of warmth without letting the whole of itself become exposed.

At midday it is usually nowhere to be seen.

This is not because it fears the light. It can endure much more brightness than warmth. What it avoids is the hour when wet surfaces lose their sheen almost at once. During that interval it folds into a crack whose entrance seems far too narrow for it. The plates overlap closely; the limbs disappear in a sequence practiced beyond hesitation. Last of all, the head withdraws.

From outside, only a pale edge remains. A climber might use it as a handhold.

The inhabitant would yield a little, then hold still.

It has no cry that could be distinguished from the other sounds here. Occasionally a plate strikes a neighbor and produces a dry click. When its seams are wet, stretching gives rise to a minute creak. Once, as it withdrew from the freezing basin, a thread of water held between mouth and surface broke with a sound almost like a syllable.

That may be the most voice it possesses.

The damp air brings it out again. It stands in the cold passage with its plates loosened and its limbs extended, giving the moisture access to places normally enclosed. Beads gather along the rims. Some run inward and disappear. Others drip from its underside.

It does not eat the mineral combs. It passes among them cautiously, sometimes pausing while a projecting shelf touches its back. Where drops have dried and left a crust on the stone, it presses its mouth against the deposit. Afterward the patch is smoother. Whether it takes nourishment there or merely something necessary to retain water is difficult to tell.

Its own plates grow unevenly. A damaged edge may remain damaged through several seasons. New substance appears beneath it, milky at first, then clearer. Old plates sometimes loosen and fall.

Those cast-off pieces are few. They lie among the fragments of the hollow, recognizable only by the shallow channels on their inner faces and a slight curve the surrounding rock does not have.

It is not known whether there is another of its kind.

There are places under the roof where a smaller body could fit. There are faint tracks that might have been made before it reached its present size. It occasionally pauses at the notch, but never where the sky is most visible. It waits beside a chipped foothold, close to the route of arrival, with its head extended into the rising air.

When cloud enters the hollow, it lets the cloud pass over it.

The three little stacks of stone remain standing. Once, after frost displaced the topmost fragment of the lowest stack, the inhabitant approached it during the afternoon. It felt along the fallen piece, braced two limbs against the slab, and lifted the fragment with the divided pads of another pair. Several attempts were needed before the stone stayed.

The stack was left lower than before, but legible from below.

At night it returns beneath the roof. It does not occupy the center of the old sleeping hollow. It settles against one side, leaving most of the depression empty. Its outer plates close over the dark seams, and its head comes to rest facing the entrance.

High on the wall, frost works behind a flake.

When the flake falls, the inhabitant unfolds one limb and lays the pad against the shelter floor. It stays that way through the impacts, feeling what reaches it through the stone.

Then it draws the limb back beneath its body. By morning, the nearest plate has acquired a fine rim of ice.
