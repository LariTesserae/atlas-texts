What lives in the settling yard has no single place where you could stand and say: here it is.

There are places where it is nearer the surface. The gardener finds them with the back of her hand. She rests her knuckles on the soil between plants, waits, and moves on. In some beds the earth is merely cool. In others, beneath the coolness, there is a faint warmth that does not change with the weather.

She plants differently in those places. Shallow roots, nothing that needs lifting each autumn. She has never explained this as a precaution. She says certain plants do better there, which is true.

The workers encounter it through their instruments. A string stretched between two bolts hangs clear of the paving on Monday and touches it on Thursday. The brass level gives an unfamiliar reading. Someone checks the level against a known surface, then checks the known surface against another.

Most of its existence enters the records as a discrepancy.

***

Below the yard, there are still rooms.

The old buildings were taken down to ground level, but their lower parts were filled rather than removed. Cellars received broken brick. Inspection pits received ash. A coal passage was blocked at both ends, though no one can now locate one of the ends. Between foundations, the fill lies loose; beneath the engine house it has been compacted until a driven spike rings almost as it would against stone.

What lives there has grown differently in each material.

In loose rubble it is fine and branching. In ash it spreads in close sheets. Where it meets an old wall, it follows the mortar, occupying the weakness without disturbing the brick. Around buried iron, its growth is thicker. Rust becomes caught through it in orange grains.

Very little has been brought up intact. A length of pale material came out wound around a drill during the trench work. Exposed to daylight, it tightened so sharply that it broke into several pieces. The pieces looked fibrous, but did not fray when pulled apart. By the following morning they had gone soft.

The sample is still in a jar in the engine house. There is nothing remarkable left in it: water, sediment, a strand resembling the skin from a cooked onion. The label gives the depth and date.

Its living portions remain attached to the place.

***

It does not occupy all the ground equally. There are bare reaches where it seems scarcely present, then a thickening beneath a tree, a wall, a length of disused pipe. Some of these concentrations are connected. Others may not be.

The largest tree has become involved with it so gradually that a clean boundary between the two would be difficult to find. The tree’s roots pass through the pale growth. Fine threads enter the roots in return. When the tree draws water, the surrounding ground settles slightly; when rain arrives, that same ground rises before the other wet patches do.

The gardener has noticed this. She takes her tea beneath another tree.

Under the northern staircase, it has made use of an old cavity whose opening is too narrow for a person’s hand. A torch reveals wet brick, the curved side of a bottle, and something close against the underside of the lowest step. It has the dull surface of a peeled mushroom stalk. Dust sticks to it. Once, a worker marked its visible edge with chalk on the brick. The following week the edge lay beyond the mark.

Nothing has ever come out of the cavity.

There would be little advantage in coming out. Aboveground there is dry air, direct sunlight, and very little to hold onto.

***

The engine changed it.

Before the machine was installed, the pressure under the yard accumulated in broad, slow swells. The first serious uplift raised a loading platform along with the paving around it. For several days the platform stood at an angle that made its old purpose seem absurd: a place for moving goods had become a place from which goods would slide.

The plates broke that movement into smaller movements. Each downward stroke displaced moisture, tightened the fill, and pressed the living material against its surroundings.

For a time the treatment worked almost completely.

Then the ground began to move between the strokes.

Now, beneath each plate, the growth is dense and flattened. Between the shafts it remains looser. Some of the newer ridges follow those intervals exactly. The engineer has drawn this correspondence on a plan, in red pencil, and written *check sequence* beside it.

The continued knocking after shutdown is harder to place on a diagram.

The first blows are recognizably those of the engine, carried through the foundations. Later ones differ. They come too far apart. Sometimes two arrive close together and are followed by a pause long enough for a worker to decide that the sound has finished.

On one evening, the gardener stood beside the largest tree with her palm against its trunk. At each sound she felt a small movement under the bark.

She took her hand away. The sounds continued.

***

Other things live in the yard without much concern for this.

The frog beneath the drainage cover has an excellent shelter. The iron blocks the wind, the grass provides shade, and insects gather along the damp underside. In hot weather the frog works itself into the soil until only its eyes remain visible. The cover tilts a little farther each year. So far, that has improved the shelter.

Snails use the freshest cracks. Their soft bodies pass through openings too narrow for their shells, so they feed at the entrances, stretching down into the damp. The living material below is sometimes scored with tiny parallel marks.

The starlings pull things out of those cracks. Usually worms. Occasionally a pale, elastic scrap that they shake, peck, and leave on the concrete. By noon such scraps have shrunk to almost nothing.

The beds over the thickest growth hold their moisture longest. Beetle larvae thrive there. Woodlice gather in the loosened bark at the base of the trees. The enormous trench leaves are often eaten full of holes, yet keep producing new leaves.

Whatever is below participates in these exchanges. Water passes through it. Roots take something from it. Small mouths consume its exposed edges. Bird droppings return to the soil.

It can be injured. Around a shaft repaired with fresh cement, the ground stayed flat for months, and the nearby ferns yellowed despite being watered. Gradually, growth resumed around the far side of the repair.

***

The workers live with it by attending to small distinctions.

A ridge with clean stone showing is marked immediately. An old ridge carrying moss can wait. A hollow sound under a cart wheel matters more near the railway than near the wall. Water where water has always gathered is ordinary; a new wet patch receives a date and a painted border.

The evening worker looks into the trench because once the leaves were all turned toward the engine house.

There had been no wind. By morning they had returned to their usual overlapping arrangement. He made a note in the book, and the next worker wrote *normal* beneath it.

The book contains years of such entries. It also contains requests for replacement gloves, complaints about the extractor, and a sketch showing where a sparrow’s nest should be left undisturbed during repairs.

No one working there needs a final explanation in order to know which gate sticks after rain.

***

At night, the living material takes up the space the day has made available.

Water moves into compressed places. Fine branches lengthen through ash. A sheet separates from the face of a buried brick, leaving a gap so slight that the brick remains supported. Beneath a painted circle, a root turns.

These movements are slow enough to accommodate much smaller lives. A snail can spend the whole night crossing a rising seam and reach the other side. The frog sleeps. Bats pass over the open paving and vanish behind the handless clock.

Near dawn, a few loose stones shift inside the fill.

At seven, the worker unlocks the engine house. Before starting the machine, she walks the yard with the book tucked beneath her arm. Beside the largest tree she stops and looks down.

A little crescent of concrete has lifted at the edge of the painted circle. The paint is intact on either side of the break.

She measures it, writes down the figure, and goes inside.

The first stroke passes through the block. Sparrows stir in the ventilator. A bead of water falls from beneath the walkway.

Below them, the new growth holds against the pressure.
