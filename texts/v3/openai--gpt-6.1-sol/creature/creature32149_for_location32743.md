A woman comes out of a doorway carrying a door.

It is not a large door, but it is awkward on the steps. She has tied it across her back with the hinges wrapped in wool, and when she turns a corner the lower edge knocks against the wall. A boy follows with a bundle of pegs and a latch. He is meant to warn her when something will catch. Instead he is watching a dog on the roof above them.

They are moving three streets uphill. The room they have left will become a fodder store; the room they are going to has belonged to her husband’s aunt, who now prefers to sleep near her daughters. The door was made by the woman’s father. Nobody has seriously suggested leaving it behind.

At the landing she sets it down, straightens, and complains at length about the aunt.

The boy sits on the bundle.

These are the people who live here: people with things to carry, relatives to accommodate, and strong opinions about which of them has been made to do the carrying.

Their bodies are various. There are tall, narrow people who fold themselves through low entrances, broad people who seem to fill a stair, old people whose progress is slow enough to become part of the traffic. Children race uphill until breath stops them. Visitors often mistake the inhabitants’ pauses for solemnity. Usually they are recovering, calculating the remaining climb, or waiting for someone ahead to finish an argument.

Most have capable legs. Not all have sound ones. An injured knee can alter the life of a household as thoroughly as a failed spring. Someone moves downstairs; someone else begins collecting their water. A person unable to climb may become the keeper of a courtyard oven, a reader of accounts, a minder of small children, or simply a person whom others must help. There is no useful work that abolishes the need for help altogether.

Their clothing is thick where it must be and patched where it can be. Wool is worn close to the skin, sometimes softened by years of washing, sometimes still coarse enough to raise a rash. Outer garments overlap generously. Belts hold little bags, knives, keys, and loops for securing a load. The best clothes differ from ordinary ones less in their cut than in the amount of cloth allowed to hang without doing anything.

Inside a coat, a lining may be blue.

A bride may have red thread running beneath the edge of her cuff, visible only when she reaches. A shepherd’s cap may carry a pattern of yellow squares no larger than fingernails. Dyers work with small quantities. Color is expensive, but people find ways to put it where they will encounter it often.

They notice hands. A chisel worker’s hands, a spinner’s hands, the swollen knuckles of someone who has cleared frozen gutters for forty winters: these are recognizable even when the rest of the person is wrapped against the weather. Rings are uncommon during work. They are kept on cords or tucked into boxes, and brought out when there is reason to sit still and look at one another.

Much of their courtesy concerns passage.

You do not stop to talk at the foot of a difficult stair. You step into a doorway when a loaded animal comes through. If someone carrying water approaches from below, you make room before they have to ask. Children learn to read a load almost as early as they learn to count: which basket contains something fragile, which sack will shift, which person cannot see their own feet.

Such courtesies can become weapons. A woman offended by her neighbor may yield with such elaborate care that the insult is understood by everyone on the stair. Two brothers who have quarreled can exchange perfectly correct greetings for years.

The walls bring people close without necessarily making them intimate.

Through stone and timber they know the times of one another’s rising, the sound of a particular cough, whether the loom next door has been idle. They learn when not to mention what they have heard. A household can conceal a grief for several days, but usually not an empty grain jar or an animal in distress.

Privacy is maintained partly by agreement. Someone can be plainly visible across a courtyard and nevertheless be understood to be alone. A shutter left half-open may mean that callers are welcome; in another district it means that a sick person needs air. Marrying into a neighboring town involves learning a surprising number of ways to offend people who appear to live exactly as one’s own family does.

The towns’ old names remain alive in these distinctions.

One quarter salts its curd too heavily. Another makes wedding bread so hard that guests joke about using it for paving. People from the western terraces are said to linger over a bargain; those from the spring houses are accused of thinking themselves indispensable. These claims travel far beyond the places they describe. Some are centuries old. Others began with an objectionable uncle still living.

There are wealthy families here.

Their houses have better light, more dry storage, and beams long enough to span a room without a central post. They can keep an animal through a poor winter instead of slaughtering it. They own shares in distant herds, lend seed, and employ other people to climb the stairs for them. A copper basin displayed during a feast may represent several seasons of somebody else’s labor.

There are also households whose belongings fit into two carrying frames.

The careful sharing of water does not make everyone equal. It makes everyone attentive. A prosperous owner may argue for an older interpretation of a mark on a lintel. A family without influence may arrive early at the cistern and bring witnesses. The people who settle these disputes need good memories, but memory alone is not enough; they must be willing to contradict someone whose grain they may later need to borrow.

A respected water keeper is not necessarily liked.

Food is eaten hot whenever possible. Barley thickens in pots. Roots are sliced small so that they soften quickly. There are sour milk, dried curds, pulses cooked with scraps of fat, greens bitter enough that children push them to the edge of the bowl. A piece of meat can flavor supper without anyone receiving much of it.

People argue about cooking despite the narrow range of ingredients. One cook scorches grain deliberately; another says this is an excuse made by careless people. A grandmother insists that a certain herb belongs in the winter broth. Her grandchildren remove it when she is not looking.

Feasts require advance negotiation with fuel, space, and neighbors. The room is warm before the guests arrive, then too warm once they are inside. Coats pile on sleeping platforms. Children become drowsy beneath the table. Someone opens a shutter and is immediately told to close it.

There is singing.

The songs used outdoors tend to be short calls, shaped to carry from one terrace to another. Indoors, voices can become intricate. Some songs have parts that enter one after the next, so a melody heard alone is incomplete until other people join it. Good singers are invited for reasons that have little to do with their general agreeableness.

There are comic songs about failed bargains, reluctant lovers, goats, and officials. The goat songs are considered suitable for children, though this depends on which verses are included.

Children know the country first as a collection of reachable places.

The whole plateau may mean little to a child who can name every doorway between home and a grandmother’s hearth. They have games involving channels, knucklebones, pebbles rolled down grooves in the paving. They make small enclosures for beetles and quarrel over whose beetle has escaped into whose yard. They climb where they should not. Adults call them down in voices calculated to carry just far enough.

By adolescence, many want to leave.

Some want a town where nobody knows their parents. Some want to see trees too tall to shelter behind a wall. Traders return with accounts of rivers crossed by bridges, fields that cannot be taken in at a glance, rain continuing all afternoon. These things are difficult to imagine and easy to desire.

Those who return may find the stairs worse than they remembered. They bring unfamiliar cloth, new tools, opinions about drainage, and a taste for food unavailable at home. Their relatives are pleased to see them and soon become tired of hearing how things are done elsewhere.

Others never return. A letter, when one arrives, is read repeatedly and then stored with documents concerning rooms, marriages, and water shares.

Animals belong to the country in overlapping ways.

A goat can be a source of milk, a debtor’s remaining asset, a child’s favorite, and an intolerable nuisance to the household below. Pack animals are known by their temperaments and by the corners they refuse to take. People speak patiently to them until patience fails. A good animal handler notices the failure before anyone else does.

Dogs learn which thresholds permit them to sleep and which merely tolerate their passing. Some accompany herds into the open country. Others live almost entirely along one chain of courtyards, receiving scraps from several families and obeying none of them reliably.

The birds use the cities without learning their boundaries. They nest above ownership marks, drink from disputed channels, and carry fibers from one household’s washing to another household’s roof. People protect a nest in one wall joint and block the next with a stone. Their affection has practical limits.

Beyond the houses, the wild creatures have their own necessities. A fox visiting a threshing terrace may be hunting mice rather than poultry, but nobody grants it the benefit of that distinction. Burrowing animals are watched for signs of spring. Predators are feared, admired from a safe distance, and pursued when they begin taking domestic stock.

The inhabitants know a great deal without all knowing the same things.

A mason can hear something wrong in the strike of a stone. A herder remembers a shallow depression where grass stays green after the surrounding basin dries. An old woman recognizes the smell that comes from a particular storeroom before damp shows on the wall. Such knowledge is taught willingly, withheld jealously, forgotten, rediscovered, or lost when its possessor dies.

They have explanations for the mountain’s movement. Some speak of pressures in the buried beds; some of beings beneath the plateau who turn in their sleep. Often the same person uses both accounts. Whatever the explanation, someone still has to inspect the crack.

When a district begins to empty, people do not leave together.

One family goes because its spring share has become too small. Another stays because a mother will not move. A workshop remains after its customers have begun coming from farther away. For a while, one occupied house may keep a whole abandoned stair swept.

The last household takes away what it can. Useful stone may be left for a later journey. A heavy jar remains in a corner. The door goes.

In the growing quarter across the ridge, someone waits for it beside an opening measured twice and still made slightly too narrow. There will be trimming, complaint, a search for a borrowed tool. Before evening the door will be hung. Smoke will rise from the new hearth, and a child will be sent down unfamiliar steps to fetch water.
