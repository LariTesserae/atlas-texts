The people who live here have dark hands.

This is not their natural coloring. They rub soot and softened wax into the backs of their fingers, around the nails, and across the wrists, where sleeves pull away during work. Against the white country an untreated hand is difficult to watch. A tool can be lost in its own brightness. The blackening makes the fingers distinct from one another, and a moving hand readable at a distance.

Children are blackened carelessly. There are thumbprints on their cheeks, dark crescents inside their ears. They learn early not to touch the luminous edges of new growth, but learning takes time, and the marks left by cold tissue are pale enough to disappear against the skin until the child comes indoors.

Most people have several of these scars.

Their clothes are less white than the landscape and less dark than their hands. Felted fiber, smoke-gray, patched with panels of waxed peel. The outer garments stand slightly away from the body on ribs of split stalk. Warmth depends on keeping a still layer of air around yourself; a close-fitting sleeve is a poor sleeve, however beautifully sewn. At the knees and elbows the fabric becomes thick and awkward. Travelers look broad until they undress.

A person’s good coat may have belonged to three people of different heights. The alterations remain visible. There is little reason to remove sound stitching.

Their faces are usually exposed, though they cover the mouth in pollen weather. Their eyes rest under deep hoods. Many wear narrow screens of smoked membrane, hinged so they can be lifted when examining a seam. You can recognize someone across a drinking place by the manner in which they raise that screen: both hands for the cautious, one finger for those occupied with something else, a toss of the chin among people too young to have lost one.

They walk with their feet a little farther apart than seems necessary. Each step is an inquiry. The sole descends, accepts weight, and only then allows the other foot to lift.

On a trusted surface they can move quickly. Watching them run is unsettling. Their bodies remain level while their legs make brief, precise adjustments beneath them. They can cross a field of narrow ridges carrying a full cup.

On an unfamiliar surface, even an angry person walks slowly.

***

The first thing a visitor is offered is a place to put their water.

There is a rack just inside most doors, shaded from the hearth and padded against the cold wall. Each household vessel has its own notch. Some vessels are stitched bladders; some are lengths of hollow stem with plugs at both ends. A few are ceramic, dark and heavy, brought from mineral country and inherited along with accounts of how far someone once carried them.

The host takes your container and weighs it in the hand. This is an intimate gesture, though not an intrusive one. It tells them what hospitality will cost.

Water is seldom poured out for display. A guest receives a lidful, then another when the first is finished. People drink without looking away from one another. The mouths of vessels are wiped with a clean scrap, never rinsed.

The drinking places have keepers.

A keeper knows which hanging points begin to drip first when warm sap approaches the surface, which stop without warning, which deliver water with a faint bitterness that means a new skin is forming above. They listen with one ear against an insulating funnel pressed to the floor. They keep records of intervals. They recognize certain changes by the way a bead gathers light before falling.

It is work requiring attention for which there may be no reward that day.

A keeper can spend an entire morning beside six dry points while everyone else prepares to leave. They may be called foolish. They may be called stubborn. If a bead swells beneath the fourth point shortly after noon, the same people will bring them warm food and pretend they had always expected it.

The soft growth around a pool belongs to nobody individually. It is carefully divided nonetheless. Children collect the outer leaves under supervision, leaving the tightly pressed centers intact. The black worms are lifted out of the cups with a hooked fiber and laid on a damp pad nearby.

There are arguments about the worms. Some keepers say they clean the water. Others say they merely arrive where the water is clean. Nobody has enough spare water to settle the matter properly.

A child given charge of a worm pad will sometimes name every worm on it, despite their resemblance. The names survive only until one retreats into the floor.

***

Food is less scarce than drink, but extracting it is a complicated business.

The great body offers several things that can be eaten and many more that resemble them. A pale swelling on the underside of a ridge may contain sweet, starchy tissue. An almost identical swelling may contain a fluid that stiffens the tongue and makes swallowing difficult for hours.

Harvesters judge by translucency, smell, the resistance of the outer skin, and changes recorded at that particular seam. They distrust instructions detached from a place. A woman who has cut food safely for forty years in one chamber will ask a twelve-year-old to guide her in the next.

Most edible tissue is sliced thin and dried. The bitterness concentrates before it fades. There are racks full of food that nobody can eat yet, and people whose occupation is turning it and waiting.

The six-legged browsers help.

Up close, they are less smooth than they appear from a distance. Their bodies carry overlapping plates of soft wax, scratched gray along the underside. The legs end in divided pads that fit around the lips of fissures. When grazing, they brace four feet and use the front pair to pull a tender edge toward the mouth.

Their eyes are small and dark, set far apart beneath a shelf of tissue. They appear to consider people without much concern.

No one has made them reliably follow a herd. Their interest in a seam exceeds their interest in a keeper. They will abandon a sheltered pen to stand beside a fresh wound all night, and then move away just before that wound closes.

Some families keep one near the house by bringing it strips of suitable growth. This succeeds until it does not.

People speak respectfully of the animals’ judgment, except when describing their own.

Their shed plates are collected for spoons. The young have six soft feet and no competence. They climb into drying racks, chew door lashings, and must be carried away from green cracks too deep for them to survive. Children love them with an affection adults consider excessive right up to the moment an adult is found sleeping with one inside a coat.

***

There are villages, but there are also arrangements of people who do not call themselves villages until a stranger asks.

Six households may share a keeper and separate when the seam fails. Two may travel together for years without joining their stores. A large settlement can disperse into neighboring hollows and continue to regard itself as one place, its name carried by a particular trough, a set of records, and the people responsible for answering disputes.

The houses move more often than the names do.

When a settlement departs, the order is familiar. Water first, then food that cannot be replaced quickly, then sleeping covers, children, tools, and the rest of the walls. A hearth tray is carried level by two people even when the fire has been put out. It is easier to lose half a roof than to replace one sound tray.

There are fastenings everyone is allowed to undo. They are marked with dark knots. A passerby who sees a wall beginning to strain may pull it apart without asking the owner.

Ownership resumes afterward. This causes plenty of trouble.

Someone will insist that their neighbor mistook an ordinary creak for a growth front and detached a doorway out of spite. Someone else will remember who failed to release a roof when a supporting peg began to tilt. These disagreements are discussed around communal meals, with the disputed knot or broken peg passed from hand to hand.

The elderly are not expected to carry heavy loads, but they are often the people nobody wishes to leave behind during an evacuation. A long memory is useful in a country where the same seam can behave differently over several lifetimes.

Memory also makes people overconfident. An old harvester may keep referring to a route that vanished before the youngest adults were born. Their companions listen politely until the harvester tries to lead them onto it.

***

Children grow up among measurements.

They are measured against doorposts, against staffs, against one another. When a doorpost is discarded, the strip bearing the height marks is peeled away and taken along. Some adults keep these strips bundled in their bedding. They can unfold the growth of a dead child, or of a son who now lives several chambers away.

The marks on the floor belong to a different kind of watching.

Near stable camps, people scratch lines beside pegs and ridges to see whether distances have changed. Children add their own lines. They try to measure a crack by lying down across it, or the movement of a wall by staring very hard. They argue about whether a surface that grows beneath both ends of a measuring cord can be measured at all.

Adults usually tell them to move away from the crack before continuing the argument.

The great body is spoken about at meals, during repairs, while walking. It has many names, some affectionate and some accusatory. The names change between chambers. People who believe it can hear them still cut its tissue. People who deny it any awareness will occasionally apologize to a stubborn peg-hole.

There are stories about the outer surface. In some, it is dark. In some, there is water falling freely through open air. In others, creatures walk around the whole body and mistake it for an ordinary plant.

These last stories are difficult for children. They want to know what the creatures stand on.

The storytellers generally supply another body, larger still, or decline to explain.

***

Not everyone remains near the seams.

There are walkers who carry messages, peel maps, samples of edible growth, and small objects whose owners have become separated from them. They travel alone when they can find no companion. Their packs are narrow so they can pass through constrictions; their black sleeping covers wrap around the packs by day.

They remember country as a succession of places where they stopped.

A ridge with a hollow large enough for two sleeping people. A warm patch beneath a branching light-fan. A cup that held water once, and might again. An empty shelter whose roof was still serviceable.

They know how a deserted settlement smells before its buildings become visible. Cold ash, worked sap, human grease preserved in fiber. Sometimes they find a message weighted under a hearth tray. Sometimes only the deep runners of the trough remain, engulfed by new skin.

At a fresh wall where a passage used to be, a walker may wait several days.

They inspect its margins. They note currents of air. They sleep, wake, and inspect again. Beyond the wall there may be a household expecting medicine, a sister with whom they quarreled, or merely the next stage of a paid journey. Whatever lies there, the wall responds on its own schedule.

Many walkers carry unfinished messages home.

***

The rare winged creatures are known chiefly by what they leave behind.

A hooked filament caught high on a shelter. A scatter of husks in a place where there is nothing to eat. Once, a whole animal was found beside a drained pool, its wings folded tightly along a body the length of a person’s forearm.

Under the outer wings were smaller membranes, densely veined, and beneath those a warm fur of short fibers. Its mouth could unfold into a narrow tube. There was dried moisture on the tip.

The people who found it kept it wrapped for two days, then opened it carefully. They wanted to know where it stored its water. What they discovered was little enough to disappoint them, though one of the membranes made an excellent repair to a light screen.

Afterward, people watched its kind more closely. There were claims that the animals descended when everyone slept, that they drank from droplets too high for a ladder, that they could find shafts by sensing the light through closed tissue.

Most remained claims.

A sleeping camp offers little opportunity to investigate the ceiling.

***

At night, under the black covers, the settlements become audible.

A cough passes through several walls. A buckle taps against a bed runner. Somebody shifts a vessel in its rack, and another person wakes long enough to ask whether it has leaked.

Inside one house, a man is repairing a glove by touch. He knows the holes well enough. Beside him a child has pulled the cover away from her face to look at the pear-yellow light showing through the wall.

She asks whether the body is asleep.

He puts a finger through the glove to test his stitching. Somewhere under the house, a thread-tight vessel carries water toward a part of the country neither of them has seen.

He tells her he does not know.

The child watches the wall a little longer, then draws the cover down. He finishes the glove, lays it where he will find it in the morning, and rests his dark hand on top of hers.

Outside, the hanging plumb threads remain straight. The water keeper is awake beside the cup. One drop is gathering overhead, slowly growing heavy enough to fall.
