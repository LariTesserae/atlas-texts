One of the animals here is most easily found by noticing a gap that stays open.

The smaller creatures crowd every other available surface. They press into joints, overlap along ledges, and fill the space beneath a loose stone before it has finished settling. But certain gaps remain clear: narrow black intervals between two slabs, sometimes no wider than a finger. Traffic approaches them, divides, and resumes on the other side.

Put a light into one and there is little to see. Two pale surfaces face each other across a wet space. Farther in, something contracts.

The gap is part of an animal.

Its body extends beneath both slabs and, usually, beneath several more. The exposed edges are lips, though they have no softness that invites the word. They are firm, finely ribbed, and darkened by incorporated mineral grains. An old animal’s lips can be worn as smoothly as the stone around them. Their outer margins support the same flat creatures that live elsewhere on the rock.

The animal feeds through the gap. It also breathes through it, releases its young through it, and puts out the slender organs with which it examines its surroundings. Most of its dealings with the basin occur along this one protected opening.

There may be other openings farther away.

A specimen occupying the underside of a single slab has a fairly simple body: a flattened central sac, paired gripping surfaces, and a fringe of short muscular extensions. Larger ones grow around obstructions. They send a portion of themselves through a crack, broaden on the far side, and establish another mouth wherever the rock allows it. Their connecting tissues become long and narrow. Sometimes these pass through channels too small to admit anything except a folded membrane.

It is difficult to say how much ground one animal occupies without taking the ground apart.

They do not dissolve the stone. They worry it.

Inside each opening are rows of blunt, replaceable teeth. These work slowly against loose mineral crust, dead shell, and the hardened sediment that accumulates around old seeps. A mouth may grind at the same projection for months. Between bouts of grinding, the animal draws water over the exposed surface and eats whatever has begun to grow there.

Its food includes microbial films, eggs, fragments of other animals, and its own shed tissue. Nothing that enters the mouth is sorted immediately. Material passes into a series of shallow chambers where it is held, wetted, and tested by small changes in pressure. The useful portions disappear first. The rest is worked against the teeth again.

Very little emerges in recognizable form.

Because they keep their passages open, other animals use them. The jointed grazers travel along the margins, taking food from places where the teeth have freshly uncovered stone. Thin scavengers enter the outer chambers and pick at the coarse residue. Some lay their eggs there.

These visitors are tolerated up to a point.

At intervals, an opening closes almost completely. The lips come together with a slow, even force. Anything able to retreat does so. Anything that has fastened itself too deeply must detach, break, or remain.

The animal then presses its internal surfaces together. A small quantity of cloudy water escapes. When the lips part again, the passage is clean enough to admit the next traffic.

There is no particular season for this. Different mouths of the same animal may close at different times.

Its whole body is held against the underside of the rock by broad muscular pads. These can adhere to a polished surface or grip an irregular one by swelling into its imperfections. Between them, the skin is translucent and loosely folded. Through it, a close light reveals bundles of pale tissue, dark digestive pockets, and the slow passage of fluid.

The body looks delicate there. It is delicate. Lift a slab carelessly and much of the animal may remain attached to it while the rest stays beneath the neighboring stones.

For several minutes the torn ends continue to reach toward each other.

If the slab is replaced, they sometimes join.

If it is not, both portions seal themselves. Each survives provided it contains enough feeding tissue and at least one of the dense sensory knots distributed through the body. An animal broken by a rockfall may become several animals. Two separated portions may meet again years later and fuse along their damaged edges.

They can also fuse with individuals to which they were never attached.

Such unions are not always successful. Along an incompatible seam, the tissues thicken and turn opaque. Each portion begins grinding at the other. The surrounding scavengers gather well before there is anything for them to eat.

In a successful union, fluid begins to pass across the boundary. The mouths retain their local habits for a while. One closes readily at a sharp vibration; another keeps feeding through much heavier disturbance. Gradually their responses change.

The combined animal acquires a different disposition.

This is most evident in what it will allow to cross it.

Though it has no eyes, it knows the weight and gait of animals moving overhead. Its sensory knots lie against the stone, each supplied by taut strands of tissue. Pressure travels along these strands from the gripping pads. A grazer produces a fine succession of catches and releases. A shell-bearing scavenger has a short dragging interval after every step. The great six-limbed hunters announce themselves even when they are still, because the traffic on their backs reaches the floor differently.

The gap animal receives all of this through its shelter.

It also makes a sound of its own.

A small tooth-bearing plate lies near each sensory knot. The plate can be drawn back and released against the stone. Close by, the strike is a dry tick. At a distance, through the floor, it becomes a brief tremor with a weaker second pulse.

Several mouths may produce these signals in sequence.

Sometimes a sequence runs back and forth across a buried body for an hour. Its timing changes as animals pass above it. On crowded stone the signals accelerate; when a heavy predator settles nearby, they become widely spaced.

Neighboring gap animals answer. So do a few of the grazers, which pause and stamp with their rear limbs before continuing.

What the exchange accomplishes is unclear. It seems to reduce congestion at some mouths and increase it at others. Occasionally a well-used opening closes before anything dangerous has approached it. A minute later, the feeding chamber of a large hunter opens over that precise patch of ground.

The gap animal may have felt the hunter preparing to rise.

The grazers that took its earlier signal have already left.

When a seep fails, the animal can move.

It first withdraws from its least productive openings. The lips soften and turn inward. Gripping pads release in succession, while the folded tissue between them thickens. For a time, a former feeding mouth becomes a blunt, muscular end that can push through grit.

The movement is slow enough to overlook. A dark line under one stone shortens over several days. Another appears beneath a slab a few feet away.

Most of the body stays anchored during this process. Extensions go ahead through cracks, test the dampness, and broaden where they find food. The old portions follow only when the new ones can support them.

In difficult ground, the connection becomes very thin. An established section may continue feeding while supplying a distant extension through a cord buried under the rubble. If the cord is cut, the extension has to finish becoming independent with whatever it has already received.

Many of the smallest animals found beneath stones began this way.

Others begin as offspring.

They are released during rising water, when the shallow pans have started to connect. At first they are translucent crescents scarcely larger than the grit. Each has two adhesive surfaces folded together and a narrow swimming edge that beats unevenly. They remain close to the bottom. Even a slight current carries them farther than they could propel themselves.

For a few days they eat nothing.

Then the swimming edge contracts, and the two surfaces unfold. A young animal must catch opposing faces of a crack before its stored food is exhausted. Those that settle on a single exposed surface can hold on, but their first feeding chamber has no shelter. Most are eaten.

The successful ones begin to draw the crack around themselves.

Their earliest teeth scrape at deposits finer than dust. Their first signals are too weak to travel through more than a few inches of stone.

Sometimes an adult answers.

It sends out an extension toward the juvenile, carrying food in its outer folds. The small animal feeds there until it can maintain its own opening. Some eventually detach. Some become part of the larger body.

An old individual may contain portions that settled in different cracks, in different years, with no common parent.

Along the eastern shell field, these animals are especially extensive. Their mouths occupy the hollows of dead shells, and their pads hold together fragments that seepage has not yet cemented. The teeth continually open new routes through the accumulated remains. Other inhabitants enlarge those routes, bring in sediment, die there, and provide more walls.

A section of the escarpment can persist for centuries while the bodies holding its innermost surfaces change.

Even there, an animal can die completely.

A lasting interruption in the deep water does it. So does a deposit that seals all the useful mouths before another can be opened. The body shrinks beneath its stones. The gripping pads loosen. Scavengers enter chambers that would once have closed on them.

For a long time afterward, traffic continues to divide around the old openings.

Eventually something settles in them.

To find a living one, wait beside a clear gap without touching the slab. Let the basin’s larger sounds pass. Watch the grains nearest the lip.

A fine, colorless extension may emerge, carrying a bead of water at its tip. It will touch the stone in several places. It may touch your boot and remain there, following the pressure of your weight through the leather.

Beneath you, a plate strikes once.

Somewhere beyond the slab, another answers.

The extension withdraws. The lips part a little farther, and the grazers begin to cross.
