The bench is usually occupied.

What looks like a fold in the growth along the wall is the back of someone sitting there. The shoulders have the same gray-green color as the tile joints. Below them, a thin body descends through the gap between seat and wall; two longer portions lie along the underside of the bench, where they can feel the warmth coming through the floor. Only the hands rest in the open.

They are recognizable as hands, though they have too many joints to close in quite the familiar way. The fingers lie loosely crossed. Fine roots pass between them without entering the skin.

When the shadow of the flywheel reaches the bench, one hand uncrosses. It takes much of the afternoon.

These people are difficult to see even when one knows where to look. Their bodies admit light unevenly. There are opaque places at the joints, along the spine, around the mouth; elsewhere the color of whatever lies behind them comes through. Seen against a brick wall, a person can resemble a patch where the mortar has worn differently. Against a window, the same person becomes a dim obstruction, with a few bright lines inside.

They have no lungs. Water occupies the branching chambers of their chests and passes through them slowly. Within each chamber hangs a tissue like wet silk, gathering what the water carries. A body recently returned from the deeper galleries has a faint amber tint. One that has spent a long time among the upper windows becomes almost clear.

Their heads are narrow and usually bowed. The eyes are small, set far apart, with lids that close sideways. They see movement well, but daylight poorly. Most keep one hand in contact with something: a rail, a pipe, the wall of a house. Through it they feel the district’s circulation, including disturbances too slight to turn a leaf.

A visitor can pass within arm’s reach of several inhabitants and notice none of them. The visitor is moving so quickly that their small adjustments merge with the motion of weeds.

The inhabitants, meanwhile, feel the visitor for hours.

A sleeve dragged across a doorway produces a complicated wake. Heat leaves a living body in bright, brief eddies. The force of a kick travels down rails and through loose window frames. After a swimmer has gone, people in neighboring rooms lift their heads, or open a hand against the wall. Farther away, someone pauses over a task, waiting for the unfamiliar pressure to pass.

They are neither alarmed nor particularly incurious. Some approach the route afterward. They touch the place where a fin struck a stair, examine a displaced thread, hold their fingers in the cooler water brought down from above. A few remain there until the disturbance has entirely disappeared.

***

There are households in the small houses around the clock.

Their members do not necessarily resemble one another. An old person may be broad and deeply colored, with mineral deposits along the forearms. Another, equally old, may have become so thin that the outline of the room shows through their chest. Age depends partly on where a person has lived. Those who work near the engines thicken. Those who tend the upper passages grow long and light, able to hold themselves between two window frames with very little effort.

In one house, three people occupy the room with the chair pushed back from the table. One rests against the ceiling roots. One lies beneath the table, a hand curled around its central leg. The third stands in the space behind the chair.

They have been there together long enough for the curtains to grow heavy.

They do leave. They have routes and obligations and preferences. But this room is where they return, and the furniture retains the shape of their arrangements. The person beneath the table dislikes the current at the window. The one among the roots likes the pale hour before the surface clock lights more brightly. The third often goes out to the market rail and stays until someone comes to fetch them.

Fetching consists of laying a hand beside theirs.

After a time, they turn together.

There is affection here, though it seldom produces an embrace. A hand laid over another hand would interrupt its contact with the rail. Instead, they touch at the sides of the fingers. They make room along a warm pipe. They keep a familiar clearance open.

Someone has spent years preventing a curtain from fastening itself to a particular window latch because another member of the household likes to look through that opening.

***

The bright patch on the brass handle belongs to a woman whose body has darkened almost to the color of the door.

She visits several rooms beyond it. In each, she checks the direction of the water by lifting a loose length of thread. If the thread fails to incline, she searches the passage leading inward. Sometimes she removes a fallen tile. Sometimes she eases a root away from a narrow gap, returning over many months until it has learned another direction.

She carries very little. A strip of ceramic serves as a wedge. A hook, worn smooth, hangs inside a fold at her hip. She also carries a cup.

The cup has a crack across its base and cannot retain anything finer than silt. She takes it with her nevertheless. At the end of a visit she sets it on a table, holds it there for a while, then picks it up again.

No one else uses that cup.

Near the engines, people work with both hands and much of the body pressed to the machinery. They listen through their bones. A change in a bearing may be perceptible several weeks before it alters the flywheel’s movement.

Their work is mostly small. They lift pale fibers away from an opening that must remain clear. They loosen a colony where its growth has begun to obstruct a valve. They press a failing seal together and remain against it while the algae thickens around their fingers.

The growth feeds them in return. Clear oil collects along shallow channels in their skin and is absorbed there. They take other nourishment from the films on stone and metal, drawing it through the soft inner surfaces of their palms. A feeding hand leaves a clean oval that darkens again over the next several days.

They cannot live long beyond the circulating water. At the boundary, their bodies begin to lose definition. The tissues between their ribs become slack; their fingers stop receiving enough from what they touch. They know the outer sand mostly by feel, from journeys made to retrieve something drifting away.

Some have gone farther. Their households still preserve places for them.

***

Their young are first noticed as an extra weight beneath a parent’s arm.

For a long time a child is little more than a transparent fold, attached along one side of the adult’s body. Water passes through both. As the child develops, separate chambers appear, then a faint spine, then hands whose fingers stay curled around whatever they first encounter.

Parents choose that first object carefully. A warm brass ring is common. So is the handle of a porcelain cup. In the pumping house there is a smooth ceramic insulator that has served this purpose many times.

The child’s earliest excursions follow the object as the parent carries it from place to place. Eventually it is set down.

A young person may spend an entire season on a single stair tread. There is enough to learn there: the roughness of the iron, the warmth entering from below, the minute pressure changes when someone crosses an adjoining room. Before venturing into a passage, children must learn to distinguish the district’s steady pull from the wake of another body. Otherwise they follow someone away without knowing how to return.

They play in the warmer clearances. One presses a loose pane and releases it; another waits farther along the wall to feel the tremor. They repeat this with variations too small for a visitor to notice. Sometimes a child continues after the others have lost interest, until an adult comes and gently steadies the pane.

The white eel in the southern exchange is watched with particular attention. Its journey along the handrail is fast enough to be astonishing and regular enough to be studied. Generations of children have waited for it. None has succeeded in anticipating when it will emerge.

***

They do make records.

The pencil beside the pressure gauge is used by a man whose forearms bear pale grooves from years of resting against the housing. He takes it out, makes a mark, and replaces it. The marks go on small porcelain panels stacked beneath the gauge. Most are curves, spaced dots, or lines that approach one another without meeting.

They record things for which the instrument has no dial: a new warmth beneath the western houses, the interval between two clicks, the time a familiar hand failed to appear on the market rail.

He sometimes adds a mark to an old panel. What seemed complete to him before has changed.

No one touches the second face of the public clock. Its slow hand can be felt through the casing, but the inhabitants disagree about what it measures. A few believe it keeps the engines in agreement. Others visit it when someone has been born, or when a household has become smaller.

The person on the courtyard bench comes for neither reason.

They like the open space above the tiles. They have spent much of their life in crowded rooms, feeling arrivals through walls before seeing them, accommodating roots and neighbors and the small movements of furniture. Here there is room in which nothing needs to be moved aside.

They sit with their hands exposed.

During the afternoon, as the flywheel’s shadow crosses the courtyard, the leaf beside the bench slowly unfolds. Its edge comes to rest against one finger. The person moves the finger enough to give it room, then remains there as the leaf opens farther.

Warm water passes beneath them both.
