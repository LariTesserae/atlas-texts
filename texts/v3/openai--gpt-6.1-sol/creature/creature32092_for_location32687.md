The people who live here carry two kinds of footwear.

One pair is light, clean, and usually left at work or beside the inner door of an apartment. The other is broad-soled, waterproof, and scuffed pale at the toes by the grit. Even people who spend most of their lives underground own the second pair. They stand in entrance cupboards beneath coats that can be closed at the wrists and throat. Children’s coats have loops between the shoulders so an adult can catch hold of them near water.

Coming indoors takes a little time. People unfasten things, shake out hoods, check one another for wet cuffs. A conversation begun outside often stops at the threshold and resumes several minutes later, with everyone looking slightly smaller.

They are not a uniformly quiet people. In kitchens, workshops, and the rooms beside school entrances, they interrupt one another and laugh loudly. Their public interiors are full of the sounds absent from the surface: dishes, announcements, arguments, music from a room whose door has been left open. A station concourse can feel surprisingly crowded after an hour on the plateau.

But outdoors they tend to spread apart. Two friends walking together may drift several paces from one another without breaking off their conversation. When the wind turns, they draw close again.

They learn early that visibility is not the same as nearness.

A small child will point to a building across a lake and ask to go there. An older child will ask which station serves it. Adults judge distances by the intervening ground: two drainage folds, a freight seam, the ridge where the sedges stop. They are much less likely to say that something is “just over there.”

Their directions include conditions.

Take the upper passage if it has frozen. Follow the shore unless the outlet has shifted. The shelter is beyond the grazers, but don’t go through them.

There are official names for all these routes. There are also names that appear on no map: the warm stone, the old bird shelter, the stairs that used to end at water. Some survive long after the thing they refer to has changed.

Much of their work consists of noticing.

A maintenance worker arriving at a service shaft pauses before opening it. A stain around the rim, the shape of a puddle, a patch of thawed gravel wider than yesterday’s—any of these may matter. Lake wardens remember where ice first broke in previous years. Drivers know the feel of a track joint through the soles of their feet. A school caretaker reports a door that has begun to close too readily.

None of this makes them solemn. The caretaker will complain that the door has more authority than he does. The driver will insist she knew about the joint before the instruments did, whether or not that is true.

The city depends on people willing to report small inconveniences before they become impressive failures.

There are specialists whose jobs take place far below the inhabited rooms, among the systems that hold the managed rock in its improbable arrangements. They travel down through levels where the walls grow warmer and the air becomes dry enough to sting the nose. Their tools are mostly screens, probes, and remote machines. Some shifts pass without a single adjustment.

They come home smelling faintly of heated metal. Their families ask whether they had a quiet day, and understand that the answer can be complicated.

Other workers spend their days on the surface, following water.

They carry measuring rods, sample containers, and lunch in battered cases. They kneel beside pools with no names yet. They watch a trickle cross an almost imperceptible rise, turn back on itself, and finally escape through a gap in the stone. Sometimes they stay late because a basin is close to spilling and they want to see where it goes.

Their maps contain uncertainties that other maps usually conceal. A stream may be drawn as a broad translucent band, with several possible channels inside it. Children are taught to read these bands. They are also taught that a line on a map is no guarantee that the ground will agree.

The children are particularly interested in the new pools.

They find them in hollows on the way home, where yesterday there was only a wet sheen. They crouch at the edges and wait for something to move. Sometimes there is nothing visible. Sometimes a tiny swimmer has already arrived through a crack below the waterline.

A pool can acquire a child’s name before it acquires algae.

The first inhabitants of such places are hard to see. Films spread across submerged mineral. Small grazing creatures follow, moving through passages too narrow to admit daylight. In the clearer shallows, their pale bodies cast distinct shadows onto the stone beneath them.

The fish are more conspicuous and often seem badly concealed. They hold over pale bottoms with every movement of their fins visible from above. At the disturbance of a footstep they vanish sideways beneath a ledge, into a space that looked too thin to contain them.

People who know the lakes can tell whether a fish has come from deeper water by its colour and the way it hesitates at an exposed margin. There are arguments about which populations travel through the seepages and which remain in one basin all their lives. In some households these arguments recur at meals for years.

The grazers are neighbours in a more immediate sense.

Each has a broad head, a damp muzzle, and long outer hairs that hang in uneven locks over dense underfur. Their feet spread under their weight. On wet gravel they walk with slow assurance; on smooth paving they become cautious and faintly indignant.

They learn where the city offers shelter. Several groups favour the lee of residential buildings, standing beneath the windows while rain streams off their backs. Older animals remember passages that were open in their youth. Occasionally one will lead the others to a sealed entrance and wait there, as though the building might correct its mistake.

Residents recognise individuals: a torn ear, a pale shoulder, a female who always stops before crossing a particular drainage channel. Recognition does not make the animals tame. Notices in the entrances remind people not to feed them, approach their calves, or assume that a resting herd has left enough room to pass.

The animals watch people with a steadiness that can be uncomfortable at close range. Then one lowers its head to eat, and the others gradually do the same.

The predators are less often seen whole.

A back passes through a shallow fold. A narrow face appears above a stone. They are long-bodied, thick-furred animals, able to wait in ground that seems to offer almost no cover. Their coats darken when wet. At a distance, a crouching adult may look like another patch of exposed rock.

They avoid the busiest entrances but use the quieter parts of the city readily. Cameras record them beneath exterior stairs and crossing freight routes at night. People check those recordings before opening certain paths in the morning.

Children know what to do if they see one. They practise standing together, calling an adult, and walking toward shelter without running. The lesson is repeated until it becomes as ordinary as using a crossing.

The birds are less respectful of boundaries.

They nest on ledges, beneath roof rims, inside the sleeves intended to protect exterior sensors. Maintenance crews arrive with brushes and replacement covers and find themselves watched by furious little faces. A few species have learned precisely how long an entrance door remains open.

Underground, a stray bird can bring a concourse briefly to a standstill. People who would hurry past a dropped bag look up together as it circles the lights. Someone fetches a net. Someone opens a service route toward the surface. For a few minutes the whole station concerns itself with one small, frightened body.

Then it is gone, and the trains continue.

Most lives here are occupied by ordinary matters. People want better rooms, less awkward shifts, someone to sit beside them at dinner. They lose gloves. They grow plants under lamps and disagree about whether the plants need more light. They form friendships across distances that make an unplanned visit difficult.

An invitation commonly includes a place to sleep.

In an apartment facing the open ground, a guest may wake before the household and sit by the window. Beyond the glass, birds work along the lake margin. A grazer lifts its head from the sedges. Rain approaches one district while sunlight lies on another.

Inside, the heating clicks. A child turns over in the next room. Someone comes into the kitchen, sees the guest awake, and puts on enough water for two.

Later they will go out. They will choose a route, check the weather, and see whether the little stream beside the entrance is still running where it ran yesterday.
