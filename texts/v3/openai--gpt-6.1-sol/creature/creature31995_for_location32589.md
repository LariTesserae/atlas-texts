The first resident you meet appears to be waiting for someone.

She stands outside a numbered entrance with a folded cloth over one arm. She is tall, though not unusually tall for this city, and dressed in several thin layers whose edges have been weighted to keep them from stirring. Her face is narrow. At her temples the skin has a slight, milky translucence, like the edge of a pale stone held against a lamp.

She watches you cross the last stretch of open ground.

“Stay on the paving,” she says.

There is no alarm in her voice. You look down and see nothing that would justify the warning: pale dust, a few tough stems, your own footprints.

Then, halfway between you and the entrance, the dust darkens in five places. Each mark is no larger than a fingertip. They persist for a moment and disappear.

“Thank you,” she says when you reach the curb.

The cloth is not folded clothing. It is damp linen wrapped around a shallow bowl. She has brought it outside because something belonging to her is coming home, and the water will help it find the door.

Only later do you learn that she has been standing there for less than a minute. She has spent most of the evening upstairs, repairing a sleeve.

Both things are true.

***

Most of the city’s inhabitants have bodies that would fit into an ordinary chair.

They use chairs, though they prefer stools with an open space beneath them. They wear shoes. They carry parcels against their ribs, quarrel over repairs, forget appointments, and return from the groves with leaf fragments caught in their clothing. In the transit halls they lean close to read announcements. An elderly man occasionally falls asleep with one hand on the warm wall and misses his train.

From a distance, their movements are familiar.

Nearer, there are small difficulties.

A person may begin turning before deciding which way to go. Their shadow, when the light is sufficient to produce one, sometimes holds its position for a fraction of a second after they have moved. Two residents passing in a narrow doorway pause with an attentiveness that seems excessive until you see the fern beside them bend, first toward one, then the other.

What occupies the visible body is not all that must get through.

The residents have an extension for which the nearest local word means something between *weather* and *reach*. It is not a limb, although it can be injured. It is not a possession, although parts of it can be left behind. Most adults extend across several rooms and a portion of the ground outside. Some extend farther. Its limits vary with health, fatigue, season, and considerations they do not readily discuss with strangers.

They do not usually perceive it as an outline.

Ask where someone ends and they may answer by naming a doorway, a tree, a troublesome patch of pavement. Ask again next week and the answer may have changed.

This is one reason the towers stand apart.

In a crowded building, a resident can wake with another person’s thirst. A disagreement on the floor above can spoil the taste of water below. Sleep is especially difficult when several unfamiliar people share a wall: one person wakes to fetch a cup, and four others dream of rain.

The rounded chambers attached to the towers are bedrooms. Their placement has been adjusted over generations of municipal calculations and private complaints. The chambers of couples are sometimes close together, sometimes on opposite sides of a building.

There is no reliable way to tell, from architecture alone, who loves whom.

***

Children are more compact.

For their first few years they remain almost entirely within their skins. They can be carried without preparation, laid together in a bed, or gathered around a table in noisy groups. The nursery near the third transit junction is the loudest place in the district. Outside its windows, adults stop speaking and listen with expressions that are not quite nostalgia.

A child’s reach usually appears during sleep.

There are practical signs: a cup empties without spilling; a nearby leaf turns its copper underside toward the bed; the child complains that a room they have never entered is too bright. Families place shallow trays of damp clay under the nursery furniture and examine them in the morning. Eventually there is an impression.

It seldom resembles a foot or a hand.

One child left a pattern like a folded fan. Another produced a single groove running from the tray’s center to its edge. For several weeks a little boy’s clay showed nothing at all, although every lamp in the apartment flickered when he dreamed.

His mother was pleased. His father wanted the wiring inspected. Both took photographs.

As children grow, they are taught to gather themselves before crossing a busy junction. They practice beside painted circles in school courtyards. A teacher rings a small bell, and the pupils stand still until all the leaves around them have stopped moving.

Some learn quickly. Others leave portions of themselves in the playground and have to be brought back after supper.

The teachers are patient, but not endlessly so.

One of them has taught the same exercise for thirty years. She keeps a cupboard of bowls, cloths, smooth stones, and other things that particular children find reassuring. She also keeps a notebook in which she records, with increasing severity, the names of parents who arrive late.

***

The city’s horticulturists know its people unusually well.

A broad-leaved plant in a bedroom may draw off an uncomfortable accumulation during the warm interval. A certain moss can help a resident remain gathered through a journey. Other plants make it easier to extend into unfamiliar ground, though their use requires care.

The little lamps among the roots are adjusted for individual inhabitants. A woman who has been ill may need one pale leaf illuminated all night. A man recovering from a difficult separation may need three lamps, widely spaced, with none visible from his window.

These are gardens, nevertheless. People take pleasure in them. They choose foliage they like, argue about pruning, cultivate flowers with no known benefit. One resident has spent nine years persuading a dry shrub to grow sideways over his balcony. His neighbors consider the result ugly. He considers it nearly finished.

In a wealthy grove, the same family may have tended the central hollow for so long that their presence is almost impossible to distinguish from its conditions. Visitors feel welcome or unwelcome before anyone comes downstairs.

Poorer residents live near the dry margins, where gathering oneself requires effort. Their rooms can be immaculate and still feel exposed. They leave cups in every available recess. They wait for the walls to sweat.

There are municipal allowances for water, and disputes over who qualifies.

***

Not all the inhabitants have a visible body.

The residents are cautious about making claims concerning these others. An empty room that consistently remains warm may contain a person, or faulty insulation. A stretch of canal that changes course around a particular stone may be occupied, or badly laid.

Evidence accumulates slowly.

In one public building, an unused ticket window has been supplied with a fresh cup of water every morning for eighty-six years. No one remembers why the custom began. Once, during renovations, the window was boarded over. By evening all the numbered entrance plates in the district had turned their blank sides outward.

The boards were removed.

Now the cup is included in the building’s maintenance budget.

There are also presences that move. The large thing in the fern channels is one of these, although *large* refers chiefly to the amount of water it takes. People who have encountered it report different impressions. One heard the sound of wet cloth being wrung. Another smelled a kitchen from her childhood. A maintenance worker saw the ferns flatten around a shape that seemed, briefly, to have a bent head.

None of them saw it arrive.

After a passage, gardeners inspect their hollows. Someone usually leaves an extra vessel of water near the closed path. Someone else usually empties it, on the grounds that encouragement will only make matters worse.

***

The small six-legged creatures from the ridges are less mysterious to look at and no easier to understand.

They have narrow joints, blunt heads, and backs studded with mineral fragments. In the groves they climb until they reach a moist surface, then remain motionless for days. The fragments on their backs grow dull. Occasionally one loosens and drops.

Children collect the fallen pieces.

Adults discourage bringing the creatures indoors. They are harmless in most respects, but a resting one can make a wall begin to open along a line no builder remembers placing there.

Once a creature has shed its last bright fragment, it descends and disappears into the soil. No empty shells have been found. No one has seen one eat.

A woman in the northern district has recorded them for most of her life. She knows which ridges produce broad-backed ones and which produce narrow ones, and has identified several that emerged twice, years apart, with new stones attached.

Her neighbors bring her specimens in ventilated boxes. She returns the boxes promptly and the specimens never.

She dislikes being called an expert. She has a room full of labeled drawings, and nearly every drawer contains something that contradicts the label.

***

Old age often makes the visible body smaller.

An elderly resident may seem frail while occupying more of the city than anyone nearby. Visiting requires an appointment, not because they are busy but because they need time to draw enough of themselves into the room to attend to you.

They tire after conversation. For hours afterward, a familiar crossing may feel oddly vacant.

Some continue to work. A transit engineer who no longer leaves his chamber can tell, by placing his bare feet on the floor, which arriving car will take too long to condense. He sends the platform attendants a message. They add a few seconds to the waiting interval.

Others become troublesome. They extend into places where they are not wanted, hold onto demolished rooms, or refuse to relinquish trees scheduled for removal.

Death is not always clear.

A body may stop breathing while the reach persists. Water continues to vanish from its accustomed cup. Lamps fail when placed in the wrong position. For a time the family speaks to the empty chair. Eventually most of these responses cease.

In some households they do not.

The city keeps a register of residences that cannot yet be reassigned. The entries are reviewed, with considerable tact, every twelve years.

***

At the southern edge, the newest inhabitants are difficult to meet.

Some have come from elsewhere and are still learning how to rest on this ground. They rent rooms with as little vegetation as possible. They distrust the upward-running water. When a tower shifts at night, they wake sick and spend the morning sitting close to one another, despite the discomfort.

Others may never have arrived.

In the open foundations, cups sometimes fill before any plumbing has been connected. A surveyor feels someone listening from a room whose walls are still stakes and string. On the bare plain, a shallow depression holds moisture through the dry interval, and a single seedling appears at its center.

The municipal staff mark these places on their maps. They leave more space around them than the regulations require.

Occasionally a resident goes out to sit beside one.

The woman you met at the numbered entrance does this on her free afternoons. She carries the shallow bowl wrapped in linen. She places it on the dark ground, far enough from her stool that you might mistake it for something she has forgotten.

For a long while nothing happens.

She repairs a sleeve, reads, or watches the newest seam lift grains of dust along its edge. Before leaving, she checks the bowl. Sometimes it is full. Sometimes it is empty. Once she found a small ridge running through the water, dividing it into two clear halves without breaking the porcelain.

She has brought that bowl back every week since.

Asked whom she visits, she says she does not know yet.

Then she folds the damp cloth around it, carefully leaving a little space above the rim.
