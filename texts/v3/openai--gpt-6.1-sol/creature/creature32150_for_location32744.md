The permanent inhabitants are easiest to find after a chamber has become too small to enter.

A traveler returning to one of the pale shelters may discover that its doorway has narrowed to a slit. Water still seeps out through the opening. If a hand is laid against the wall, the noise of the plain diminishes, not in the air but inside the arm. The fingers can remain there without aching. Beneath them, something moves with such restraint that it might be mistaken for the slow displacement of wet clay.

The movement stops when the hand is lifted.

For years, this is all that may be seen of the thing.

Its body lies among the nested stones beneath the chamber. It is pale where the stones protect it and brown where sediment has worked into its surface. There is no clear edge to it. Thin portions pass between adjacent slabs; thicker portions fill spaces that seem too regular to have occurred by chance. Some of the stones are enclosed altogether. Others can be lifted away, revealing flesh that closes over the vacancy without hurry.

The foundation is neither a skeleton nor a collection of possessions. The inhabitant needs those separate weights. When a pressure moves through the country, each stone bears against another, and the body between them changes thickness. A hundred small motions take place beneath the apparently motionless room.

Sometimes a cupful of water spills from the doorway before the concussion arrives.

Sometimes it spills afterward.

The animal does not appear to distinguish these occasions.

Its upper surface produces the chamber. New material rises at the rims of the walls, soft enough at first to take the impression of a fingertip. It becomes firm under water. Rain falling into an unfinished room leaves rings in its floor which persist after the floor has hardened.

There is no consistent number of doorways. A shelter may grow one opening, then close it and make another on the opposite side. Very old chambers sometimes contain the beginning of an entrance inside a wall, a smooth tunnel that never reached the air.

The visible room is a small part of the animal’s life. It can remain open for decades. It can close in an afternoon. Once closed, it is gradually drawn down into the foundation, its roof settling without breaking, its walls passing one another in folds. The place where it stood becomes an oval of unusually clear water.

A new chamber may begin nearby.

It is not certain whether this is the same inhabitant.

***

They feed through surfaces that are never exposed.

Under the mud, the country is full of things which have almost ceased to be things: root fibers worn into translucent threads, skins without the bodies that shed them, grains of mineral carrying a trace of something that died before the nearest reed began to grow. Water brings this material against the pale flesh. A little remains. The rest passes onward.

A meal may take longer than a traveler’s life.

Yet the inhabitants can act quickly. If one of the six-legged animals steps onto a young foundation, its feet may sink between stones which were firmly nested a moment earlier. Usually it withdraws them. Sometimes it stands until the disturbance is over, its flank hollows open, while a pale edge rises around one foot.

Then it pulls with all six legs.

Such struggles leave very little evidence. A plate from the animal’s underside may remain fixed in the wall of a shelter, polished on one face and furred with copper crust on the other.

Human bones occur there too. Not all have been carried in by the mud.

Those who use the chambers learn to sleep with a hand against the doorway. A change in the opening wakes them sooner than a change in the room. They keep their knees drawn up and leave nothing wedged between themselves and a wall. They do not trust a chamber because it is large, or because someone spent a night there safely.

A room can hold a family through three seasons of rain and close upon a solitary visitor.

The inhabitants do not seem to be hunting. They do not close more often when occupied. There are chambers that have drawn inward around nothing but water, and empty foundations containing small, perfect rooms no larger than a fist.

Nevertheless, a sleeping body is something the walls must grow around, or through, or shut beneath them.

***

There are people who remain in the country.

They are few enough that a traveler may cross for months without meeting one, though signs of them are more common: a smooth stone placed in a doorway; three reeds loosely bound where their submerged roots do not overlap; a strip of dark skin stretched across a crack in the rock, trembling whenever the crack exhales.

They do not live in the chambers continuously. Each household keeps several, moving among them according to changes felt through the floor.

Before settling for a night, someone kneels outside and places both palms in the water. Another waits well away from the foundation. The one kneeling presses down, releases, presses again. Beneath the water, the stones make an answer too slight for a visitor to notice.

Occasionally the waiting person shakes their head.

Then everyone leaves.

Their belongings are carried in shallow, flexible bundles held high against the ribs. There are few hard objects: cutting edges, small wedges, vessels lined with the yellow tissue of reeds. A vessel must be kept wet. Once its lining dries, even briefly, it begins to admit water from the outside.

They eat the black reeds very sparingly. Cutting a stem can spoil a feeding ground larger than any field they could tend. More often they slit it near the base and collect what wells out, a bitter fluid which thickens when warmed against the skin. The slit is bound shut before the reed opens at night.

They also gather the copper crust when it loosens. Some pieces are eaten at once. Others are kept folded in wet cloth until they lose their metallic sheen. No visitor is offered these without first being watched through a full afternoon. What nourishes one person can leave another unable to stand.

The people have words spoken aloud, but indoors they use them quietly. Most conversation takes place through contact.

Two people sit with their wrists together. A thumb presses below the other’s palm; fingers shift along the tendons. This can continue for an hour with scarcely a visible movement. Children interrupt by putting their hands between the adults’ hands.

Under the great roar, they communicate through shoulders, knees, the soles of the feet.

A stranger tends to grip too hard. The inhabitants loosen the stranger’s fingers one at a time and begin again.

They are not without tenderness. They are simply accustomed to tenderness having to be felt.

***

Children are taught early that the person visible across a stretch of water may not be reachable by walking toward them.

An adult waits on one bank while a child crosses to another. The child must look away, put a foot into the current, and feel where the ground takes the weight. If the adult’s voice comes from beneath the water, the child stops. If the bank draws suddenly near, the child does not leap.

The lessons are repeated even after they seem to have been learned.

There are places where the children are allowed to play. These are not the quiet places, but places where the disturbance is familiar. They float pieces of reed rind through intersecting channels. They stamp on thin water skins to make small, satisfying reports. They place their cheeks against warm stone and try to identify who is approaching.

For a little while after someone has crossed, the stone holds a difference in warmth.

A child may announce the arrival of a parent who is still sitting beside them. The parent asks where the warmth is strongest and takes another route when they leave.

Other predictions are ignored. There is only so much a household can alter in a day.

People occasionally meet themselves.

These meetings are seldom face to face. A person sees a familiar bent shoulder retreating through spray, or recognizes their own way of holding a bundle. They do not ordinarily call out. If the figure turns, they lower their eyes and wait for the ground beneath them to settle.

They speak afterward about what the figure carried, whether it was limping, whether it was alone.

They disagree over what should be done with this knowledge. One woman stopped visiting a certain chamber after seeing herself crawl from it with blood running down her neck. Years later, she still slept elsewhere. Another man followed his own footprints to a foundation he had never found before and established his household there.

There is no settled wisdom about either choice.

***

The people know the chamber inhabitants more closely than travelers do, though not completely.

They recognize differences in the walls: one shelter hardens smoothly; another lays down coarse ridges; another includes so much black root fiber that its interior remains dark even when the clouds open. They can sometimes identify a new chamber as belonging to an old foundation after every visible part of the old room has vanished.

At certain times they clear sediment from the foundation’s outer stones. They work with their fingers, never levering the slabs apart. Pale flesh swells into the freshly washed gaps.

They leave a little of the gathered copper crust there.

Not every household does this. Some say it makes the rooms safer. Others say a well-fed foundation grows too quickly.

In the oldest shelters, shallow depressions sometimes form along the inside wall, just above the floor. They contain clear fluid, warmer than the water outside. For a season or two they remain empty.

Then something appears in each.

It is small, folded, and nearly transparent. Its surface bears fine lines that continue into the wall around it. When the plain shudders, it draws tight; in the intervals it opens a little, showing several blunt lobes around a central hollow.

The people avoid these chambers.

They do not remove their belongings all at once. They take the cutting edges first, then the vessels, then the bundles of food. The last person to leave wipes the inside of the doorway with both wet hands.

For a while the room remains large enough to enter.

Then the floor rises.

***

What comes out is rarely witnessed.

A chamber closes during the long afternoon. By morning its roof has subsided, and its foundation lies loose under the water. There may be a broad track leading away: stones turned flat side upward, sediment pressed into overlapping crescents.

The track might extend twenty paces.

It might begin again several miles away.

Once, after a front had passed, a traveler found one of the young inhabitants on exposed rock. It was the size of a sleeping dog and much flatter. Small stones were held beneath it in separate pockets of flesh. Along its back stood a soft, unfinished wall, interrupted by two openings.

It moved by adjusting the stones.

The traveler watched it approach a shallow pool. At the edge it paused. The wall inclined; water entered one opening and ran from the other. For several minutes the animal remained there, filling and emptying its little room.

Then the rock began to tremble.

It spread itself thin. Its stones shifted outward, and its wall folded down until nothing stood above its back. The concussion passed through the pool, lifting a rim of water around it.

When the water settled, the young animal resumed its crossing.

There was no haste in it. It had somewhere to establish itself, and a small quantity of the country already held beneath its body.
