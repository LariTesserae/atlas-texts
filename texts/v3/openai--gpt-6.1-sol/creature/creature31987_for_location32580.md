The first person you notice is not swimming.

She is standing beside a doorway with one foot hooked under a root, holding a bowl against her stomach. Something small moves inside it. Every so often she takes out a snail, examines its underside, and puts it on the wall. The ones she does not want she drops into a basket between her feet.

Her hair is braided close to her scalp. A loose strand rises beside her cheek. She has broad shoulders, a soft, heavy body, and hands that look almost delicate until she presses a snail into a crevice: then you see how wide her fingers spread, how securely they hold against wet wood.

When she needs to breathe, she sets the bowl in the fork of a branch and releases her foot. She rises without haste, catching a window brace on the way. Her legs make two slow movements. Then her head disappears through the silver underside of an air pocket.

The bowl stays where she left it. The snails continue trying to get out.

These people do not breathe water. Their lives are arranged around that fact, but they seldom appear to be thinking about it.

A visitor spends much of the first day thinking about nothing else.

How far is the next room? Is there air beneath that roof? Which opening leads upward, and which leads into another flooded chamber? A visitor looks at a street and sees the distance to be crossed. A resident sees three places to rest, a handhold beneath a sill, and a ceiling that can be reached without entering the house.

Children learn these things before they learn the names of all their neighbors.

They are thick-bodied people, most of them, with short lower legs and long, flexible feet. Their toes can hold a cord while their hands are occupied. The skin of their palms and soles is pale and finely creased; their elbows, knees, and knuckles are rougher. In winter, their faces acquire a reddish darkness that remains well into spring.

They differ from one another as people do. Some are slight and quick, some broad enough to have difficulty in the older doorways. One elderly man is so buoyant that he wears his working stones even to cross the square. A woman who repairs ventilation stems sinks readily and owns only one small weight, which she uses to anchor her basket.

There is no honor in needing fewer stones. Stones are fitted to the work.

The good ones are smooth, drilled or grooved, and easy to release. They hang at the hips from a belt with a knot that can be pulled open with either hand. Children practice undoing these knots long before they are allowed to wear a full working weight.

No one ties a stone permanently to a person.

Their clothes are close-fitting where cloth would otherwise catch, loose where a fold can hold a tool. Most garments are made from softened plant fiber, worked and reworked until they no longer rasp the skin. A narrow apron with several loops is enough for many kinds of labor. In the air rooms, people put on heavier wraps stored above the waterline. These are among the few possessions that are never casually lent.

Hair is braided, bound, or cropped. Beards are uncommon among those who work in roots, not because anyone dislikes them but because fine growth tangles in them. Jewelry is small and usually useful: a polished bone pin, a ring that holds a knife cord, a bead marking which end of a complicated fastening should be pulled.

Their movements are economical. They do not constantly swim as fish do. They walk, pull, brace, drift, and push off. In the streets, someone traveling in the opposite direction may offer a forearm, giving both people something to turn against. It is an ordinary courtesy, scarcely acknowledged.

An unexpected grip is another matter. You do not seize someone from behind underwater, even in play. You do not pull on a belt unless you mean to release its weights. You do not stand beneath a person rising to breathe.

Most of these rules are taught without speech.

The town has a large vocabulary of gestures, although much of it would look unremarkable from shore. Two fingers against the wrist ask how much breath remains. A flat hand moved downward means wait where you are. Fingers opening beside the mouth mean that an air room is bad, or becoming bad. The precise urgency is in the speed.

There are local gestures, family gestures, and impatient gestures that everyone understands.

They also knock. A tool handle against a door brace can bring someone down from an air room. Three widely spaced knocks call for a second pair of hands. A rapid series, repeated, causes people to stop what they are doing and look for the source.

Nobody uses that call merely because supper is ready.

Underwater, conversation has to wait. Anger often has to wait as well.

You can see an argument forming while two people work: the repeated rejection of a offered cord, an exaggerated inspection of a cut, someone turning away so deliberately that the movement carries them into a wall. Later, above the silver ceiling, they will say what they meant. Sometimes by then one has decided it was not worth saying. Sometimes the waiting has made it worse.

They are not uniformly patient people. Their town requires patience, which is different.

There are people who prune too much, people who put off checking the tubes, people who borrow tools and return them with their cords tangled. There is a household whose doorway is perpetually encumbered by baskets. Its neighbors complain every season. The baskets are moved, and gradually return.

Open water is common property, but everyone’s idea of a reasonable obstruction favors their own belongings.

The person who keeps the central square clear is consequently not always liked. At present it is a narrow-faced woman with one clouded eye and an excellent memory. She knows which root was guided by whom, and which apparently stray shoot has a purpose. She can remove a year’s careless growth in a morning without touching the small stems that someone is training for a roof.

She is less willing to spare a decorative plant.

People say she has no appreciation of beauty. They say this while floating in the spacious square she maintains.

Children are the least restrained inhabitants.

At first they stay in the upper houses, where the nearest air is only a short pull away. Infants spend much of their time in woven cradles suspended above the water. A parent sitting beside one can lower a foot through the floor opening and feel who is arriving before they come up.

A very young child is never carried underwater by someone wearing work weights. The journey between rooms is brief, practiced, and made with a hand supporting the child’s head. There are songs for the moments before a crossing and songs for the first breath afterward. Older children find these songs embarrassing, until they have a younger sibling to sing them to.

Later they learn to remain still underwater. This is harder than learning to propel themselves. They want to chase the fish, overturn the baskets, and investigate holes in walls. Adults teach them to hook a foot beneath a root and wait until the urge to rise is clear but not urgent.

No lesson is a contest.

Contests occur anyway. They take place out of sight, are discovered, and lead to angry conversations in air rooms.

The southern rail has particular rules. Children may approach it with an adult. They may lean over it while held at the waist. They may watch a pale fish move below them until its outline disappears.

They may not drop things over to see how long they remain visible.

Several households can identify the exact depth at which a favorite object was lost. One old woman still describes a little carved animal descending with its legs upward, though it happened when she was eight.

Food grows in and around the town, but not everything living there is food.

Certain snails are encouraged on walls because they remove the growth that would otherwise seal small channels. Others eat the tender bark and are collected. The woman at the doorway knows the difference by touch as well as sight. Her basket will be taken to shore, where its contents are cooked over a sheltered fire.

The larger fish receive scraps, but some are trapped at particular times of year. A fish that waits beneath a cutting block is not necessarily safe; nor is it necessarily destined to be eaten. Individual households have their own arrangements. One workshop has fed the same scarred fish for so long that visitors are warned about it before they are warned about the saw.

They eat soft stems, seeds, roots, eggs, and foods brought from lower country. Cooking, drying, and smoke-curing happen at the shore landings. The people who tend those fires return smelling unlike everyone else. Children put their faces against a parent’s wrap to breathe the smoke and warm bark.

Waste goes out of the town in covered vessels. This is unglamorous work, carried out with care, and a household that neglects it hears about it quickly. The lake is large, but the people know exactly how close their own mouths are to what they release.

Illness changes a house.

Tools are moved aside. The air tube is checked more often. A sleeping platform is brought nearer the opening so that someone below can pass up a cup or put a hand on the sick person’s ankle. People who cannot work submerged still split dry fiber, sort seeds, mend garments, teach children, and keep account of what is being grown.

An old person may know a roof’s history better than its present owner. Before a large cut is made, someone goes up to ask.

The answer can take a long time. It can include the name of a dead uncle and a disagreement about a winter no one else remembers. The worker below waits, holding the blade away from the bark.

There are deaths in the water. There are failures of judgment, broken stems, sudden illnesses, and the rare morning when a ventilation tube has closed without anyone noticing. The town does not treat these as proof that the lake is hostile. Nor does it forget them.

After a drowning, handholds are inspected along the route. Sometimes a new brace is fitted. Sometimes nothing can be changed, and that is harder.

Their dead are carried above water. Beyond one of the landing places, among roots that grow in air, are the graves. The living go there on errands as well as visits. Someone gathering seeds may stop to clear fallen leaves from a name cut into wood. Children are told whose hands first bent the roof they sleep beneath.

At night the town becomes more audible to its inhabitants.

People sit shoulder to shoulder in the air chambers, knees still in the water. They eat from small bowls, tease one another, and discuss work that will take another six years to finish. A person entering from below brings a little cold into the room and is usually handed a wrap before being asked for news.

They have stories about the valleys. Roads so dry that dust rises under every foot. Houses whose walls do not change unless something damages them. Rooms in which a person can walk all the way from one end to the other while breathing.

Some have seen these things. Some would like to. Some have gone away and not returned.

Those who do return sometimes need several days to become easy in the water again. Their first descents are awkward. They reach for a handhold that has thickened or been moved. They rise sooner than they intended.

Nobody remarks on this unless they are unkind.

Someone makes space beside them in an air room. Someone tells them which roof is being trained inward, who has had a child, and why the square keeper has forbidden baskets beneath the northern arch.

The following morning they are given a small task close to air.

Below them, a familiar doorway has grown a little narrower. They put a hand against its pale edge, brace a foot, and begin.
