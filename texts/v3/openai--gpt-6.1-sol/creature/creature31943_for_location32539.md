The eel lives beneath a flange on an unused return pipe.

The pipe is wide enough for a person to crawl through, but a plate has been bolted across its mouth. Beneath the flange, where the pipe meets its concrete footing, there is a gap left by an uneven pour. Two fingers could enter it. Farther in, the gap widens into a low chamber containing a little sediment, three empty shells, and the eel.

Usually only its head is visible.

It is grey, with a paler lower jaw and eyes that look blue when the light reaches them. The mouth does not quite close. A narrow space remains between the jaws, enough to show the tips of teeth. Water passes through the mouth and out behind the head in small, regular movements. Seen from a distance, these are the only indication that the face under the pipe is alive.

Its body is considerably larger than the entrance suggests. Inside the chamber it lies doubled back on itself, muscular and unhurried, with the tail extending into a narrower passage along the footing. There are places on its skin where pigment has faded around old injuries. One flank bears a healed crescent, perhaps from a seal. Behind the head is a patch that repeatedly rubs against the same projection of concrete.

The chamber is not an ideal refuge. Its ceiling is abrasive. The current sometimes packs it with loose weed. When the nearest pumping station starts its secondary impeller, grit trembles down from somewhere above.

But it has two exits, and neither admits a seal.

***

The eel does not experience the basin as a plain.

A plain is what it becomes when looked at from above, in daylight, by something that travels through open water. For the eel, the bottom is a succession of edges, gradients, and passages. A seam holds the smell of a crab after the crab has left. A shallow drainage groove provides cover, though not enough to hide the whole body. The downstream side of a lifting socket contains a small slackness in which it can pause without working against the current.

It knows a route from the pipe to the pumping station that passes through almost no open ground.

First the footing. Then a channel partly filled with shell fragments. Then the edge of a raised panel, followed until it meets a transverse joint. At the joint it crosses six metres of exposed concrete to a row of anchor blocks.

There is no weed along most of this route. The eel follows it in darkness, with its head close to the floor.

Some nights it leaves the route. A fish has died somewhere, or a damaged crab is sending out a trace that comes and goes as the pumps alter the flow. The eel raises its head. It moves sideways across the current, loses the trace, finds it again. What looks like aimless searching is a series of corrections made in water that will not hold still.

A meal may be waiting beneath a grille. It may already be inside the station, beyond a screen the eel cannot pass.

There are nights when it spends hours approaching food it never reaches.

***

The pump house is the richest part of its range.

Crabs feed among the mussels on the grilles. Small fish gather in the disturbed water. Creatures injured against the screens sink into the hollows at the base of the building, and the eel has learned which hollows retain them.

It has also learned something about the machinery.

A particular change in the station’s beating is followed by an easing of the current along one wall. During that interval it can enter a recess that is difficult to approach at full flow. The interval does not happen every night. Sometimes the change in sound comes without the expected slack water, and the eel is pushed back, its body streaming away from the wall while its head remains turned toward the recess.

It retreats then. It waits behind a buttress.

The sound itself is too continuous to mean danger. Much of its life has occurred inside that sound. Danger is more often a disturbance within it: a new vibration through the floor, a shadow moving against the light, the abrupt flight of fish.

A seal passing overhead makes the eel flatten close to the concrete. There is little concealment in this gesture. Its outline remains visible. But motion stops, and sometimes the seal continues toward the station, where prey is more abundant and easier to flush from cover.

If the seal turns, the eel runs for an edge.

Its body, so languid under the pipe, becomes a powerful travelling curve. It can disappear into a joint that seems much too small for it. For a few seconds the tail remains outside, lashing hard enough to lift sediment from the seam.

***

No one placed the eel in this chamber.

It entered the basin from somewhere else, through an open gate or in the long movement of water between compartments. Its arrival was not recorded. The first survey that photographed the pipe showed only a dark gap beneath the flange. In a later image, two pale eyes caught the survey lights.

The images sit in a maintenance archive under the number of the pipe.

Occasionally a diver sees it. Most pass without noticing. One stops, lowers a lamp, and finds the head facing outward. The eel withdraws as the beam moves closer. The diver sees the size of the body for an instant, then only suspended grit.

The animal is familiar with lamps in the limited sense that it has survived them before. It does not come out to investigate. It stays behind the turn in the chamber until the pressure of the diver’s movements and the intermittent rush of bubbles have gone.

Afterward it may wait much longer.

***

In winter it travels less.

Cold water advances along the floor and settles in the channels. The eel remains in the pipe chamber through several cycles of darkness and daylight, its breathing slow, its body pressed into the sheltered space. A shrimp walks across the sediment near its jaw. The eel does not move.

Later, when it feeds, it takes what is close.

In spring there are more small fish at the station. Some enter the old return pipe through openings farther along its length. The eel encounters them inside, where the water is dark even at noon. There is little room for pursuit. It waits near a restriction in the pipe, and the fish pass close enough to touch.

Its life is not confined to the chamber, but much of it consists of returning there: after feeding, after a seal’s approach, after a storm has made the familiar route difficult to hold.

Each return carries material inward. Shell fragments shift beneath its belly. Fine sediment is swept toward the rear passage. Over time, the place where its body rests has become smoother than the surrounding concrete.

***

When the leaking panel farther out in the basin begins to lift, the eel visits it.

The plume brings shrimp and exposes animals that had been working in the thin sediment. The eel approaches from downstream and settles against the edge of the hollow. It feeds there on three successive nights.

On the fourth, a temporary barrier surrounds the joint.

The eel follows the smell to the barrier and moves along it, looking for the opening that was there before. Its head presses into the lower edge of the fabric. The fabric gives, then tightens against its supports. Beyond it, grout hoses lie across the floor.

For a while the eel continues searching.

Then it leaves for the pump house.

Weeks later, the steel strip over the repair is part of its route. A crab shelters beside one of the bolt heads. Silt has begun to collect along the downstream edge. The eel pauses there with its chin almost touching the floor.

It is thinner than the pipe, older than some of the machinery, and easily missed in the immense volume of water. Yet around its chamber there is already a small history: worn concrete, displaced shells, fish that have learned not to enter the gap.

At dawn it returns beneath the flange. The head turns outward. The long body settles into its accustomed bends.

Above it, the water brightens.
