The ones who live longest are easy to mistake for part of the floor.

They lie in the shallow hollows between terraces, their backs covered in the same transparent film that grows over the wood. Silt settles along their sides. Small grazers cross them without changing direction. Only when one lifts does the distinction become visible: a broad body peeling slowly from its resting place, a pale underside, the dark space left beneath.

They have no single outline that would serve to identify them at every age. The young are narrow and almost fish-shaped, with six pairs of fins and a clear sheath through which their organs shine. As they grow, the fins broaden and divide. Their bodies flatten. The sheath becomes thick, faintly ridged, opaque except around the mouth. In the oldest, the rear pairs of fins have become long, supple limbs that can reach around a buttress and hold it.

An adult is large enough to cover one of the sheltered courts. Most are smaller than that. Their growth depends partly on where they settle, and partly on how often they leave.

They do leave. Sometimes a hollow that has held the same animal for longer than several generations of fish is empty, and its occupant is moving through a distant arch with its fins folded close, carrying a fringe of displaced silt.

Underneath, they are unexpectedly delicate.

The belly is crossed by hundreds of soft ridges, each capable of lifting independently. Between these ridges are short, branching organs that rest against the wood. They taste the floor, but taste is too limited a description. Through them an animal can distinguish new growth from old, a strained joint from a thickening one, a corridor that carries more water than it did before. It can feel a grazer approaching along a terrace that its eyes cannot see.

Its eyes are small and numerous. They form two uneven rows along the front edge of the body. Some look forward; others look down. Their light is a low amber, almost lost in the green and blue lights of the other animals.

The mouth lies between those rows.

It is a narrow opening, able to extend into a soft tube. When the animal feeds, the tube enters the living nap and withdraws with a clot of filaments and tiny translucent bodies. There is no tearing. The food separates along a plane too fine to see.

A feeding adult advances a little, stops, advances again. Behind it, the floor is patterned with pale circles. Each circle darkens from the edge inward as the crop returns.

Other animals follow these paths. Certain small fish will wait beside a resting adult for days, then accompany it when it begins to feed. They eat what rises from the disturbed nap. The adult makes no effort to catch them. At times it lifts a fin so that the shoal can pass underneath.

There are preferences that persist for centuries: a particular hollow, the inner face of an arch, a ledge where two currents touch. An animal may make a circuit across half the basin and return to put its mouth against precisely the same place.

There the wood is often altered.

A shallow groove extends beneath its jaw. A ridge has grown to support the underside of its head. The resting hollow fits the lifted portions of its belly as well as the portions that touch the floor. When the animal shifts, the fit slowly changes with it.

This accommodation is not immediate. A restless young one lies badly in many places. It braces itself with its fins and slides when the current strengthens. Only after repeated returns does a place begin to hold it well.

The old ones fit so closely that water scarcely moves beneath them.

Yet they never become fixed. Even the largest can lift all its weight from the floor. Beneath an abandoned resting place there is no attachment scar, only a smooth depression and, sometimes, a growth of unusually bright buds.

They hear through their whole undersides.

The ceaseless concussion of the overflow passes through them, carrying the lesser sounds of the basin. A shoal rounding a ridge makes a change in that sound. A grazing company distributes its scraping among several pitches. The settling of a large jaw against an arch is distinct at a great distance.

The long-lived animals contribute very little noise of their own. Their calls are made by drawing a small region of the belly away from the floor and pressing it down again. The resulting note enters the wood directly.

Usually it is answered.

The reply may come so much later that a swimmer would not connect the two. It may be repeated by a third animal, farther away, with one tone changed. Through the terraces these exchanges continue beneath all the more immediate noises of feeding and movement.

Not every animal joins them. Some remain silent for years.

Others seem to listen chiefly to a single distant companion. They keep their bodies turned toward it even when the route between them bends through several courts. If one moves, the other eventually moves also, though seldom nearer. Their relation can occupy a large part of the basin without requiring them to meet.

They are capable of meeting.

In a broad court, two will approach underside to underside, lifting themselves upright in the water. Their fins spread around them. The exposed bellies shine faintly where the sensing ridges open, and for a little while the court contains two pale, intricate surfaces facing one another.

They touch at many points.

Small creatures sheltering in their folds emerge and hang in the water. Fish pass through the loose enclosure of their limbs. The animals remain together while a cold current empties the court and another fills it.

Then they separate.

After such meetings, one may carry young.

At first the young occupy individual pockets beneath the outer fins. Their light shows through as a row of dull beads. They are fed from the parent’s body, but each has its own tiny mouth and makes feeding motions against the pocket wall.

As they lengthen, their tails protrude. They taste the passing water. Eventually they leave the pockets and cling to the parent’s back, where the algae give them something to hold.

A parent carrying young travels more often than usual. It visits narrow passages, rises beneath ledges, rests at the edge of courts it ordinarily crosses without stopping. The young detach to investigate and return by following the vibration of its call.

Not all return.

Some find a hollow in which the current presses them gently against the floor. Some join a procession of silver fish and are carried far away. A few reach the outer bank.

There, the long-lived ones are smaller and more heavily folded. They keep their limbs tucked close and anchor themselves between the overlapping straps of vegetation. Their feeding surfaces are narrow. Their young have stronger tails.

Sometimes one climbs onto the crest.

It lies for a while with water sliding over its back. The sensing organs underneath reach into seams between the straps. Its eyes show dimly through the sheet of overflow.

Then it releases its hold.

It goes over folded lengthwise, no broader than a large fish, and its lights descend with the little lights around it.

Others remain at the lip. Their calls can be felt beneath the flow: short, close-spaced notes quite unlike the slow exchanges of the deep terraces. There are worn depressions on both sides of the crest, including the outer side, where nothing resting can be seen.

In the interior, death comes quietly.

A very old animal may begin to miss the returning shoals. Its feeding circuit shortens. One after another, the ridges beneath its belly cease to lift. For a long time the small followers continue to wait beside it.

When its mouth no longer opens, they find another.

The body remains in its hollow. The floor’s fine filaments grow across the pale underside and enter the spaces between the sensing organs. Its skin loosens, allowing the inhabitants of its folds to spread inward. Light appears briefly in places that were dark while it lived.

Nothing large comes to consume it.

The body subsides over many years. Wood rises into the space it leaves. For a while the court has a low mound with a darker patch on top, then only a slight alteration in the grain.

Nearby animals still turn toward it when they call.

Eventually one of the young comes to rest there. It does not fit. Its belly is too narrow, its fins too short to reach the supporting ridges. It spends much of its time away, feeding in open water or wedging itself badly beneath a ledge.

But it returns.

Under its weight, scarcely enough to matter against the pressure of the sea, the hollow begins to change.
