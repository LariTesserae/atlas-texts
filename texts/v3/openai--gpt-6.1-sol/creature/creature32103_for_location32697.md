The people who live here can tell who is coming before the person reaches the door. A quick foot on the public steps belongs to the woman who keeps the storehouse keys; she has a way of taking the last two steps together, even with a basket on her hip. A dragging sound followed by two ordinary footfalls is the old mason’s left sandal. Children are harder to distinguish. They change their pace, run where they usually walk, and sometimes come down a lane backward, pulling something.

There are fewer inhabitants than the houses suggest. Several households use two rooms that once belonged to separate families. One house holds sacks, spare roof slabs, and a loom too large to carry through any other doorway. Another is opened only when a daughter returns with her children from the lower country. Her mother airs the bedding beforehand and leaves the shutters unlatched for three days. While the house is empty, she still clears its drain.

Most of the people have relatives elsewhere. The town does not provide everything they need. Grain comes up the ridge in panniers, along with oil, salt, and lengths of timber that are discussed long before they arrive. What goes down is less bulky: woven cloth, repaired tools, a little cheese, sometimes a pair of hands hired for the harvest. There are people here who spend several weeks each year sleeping in fields below the ridge, and return with dirt in their clothing that is darker than any soil at home.

They talk about the lower country as somewhere particular, full of names and obligations. The blue hills visible between the houses contain a cousin who owes money, a mill with an unreliable wheel, a woman who makes excellent pots and charges accordingly.

The woman with the storehouse keys is not an important person in every respect. She cannot mend a roof, and when animals are brought through the town she keeps well away from their hind legs. But she remembers quantities accurately. She knows which sacks came from which field, which household has already drawn its share, and how much should remain if everyone’s account is correct.

She sometimes opens the storehouse alone and stands without moving until she hears the mice.

There is a pale cat with a ragged ear that belongs, provisionally, to this building. It sleeps elsewhere and accepts food at several doors, but the storehouse woman calls it hers when it catches something. When it tears a sack, she calls it nobody’s. Its path across the roofs is more direct than any route available to the people below. In winter it becomes heavy and reluctant to move; in spring it appears lean again, with dust along its spine.

The mice live inside arrangements the humans cannot see. A narrow space behind a sill leads to a gap between two wall faces, and that gap leads somewhere warm. They carry away single grains, husks, bits of thread. Generations of them have inhabited the same few lengths of wall without understanding that the sacks are periodically counted.

Water brings the people into the lower court at different hours. Some prefer to draw before anyone else is awake. Others wait until there is company.

A man with broad, stiff fingers always sets his bucket down before hauling. He broke his wrist years ago and cannot comfortably hold one weight while lifting another. A girl of eleven uses both hands on the windlass and leans her whole body into the turn. She is allowed to bring water home now, but not to lift the cistern cover. This distinction seems unreasonable to her. She tests the cover when no one is looking, finds it still too heavy, and is relieved as well as offended.

Everyone learns what must be kept away from the cistern mouth. A child dropping a crust into it is scolded more sharply than one breaking a cup. The settling basin is cleaned by people who would rather be doing something else. They argue over whose turn it is, lift the cover together, and scrape out the grit. Afterward, one of them washes the tools with water the other considers unnecessary.

They are not uniformly sparing. A woman has a blue glazed bowl she uses only for kneading, although a plain one would serve. One man burns more fuel than his neighbors think proper because he likes to sit awake late. Two sisters keep a small scrap of red cloth wrapped around a comb that belonged to their mother. Neither intends to wear the cloth or give it up.

The old mason lives close to the upper square. His room contains a bed, a stool, and more stone than would seem useful indoors: pieces with a good edge, pieces that show how a crack runs, one block he has been intending to split for several years. He no longer lifts the larger slabs. He goes where work is being done and puts his hand on them instead.

He can become tiresome over the position of a doorstep. Once, he insisted that a new threshold be lowered by less than the thickness of a finger. The household complained for a week. In the next heavy rain the water passed their door without entering, and afterward they repeated the story as though they had supported him from the start.

He has forgotten some names. He has not forgotten where water goes.

The children know other things. They know which wall can be climbed without a loose stone betraying them, which roof gives access to the pear tree, and which adult will look upward if a little grit falls into the lane. They know where lizards emerge after a cool morning. They catch them occasionally, holding their small bodies too firmly, then learn to open their fingers.

There is one child who spends a great deal of time examining the shells in the stone. He traces their curves with a wet fingertip to make them show more clearly. He has asked how they came to be there. Several people have answered, and the answers do not agree. He keeps a fragment with half a shell in it beneath his bedding. The broken face is sharp enough to catch the cloth.

The pear tree belongs to no household, though ownership is often implied by whoever has most recently carried soil to its roots. When the fruit ripens, there is a brief period of close observation. Someone claims a pear was already falling and would have been wasted. Someone else counts what remains on the lower branches. The smallest children receive soft fruit in halves, with the bruised portions cut away.

In good years there is enough for the quarrel to become a joke. In poor years it stays a quarrel.

People gather in the broad chamber because it can hold them, not because they always wish to be together. They mend things there in bad weather, settle payments, wait beside the sick, and eat when a household has reason to feed more people than its room will contain. Voices that sound restrained in the lanes grow louder indoors. There are chairs people habitually take and one place near the door left clear for a man whose knees will not bend enough to sit low.

Someone sings well. Someone always asks for the same song. Someone leaves when the singing starts.

At night, the town contains more wakefulness than its dark windows disclose. A nursing mother sits with her feet cold on the floor. The man who likes a late fire moves a piece of wood inward with his boot. A young woman lies listening for the roof cat and thinks about leaving after the harvest. She has said nothing yet. In the house next door, her father is calculating whether they can afford another length of sound timber before winter.

Toward morning, the small creatures become busy beneath the human quiet. Moths settle into the undersides of shutters. A spider takes down part of a web across a doorway. The cat pauses above the storehouse, one forepaw raised, hearing movement through the stone and wood.

Then a latch lifts. Someone steps outside with a basin. She glances at the strip of distant country between the houses, empties the water where it will reach the pear roots, and goes back for the next thing she needs.
