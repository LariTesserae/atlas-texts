Inside the second dead pump, behind the flange where a pipe has been removed, something keeps the sediment from settling.

The opening is not large. A fish could pass through it easily, but a hand would have to enter sideways. Its lower edge has a lip of rust, thick enough to hold a little drift of shell fragments. Elsewhere, that drift would remain until the working pump changed load. Here it is disturbed several times a night. Pieces are lifted over the lip and left outside: the split valve of a mussel, a crab’s joined legs, a white chip with one small round hole in it.

There is no visible occupant. The interior is darker than the space beneath the fallen panel. Occasionally, when the water clears, a pale strip shows well back inside. It could be an old gasket. It withdraws without stirring much silt.

The animal has other ways out.

A fracture runs around the back of the housing, where two cast sections have separated. There is also the bore through which the shaft once entered. That is the route it uses most often. It leaves by unfolding a little of itself into the gap, finding purchase, and drawing the rest through. What seemed a narrow, blunt piece of flesh lengthens along the metal. Another appears beneath it. The body follows only after several limbs are already outside.

It is an octopus, though in this room its outline is rarely complete enough to make that immediately clear.

Most of the time it is the same dull grey as the housing. Its skin carries small raised points, irregularly spaced, which resemble the deposits on the iron. Along one side it has a darker wash, almost black. This does not match the pump exactly, but neither do the different layers of corrosion. When it spreads against the casing, the dark area looks like another patch in the surface.

Its eyes are easier to find than its body. They remain smooth while everything around them becomes rough. A narrow pupil opens across each one. The animal can keep one eye beyond the edge of the housing while nearly all the rest of it stays sheltered.

It is not especially large. When moving freely, it could fit between the sides of an open control-panel door. Spread out, it reaches much farther. Its arms can occupy several separate recesses at once, giving the impression of more than one animal working along a wall.

One arm ends early. The stump is rounded and lighter than the skin around it. A small new tip has begun to grow there, with suckers too shallow to grip the rough concrete well. The other arms compensate without any obvious hesitation. On the floor, its movement is low and practical: reach, attach, draw forward. It seldom jets through the open water. There are too many hard things nearby, and too few places where a sudden movement would carry it beyond sight.

The surviving lamps make islands it sometimes has to cross. Beneath the yellow one, its skin warms to the colour of wet mortar. In the dark interval beyond, that colour disappears. For a moment only the pale rims of its suckers can be seen as it rolls an arm off the floor.

It hunts around the places where other animals have found shelter.

At the fallen panel, it does not immediately reach underneath. It settles beside one corner and waits. The shrimp resume moving. Their antennae extend past the edge; their legs work through the fine deposits beneath them. Then a thin arm enters from the opposite side, slowly enough that the panel does not shift.

A shrimp escapes. Several others withdraw deeper. The arm follows the underside of the metal, feeling along a surface the octopus cannot see.

When it leaves, the panel is still where it was.

Crabs are more difficult. They brace in holes, present their claws, or wedge themselves among the broken instruments. The octopus works at them with the ends of its arms. It can remain for a long time outside a circular gauge opening, with one eye turned toward the chamber and two arms engaged inside the wall. Sometimes it gives up. Sometimes a crab emerges attached to it, legs opening and closing as they are drawn beneath the web.

The remains collect outside the dead pump. There is enough current to scatter the smaller pieces, so the pile never becomes much of a pile. A few heavy claws persist. The animal occasionally moves those too, carrying them a short distance before letting go.

It has learned the working pump in ways that do not require seeing it.

At the ordinary low note, it can cross the slope and return without difficulty. It grips the floor where the concrete is least fouled and lets the ends of its arms trail where they will not be caught. It avoids the grille. Even when a piece of food comes to rest there, it approaches only far enough to test the water with an arm.

Before a load change, a different vibration passes through the metal.

The octopus feels it while resting inside its housing. Its suckers are attached to the inner wall; much of its body lies against the curved floor. Often it begins withdrawing an exposed arm before the chain has lifted. By the time sediment starts to peel from the chamber floor, nothing soft projects from the shaft opening.

Within the pump there are still thick ribs around the places where the bearings were seated. It gathers itself between these, holding at several points. Water moves through the housing, but the casting breaks the strongest pull. The animal’s skin presses against iron from which every moving part has long since been taken.

Afterward it waits.

The fish come out first. Silt descends around the piers. The valve gives its uneven knocks. Only when the loose chain has resumed its occasional contact with the pipe does an arm appear at the rear fracture.

The altered floor offers new things. A burrowing animal has been uncovered. A shell has rolled out of a recess. A crab’s usual route now crosses an exposed strip of concrete. The octopus moves through the chamber again, stopping often, putting the tips of its arms into hollows and under edges.

It also examines things it cannot eat.

The newer cable receives particular attention. The clamps are bright and smooth, unlike most of the room. A sucker adheres to one, releases, then adheres again. An arm follows the black loop downward to the sensor. The octopus pulls lightly at the cable, enough to lift the slack between two clamps. Nothing comes loose.

The red indicator flashes against the underside of its arm.

It remains there through several flashes. Then it moves on to the cracked conduit beside the pier, where a small worm can be drawn from the joint.

There is no dependable boundary between useful and useless objects here. Food retreats into steel; an empty cavity may contain a crab tomorrow. The octopus returns to places where it found something and to places where it did not. It probes the worn steps near the sealed doorway, follows the edges of plinths, and reaches into mounting holes. A broad bare wall offers little. A bolt with a loosened washer can occupy it for several minutes.

Inside the dead pump, its body is softer than any of these surroundings. At rest, the mantle rises and falls gently as water passes over its gills. The arms lie folded in unequal lengths. Some suckers hold; others turn outward, pale and idle.

Fish sometimes shelter just outside the opening. They face the current while the octopus watches them from within. A small one can remain there for hours, making corrections with its tail, close to an arm it has not noticed.

The octopus does not always strike. It may have fed. The fish may be too far out, or the water may be wrong for an attempt. Eventually the fish shifts to another shelter, and the eye withdraws behind the flange.

During the next load change, the shell fragments outside the opening begin to move. A claw rocks against the rusted lip. Fine black sediment lifts through the shaft bore and passes over the folded animal.

Deep in the casing, several suckers tighten. The rest of it stays where it is, breathing in the small space the old machine still protects.
