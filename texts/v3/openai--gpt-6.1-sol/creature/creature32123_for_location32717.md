The inhabitant is easiest to see when it reaches for the earthenware vessel.

Until then, what shows of it might be another low shelf: a broad, cream-colored back rising just above the water, with a darker seam where one shoulder rests against the wall. It does not conceal itself. There is simply little in its shape to draw the eye away from the stone. Water coats it, and the light on that water is the same light that lies on everything else.

Then an arm unfolds beneath the fitted slab.

The arm is longer than expected, slender near the wrist, with a heavy elbow. Its hand has four blunt fingers and a small opposing thumb. The fingers curl through the vessel’s remaining handle, lift it clear of the bottom, and hold it tilted while something fine and brown settles out. The movement is deliberate without being hesitant. It knows the distance between the pot and the underside of the slab exactly.

Its body is not wholly mineral, though much of it has acquired a mineral covering. There are plates along the spine and around the shoulders, pale at their outer edges and yellow where friction has worn them thin. Beneath them is a pliant, gray-brown skin. At the elbow, where the arm bends, this skin gathers into close folds. The palms remain bare. So do the soles of its feet and a narrow region around the mouth.

The head is low and wide, carried almost level with the shoulders. Two small eyes look out from beneath a projecting ridge. Their color is hard to distinguish in the shade; they catch little light. It has no external ears. When it attends to a sound, it rests one hand flat on the ledge beside it.

Seen moving, it is less ponderous than its back suggests. Most of its weight stays in the water. It draws itself through the narrow intervals with its hands, turns sideways beneath a shelf, and emerges into another basin without disturbing more than a few overlapping rings. Out of water it is awkward. The three leveled stones are necessary to it. It climbs them one at a time, bringing both feet onto each before reaching for the next.

The dry shelf is where it keeps its tools.

There are only two: a dark hammerstone, hollowed slightly by use, and a short piece of harder rock with one working edge. The tool marks on the stair were made by these, or by others much like them. It has also worked the shallow bowl in the ledge. Whenever the notch begins to close with fresh deposit, it scrapes it open again. This takes several sittings. Between them it leaves the tool in the bowl, where the water softens the newest crust.

It tends other passages too, though not all of them. A narrowing beneath the rear shelf must remain wide enough for its shoulder. The outlet between the fallen blocks must remain clear. Elsewhere it lets the spring build as it will. Some pools have grown shallower within its memory; others have joined where a thin dividing rim wore through. It has changed its sleeping place several times.

What it eats is difficult to see. At the back wall, it presses its mouth against the wet stone and remains there for a long while. Sometimes its fingers sweep a basin floor, gathering the faint sediment into the vessel. Once settled, this leaves a soft layer that it takes out with the side of one finger. There is very little of it. Meals are small and widely separated.

Its breath is nearly silent. A fold beneath each side of the jaw opens in the water and closes again. On the dry shelf, its chest moves slowly enough that a visitor may have to wait to be sure.

Visitors have sat beside it.

It allows this, provided their feet do not cover the opening beneath the slab. If someone does obstruct it, the inhabitant touches an ankle and moves it a few inches aside. Its hand is warm, rough across the knuckles, soft at the fingertips. It does not repeat the gesture once the passage is clear.

It is interested in hands. It watches them wash, mend, turn a cup, test the roughness of a ledge. A person sitting still may eventually feel those blunt fingers laid beside their own—not grasping them, merely resting on the same stone. It will stay that way until the person moves.

No one has heard it speak. It makes a low sound occasionally while lifting something heavy, and a faint click when grit catches between its teeth. It seems to understand the immediate things visitors say to it, especially when they point. More distant matters leave it watching the speaker’s face.

When alone, it spends much of its time beneath the fitted slab, with one arm extended into the adjoining pool. The palm lies upward. Small changes in the current pass over it: water displaced by a falling bead, by a moth touching the surface near the entrance, by the gradual filling of a basin farther in.

After a time it lifts that hand, turns the vessel, or moves to clear a little sediment from a seam.

Then it settles again. The shoulder fits against the wall. The pale back breaks the surface. Only the bare skin at the wrist remains plainly visible, flexing now and then beneath the water.
