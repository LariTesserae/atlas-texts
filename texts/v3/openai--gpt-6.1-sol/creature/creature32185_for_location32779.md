The first part of the inhabitant you might notice is a pale fold beneath a valve.

It resembles packing material pushed out of a joint: a short lip of something soft, squeezed between the housing and the flange. Rust has stained its edge. A few grains of sand adhere to it. When the valve opens, the fold draws inward. When the valve closes, it returns, though not quite to its former shape.

There are similar folds elsewhere. One rests behind a pressure gauge. Another fills the space where a stair tread has pulled away from its supporting wall. In the pumping house, a length of pale material lies along the underside of the piston rod, thin enough that the rod can still enter its sleeve.

These are not separate animals.

Following one of them would be difficult. The exposed tissue narrows as it enters a seam, becoming nearly transparent. Inside the wall it divides around a bolt, rejoins beneath a plate, then spreads into a membrane across the mouth of an abandoned pipe. Beyond that membrane is a thicker portion, folded into the pipe’s dark bore. Beyond that, more divisions.

Much of its body occupies spaces that were never intended to contain anything: the clearance around a shaft, the hollow back of an enamel sign, the gap between an old brick wall and the metal cladding fastened over it. It has entered the district as water enters, but it cannot pass through every opening water can. Here and there, an old branch ends in a swollen plug against a crack too narrow to admit it.

The plugs remain alive. They press against the stone for years.

Its colour varies with the substance around it. On glass it is clear, except for a fine scattering of white points. Against iron it becomes cream-grey and opaque. Where it touches the surviving grease, its surface looks yellowish and waxy, and may in fact contain some of the grease. It incorporates materials slowly. Particles of rust settle into it and cease moving. Fragments of gasket become enclosed. A broken brass washer in a service corridor is visible beneath its skin, held there as securely as something mounted in resin.

There is no central mass large enough to call a torso. The thickest parts lie in the buried chambers, where broad sheets of tissue cover the walls without obstructing the passages between them. Each sheet is divided into shallow pockets. Water enters these pockets through fine openings and leaves them through others. As a chamber fills, the pockets swell in sequence, a slow movement passing across the wall.

The creature contracts very little by itself. It lets the machinery pull on it.

A strand around a descending counterweight becomes taut. That tension draws open a pouch beneath a distant floor. The pouch empties through a narrow living tube, sending a minute pulse into a portion of the body near the outer grates. Several minutes later, a membrane there stiffens. The current presses it against the bars, and the small crustaceans standing on those bars lift their legs.

In this way, movement in one building can be felt in another.

It has no eyes at the windows. Its sensitive surfaces are mostly hidden: a ring of tissue inside a gauge connection, a patch stretched across the underside of a drain cover, delicate branching tips held just clear of a turning shaft. These distinguish pressure from pressure with extraordinary precision. A grain passing through a bearing produces a disturbance it can detect. So does a fish moving along the open corridor, though the fish is gone long before the disturbance has travelled through the whole body.

The inhabitant’s response is usually small. It tightens somewhere. It lets go somewhere else.

When a crustacean dies on a grate, a fold advances beneath it. Nothing seizes the body. Over several days the fold broadens until the shell rests in a shallow depression. Clear fluid collects there, thicker than the surrounding water. Eventually the shell is empty. Eventually it falls away.

Most of the animal’s nourishment comes from less visible life. The films on the sheltered metal continue inside it, lining its pockets and tubes. They flourish where the water passing through the district brings something useful, or where a working surface supplies a little warmth. The inhabitant keeps these inner films in circulation. It carries waste toward the settling boxes, gathers what it can from the discharge mouths, and closes passages in which growth has become too dense.

It is not keeping the district in repair. It cannot replace a gear or free a seized pump. Nevertheless, parts of the waterworks have survived because of it.

One bearing has a soft collar that excludes grit. A cracked pipe is held inside a muscular sleeve. Beneath the crane, living tissue cushions a counterweight whenever it reaches the bottom of its travel. The cushioning is uneven; the hook still rises and falls. But the impact no longer loosens the bolts.

Other accommodations have damaged the machinery. Tissue has filled a small bypass and turned its valve into an immovable lump. A pressure gauge has been isolated from its pipe for so long that its steady needle records nothing outside the glass. The animal maintains the pressure behind it. The needle’s trembling is one of the movements that pass through its body.

It has preferences of this kind.

There is a narrow channel under the lowest street through which it repeatedly sends a pulse of water, although the channel leads only into a blocked cellar. There is a particular stair rail around which it has thickened, leaving the neighbouring rails untouched. At every reversal of the current, it empties and refills the casing of a dead inspection lamp.

The reasons are contained in its growth. A portion of the body remembers a route by continuing to use it. If the route changes, new tissue forms around the change, but the old portion may persist, attempting its former movement. Under some floors, two such arrangements pull against one another. The resulting motion is faint and irregular, like a muscle twitching in sleep.

It does sleep, though never all at once.

A branch can cease responding for months. Its membranes become slack; its pockets remain open. Water passes through it without being measured or redirected. Elsewhere, the animal grows more attentive. The active portions take up loads formerly carried by the sleeping ones. A pale fold at a valve becomes thicker. A tube inside a wall acquires a second passage.

Sometimes a dormant branch does not wake. Its tissue turns chalky, then breaks into soft flakes. These travel through the machinery until they reach the settling boxes. There are layers of them beneath the rust and grit: thin, curved pieces preserving the shapes of joints, screw threads, the interior of a socket. The animal has shed portions of the district from itself many times.

Its youngest tissue grows against the windows.

Here it is too thin to see directly. It spreads between the mineral film and the glass, finding what little nourishment the film offers. In the brief green light, its minute fibres change their alignment. A pane that appeared opaque becomes reflective. An interior appears beyond it.

The creature responds to these interiors.

When the second piston reaches the end of its shorter stroke, tissue beside the real piston tightens. In the window reflecting the long workshop aisle, several fine strands draw straight, as though a passage through the body has opened. The stairwell reflection produces a slower response. Something beneath the stairs rises, meets the ceiling, and remains pressed there until the lamp fades.

Whether it sees these rooms is difficult to say. There are no organs facing them. Yet its newest growth gathers along their apparent entrances, tracing thresholds on panes of glass.

During a sediment release, much of the inhabitant withdraws.

The pale lips disappear from the joints. Membranes flatten against chamber walls. Living tubes close so firmly that they look like narrow cracks in the masonry. As mud moves down the street, the district seems for a little while to contain only machinery.

Then the covers settle.

A clear thread emerges beside a newly exposed boot scraper. It extends over one of the teeth, pauses, and divides. Farther away, beneath the pumping-house window, a fold returns to the piston sleeve. Water enters it. The whole long arrangement begins taking up its tensions again.

At the boundary curb, its growth is sparse. A few tendrils have crossed onto the open bottom, lying in the mud like lengths of discarded filament. They are still connected to the body inside. When the buried weights descend, these tendrils stiffen, and their ends lift slightly.

There is nothing out there for them to hold.

By the next reversal of the current they have settled back into the mud. Within the curb, the animal continues moving through the spaces it has found, touching one surface with another, drawing the cold water into itself and letting it go.
