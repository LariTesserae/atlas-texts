The builders are softer than their houses.

When one comes out to feed, it first appears as a crease in the pale rim of its chamber. The crease widens. A collar of almost transparent flesh rises through it, carrying a ring of filaments folded together. These separate slowly, each bending at a slightly different angle, until the animal has become something like an open flower, though there is no center that can be seen clearly and nothing in its movements resembles blooming.

The filaments bear hairs too fine to distinguish in the blue light. Their working is visible in what approaches them: a speck hesitates, curves, and travels toward the mouth against the broader current. Another speck is turned away. The animal is not simply catching whatever arrives. Along the inner surface of its crown, particles pause and are sorted. Some disappear into the body. Others leave wrapped in a little mucus, heavier than they were.

Withdrawn, the same animal is a folded ribbon behind a door.

The door is not quite a door. It is a thickening of the body’s outer skin, pressed against the opening from within. Through it, the crown remains faintly visible, compressed into a shape that seems too small to contain what was just extended. A narrow muscle holds it there. When a swimmer brushes the chamber, the muscle tightens before the swimmer has passed.

The touch travels farther than the swimmer does.

Under the chalk partitions, slender living strands join one chamber to the next. They run through holes left as the walls were laid down. A contraction at one mouth becomes a slight tension in those strands. Three openings close nearby, then another behind them. The warning weakens as it crosses the settlement. At the far end, an animal merely bends one filament inward and continues feeding.

They are joined, but they do not share a single attention.

One chamber may be taking in food while its neighbor is making a wall. Another holds a body with its crown pulled deep inside, working at something that has lodged against the gut. At the base of a tower, an old inhabitant no longer extends all its filaments. Two remain short, thickened where they were damaged. It feeds with the others. Its chamber is almost hidden beneath newer growth, and its opening faces into a passage kept clear by the small current its own feeding helps produce.

There is no unoccupied lower storey simply because an upper one has appeared.

New bodies begin as swellings along the living strand. Before there is a cup, before there is a mouth, there is a place where nourishment goes and does not immediately return. The swelling develops a fold. The fold deepens into a pocket. Within the pocket, minute motions begin.

The clear blisters on the tower contain these unfinished animals. Their crowns lie against their bellies. Their walls are yielding because the mineral has not yet been laid down through them. Sometimes a passing crustacean dents one with its heel. The dent persists for a while, then rounds out as pressure returns from inside.

Not every blister becomes a chamber. In one, the folded crown has stopped changing. The connected strand still brings material to it. Around its base, the wall is whitening.

Between the builders live animals that cannot stay anywhere long enough to build.

The flat glassy ones have bodies bordered by a fringe of short legs. They move sideways through gaps, feeling ahead with their first pair, drawing the others close whenever the passage narrows. Their backs carry a few grains caught in a sticky film. In open water these grains would weigh them down. Here they give the animals purchase against smooth young walls.

One stops over a deposit of pale waste. Its mouthparts work beneath its body, out of sight. As it feeds, a darker shape advances along its middle. It leaves behind a patch cleaner than the surrounding floor and several compact pellets too large to remain suspended.

The worms are less firmly committed to a place. A body threads through a pore, emerges beside one chamber, and later appears from another. What looks like several worms testing the water may be different lengths of the same animal. It extends its front end into a quiet corner and holds it there, taking up the dissolved remains that have collected around an egg membrane.

When the heavier brine descends, that end bends upward. The rest of the body follows, slowly enough that the last coils are still below after the first have reached an upper ledge.

Under the ceiling, the crustaceans wait with their antennae spread. Small joints tremble as water passes. They can detect an opening of the entrance before the first incoming particles reach them. Then their legs begin to work, and they leave the roof to intercept what has entered.

Some carry eggs against their undersides. The eggs alter how they move through the crowded lanes. A mother turns her narrow front between two cups, then stops when the clutch touches both walls. She backs out without turning, rises, and tries a wider way. At the next constriction she lifts her body and draws the eggs closer.

The hunter moves with almost none of this preparation.

It has enough transparent flesh to make a pocket around another animal. At rest, its edges hang loose. Its two dark points do not always face the same direction; beneath them, the pale contents of its gut shift as digestion proceeds. It waits where the current slows behind an opening, in water crossed by larvae leaving the chambers.

The larvae have no houses yet. They turn using bands of moving hairs, tracing short loops interrupted by sudden straight runs. A few carry enough stored food to ignore the settlement’s currents for a time. Others already pause at walls.

One touches a clear chamber, pulls away, and touches it again farther down. It remains there while water passes over its back. The hairs along its body continue beating, but their strokes grow uneven. By the next exchange of water, part of its surface will have fastened to the wall.

Another reaches the bare floor at the darkest end. It searches the sediment edge, settling briefly and lifting again whenever the grains move beneath it. Near the two new chambers, it finds a firm projection no larger than itself.

There it holds.

Above it, a feeding crown opens. Waste drifts down, misses the larva, and catches against the projection. The next current bends around this small obstruction. Dust gathers on the sheltered side.

The larva keeps moving its hairs. For a while they draw food toward it. Later, as its body shortens and a fold develops beneath its surface, they cease to move at all.
