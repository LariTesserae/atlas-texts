For much of the year, the largest creature that lives in the hollow is the one with the round ears.

It is smaller than the bowl in the shelter. Its coat is the color of gravel when gravel is wet, except beneath the chin, where the fur is pale and unusually fine. Seen from above, it has almost no neck. Seen close to the ground, it is all neck and attention, lifting its head above a stem, turning one ear toward the cleft while the other remains directed at the sky.

It knows the hollow by passages that would not appear on any map. Under one shrub there is room to run without flattening the back. Under another there is room only to squeeze, and the animal comes out with its fur parted along the spine. A stone that looks firmly seated can be passed beneath. A dark crack that looks inviting ends after the length of a paw.

It keeps several stores. This is not abundance but insurance. Water has spoiled one cache before; a fallen slab has sealed another. It lays its clipped plants separately on the drying ledge, shifting them when the sun moves. The leaves must dry before they are carried inward. Stems too thick to cure are sometimes abandoned, though it will return to an abandoned stem and examine it more than once.

When the person arrives, the animal disappears.

The person stands just inside the hollow until her breathing becomes quiet. She has climbed the last part slowly, with one hand against the rock. Nothing about her arrival suggests that she owns the shelter. She looks first at its roof, then at the gravel above it, then at the pool.

Her name is used below the mountain. Up here there is seldom occasion for it.

She is neither especially old nor young, but certain parts of her have aged faster than others. The backs of her hands are dark and finely cracked. Her teeth are still sound. One knee will straighten comfortably only after she has been moving for a while. When she kneels, she chooses her place before committing herself, because getting up is more difficult than going down.

She wears a strip of woven cloth across her brow, with a narrow opening through which to look. Without it, the brightness gives her a pain behind the eyes that can persist into sleep. Even with it, she sometimes sits facing the shelter wall until the colored spots fade.

In her pack are meal, a little fat wrapped in skin, spare foot bindings, and several empty packets folded from thin bark. There is a needle pushed through the edge of a pouch so it cannot work loose. The pouch contains thread, two fishhooks, and a tooth that has no practical use but has traveled with her for years.

She does not arrive hungry enough to dig immediately.

First she draws the bedding out into the sun. Beetles fall from it. One of the hanging cords has been chewed through, and she ties it again, making the usable length shorter. She removes mouse droppings from the bowl, rinses it with a little water, and rubs it with gravel where the water is insufficient. Then she sits beside the doorway and eats.

By then the round-eared animal is watching from the cleft.

It does not become tame. After two days, it will pass her if she is still. After four, it will pass while she moves one hand. If she stands unexpectedly, all four days are lost.

She comes during the short season when certain plants can be gathered and dried. Some go down the mountain as remedies; some are kept for food; others she collects for people who want seed rather than root. She knows the plants best at hand height. From the ridge, even she cannot always distinguish one patch from another.

Her work is slow partly because of the ground. A root may have entered a seam and followed it beyond reach. Pulling harder will bring up the crown and leave the rest below. She uses the digging stick to work around it, listening to the small sounds made by wood against stone.

She leaves many plants untouched. Some are too young. Some have already been gathered from. Some stand in places where removing them would release the dust held among their roots. There are also plants she misjudges, digging for some time before discovering that they will not come free intact. These she presses back into place, without knowing whether they will survive.

She is careful, but not harmless. Where she sits repeatedly, a moss cushion acquires a flat brown edge. Once, while reaching for a packet, she puts her elbow through a web she had spent the morning avoiding.

The spider remains beneath a leaf. By evening, two new lines cross the gap.

Her days are arranged around the thaw. Before it, she repairs things, sorts what has dried, and holds her hands inside her clothing. During it, she works. After it, she carries everything under cover.

Cloud is useful, but it also means she may be unable to see the descent. When it rises into the hollow, she spreads a spare cloth over a rough slab and later wrings it into the bowl. She checks the covered cup beneath the groove. If both yield little, she drinks less rather than scrape open the pool while it is still frozen.

At night she lies curled around her pack. The roof is too low for her to sit upright beneath its highest slab. To turn over, she must first move her knees.

She hears the mountain differently from inside. Small falls of gravel make a brushing sound through the turf. A chip striking the roof is surprisingly loud. Sometimes she places her palm against the wall beside her bed, though this tells her very little.

There are nights when she sleeps badly and is impatient the following morning. She fumbles a knot, drops something into a crack, and swears loudly enough to send a bird from the upper fin. Her voice sounds unfamiliar after days of being used only at a murmur.

Late in her stay, the animal begins drying grass close to the shelter entrance. She moves the stems once to reach the doorway. It carries them back. The next time, she steps over them.

When she leaves, the packets weigh more than the provisions did on arrival. She checks the roof again and places a stone over a loose edge of turf. Nothing is left in the bowl. The bedding is pushed toward the driest wall.

She pauses at the small cairn to tighten a foot binding, then goes down out of sight.

The animal waits longer than necessary before crossing the open ground. At the doorway it stops, rises a little on its forefeet, and smells the threshold. Then it goes inside.

It finds one dropped stem, too green to store, and brings it out into the sun.
