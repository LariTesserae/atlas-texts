At first, it would be mistaken for one of the folded ridges on the back wall.

Its color is not quite the color of the limestone. It is a little warmer, though only in the way that an old bone is warmer than a fresh fracture. Along its underside, where it rests against the rock, it is dark with damp. Fine mineral grains adhere to its upper surface. There is no clear division between these grains and its own skin until it moves.

It does not move when someone enters.

The step at the doorway tells it that something has arrived. It feels the weight pass through the worn stone, then the deeper pressure of the unexpected second step. These are small disturbances inside the waterfall’s continuous shaking, but they are disturbances it knows. It has lived here long enough to distinguish a foot from a stone loosened by frost.

It waits through the visitor’s first attempts to see.

Its body is longer than a person is tall, but it folds into a narrow space. There are joints beneath it, more than a human body has, and short, broad limbs tucked close against its sides. Resting, it resembles a length of wet cloth laid over several uneven stones. Unfolded, it stands no higher than a stool.

The end that turns toward the entrance has no obvious face. Two shallow pits lie under a projecting fold, sheltered from falling grit. They register light, but do not form much of an image. Below them is a small mouth whose edges fit together so closely that, when shut, it looks like another crease.

Most of what it knows comes through the pads beneath its limbs.

These pads are soft, pale, and slightly raised at their centers. It sets them on the floor one at a time. Each placement gives it a different part of the chamber: the heavy pulse from the neighboring groove, the finer tremor of water striking an exposed shelf, the faint irregular pressure of someone shifting their weight near the doorway. A crack opening deep in the bank reaches it distinctly. So does the scrape of a root moving as the tree outside leans in the wind.

The broad middle of the hollow is kept clear because it crosses there.

Not often enough to make a path. Its weight is spread too widely, and the dry dust settles back around its low feet. But sometimes a loose stone interferes with the place where it puts a pad. It moves that stone to the edge, carrying it in the soft underside of a limb. Pebbles go against the walls. Larger pieces are rolled, slowly, until they settle somewhere useful.

A person seeing it do this might think of housekeeping. The resemblance is fair.

It has preferences. It likes the central patch to remain dry. It dislikes a twig lying across the shallow channel near the entrance, where the occasional windblown spray must be allowed to drain. It returns displaced things to places it has chosen, though not always immediately. A stone might trouble it for several days before it gets up to move it.

The little basin belongs to its daily rounds.

It drinks by setting the lower edge of its mouth against the water. The movement is slight. Afterwards a small wet crescent remains on the polished lip. In dry weather, when the basin has almost emptied, it sometimes waits beneath the place where moisture gathers overhead. It holds one broad limb upward and catches the drop there, then bends the limb inward.

It feeds mostly at the threshold.

There is enough for it among the moss and old fern stems: small soft-bodied things, pale growth beneath rotting leaves, whatever has washed into the sheltered pockets and lodged there. It is deliberate in its searching. With the edge of a limb it lifts a flattened frond, examines what lies beneath, and lowers it again. It leaves the rooted plants alone.

On warm nights it may go as far as the ledge outside. It remains beneath the ferns, its body fitting closely to the uneven stone. Morning usually finds it back against the rear wall.

The earthen bowl was not brought here by it.

Someone stayed in the hollow once, sleeping on a plank above the low arrangement of flat stones. Whoever it was had a small flame, a bundle of bedding, and the bowl. For several nights there were unfamiliar pressures in the floor. Then the inhabitant began to recognize them: the long, uneven weight of a sleeping body; the quick repeated movements of shivering; a hand laid flat on stone.

It approached that hand.

Very slowly, it placed one of its pads beside it. The person withdrew. Later, perhaps on another night, the hand remained.

Warmth passed into the pad. The creature held still until the hand lifted away.

There is no way to know what the person made of this. The bowl was eventually broken. The bedding and plank were taken out. The wooden peg remained in its crack.

The creature still touches the exposed end of the peg when it passes.

Its skin gives a little beneath the pressure, and the wood is polished where the same part of the pad meets it. If the peg loosens, the creature presses it back. It has done so many times.

It lives alone now. There may be others farther along the limestone, in cavities reached by cracks too narrow for a person, but none visits this chamber. Now and then it stops with every pad touching the floor and stays that way for a long time. Nothing visible happens. Afterwards it resumes whatever small task it had interrupted.

Age shows at its edges.

Some of the folds have grown stiff with mineral. One limb no longer opens fully, and it favors that side when descending the lower step. In places its skin is finely crazed. These patches shed in small, thin flakes, which it carries to a recess behind the flat stones. Beneath them the new surface is smooth and almost translucent.

When heavy rain deepens the waterfall’s roar, it leaves the wall.

It comes into the open middle and unfolds as far as it can, setting its limbs widely on the stone. The pressure pits at the front lift from their sheltered crease. A delicate movement passes along its sides, too regular to be caused by the shaking floor.

This is when it is easiest to see alive.

It stays there until the flood begins to ease. Then it makes a circuit of the room, inspects the basin, clears any new grit from the drainage channel, and goes back to its resting place.

A visitor who stands still long enough may eventually notice that the ridge on the wall has lowered itself.

There is no sound of its approach. It comes only a short distance, then sets a pale pad on the floor between itself and the visitor.

If the visitor puts down a hand, it waits. If the hand stays, it moves its pad a little nearer.

The stone is cold. Its skin is cold too, but softer than expected, yielding gently under the fingers. Beneath that softness, the waterfall can be felt moving through it.
