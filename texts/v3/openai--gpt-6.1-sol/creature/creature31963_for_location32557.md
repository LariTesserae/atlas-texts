The nearest inhabitants are difficult to see because they gather on the side of a moving thing that is not quite there yet.

Beside an elbow, behind a heel, beneath a stone that has just begun to fall, the water parts for an instant. Something occupies that parting. Seen from the right angle, it resembles a row of clear knuckles, each with a white fleck inside. Seen directly, it is gone.

These are the makers of the clicking.

They follow a visitor without following the visitor’s scent or warmth. A still body interests them very little. What attracts them is the slight disagreement between where a body was and where it is: the narrow wake of an arm, the space relinquished by a turning head. They enter it before the water can close. Their joints snap into place, hold briefly, and release.

A slow movement draws only two or three. A sudden struggle may bring hundreds. Then there is a pale cuff around the wrist, a fringe along the jaw, a second set of fingers opening just behind the first. They do not bite. Nevertheless, after their attention has passed, small things may be missing: a loose thread, a scab, the sharp edge of a fingernail. Occasionally they leave something in exchange. A grain of amber. A splinter with no apparent point of entry. Once, a tiny jointed piece that continues to flex when laid on the palm.

They are easier to observe around falling rubble. A large fragment descends into the basin with an almost invisible congregation along its upper face. When it strikes the floor, the congregation opens, flashes white, and disappears into the collision.

Afterward there are more clicks.

Their numbers rise sharply when the floor changes, but they never occupy the basin evenly. Long stretches of water remain empty. They cluster around what has moved and what is about to move, leaving the quiet water beyond the rim untouched.

***

The pale creatures that cross the hollow are less numerous. Sometimes none appears between one collapse of the northern cleft and the next. Sometimes three arrive together, separated by exactly their own breadth, and take different routes across the floor.

Their bodies are thin at the edges and thick at the center. A faint seam runs around each one, dividing a smooth upper surface from a softer underside crowded with minute folds. There is no visible head. Any fin can become the leading fin, though a creature approaching a crack usually turns one particular edge toward the light. Along that edge are small cloudy spots. These clear as it descends.

It does not feed by scraping. It settles a little above the new stone and holds itself there while the underside opens.

What had looked like a continuous pale belly separates into overlapping leaves. They expose orange tissue, wetly bright even under water. The tissue swells toward the crack without quite touching it. Fresh mineral shavings rise and lodge between the leaves. For a moment the animal looks heavier, more complicated, almost untidy. Then the leaves close in sequence, and it is flat again.

The orange stain it carries away slowly travels toward its center.

There are individuals with three fins, four, six, and once a much larger one with nine unequal fins crowded along one side. None seems malformed. Each moves with the same unhurried precision. A current strikes its margin, divides, and rejoins behind it, although loose grit may hammer against the creature’s back.

They can remain calm because they are not entirely rigid. Under a strong pressure wave, a fin may fold inward and emerge elsewhere along the seam. This happens too quickly to follow, but the result is plain: a creature that had been holding against one current is now shaped for another.

The larger changes require a chamber.

One enters low, drawing its fins close. It presses against the back wall while the room inhales. Bronze light outlines its body through the widening slab joints. During the next few booms, something pale repeatedly crosses the doorway, though nothing comes out.

Afterward the animal may leave with fewer fins, a thicker center, or a notch in its edge that was not there before. It tests each new surface against the water. Sometimes it returns inside.

Sometimes the doorway stays empty.

***

The chambers themselves grow slowly between violent adjustments.

Their honey-colored slabs have rough outer faces, but within the joints the stone is translucent. Fine strands pass from one slab into another. When a wall spreads under pressure, the strands stretch across the opening. They do not break. They darken, pull taut, and draw the slabs together again.

The chambers are animals, or colonies of animals so closely fitted that the distinction has little use inside the basin.

Their doorways remain open because the doorway is where they eat.

Silt and mineral flakes enter on an inhalation. Most are expelled. Some catch in shallow grooves along the inner walls, where a soft brown film covers them. The film thickens over several breaths, then hardens. A slab acquires another layer. A chipped threshold grows smooth.

Larger objects are treated differently. A stone lodged in the entrance may be held through several pressure cycles, turned, and drawn into a joint. A pale fin can disappear into the wall without leaving a stain.

The white fragments around the smallest chamber are pieces of its inner lining. Their curved faces were once exposed to the room. Their broken sides show close-set bands, and within the bands are narrow inclusions: orange grit, dark plant fiber, something like a flattened claw.

The chamber sheds them as it contracts.

It is not dying. Its doorway has become rounder, and a soft rim has begun to project beyond the threshold. When the water falls still, that rim unfolds slightly. For a few moments the chamber seems to be listening with an open mouth.

The roofless one is older. In its upper stones, little openings lead into blind passages lined with the same brown film. Small pale bodies sometimes appear in them, no larger than a thumb joint. They have no fins yet. When disturbed, they withdraw so completely that the passage seems too shallow to have contained them.

Whether the chamber produces them or merely keeps them is hard to establish. It rearranges its upper stones after one emerges, closing the passage the youngster used.

***

Along the rim, the dark green straps are tougher than they look. Their attachment is not a root but a broad, ridged foot. Under it, the fresh fracture face remains pale and uncrusted. If the stone breaks away, the foot may release and fasten to the remaining rim before the detached piece has fallen a body’s length.

The transparent threads at the tips are active even when the straps lie motionless.

They stiffen toward passing mineral flakes. A thread touches one, curls around it, and draws it into the green blade. Larger flakes are rejected. Small white grains pass along the strap beneath its skin, making brief traveling bumps.

At rare intervals, one of the threads thickens. A dark bead forms at its end and hangs out over the drop. The strap bends farther outward, away from the basin’s pull, until the bead detaches.

Most disappear into the dark surrounding water. A few return, caught by the inward current. Those become leathery specks on the rim, each with a single transparent hair searching above it.

The amber bulbs beneath the ledge grow from a different kind of body. Their stems enter a soft pad tucked so far into the rock that it is visible only when a crack opens behind it. The pad contracts before the bulbs brighten. Light passes from stem to stem, unevenly, as though each bulb must be reached in turn.

During a boom, the bulbs draw tight against the ledge. Their amber walls wrinkle around something darker inside. Afterward they release their sinking bubbles.

Each bubble contains a minute curved strip. As it descends, the strip turns slowly, pushing against the enclosing skin. Near the floor the bubble collapses without a visible burst. The strip lands, uncurls, and slips beneath a flake of stone.

Very few survive the next concussion.

Those that do make small amber blisters in the newest rock. They are the first living things to settle a gully after its floor has opened. Most are swallowed when the stone folds again. Occasionally one is carried upward on a rising ridge and finds itself beneath a ledge.

***

There is also a broad, low inhabitant under the floor.

Its presence can be found in places where the stone is thin enough for the bronze light to show through. Between two booms, a shadow moves there—not passing beneath the cracks, but stopping at each one. The light dims. The crack edges soften. New stone rises between them.

When a slab breaks free, its underside sometimes bears a patch of tissue, gray and closely pleated. Exposed to the basin, the patch makes several small contractions. Then it hardens into honey-colored stone.

The larger body has never been seen. It may extend beneath all three chambers, or each chamber may have one of its own. The deep blows come from farther down than its visible tissue, and it responds to them much as the bulbs do: drawing tight before the impact, loosening afterward.

In the interval of looseness, the floor consumes what has fallen onto it. Pebbles settle into shallow depressions. Dust sticks in rows. A fallen green strap lies against the rock until its margins sink out of sight.

Nothing disappears quickly enough to look like feeding. Yet a pebble watched through three or four booms becomes smaller, while the stone surrounding it gains a rounded swelling.

Across that swelling the fine white deposit gathers again. For a little while it remains undisturbed.

Then a pale creature settles above it, opening the leaves of its underside. Clicks collect around its fins. Beneath the ledge the amber bulbs begin, one after another, to brighten.
