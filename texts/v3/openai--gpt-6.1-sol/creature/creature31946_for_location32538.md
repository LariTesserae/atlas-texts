The people who live here wear their keys on short cords inside their coats. An outside pocket is less safe. There have been instances of a key returning from an unsettled hour with an extra tooth, or a house accepting the key but opening onto someone else’s kitchen.

Most residents keep a second key with a neighbor. The neighbor’s door is chosen for its stability rather than its proximity. A woman may leave her spare key with a retired machinist two tram stops away because his rooms have remained exactly where they are for sixty years.

They are not an especially solemn people. They complain about the price of lamp oil, although hardly anyone uses it now, and about the tram service, although most have relatives employed by it. They tell visitors that the eastern district is sunnier. This is untrue in any ordinary sense, but there are afternoons when a pale brightness collects on the undersides of its balconies, and the distinction matters to them.

Their clothes are made to accommodate dampness and long intervals of waiting. Coats have broad skirts that can be sat on. Work shirts are reinforced at the shoulder, where a person leans against a wall while a corridor regains its proper length. Children wear mittens fastened to their sleeves until they are old enough to object. Even afterward, a surprising number of adults have a length of string passing through the arms of their winter coats.

Many work short shifts at irregular hours. A foundry worker may be called in just after breakfast, sent home before the kettle has cooled, and recalled at dusk. Consequently the city is full of people carrying small, unfinished things: a peeled apple wrapped in cloth, a newspaper folded at the crossword, a sock being darned. There are shelves for these beside the factory gates. Some have names painted on them. Others have accumulated the belongings of people who changed districts or died, and no one is quite responsible for clearing them.

In the northern district lives a woman named Mara, whose employment card describes her as a route keeper.

She is neither a surveyor nor a gardener, though she carries chalk and pruning shears. Each morning she walks the passages between a boiler house, two residential blocks, and the public laundry. She notes where animals have crossed during the night. A tuft of hair on a pipe bracket, mud on the warm side of a door, the narrow moist track left by something dragging its belly: these are entered in a book with columns for location, direction, and the condition of the hour.

For twenty-three years her task has been to keep certain passages open.

Sometimes this means persuading a caretaker not to board up a hole. Sometimes it means laying a strip of rough fabric over a polished threshold so that a small creature can obtain a purchase. Once she had to arrange for a kitchen to be moved. The tenant was furious until Mara showed him the places where broad antlers had begun to pass through the wall, leaving the wallpaper intact but making the crockery ring.

The kitchen was rebuilt at the other end of the apartment. The tenant now claims this was his suggestion.

Mara is a narrow woman with an old injury to one knee. She has a married daughter in a district she rarely visits because the journey sometimes occupies most of a day. Her daughter sends parcels containing coffee, stockings, and leaves from a tree that grows beside her window. The leaves arrive fresh. Mara puts them in water, though they never produce roots.

She knows perhaps forty kinds of animal by sight and another dozen by what they leave behind. She does not give them elaborate names. The branching-horned browsers are terrace deer. The things in the rails are rail things. If pressed, she can distinguish several sorts of rail thing, but only one of them is commonly heard beneath the northern tracks.

That one has a tail like a strip of black belt and feet that strike the steel from inside.

It is longer than people suppose. Mara once saw its forequarters emerging from a break beside a set of points while the tail still lay visible far down the line. Between the two, the rail was undamaged. She recorded this without a measurement, since there was no useful way to take one.

The rail things breed in sidings that have fallen out of use but remain warm. Their young announce themselves by a faint ticking, often mistaken for cooling metal. Track workers place their palms on the rails before cutting them. If they feel a movement, they leave the work until morning.

There is an official procedure for this. There is also the procedure actually followed, which involves crumbs from a worker’s lunch and a kettle of hot water poured slowly over the next section of track.

The city’s animals are accustomed to human arrangements without quite depending on them. Birds turn up at the schoolyard before the lunch bell, but they also turn up on holidays, when no lunch is served. Goats recognize the maintenance lift and will wait beside it with the grave patience of passengers. They cannot be persuaded to enter if the attendant is wearing a new coat.

The terrace deer move chiefly in the pauses between machines.

Their coats are coarse and dark near the belly, pale over the back, where dust settles. Close up, the branching growths on their heads are not much like ordinary antlers. The tips are soft, covered in a fine greenish nap. Drops of water hang from them even on dry days. Small birds drink there, clinging sideways while the deer continues to browse.

In late winter the deer rub their heads against the sheltered faces of pillars. The green nap remains behind. A month later those patches are often moss.

Mara has tried planting them elsewhere. They survive only where the animal chose to leave them.

There are smaller inhabitants whose presence is known chiefly indoors. Laundry mice nest in bundles waiting to be boiled and move their young just before the bundles are collected. A pale beetle lives in the felt seals of lift doors. It feeds on the mineral powder brought in on passengers’ shoes and produces a clicking sound when crushed, loud enough that most people step carefully rather than endure it.

In some buildings, tenants leave an empty saucer beneath the lowest stair.

What drinks from it has never been agreed upon. Cats refuse to use those stairs after dark, but will sleep peacefully on the landing above. The saucer is found dry each morning, with a single grain of black stone in it. Residents save the grains in jars. They are useful for weighting the hems of curtains, and this seems sufficient reason to continue the practice.

Children learn the inhabitants of their district before they learn its street names.

They know which signal birds will tolerate a hand nearby, which pipes are safe to crawl under, which terrace deer become irritable when their antlers are wet. At school they draw maps of the routes animals take. The maps are compared with the municipal surveys, though neither set is considered wholly authoritative.

A child who draws a passage through a building is asked what she saw there. If she says a fox went through, the teacher writes *fox passage* in the margin. If she says she went through herself, her parents are called.

Some children are born during hours that never fully settle.

Nothing obvious distinguishes them. They grow, lose teeth, quarrel, and complain of being cold. But they may remember a room that their parents have not yet rented, or recognize an elderly person from a photograph taken long before they were born. Most of these recollections fade. Families are advised not to test them repeatedly.

A few retain a small uncertainty around them into adulthood. Their reflections are slow to appear in dark windows. Animals make room for them on narrow walks.

Mara’s assistant is such a person.

His name is Iven. He is nineteen, has large hands, and blushes when asked about anything he cannot mend. He can replace a valve, solder a seam, and carry an injured bird inside his shirt without frightening it. He cannot always say whether he has visited a place before.

Occasionally he stops in the middle of a route and looks troubled.

“We should go around,” he says.

Mara asks whether he has seen something.

“No,” he says, and then, after a little while, “Not there.”

She has learned not to hurry him. Usually they take another way. Once they continued and found a staircase descending to a floor of warm, unmarked stone. Iven sat down on the top step and began to cry with an exhaustion that seemed much older than he was.

They returned the next day. The laundry was at the foot of the stairs, as it had always been. He helped carry baskets until midday and did not mention the stone.

There are residents older than the districts in which they live, though not older than the city.

They remember streets when the streets were bridges. One man can point from his sitting-room window to the place where his father used to lower a bucket into open air. Water rose into the bucket from a cloud below. The old man is no longer able to reach the window without help, but he still corrects anyone who calls the ground outside it solid.

These people keep things that younger residents mistake for curiosities: a doorstop shaped to fit a threshold that sloped the other way; a photograph of six children on a staircase, with seven shadows; a tram ticket to a district absent from current timetables.

They are careful about their recollections. They know the difference between an event that was unusual and one they have had too many years to reconsider.

When someone dies, neighbors sit in the apartment for three evenings. They make tea, empty cupboards, and keep the lamps lit. This began as a precaution against rooms following their occupants elsewhere, but it has become a kindness that no one wishes to discontinue.

The animals often notice first. Signal birds fall quiet outside the window. A terrace deer may stand for hours beneath a balcony without feeding.

Afterward, people wash the rooms and leave one drawer closed until the next settled hour.

Beyond the terraces live creatures that seldom enter the city at all. Some graze on the skins of new stone. They are low, patient animals, with lips that leave faint scallops in the mineral paste. Others cling beneath fresh overhangs, opening translucent membranes to collect moisture rising from the depths. Seen from the path, they resemble scraps of damp cloth caught on the rock.

At the final bench, visitors sometimes encounter a small white bird with a thick beak and bare pink feet.

It comes close enough to inspect their cuffs. It is not looking for food. Breadcrumbs are ignored; a loose thread holds its attention. When a visitor stands, it may take hold of the thread and draw it out a little way, then release it.

Mara has seen the bird carry such threads to a seam in the precipice. There it pushes them into the soft material with its beak.

She made a note of this once. Later she saw several threads still protruding from stone that had hardened around them.

Now she checks the seam whenever she visits. She brings no thread expressly for the bird, but if it begins pulling at her coat, she sits still until it has finished.
