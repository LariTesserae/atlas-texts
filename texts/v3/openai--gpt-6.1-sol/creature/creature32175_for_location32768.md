At first you would take it for something living in the water behind the bank.

There would be reason for that. When the underground rushing pauses, another sound sometimes follows: a soft, repeated contact, too heavy to be dripping and too irregular to be a pulse. Something yielding touches stone, leaves it, touches it again. It can continue for several minutes. If you sit beside the mossy bank, you may feel it through the place where your heel rests.

You would imagine a large animal in a narrow passage, turning itself around.

There is an animal there. But turning is not quite what it does, and the passage is not quite where it lives.

A little of it lies beneath each stretch of empty ground.

Its upper surface is covered by sediment so fine that, when dry, it looks like ordinary soil. There are grains of quartz in it, small broken stalks, the reddish dust of weathered stone. A seed landing there finds warmth and moisture. It may split. It may send down the first white point of a root.

Then the root bends.

This does not happen abruptly. There is no recoil, no visible injury. The growing tip makes a broad curve and continues along the surface, as though it has encountered a stone too large to go around quickly. If it reaches the edge of a plant’s established ground, it enters there. If it does not, the seedling spends what little it has and lies down.

The thing beneath the sediment is softer than stone. Pressing a finger into one of the bare patches will establish that, though not immediately. First there is the resistance of wet earth. Then, at about the depth of a fingernail, a smooth firmness that gives slightly and holds.

It is warm.

If you keep your finger there, it becomes difficult to judge whether the firmness is moving or whether your own hand is tiring. The soil around the finger begins to shine. Water gathers in the depression, clears it, and makes the skin of your fingertip look unusually pale.

Eventually the surface rises enough to ask your hand to leave.

It has no fur, scales, or recognizable skin. Washed clean, a small part of it would resemble the inside of a soaked seed: cream-colored, faintly translucent, crossed by threads that vanish when viewed from another angle. Here and there, under that pale surface, a darker band slowly thickens and thins.

The animal is very flat in the places nearest daylight. Its margins spread beneath the ground cover in thin, soft shelves. Farther down, among the stone ribs, these shelves become folds. The folds descend into wet chambers, where they are thicker and can move without lifting the earth.

There is no single outline to see. Its body has grown through the available spaces. One portion passes beneath a slab, another narrows around the buried foot of a rock rib, another opens into a broad lobe under the dark soil. Some of these portions may have been separated for a long time. They still swell together in the afternoon.

The low rushing comes from deeper within it.

Water passes through channels held open by the body’s own firmness. In some places the channels are narrow and the water moves quickly; in others they widen into chambers where it turns, carrying mineral grit and the soft remains of leaves. The walls flex. A chamber fills, distends, and slowly empties into the next.

This is part of its feeding, though there is nothing in it resembling a stomach. What the water carries is taken along the way. Fine living films flourish on the inner surfaces, disappear, and flourish again. A fallen leaf may remain recognizable for days in an upper basin, while below it the substances that once made it green have already entered the animal.

It needs the plants.

Their roots occupy soil held between its lobes, reaching into pockets that remain open even when the afternoon water rises. From them come small steady losses: loosened cells, sweet fluids, the husks of root hairs. Leaves fall. Wood softens. The animal takes these slowly, and returns water that has passed through darkness and living tissue.

The plants grow large on very little ground.

There is no agreement between them that could be put into words. A root approaches a boundary and changes direction. A lobe approaches a knot of roots and becomes thinner. Over years, the boundaries shift. The spreading tree has gained perhaps a hand’s breadth of earth on one side and lost as much on the other. Its lowest branch has settled onto a rock. Beneath that rock, the animal has grown around a hollow left by an old, dead root.

Some of the fern’s oldest roots are enclosed within it. They are still alive.

The late-afternoon rising is its broadest movement.

As the surface water sinks from the pools, the deeper folds fill. Their expansion presses water upward through the thin lobes beneath the bare earth. Moisture appears evenly, first as a darkening, then as a continuous gloss. For a while each exposed patch rests on something nearly buoyant.

Along its margins are structures too small to distinguish without kneeling very close: short clear threads, crowded together, laid flat while the ground is dry. In water they stand up and move. Their motion carries silt inward and larger debris outward. A leaf progresses toward the edge so slowly that watching it is frustrating. Look elsewhere, and it has moved.

A footprint closes through the same motion. The animal does not distinguish the print from any other disturbance. Sediment settles into the pressure mark; the yielding surface resumes its shape.

A stone is harder to accommodate. Over many risings, it is shifted toward a rib, where it eventually comes to rest among other stones.

The little flat creature on the warm slab belongs to a more familiar sort of life. It has bones, a mouth, and feet with three blunt toes. Its belly is almost transparent when young. It spends most of its time in the narrow damp space between a plant’s roots and the larger animal’s margin.

It feeds there on things too small to trouble the open air.

Sometimes it presses its lower jaw against the pale surface and waits until moisture beads along its mouth. The larger animal responds by releasing water, or perhaps would release it anyway. Neither moves away.

When the afternoon swelling begins, the little creature climbs onto stone. Its tracks arrive and return beneath the same bush because that is where its passage remains open.

The larger inhabitant has no eyes that you could find. Light nevertheless changes it. A shadow cast across a washed portion of its surface produces a faint tightening, spreading beyond the shadow’s edge. It is more responsive to pressure. Kneel on bare ground and, after a while, the nearest wet margin draws in a fraction.

It can withdraw only so far. There are places where it is attached deep in cracks, and places where its own bulk holds it down.

A person standing in the hollow is present to it as a pattern of weight, warmth, and small movements. It receives footsteps separately through different portions of its body. It receives the settling of gravel after the footsteps have stopped. Rain reaches it first through the pools and then through the earth; frost reaches the shallow margins long before the deep chambers cool.

Its responses travel slowly.

You might step away before the part beneath your foot has begun to draw inward. Then, several minutes later, a thin shine appears where you stood. Nearby fern tips incline toward the newly wetted patch. By the time the water withdraws, you may have forgotten exactly where you placed your feet.

It is old, but its oldest parts would be difficult to identify. The exposed margins wear away and renew themselves. Deep folds thicken, divide, or cease to carry water. A chamber abandoned by the living tissue remains lined with a pale deposit. Water can continue through it for years.

There may be many such chambers beneath the hollow.

There may also be younger portions, almost independent, spreading into soil beyond the rim. Where the mountain becomes colder and the roots closer together, they stay small. They take water from a crack, gather a little sediment, and persist beneath a patch of moss. Some fail during winter. Others are reached, slowly, by a fold growing outward from below.

You would not find a place where the whole animal could be uncovered. Lifting the soil from one lobe would reveal only that lobe. Breaking through the mossy bank would open a chamber of flowing water, pale walls, and darkness continuing between stone supports. Much of the body would remain elsewhere, receiving the disturbance.

Toward evening its upper surface grows still.

The water withdraws. Clear threads lie down beneath the sediment. In the last light, the bare patches look dry enough to cross, although they are not dry. Beneath them, warmth continues to move from the deep chambers into the thin outer folds.

The fern releases its long-held drop.

The small three-toed creature comes down from the slab and slips beneath the bush. A shallow basin fills to its notch. Behind the bank, water presses open a passage that has been narrowing all afternoon, and the deeper rushing returns.

The animal continues to fill the spaces it has made, with the plants standing between its folds and the sky lying open above them.
