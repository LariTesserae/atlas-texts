It lives below the bats, but not directly beneath them.

The centers of the great roosts are too busy. Droppings fall there continually; beetles crowd the soft deposits; the air has a sharpness that catches in the nose. It keeps instead to the margins, where old guano thins into limestone dust and the undersides of fallen slabs remain clean. Its resting places are in those narrow intervals where one immense stone has come down against another without quite touching it.

You could pass within an arm’s length of one and see only a darker space under the rock.

At first, what shows is a length of fur. It has the dry, uneven appearance of something shed and caught in a crack. There are pale grains in it. Then the grains move together, and a foot draws inward.

The animal is about the length of a fox, though lower and broader through the shoulders. Its body fits spaces that seem inadequate for it. The ribs are shallow, the neck short, and the loose skin along its flanks folds rather than bunches when it presses beneath a ledge. Even at rest it does not make a compact, rounded shape. It lies extended, chin on stone, forefeet well ahead of its chest, as though it has paused halfway through entering the floor.

Its fur is gray-brown close to the skin and almost white at the tips. On the back, where dust settles, this makes it pale as the surrounding chips. Along the belly the fur is sparse and darker. The elbows and heels have small bare calluses, polished by years of contact with limestone.

It is not blind. Its eyes are black, deep-set, and more useful near the roof openings than elsewhere. A change in the gray light will make it turn its head. It can distinguish a moving body on a pale slope. But when it goes into the farther basin, it does not proceed as an animal straining to see.

The muzzle goes first.

Long whiskers spread from its cheeks and above its eyes. Several are bent or broken; the longest reach past the width of its shoulders. It brushes them against the mouth of a gap, pauses, and then enters without scraping its sides. Shorter stiff hairs on the wrists touch the stone just before the feet take its weight.

The feet are unexpectedly delicate. Each has five spreading toes, dark pads, and curved claws that can be lifted clear of the ground. On loose dust it leaves a print like a small hand. On sharp limestone it sets the pads individually, finding purchase on surfaces too narrow for the whole foot.

It seldom jumps. A leap would be quick, but the landing would be uncertain. Instead it climbs down facing the rock, stretches across a cleft until its forefeet find the other side, or backs out of a passage and takes another route. Its progress can look hesitant until you watch long enough to notice how rarely it has to correct a movement.

There are several in the basin, though they are almost never visible together.

Each knows a set of passages through the fallen stone: ways beneath heaps, dry shelves beside damp pockets, gaps that lead around an obstruction rather than into it. These routes are not cleared. A pebble displaced by a foot may remain where it rolls. What repeated passage changes is subtler. Dust is rubbed from the underside of a low arch. A corner grows slightly darker from the oils in fur. Beneath one slab, the floor has a shallow groove no wider than a body.

It eats chiefly what the bats sustain.

At the edge of a guano bank it lowers its head and listens. Its ears are small, rounded, and nearly hidden in the fur, but they move independently. The crowded sound overhead does not seem to trouble it. It attends to nearer disturbances: a beetle touching a dry leaf, a cricket shifting under a chip, larvae working in the damp crust.

Sometimes it opens the crust with one forefoot and takes several quick mouthfuls. Sometimes it waits so long beside a stone that dust begins to soften the outline of its toes.

Its teeth are small at the front, suited to picking something out rather than seizing it indiscriminately. Farther back they are broad enough to crush beetle cases. After eating, it wipes its muzzle along the inside of a foreleg, leaving a dark streak in the pale fur.

It also finds what falls.

A bat that has died in the roof may lodge on a shelf for days before coming down. The animal can smell it before it reaches the floor. It waits beneath the shelf, returning between other errands. Once the body falls, it carries it away by a wing and eats under cover, leaving the finger bones and scraps of leathery membrane in the dust.

A living bat on the ground is different. That struggle is noisy and sharp-toothed. The animal approaches from behind, stops when the wings open, and waits for them to fold. It has no interest in making a contest of the meal.

It leaves the toads alone. A young one learns this after the first attempt and thereafter gives a motionless toad almost the same consideration it gives a stone.

Water draws it into the lowest terraces. It drinks from the edges of the black pools, bracing both forefeet above the water so that its belly stays dry. Where there is only a film, it licks the rock. The mineral rims are worn smooth in a few places by generations of tongues.

It knows other sources, too. Beneath the dark shelf, condensation gathers on points of limestone. It rises on its hind legs, supports itself with one forefoot, and catches the drops before they fall. In dry periods several animals may visit that shelf. They approach separately and usually wait their turn in the surrounding cracks.

Their dealings with one another are quiet but not altogether peaceful.

A resting place can be defended. So can a fresh carcass. An animal that comes too close hears a low sound made with the mouth nearly shut—a short, rough vibration that does not travel far. If it persists, the resident comes forward with its head lowered and its back broadened. Most disputes end there.

They recognize one another at closer range than a human would expect. A nose touches the fur behind an ear; breath is drawn through half-open nostrils; one animal steps aside. The smell of a familiar neighbor remains in a passage after the neighbor has gone.

Young are born well back from the light, in a dry hollow above the level reached by seepage. Usually there are two. They begin dark and almost naked, pressed against the mother’s belly. As their fur grows, the pale tips appear first along the spine.

For a time their world is the hollow, the warmth of another body, and the enormous noise outside. When they begin to follow her, they stop at every opening. They put their noses into cracks too small for their heads. They paw at the moving antennae of crickets and start backward when the insects spring.

The mother does not bring them into the open center of the basin. Their first journeys run under stones, across sheltered ledges, and back by the same way. Only later do they learn the exposed crossings: a stretch of pale dust between heaps, a sloping slab beneath a pigeon shelf, the shallow terrace where no roof fissure offers cover.

In those places they move faster.

At the breaches, they sometimes encounter daylight strong enough to cast a shadow. They stop short of it. Wind descending through an opening lifts their fur in the wrong direction and brings too many smells at once: grass, warm soil, distant animals, rain not yet fallen. Some venture onto the slopes immediately below. They eat insects in the moss and investigate seeds dropped by pigeons.

They do not climb out.

Most of their lives pass in the settled coolness, among distances measured by gaps in stone and reliable patches of damp. They sleep through rain on the plateau until runoff begins to strike the hollow below an opening. They wake when the sound of the roof changes as bats loosen their hold and begin moving toward the outside.

That is when they most often emerge.

As the colonies depart, small things fall: droppings, parasites, fragments, an occasional young bat unable to keep its grip. The animals move along the edges of the roosts with their heads raised. For a while, their pale backs can be distinguished crossing the dark ground.

Then the flutter overhead diminishes. The basin’s separate sounds become audible again.

One goes to drink. One disappears beneath a slab with something held in its mouth. Another remains beside an old deposit, lifting the crust a piece at a time.

By the time the bats return, they are mostly out of sight. Beneath the heaps, their whiskers lie against the stone. Dust settles on their fur. The narrow spaces are occupied again.
