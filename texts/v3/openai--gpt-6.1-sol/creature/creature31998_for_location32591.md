The people who live there carry very little that can fall.

Their cups fasten to the palm. Their tools have loops through which two fingers remain threaded even when the hand is open. A child’s first toy is usually a knot of warm cord, something that can be dropped without becoming lost through the floor. Visitors notice the absence of pockets before they notice anything stranger. Most possessions are held beneath the clothes, close enough to the body that they enter a room already warm.

The clothes themselves are thick at the shoulders and thin across the hands. In the oldest families, the outer two fingers are left bare even outdoors. These are the fingers used for reading walls.

People wear metal over their eyes when crossing certain districts. Not blindfolds, exactly: narrow shields against the sudden pale opening of a valley, or the glare of a work lamp exposed after years beneath a colony. Their eyes can still be injured by light they do not expect. In the deeper streets, a person may go several months without seeing their own hands clearly.

They recognize one another by the way weight arrives.

Someone approaching through a bridge sends a familiar sequence ahead of them: heel, pause, the slight drag of a guarded knee. A household knows which of its members has entered the lower shaft before the capsule opens. When people grow old, their friends sometimes fail to recognize them because their steps have become lighter.

Names have spoken forms, but also forms tapped against a railing. The tapped names travel farther. They cannot be shouted in anger.

There are people whose work is to sit.

They sit beside a wall with their boots off, one foot resting on a ceramic plate and the other on the bare floor. A cable carries the plate’s vibration from a foundation several streets away. By comparing the two sensations, they learn whether a sound belongs to the building or to the stone beneath it.

The instruments are more sensitive. The sitters are better at deciding which differences matter.

A sitter may interrupt a meal, place a hand beneath the table, and ask everyone to move away from the north wall. This request is obeyed without discussion, even in houses where nobody agrees about anything else. Later there may be an argument about what was heard, how much work was lost, whether the warning should have been entered in the district record. At the time, people move.

The best sitters do not remain best forever. Their bodies learn the familiar disturbances too thoroughly. A change that would frighten a novice begins to feel ordinary. For this reason, an experienced sitter is usually paired with someone young enough to be continually afraid.

The young resent this arrangement. They know when they are being frightened on purpose.

At the youngest foundations, the work belongs mostly to people who have not yet acquired a household.

They arrive in pairs, carrying sleeping folds, salt gauges, and the small instruments used to ask a foundation seed how far it has opened. For months their room may consist of an insulated sheet stretched inside a shallow stone curve. Water enters from all sides. The field around the sleeping folds keeps their bodies warm but does nothing for their breakfast, which must be eaten quickly or held against the chest.

Some of these pairs become lovers. Others become incapable of enduring each other anywhere except the unfinished house.

There are friendships in the city that have lasted forty years and cannot survive a quiet room. Their members need the knock of water, the changing pressure underfoot, something to attend to while speaking. They meet beside conduits and say very little. In periods of unusual silence they quarrel.

A new foundation has to be visited in every weather. The ribs beneath it must be encouraged into some seams and restrained from others. If an animal settles over the stud, someone must decide whether to wait, coax it aside, or allow the room to grow beneath it.

Waiting is often the cheapest course. It is also the course most difficult to explain to those who are paying.

Thus some of the city’s oldest legal disputes concern animals that have moved only a few body lengths since the disputes began.

No one quite agrees on where an animal ends.

The large ones have names because they affect too many lives to be referred to by species alone. But the names do not always describe single creatures. A broad weight-bearer under three streets may be one body, three bodies pressed together, or the remaining flank of something whose other parts have withdrawn into the cliff.

It matters to the people who tend it. Different arrangements require different concentrations of brine. It matters less to those living above, who call the whole support Old Sill and complain when it breathes during the night.

Old Sill has been called old for longer than anyone has records.

Its keepers enter through a passage that closes slowly behind them. They take with them no lamps. Along the inside of the passage, they can feel the chambers where the animal cultivates its throat colonies: soft combs, each crowded with minute hard mouths. The mouths scrape mineral films from the water Old Sill draws in. What they release nourishes the larger body.

A keeper tests a comb with the back of a fingernail. A healthy colony grips and lets go. A hungry one holds on.

Keepers return smelling of metal and cold fat. Their partners can tell when they have been bitten, however carefully they wash.

The smallest plated creatures have no such attendants. They belong to everyone and make use of that fact.

They gather beneath the lip of a warm cup, between the layers of a glove, inside the clasp of a door. Most feed on films too thin for a person to detect. Some graze the oils left by a hand. Others are attracted to the minute leakage from heating fields and arrange themselves around a sleeping body in a pale, articulated border.

Children collect them.

A child will keep six or seven in a shallow case, feeding them household brine and arguing that the arrangement of their plates has changed. Usually it has. The adults pretend not to be interested, then inspect the case when the child is asleep.

There are domestic strains now, though no one set out to produce them. They are slower, thicker at the rim, less inclined to pack themselves into mechanisms. In some households a line of them has lived beneath the same warming bench for generations.

Removing that bench requires a family meeting.

Food comes from animals, from cultivated membranes, and from vats in which organisms too small to see turn mineral seepage into something the body can use.

The vats are among the warmest places in the city. Their workers have flushed faces and a persistent smell that does not come out of cloth. People complain about the smell on transit capsules and then buy the workers drinks.

Much of what is eaten has the texture of a tongue.

It is sliced, pressed, dried against heated ceramic, or kneaded with salts until it can be torn into strips. There are sweet foods, though sweetness is expensive: a secretion from a particular throat colony, gathered only after its host has begun preparing to divide. Children are warned not to lick unfamiliar animals. Almost every adult remembers having done so.

The larger species are seldom killed for food. Their useful flesh is deep inside structures that cannot be safely opened, and a dead body changes the drainage of a district. Even small carcasses are troublesome. They harden unevenly, retain pockets of corrosive fluid, and attract narrow jointed scavengers that can pass through a pressure seal while it is closing.

Instead, the city harvests what animals shed.

Feeding veils are replaced. Soft feet leave thick cuffs behind as they enlarge. Certain wall dwellers extrude a ribbon of spent tissue for six weeks each year. People who work these harvests know the taste of individual streets.

A family may favor the food from beneath a bridge where a grandparent once lived. When that bridge is dismantled, they speak of the loss for years.

There are no pets in the usual sense, but there are attachments.

A repairer may spend every morning easing the same flat animal away from a valve. At first she touches it with a tool. After a few winters she uses her bare hand. Eventually the animal opens a passage when it feels her approach.

This is useful until someone else inherits the route.

The new repairer has her measurements, her tools, the markings she left along the handrails. The animal nevertheless holds shut. It must be taught a new weight, a new interval between touches. During this period the neighborhood suffers irregular pressure, and everyone blames the newcomer.

People are less patient with one another than they are with animals. They expect a person to understand explanations.

Some residents leave the city for the outer plateau.

They tend the listening instruments, inspect reservoirs, follow warm currents to see whether a channel is likely to persist. Their journeys are slow. A surveyor carries a staff broad enough to spread weight over young stone, and wears a tether that can stiffen into a frame if the ground opens.

Surveyors develop peculiar habits indoors. They test chairs before sitting. They wake when the floor becomes quiet. They stand too far from people, leaving room for a body they can hear but cannot see.

The creatures outside have no reason to accommodate streets.

An immense animal may be resting beneath a smooth rise, its hide so densely mineralized that it appears to be new rock. A surveyor discovers it when a drilled marker begins to withdraw. Others travel through the cloud below the plateau, holding to the cliffs with organs that reach farther than the visible body. Their exposed backs collect freezing vapor. The weight of the ice brings them gradually downward until a movement sheds it all at once.

In the city, an icefall arriving from below is a reason to close the shutters.

Beyond the last ropes, smaller animals live in the warmth left by the larger ones. They follow the passage of a belly over stone, crowd into the shallow channels opened by its heat, and remain there long after it has moved away. Some will die when the channel freezes. Others can close themselves so completely that surveyors mistake them for fragments of polished black glass.

Brought indoors, such a fragment may unfold after several years.

This is why collected stone is kept in perforated cabinets.

The old are not all kept near warmth.

Some ask for rooms close to the outer walls, where they can feel the calls without having them carried through an instrument. These rooms are uncomfortable to visit. A cup cools before a story is finished. Condensation thickens on the visitor’s coat. The old person remains in a small field of warmth, listening.

They can distinguish animals that the district registers classify together. They remember a note before a new gallery altered it, or the way a particular bridge used to answer. Sometimes they insist that an animal long thought gone is still present.

Sometimes they are right.

After a death, the household keeps the warming field active until the body has been taken away. Turning it off is a separate task. A friend usually does it, someone who does not live in the room and will not have to sleep beside the newly cold alcove.

The body is returned to one of the lower vats, unless the person made another arrangement. There is no cemetery, but there are rails worn smooth at places where people pause to remember someone. New residents often mistake these for junction markings.

During the settling, the city’s people become easiest to find.

They gather in suspended rooms, fasten what can swing, and put children between bodies rather than against walls. Someone will be irritated because the settling began before a job was finished. Someone will have brought food that cannot be opened without scattering crumbs. Couples who have been avoiding each other all week must sit within reach.

Below them the machinery begins its counter-rhythm.

The broad animals tighten in their clearances. Small colonies close their plates. Far out on the plateau, the buried bodies answer, and their calls enter the growing stone.

In one room a child asks whether the mountain can hear.

An adult says no. Another, occupied with securing a basin, says that hearing is not quite the word.

The child places two bare fingers against the wall. Nothing there resembles a voice. There is pressure, then a pause long enough to lose patience in, then pressure again.

The child waits for the next one.

All around the room, without being asked, the adults have gone still enough to feel it too.
