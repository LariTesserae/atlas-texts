The people have broad hands, and a habit of leaving them open.

Even when resting, they keep a few fingers around a stem, a loop of cord, the edge of a mat. A visitor might mistake this for nervousness. It is not. They are comfortable with very little beneath them, but seldom with nothing beside them. A hand touching something tells them whether they are moving.

Their palms are pale and deeply creased. Small cuts heal slowly in the warm water, so the fingers of gardeners are often wrapped in narrow strips of leaf. The best pruning blades have a loop that fits over the wrist. Nobody lends a blade without checking the loop first.

They look most like people when they have just raised their faces into air: eyes closing, lips parting, a brief expression of private relief. Then they wipe their mouths and begin whatever sentence they have brought home.

Underwater their faces are quieter. Cheeks swell slightly with held breath. Hair is braided close to the scalp or clipped short, though children like to leave one long strand floating behind them. They learn to read intention in shoulders and hands. A person coming toward you with one palm turned upward is asking you to wait. Two fingers laid against a rope mean someone is following who cannot move quickly. There are different signs for a dropped tool, a loose planting basket, and a child missing from sight.

The sign for the last is never used in play.

Children begin by living almost entirely in the upper chambers. Their earliest landscape is a ceiling of dark leaves, a rim of water trembling against the walls, the wet shoulders of adults rising beside their mats. They know the sound of a seedpod opening before they know what a seedpod looks like.

At first they are lowered into the room beneath their sleeping place, held under the arms. Later they are allowed to pull themselves around a single roof post. Their stone belts are made in small sections, so that weight can be added or taken away. A child who has learned to move without one will sometimes keep wearing it for a few days, pleased by the secret that it is no longer necessary.

No one teaches them to jump.

There are games of passing through hoops, games of finding a shell hidden among roots, games in which a line of children must get across a court without disturbing the fine dust on its floor. The smallest usually spoils this last game. The others complain, then start again after the water clears.

Breath is discussed plainly. Children count it poorly at first. They mistake willingness for capacity and have to be taught to turn back while they still feel able to go farther. Adults teach the routes between air pockets, including those in houses where nobody lives. A roof inside an abandoned garden may be useless for shelter and indispensable on a long errand.

Some of these places are tended for that reason alone. Once a week, someone cuts the growth away from an entrance, checks the woven ceiling, and carries down a small pod of air.

The town has people who are good at holding breath, but it does not especially admire them unless they are also sensible. A person who can reach a difficult root and return with it is useful. A person who must be fetched from the same place is remembered with irritation for years.

The air carriers tend to be lean, with strong backs and scars where harnesses rub beneath their arms. They work in pairs. Near the surface, where the hanging forest thickens, they pass pods through gaps between trunks and roots, testing the plugs by touch. A badly sealed pod becomes light too early. It pulls upward against its ballast, wasting its burden in a trail of little silver beads.

People below can recognize a leaking pod at a considerable distance. They watch it with the peculiar distress of seeing food spilled where it cannot be gathered.

Carriers know which households need more air than others. A sleeping infant, an illness, visiting relatives, a gathering after a death: all change the amount required. They also know which roofs leak. Sometimes, after emptying a pod, a carrier stays to press fresh wax into a seam while the household continues talking beneath them.

They hear a great deal and are expected to remember very little.

Most people work in the flooded rooms. Weavers sit with one foot hooked beneath a floor loop, drawing living stems through frames. Stone cutters brace their shoulders against posts and shave thin plates from the firmer seams. Their work produces a pale dust that settles on eyelashes, in leaf folds, along the backs of hands. They stop often to breathe. A difficult cut may occupy three or four visits to the bench.

There are people whose work is chiefly waiting: waiting for a graft to take, for a basket of young stone to stiffen, for a swollen root to soften in an oven. They do not think of themselves as idle. While waiting they mend retaining lattices, pick snails from the tender fans, and untangle the little disputes that arise when a growing wall crosses somebody else’s passage.

A good neighbor asks before cutting.

A bad neighbor says that the passage was already narrow.

Much of their clothing is close-fitting, woven from fibers that do not swell excessively. Loose sleeves catch on things. Long hems collect sediment. For celebrations they wear broader pieces inside the air chambers, fastening them at the shoulder after they climb onto the suspended mats. These garments hang differently when dry, and people who have worn the same festival wrap for years may still smooth it with surprise.

They ornament themselves with polished stones, small shells, and knots worked so tightly that they seem carved. A common gift between lovers is a hand loop made to the exact size of the other’s palm. It can be used anywhere. Its intimacy is almost invisible.

Households are not always families. A large upper chamber may belong to several adults who share its repair and its air. Children move between such houses with an ease that sometimes troubles their parents. They know where an old woman keeps sweet stem paste, where a patient weaver will let them sit at the bench, where two brothers quarrel loudly enough that visitors can eat unnoticed.

Meals are eaten with the knees or lower legs still in water. Covered baskets are lifted into the chamber and drained. People pass portions on shallow leaves, taking care not to tilt them. Food dropped through the surface is fetched if it can be fetched quickly. Otherwise a child is sent after it, and told afterward that this was work, not a game.

Conversation grows louder as the meal goes on. There is teasing, news of a new ridge, complaints about a roof that must be cut back yet again. Someone tells a story whose most important event occurred two generations earlier at a corner that no longer exists. The teller has to explain where the corner was. Three listeners disagree.

They remember the town partly through touch.

An elder can identify a house by a worn place on its doorway, even after the walls have been regrown. There are ropes with sections spliced in by people long dead. The wooden rings at crossings are taken along when a path is moved, and set at the nearest equivalent place. This causes arguments. The nearest place is not necessarily the right place.

Some inhabitants have never gone to the surface. They speak of it without much curiosity, as one might speak of a workshop devoted to an unfamiliar trade. Others go whenever they can. Young carriers sometimes linger among the floating trunks after their pods are filled, with their faces above water and their bodies below. They return describing light that hurts, wind touching only one side of the head, distances with nothing suspended in them.

These accounts are listened to attentively. They rarely persuade anyone to accompany the next trip.

Old age often makes travel difficult. The shoulders stiffen; the breath shortens; a familiar route acquires an inconvenient middle distance. Small air shelters are added beside the lanes, little woven hoods beneath which one or two people can raise their heads. Nobody calls them shelters for the old. They are useful to everyone carrying a heavy basket.

The oldest inhabitants favor the unfinished paths because there is less to obstruct the view. They sit in slings fastened between posts, close enough to an air hood to use it without assistance. Their hands remain on the guide ropes. They can feel a visitor approach before turning to look.

They watch the mountain with practical attention. One presses a heel against a swelling and decides it is still too soft. Another notices that a seam has darkened since yesterday. Someone who once built houses may spend a whole afternoon examining a place where a house might eventually stand.

They are not always right. A promising ledge can remain tender for decades.

Still, they arrange for baskets and roots to be brought.

Among them is usually someone who has stopped speaking much. This person may watch a single pale patch for weeks, leaving only to eat and sleep. Children visiting the open paths find the silence awkward at first. Then they begin to look too.

Eventually one notices something: a hollow has grown shallower, or a small ridge now hides the end of a cord that was visible the day before.

The old person puts a hand beside the child’s hand on the rope.

They stay there a little longer.
