The largest of the armored animals is not quite symmetrical.

Its left flank carries seven outlets; its right carries five, one of them sealed by a thick puckering of scar tissue. The plates above that scar have grown together. Under ordinary daylight they look like the rest of its back, but when a wave brightens the hollow, a faint brown line shows where the old edges meet.

Something once bit it there, or a piece of mountain fell against it. The injury is old enough that smaller animals have settled in the sheltered depression. A pale fan opens beneath the scar whenever the neighboring outlet discharges. Behind the fan, a worm with a hard, square head has made a tube along the seam.

The great animal carries these occupants without attending to them. It cannot see its own back. Its eyes, if they are eyes, lie beneath the forward edge of its armor: two cloudy strips interrupted by dark pinholes. They distinguish open water from stone, movement from stillness. For the placement of its feet, it has better senses.

Each foot is soft at the center and armored around the rim. When it rests on the mountain, the soft part spreads into every shallow irregularity. Thousands of short filaments emerge, taste the rock, and withdraw. Some return carrying mineral grains. Others remain pressed flat, receiving the tremors of the hollow.

The animal knows a crack before the crack becomes visible.

For three days, its rearmost foot has been drawn slightly inward. The others bear more weight now. Beneath the abandoned patch of stone, a fracture is lengthening.

None of this requires haste. Haste is reserved for its interior.

There, the ivory discs turn in a fluid the color of diluted milk. Their surfaces are not smooth. Each bears a deep spiral groove, and along the groove tiny dark organs swell and flatten as the disc rotates. The tendons passing over them are hollow. They carry different fluids in opposite directions, kept apart by membranes thinner than fish skin.

The toothed rings slip occasionally. When they do, the whole animal gives a short, low shudder. Its feeding limbs unfold beneath its head, sweep the stone, and fold again.

The little shells nearest it close at once.

Then the next discharge comes, richer than usual, and they reopen.

They are not all alike. Those closest to the outlets have thick shells and short feeding fans, suited to the force of the jets. Farther away, their shells become flatter and their hairs longer. At the edge of the current are creatures so thin that daylight passes through them, revealing a dark loop of gut and a pair of contracting hearts.

Their young settle where they can. Most find the wrong place.

A thick-shelled youngster beyond the reach of a jet may starve with its fan fully extended. A delicate one near an outlet may be peeled off the stone in its first afternoon. The vacant impressions left by such deaths are small, briefly clean, and useful to something else.

The transparent fish wait above them.

Seen from the side, each fish is scarcely more than an eye, a spine, and a silver pouch. Seen head-on, it has a broad mouth bordered by four clear flaps. The facing rows are feeding positions. Water leaving the central animals moves outward, meets the cooler water descending along the walls, and curls back upon itself. Each fish occupies one narrow return stream.

A fish that drifts too far forward is corrected by its neighbors. There is a flash of jaws, no more than a nip, and it backs into place.

At intervals they all turn.

The turn begins near the split wall and passes inward through the school, row after row. For a moment their bodies catch the light and become visible as a crowd. Then they face the other way and almost vanish.

Among them swims a much smaller shape with six beating paddles.

It is a young armored animal.

Its back is still clear and soft. The mechanisms beneath it can be seen whole: two turning discs, a short belt of tendon, a chamber filling and emptying. The rings have not developed teeth yet. Their edges grip by friction, and sometimes one stops while the other continues. Then the little animal sinks crookedly until the stalled ring takes hold again.

It has been searching the hollow since morning.

At every warm seam it lowers itself and spreads its feet. At every seam, something is already living there. The shell animals offer no resistance, but beneath them the rock may be exhausted, or the seepage too weak, or an adult’s feeding limbs may reach farther than they appear to.

Once it settles against the foot of the scarred animal.

For several breaths, nothing happens. The youngster presses its underside to the stone. Its little internal chamber darkens as the seepage enters.

Then a jointed limb slides out from beneath the adult’s head.

The limb does not seize it. It simply passes across the rock, lifting shells, dust, and youngster together. The small animal tumbles into clear water, rights itself, and resumes its search.

There is no room there.

Under the overhang, the cage-shaped predator closes around a fish.

Its ribs meet with such precision that no part of the prey is pinched. The fish continues swimming inside the enclosure, making shorter and shorter turns. Fine threads emerge from the clear muscle and lie against its sides.

The threads are almost invisible until they fill.

Then the predator wears a delicate branching darkness. It hangs motionless while the fish within it becomes paler. Beneath one rib are three little cages, each no larger than a fingertip, attached by soft stalks. One is already opening and closing its curved struts.

The predator’s patience belongs partly to its young. Anything that blunders against the overhang is felt by all four bodies.

Along the walls, the thousands of limestone jaws close when the light moves over them, then open again. They have no heads behind their mouths. Each mouth leads into a narrow sleeve of flesh within the mineral bead, and the sleeve branches inside the crack. Several neighboring jaws may belong to one animal.

Their teeth are yellow at the roots and clear at the tips. A fish clips off an entire mouth. The severed lips drift briefly before a shell fan catches them.

In the bead left behind, the sleeve contracts.

By evening, a new rim has begun to push out.

The silver creatures in the fissure are more restless now. Their red organs pulse from the deepest visible part of each body toward its blunt mouth. When a strong vibration enters the wall, the pulse runs the other way.

They withdraw.

A dusting of pale grains falls from beneath the suspended slab.

The central animals continue pumping. One settles into the beat of another; a third approaches it, falls away, and approaches again. The young swimmer, descending toward the dark slot, stops beating its paddles.

For a moment it hangs level.

Then every silver body disappears into the fissure.

The slab drops less than the width of a finger.

The sound is enormous.

Shells strike one another. The fish break their rows. One of the hanging sacs tears from its stalk and rolls across the floor, contracting around the wound. A cloud of white fragments fills the slot.

The slab holds in its new position.

Long before the water clears, the armored adults have resumed their separate rhythms. The scarred one shifts its forward foot. Its weight passes across the rock with a series of small knocks.

The youngster has been thrown against the lower wall.

It clings there with all six feet. One paddle is torn. Beneath its soft armor, a disc turns erratically, catches, turns again.

Beside it, the movement of the slab has uncovered a fresh seam.

Warm water threads out through black stone that has never held a shell. The youngster draws itself over the opening. Its feet flatten. Its feeding limbs, still short and nearly transparent, feel along the edges.

The dark chamber inside it fills.

For a while its paddles continue making swimming movements, although it is no longer swimming. Then they stop. The disc steadies. A narrow jet emerges from its flank.

Downstream, a shell animal opens its fan.

By nightfall, three transparent fish are holding position above the new outlet. The youngster remains fastened to the seam, its clear back faintly green in the light from under the ledge. Each turn of its discs sends a small tremor into the stone.
