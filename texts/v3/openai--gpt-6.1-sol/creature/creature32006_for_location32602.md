The largest animal has a head, but it is not at the end you first notice.

That end is a thickened limb, flattened by long use into something like a sill. Its upper surface bears shallow channels where smaller animals habitually cross. The channels divide around a raised plate, rejoin, and continue into the refuse bank. Some of the travelers are older than the channels appear to be. They pause at the same junction each time, touching the plate with one foreleg before proceeding.

The head lies farther in, beneath the fallen corner of the slab roof.

It is small in proportion to the body and covered by overlapping shields. Between two of these, a pair of feelers can emerge. Each is thin at its base, thicker toward the tip, and divided into many soft lobes. They do not sweep the air. They lay themselves on nearby surfaces, one lobe after another, until they conform to the stone or shell beneath them. The animal can examine a thing without moving it.

Usually the feelers remain folded.

The roof presses upon the head. Perhaps it fell while the animal was smaller. Perhaps the animal entered afterward and chose the pressure. There is enough clearance for it to withdraw, although withdrawing would require a considerable disturbance of the bodies lodged around it. It has not done so in a very long time.

Along its sides are legs.

There are more than you would expect, and they are not all alike. Some end in pads broad enough to bear its weight. Some terminate in a curved nail. A few, sheltered well beneath the body, are slender and pale, with fine combs on their inner edges. These move even when everything else is still. They sort the material under the animal, drawing certain fragments inward and pushing others out.

At intervals a pellet appears beneath its flank. It is pale, dense, and warm. Small inhabitants come to it before it has cooled. They rasp its surface until the pellet loses its shape, and then there is only another patch of occupied floor.

The animal’s armor is not a single continuous shell. Its plates overlap deeply, with room beneath each edge for a little darkness. The older plates are worn almost smooth except where mineral has accumulated. Those nearest the head are smaller and less weathered. At one joint, a thin crescent of new surface shows beneath the old: the color of ash seen through horn.

It still grows.

Growth here is difficult to separate from repair. A damaged margin thickens. A pit becomes deeper as its rim rises around the tenant inside it. A limb that has remained in one position for years develops a bearing surface suited to that position. The largest animal has acquired some of the hollow’s shape, and the hollow has acquired some of its shape in return.

Under its outermost plates live narrow, nearly transparent creatures. Their guts show as dark threads. They feed along the places where old armor is loosening, removing material too delicate for the larger mouthparts to reach. When the great body shifts, they retreat beneath the next plate. A few are crushed. The others return to those places first.

There are animals that spend their whole lives on this one animal.

One kind has short rear legs and enormously long front ones. It stands over a seam with its body held clear, drawing a hooked foot repeatedly through the opening. Whatever it retrieves is passed to a mouth too small to distinguish. Another kind adheres by a broad, soft underside and moves so slowly that its passage can only be recognized by the clean trail behind it. Its young ride along its margin until there is no room for them. Then they step onto the larger body and begin their own trails.

The great animal tolerates most of this. Occasionally one of its combed legs reaches up and clears a joint. The removed creatures are held for a moment, several together, in the fine teeth.

Then the leg folds underneath.

Its feeding seam is longer than it looks. The visible section continues around the buried side, where the plates turn inward and become soft. When it opens, the body lifts very slightly. That is what makes the bank subside: space appears beneath something that had been supported.

The first material to fall is mostly empty armor.

Then the deeper inhabitants come loose. Pale burrowers uncoil in the falling debris. Some are thread-thin, some thick as fingers, all moving with the abruptness of creatures ordinarily pressed on every side. For a moment they have room to bend in directions they have seldom used. The feeding edges touch them and draw them out of sight.

Afterward, the great animal lowers itself.

Near the visible mouth, one little grazer is often left stranded on a newly cleared patch. It stands with its comb raised, its passengers crowded against the highest part of its back. Presently it puts its mouth down and begins feeding. Others arrive. By the time the seam has disappeared under dust, there is no obvious place where the loss occurred.

The folded animals in the amber capsules resemble the largest one, but not closely.

They have its many-jointed legs and the same small armored head. Their bodies are narrow. Their plates are cleanly divided, without pits or salt crust. Newly emerged, they can move through a crack scarcely wider than a knife blade. They are among the creatures that sometimes unfold far enough to bridge the height of the chamber.

For their first hours they remain beside the opened capsule. Each foot grips stone. Their surfaces darken unevenly, beginning at the joints. The feelers are the last parts to harden; until then, they remain tucked beneath the head.

Some never leave that position.

A grazer finds a soft edge. A passing foot dislodges their grip. Or the gas rises higher than usual, and they release themselves too late. Their empty capsules remain overhead, indistinguishable at a distance from those of the animals that survived.

The survivors descend into the crowded wall. For years they may occupy gaps too small to reveal more than a leg or the edge of a shield. They feed on what passes through. A narrow body lengthens behind the stone. Eventually its owner must leave, or enlarge its opening, or become fixed there.

Along the wall are several rounded projections with feelers.

One has recently broken away from its surroundings. Beneath it lies a scattering of exposed stone and torn mineral crust. The animal stands above this on legs that have not borne its full weight before. They tremble, subside, straighten again. Its back is already carrying passengers.

It takes most of a night to reach the next crevice.

The oldest inhabitant does not watch it. No eyes are visible on the small head beneath the slabs, though light falling through the roof fracture causes a slow tightening along the body. The feelers withdraw. The exposed legs draw nearer. Those sheltering in its pits are carried a little deeper into shadow.

Before a pulse of underground heat, a different movement passes through it.

The slender legs stop sorting. One broad foot presses harder against the floor. Another turns until its pad lies across a crack. Beneath the buried body, a space opens, and animals begin coming out of it: pale grazers, threadlike burrowers, a small dark creature carrying an unopened capsule between its forelegs.

They leave before the inhabitants around the vents have begun to climb.

The old animal waits until the last of them has passed. Then it draws its bridge-limb inward by scarcely the width of a foot. The movement uncovers a strip of the shelter’s entrance. Within are layers of shed armor, packed thin and flat, and beneath them a smooth stone floor.

Warm gas comes through the exposed joints.

The limb settles again, slightly higher than before. For a while the familiar crossing is interrupted. Travelers gather at either end, testing the changed slope. One finds purchase in a pit and climbs. Another follows its body rather than the surface beneath it.

By the time the mountain knocks, the crossing is in use again.
