The person with the cord has broad hands and very clean nails.

This is not unusual among the inhabitants. Dirt is scarce, and a dirty blade can spoil a graft that several households will depend on. Before work, they wash their hands in running water, then wipe them on a particular kind of leaf. You can recognize a gardener at a distance by the pale stains between the fingers.

Their feet are less carefully kept. The soles are thick, crossed with shallow cuts, and stained green at the edges. Indoors they go barefoot. Outdoors they wear shoes woven from fibers that tighten when wet. A shoe must allow its wearer to feel the path without allowing the path to grow into it.

They are not especially tall. On the narrow older routes, height is an inconvenience. Children learn to duck before they learn which branches require it, and adults retain a slight forward inclination even in chambers where the ceiling is beyond sight.

Seen together, they look like people accustomed to listening to someone standing very close.

***

Their clothes have no loose hems.

Sleeves close at the wrist; jackets are fastened down both sides; hair is braided against the head or cut short. A trailing thread can catch on a feeding animal, a closing leaf, or a shoot that was not there yesterday. The clothing is soft and repeatedly mended. Patches often outlast the garment around them, so that a person may wear a square of cloth once worn by a great-grandparent without considering the jacket particularly old.

They carry shell knives in rigid cases. The cases are sometimes beautiful, but the knives are not. A good working edge becomes smaller with use. An inherited knife may be no longer than a thumbnail.

Most adults also carry a short length of cord.

It is useful for fastening a sleeve, securing a bundle, marking a doubtful turn. On an unfamiliar stair, one end can be tied to the rail and the other kept in the hand. The inhabitants do not ordinarily tether themselves to bridges. A bridge is alive, and may pull.

Children are tethered to adults until they can be trusted not to follow drops of water with their eyes.

***

Their faces do not have the uniform stillness you might expect.

At home they are quick to smile, easily bored, impatient with children who refuse to sleep. They argue over food, lovers, borrowed tools, and whose turn it is to clean the cistern. Someone who has spent a morning patiently directing a shoot toward a distant companion can become furious when a sibling takes the warm side of the cooking stem.

The patience belongs to the work. They have learned ways of continuing it when they themselves are impatient.

A binding intended to last three generations has three knots. Each is tied differently. The first can be opened without disturbing the second; the second conceals a small folded instruction; the third is not meant to be touched until the two growing surfaces meet.

An angry person can finish the first part and go indoors.

This arrangement has saved many bridges.

***

A household usually contains more people than its rooms suggest.

There are children, parents, old people, visitors whose journeys have been interrupted, and sometimes someone who has arrived from a settlement that no longer agrees about when they left. Sleeping hollows are shared. Privacy is made with shutters, turned shoulders, and the convention that a person combing their hair does not have to answer questions.

The old are useful without always being agreeable.

They remember how a branch sounded before it began to bend. They know whether the pale seam behind a bed has widened gradually or appeared since breakfast. They can identify a household’s former watercourse from a scar everyone else has mistaken for ordinary bark.

But memory alone is not accepted as proof.

Instructions are cut into thin plates of shell and hung where the work will occur. The letters are large. They describe what should be present when the instruction is followed.

*When the lower stem has divided into three, open the second binding.*

*If the hollow smells sweet, do not sleep here.*

*The other end belongs to the household beyond the rain. Ask before cutting.*

Names appear less often than conditions. A descendant may disagree with an ancestor. A divided stem is harder to dispute.

***

They have no single profession corresponding to a builder.

One person is good at reading tension in bark. Another can keep an exposed wound from taking rot. Another knows how to encourage a hollow without weakening its outer wall. Some people walk for weeks to attend a graft, bringing a tool that looks like a spoon and leaving again before the surfaces have visibly joined.

The person with the cord is a tip-tender.

Their work is small and frequent. They move shoots by the width of a finger, remove a competing bud, turn a leaf so that it shades one side of a growing stem. They can see a deviation that would be invisible to you: not where the shoot points, but where it will begin to point after the next swelling.

They do not claim to know precisely where it will be in seventy years.

The corresponding shoot across the gap has another tender. The two have never met. Their grandparents exchanged measurements, and their children may one day share a meal on the finished crossing.

In the meantime they occasionally send one another complaints.

These are carried by travelers on the long route around.

***

Not everyone wants to inherit work.

Some leave as soon as they are able to travel alone. They join distant households, tend unfamiliar pools, or follow the flowering along the remote spans. A few become messengers because the occupation provides an acceptable reason never to stay anywhere very long.

There is disappointment when someone leaves, but little accusation. Unwilling hands make poor bindings.

An unfinished undertaking may be given to another household. The transfer is witnessed beside the living thing, and all concerned examine it. Obligations are not accepted from a description.

Courtship sometimes involves walking these prospective responsibilities together.

A person may like someone very much and decide that they cannot live beside that person’s widening hollow. Another may find an awkward, unpromising partner unexpectedly attractive while watching them clean a graft.

The usual gifts are edible, portable, or useful immediately. Giving a young person a seedling that will need centuries of care is considered presumptuous.

***

They eat the grazers, but not the ones beneath their own paths.

Those animals keep the walking surfaces healthy by removing softened fibers before decay reaches deeper tissue. Households recognize particular individuals by the shapes of their backs. Children give them names and feed them scraps, which adults discourage because an overfed grazer becomes selective.

Food animals are raised on hanging strips grown for the purpose. When a strip is mature, it is folded inward and harvested. The flesh is mild, the skin tough, and the small gripping organs are dried into a salty food eaten on journeys.

The jointed swimmers from the pools are caught during their shedding season. They are cooked quickly in leaf pockets and eaten before their new shells harden.

There are fruits, though few grow conveniently. Some swell inside closed folds and must be found by touch. Others hang beyond the paths, requiring a hooked basket and someone with a steady sense of nearby distance.

Meals are usually warm, wet, and quiet. Crisply fried food is something travelers describe from elsewhere, with varying degrees of credibility.

***

The narrow-winged animals are called by words that mean different things in different settlements.

One name refers to the dry brushing sound they make. Another means a bundle that has come loose. In the oldest interior galleries, their name is also used for an unexpected visitor who eats and departs without sitting down.

They are larger than they seem while crossing the open chambers. Near a railing, one may have a body as long as a person and wings extending well beyond both ends. The filaments trailing behind it contain eggs, parasites, scraps of bark, and smaller animals that have never touched a fixed surface.

The inhabitants do not tame them.

They sometimes cultivate landing places: warm, rough pads on which the animals can grip while feeding. These are kept away from doors. A landing animal can shed enough passengers to trouble a household for months.

Children gather the discarded filaments and stretch them between their fingers. For a little while the fibers resist falling in a way ordinary cord does not.

They are never used in bindings.

***

The leaf-fold predators are dealt with individually.

A household may live beneath one for centuries. They know which approaches it watches and which it ignores. They remove scraps from the path, avoid leaning into its fold, and teach visitors where not to put a hand.

A new arrival is more dangerous.

It has not learned the movements of people; the people have not learned its reach. A route may close for a season while the animal settles. Someone will eventually test it with a bundle of warm fibers pulled on a long cord.

Sometimes the cord comes back cleanly cut.

Sometimes the bundle returns holding something that was not attached to it when it went out.

The route remains closed.

***

Their dead are placed in narrow hollows opened in old, healthy stems.

The hollow is lined, the body washed, and the opening bound shut. For a while the place is visited. Relatives bring water and inspect the edges for infection. Later, when the seam has closed, they come less often.

The stem may grow around the hollow, leaving it intact for a long time. Or the tissues may gradually enter it. There are beliefs about both outcomes, but no method of choosing between them.

A name is usually placed nearby rather than over the burial itself. This allows the name to be moved when the route changes.

They do not put their dead into unfinished bridges. It would make later decisions too difficult.

***

Some inhabitants are recognized only by the work they continue to do.

A shutter is repaired in a dwelling that has stood empty for years. A neglected water leaf is turned before a dry season. Someone places fresh binding around a shoot whose last recorded tender died without children.

The neighbors do not immediately call this haunting.

They check for a visitor. They ask whether a distant household has assumed the responsibility. They examine the binding to see whether it is genuinely new.

If no answer can be found, they leave a small portion of food where it will stay warm.

It is sometimes taken.

There is no agreement about what takes it, and people dislike being pressed for one. Whatever tends the shoot has not asked to be described.

***

Later, you see the tip-tender inside their home.

They are sitting beside a shallow basin while a child unwinds the remaining cord. The child has made a tangle and is trying to hide it under one knee. An older person is testing a cooked fruit with the edge of a spoon. Through the pale wall, a translucent animal passes slowly, pauses beside the warmth, and continues.

The tip-tender opens one hand.

There is a small cut across the palm. They wash it, hold the edges together, and wait for the bleeding to stop. The child asks whether the bridge will be finished when they are old.

“Perhaps,” the tender says.

The child asks whether they will have to work on it.

“Someone will.”

This is apparently not the answer the child wanted. They return to the cord and pull the tangle tighter.

Outside, the binding holds. The shoot has not yet begun to change its growth. That will take several days.
