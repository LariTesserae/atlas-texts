What lives here has almost no upper side.

There is a back, certainly: a low oval of grey skin, interrupted by pale flecks and short, irregular ridges. But it is less a surface turned toward the sky than a cover over the important part of the animal. Its organs lie close to the ground. Its mouth, its senses, the broad wet pads of its feet—all are directed downward, toward the narrow interval in which warm stone and moving brine meet.

Seen from above, one resembles a small collapsed pouch. Seen at ground level, it has a definite bearing. There is a front, slightly narrower than the rear, and under that front a lip the color of unglazed clay. The lip rests on the pale crust.

It does not scrape.

Along its lower edge are several hundred minute folds. Each fold closes on a quantity of crust too small to be seen, softens it with a clear secretion, and draws it inward. The folds work out of sequence, so the mouth as a whole never appears to move. A grain of white material can remain beside it for hours. What the animal takes is underneath the grain.

Its feet are stranger than its mouth.

There are six, though usually only four can be counted. The middle pair tuck into recesses beneath the body. Each foot spreads into a thick, nearly circular pad whose margin is thin enough to follow the smallest inequalities of stone. The upper surface is grey. The underside is black, glossy, and divided into close-set compartments.

A foot lifted from the ground leaves no suction mark. It leaves a dark print of moisture, and within the print a fine pattern like the end of a bundle of wires.

The animal cannot attach to a dry surface. It can cross one, slowly, but its feet narrow as it does so, the margins curling inward to preserve what little water they carry. Crossing even a few inches of exposed limestone is an undertaking. It stops repeatedly, not from exhaustion, but to place its next foot where a shallow groove retains an invisible trace of damp.

There are paths through the basin that no eye looking down from standing height can see.

The creatures know them.

***

They have eyes, but those are easy to mistake for chips of dark mineral.

Two lie far apart beneath the front rim of the back. They do not project. A translucent membrane covers each, filmed with the same bitter moisture that wets the feet. Through it, the eye can distinguish an approaching shadow, an open stretch of hot stone, perhaps the pale interruption of another animal.

Most of what they know comes through their soles.

The basin’s roar is not loud to them in the way it is loud to a visitor. Their bodies do not enclose chambers in which it can boom. They are compact, densely wet, reinforced by plates so thin and numerous that no single plate has much room to shake.

Instead, they receive the ground’s vibration as differences between one foot and another.

A channel narrowing beneath the surface registers first in the forward left pad. A wet seam behind them carries a finer tremor. A shift in pressure several yards away reaches all six feet, but not at quite the same moment.

For these animals, the basin is full of bearings.

The apparently featureless gravel contains routes, feeding places, impassable distances. A broad black shelf that seems identical to the next has a particular vibration along its underside. A pool has a margin they can recognize without touching the water. They return to a hollow not because it looks familiar, but because, with their feet in its six small depressions, the differences resolve into a known arrangement.

When one rests, it is still attending to where it is.

You can discover this by placing your palm on the stone a short distance away. Nothing happens at first. Then one of the tucked middle feet unfolds and settles somewhere else.

That is the entire response.

You have changed the load on the rock. It has adjusted its contact.

***

The white crust beneath them is alive, although much of any visible patch is mineral.

Its living portion occupies the boundary: a thickness less than that of a fingernail, threaded through the pale deposit. It draws on substances brought up in the brine and on substances altered along the warm exposed stone. As it grows, it leaves the hard white material behind.

The animals eat the active edge.

They also keep that edge available. Their saliva loosens deposits that would otherwise seal it away. Their feet spread minute quantities of brine over the crust. Beneath a resting body, the moisture loses less heat and less water than it would in the open.

A feeding place can therefore last as long as its occupant tends it.

Look closely at one of the mineral shelves. The pale crust does not form a continuous band. It thickens in low ovals, each with six darker interruptions and a faint scalloping at one end. Some ovals hold animals. Others are empty.

An empty oval can remain empty for a very long time.

Eventually a creature working its way along the seam may reach it, put down all six feet, and stop. Over the following days, its broad underside settles into the slight irregularities left by the former occupant. The mouth finds the scalloped edge.

Nothing has to be cleared away.

***

They are not solitary, though they rarely touch.

Two animals on the same shelf will usually face different directions. Their bodies are separated by several inches, sometimes more. Beneath them, the wet seam is continuous.

If one begins to move, the other adjusts a foot.

If one feeds more deeply, drawing its lip farther under the crust, the other may loosen its grip for a moment. These responses are small enough to look incidental. Watch long enough and they recur: a release answered by a release, a change of posture followed by a settling elsewhere along the shelf.

Their contact passes through stone.

In some places, the black mineral layers conduct a second kind of signal. The pads of the feet maintain slight electrical differences across their wet compartments. An animal changing the pattern of those differences can make itself detectable along the seam.

The signal is weak and local. It cannot contend with the whole basin. It travels only where the moisture is continuous, and is lost at a dry interruption barely wider than a finger.

A creature may spend its life a few inches from another and never exchange such a signal with it. Two farther apart may be linked beneath a shelf by a film neither could cross.

***

Their young are not grey.

At first they are translucent, with a cloudy white mass in the center and six blunt projections pressed against the stone. They develop underneath an adult, in a shallow recess between its body plates. There may be only one.

The adult lifts itself sufficiently to expose the young animal to the crust. The little mouth begins to work while the little feet are still held inside the shelter of the larger body.

For a long while, the parent feeds around it.

As the young animal’s plates thicken, its back takes on color from the minerals passing through its food. Iron darkens it. Pale salts accumulate in the upper skin. Its mottling comes to resemble the particular shelf where it was raised.

It leaves when its feet can hold a position independently.

Departure is a matter of fractions of an inch. First the front pads extend beyond the adult’s margin. Later the body follows. For some time, parent and offspring occupy one white oval with an awkward extra set of dark footmarks at its edge.

Eventually there are two feeding places.

There is no abundance to support a brood, and no empty season waiting to be filled. A young animal must fit into the narrow productive margin without covering too much of it. Many never leave the underside where they began.

***

An old animal becomes difficult to distinguish from its dwelling.

Mineral collects in the creases of its back. The outer skin stiffens. Its feet remain supple much longer than the rest of it, but their range of movement diminishes, and the animal stops making even the short journeys between favorable patches.

It continues feeding in the same position.

Afterward, the active crust advances under the lip and into the spaces around the feet. The softer tissues disappear slowly. The plates remain, lodged in the shallow hollow that supported them.

There are shelves in the basin whose rounded edges bear a row of low grey shapes. Some are animals. Some are the mineral-coated plates of animals. Some are impressions filled and smoothed by later growth.

The inhabited ones can be found by the moisture beneath their feet.

Even then, you may have to wait.

Crouch at one of those shelves, with your hand resting lightly beside it, and the nearest creature may alter its stance. One black pad draws in; another spreads. The front of the body lowers until the lip is fully against the white crust.

From your height, the place offers heat, glare, and an almost unbearable noise. Under that grey back there is shelter, a meal, and a small, legible stretch of ground.

The animal settles more firmly onto it.
