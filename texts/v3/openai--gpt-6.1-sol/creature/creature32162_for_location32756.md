At first, the inhabitants show themselves as disturbances.

A line opens in duckweed. Something heavy brushes the underside of a floating root mat, lifting it just enough to spill water from its edge. Beneath a willow, a branch that was lying still begins to move against the current. It advances in short, awkward stages, catches, turns, and slips free. Only when it reaches open water can you see the head beside it.

The beaver keeps most of itself submerged. Two dark eyes, small ears, and the blunt end of its nose travel at the surface. Wet fur lies sleek against its skull. There is little in this view to suggest the breadth of the animal below: the thick shoulders, the heavy belly, the hind feet opening and closing in the dark.

It brings the branch to a bank and climbs out.

On land, its proportions become almost surprising. It is low but substantial, round-backed, with forelegs that seem too short for the body they carry. Its wet coat separates into points along its flanks. Water runs from its belly. The tail comes last, broad and dark, dragging briefly over the mud before the animal settles beside its food.

It holds the willow in its forepaws.

These are much smaller than the hind feet, dexterous enough to turn a stem while the teeth work along it. The beaver does not simply bite and swallow. It braces, strips, shifts its grip. A curl of bark comes away. The branch rotates. Its orange incisors show for an instant, then disappear behind the lips.

Around its feet lie pale rods from previous meals.

Here, an animal may leave more evidence by eating than by walking. The cut faces of stems remain bright for days. Thin branches accumulate at feeding places, while larger ones pass into the banks and lodges. A piece of willow can be food at one end and building material at the other.

***

They live in families, though the settlement is too extensive to belong to one household.

Between one group of lodges and the next are quiet boundaries: a muddy mound carrying scent, a narrow reach where an animal pauses before going farther, a feeding bank that one family uses and another leaves alone. The connected water makes the whole hollow look communal. Its inhabitants know distinctions that are difficult to see from shore.

Within a family, bodies are familiar by smell and touch. A returning adult passes close to another at a lodge entrance. Whiskers meet. A younger animal follows so closely that its nose nearly touches the older one’s tail.

Some are still small enough to look buoyant even when they are ashore. Their fur gives them a soft, full outline; their movements are quicker and less economical. They investigate loose sticks, tug at roots that will not come free, climb onto surfaces that tilt under them. When startled, they hurry for water, where their awkwardness largely disappears.

The older young can travel the channels and carry branches, but they still spend much of their time near the lodges. One sits beside an adult on a feeding ledge, chewing a stem scarcely thicker than a reed while the adult works through a willow limb. Their jaws move at different speeds. Occasionally the smaller one stops and reaches toward what the other is holding.

Inside a lodge, the living space is less spacious than the mound suggests. Most of that visible bulk is wall and roof: interlocked wood, packed mud, wet vegetation, successive repairs. The chamber lies above the water, with bedding under the bodies that rest there. Its passages descend to submerged entrances.

From outside, you might hear nothing. Within are the sounds of animals close together: breathing, a shift of weight, claws against wood, the small voices of young. The walls keep out the wind. The water below remains near, audible when something enters.

***

Their lives are governed by levels.

A few inches of falling water may expose an entrance or turn a useful canal into a shallow drag. A rise may bring food within reach, while also pressing against a weak bank. They meet these changes with the materials available to them: mud lifted and pushed, stems drawn across a gap, a branch wedged among branches already there.

Much of the work takes place at the same troublesome points.

Near one dam, water pours through a notch beside a buried trunk. A beaver arrives with a leafy branch and noses it into the opening. The current bends the leaves downstream. The animal climbs half onto the bank, presses with its forefeet, and goes back for mud.

Its repair is rough. Water continues to escape around it. Yet the flow becomes thinner, dividing into several smaller threads. Later, another branch catches there. Silt gathers against the leaves.

By morning, the notch is harder to find.

Elsewhere, a newly placed stick lies unused. Another has been carried partway up a bank and abandoned, perhaps because it caught in the roots or because the animal went elsewhere to feed. Their works contain such interruptions. There are loose ends everywhere, some taken up again, some incorporated by accident as other material settles over them.

The beavers keep returning to a landscape that never stays quite as they left it.

***

On cold mornings, they remain in the water longer than a person would willingly stand beside it.

Beneath the wet outer coat, dense fur holds insulation close to the skin. They tend it carefully. After feeding, a beaver may settle on its haunches and comb through the coat, working over its belly and sides with its paws. A hind foot reaches forward to scratch and groom. The broad tail lies against the bank.

It is an unhurried occupation. The animal bends until its head nearly vanishes into its own fur, straightens, then begins on another patch.

Nearby, water freezes around stems in an unused pool. In the travelled channel there is still movement. A dark back passes under an overhanging branch; ripples reach the thin ice and make its edge tremble.

Their routes are protected by habit. They know where the channel deepens, where a root projects, where a floating mass will take their weight. On the margins, that knowledge has to be renewed. Yesterday’s landing place may have slipped. A feeding path may now end at a steep face of clay.

When a slab of turf falls, the first approach is cautious. A head remains in the water below it. The animal noses along the submerged edge, climbs a little, backs down. Later there are footprints on top.

***

Other lives fit into the spaces around theirs.

A water vole runs along a dam where sedge roots bind the mud. Its passage is nearly hidden, a small movement low in the leaves. It can cross places too slight to support a beaver, using the same wet structure at another scale.

Ducks feed in the spreading shallows. Where water has reached standing grass, they work among the submerged blades, drawing up food from ground that was recently dry. A lodge makes a convenient resting place until its occupants emerge beneath them.

Fish hold near the drowned roots. Larvae occupy still pockets behind sticks. Under loose bark, small creatures remain sheltered from the current. The barriers provide many kinds of water close together: shallow and sunlit, deep and shaded, nearly motionless, quietly flowing.

The heron uses these differences. It chooses a constriction where prey must pass within reach, then waits with its long toes set in mud. A beaver swimming by may cause it to lift its head. Once the wake has subsided, it resumes watching.

An otter is less easily ignored. Its arrival sends fish into cover and ducks toward open water. It slips along the canals with a flexibility the heavier builders lack, rolling through a turn, diving beneath a lodged branch. For a while the channels carry quick ripples from several directions.

Then it moves on.

***

Toward evening, the beavers become easier to see, although never all at once.

One leaves a lodge and vanishes beneath the bank. Another comes ashore farther away, already carrying food. A young animal swims in a short circle outside an entrance before following a route through the reeds. Their movements connect places that, in daylight, seemed separate.

An adult reaches the new fall of turf.

The flowering plant is still upright on its tilted patch of ground. Soil hangs from the exposed roots. Below it, a willow branch has lodged across the channel, its leaves trembling in the seepage.

The beaver pauses with its nose against the wood. It takes hold, pulls, and the branch comes loose with a small wash of clay. For a moment the animal has to turn sideways in the narrow water. Then it swims toward the lodges, towing the leafy end behind it.

On the fallen turf, water begins filling the impressions of its feet.
