The animals have faces, though you may spend a long time beside one before recognizing this.

What appears to be the flat side of the head is a pad of very fine plates. Each plate can tilt independently. When the animal presses its head to the stone, the plates settle into the smallest irregularities of the surface, and a pale line appears around the contact. It stays that way for minutes, sometimes hours.

Above the pad are two shallow pits. They do not reflect light. Dust entering them disappears, but they are not mouths. An animal can close them with a slow movement of the skin when a terrace begins to shed powder.

Its mouth is underneath, small and soft-edged. You see it only when the creature hangs above you. There are no teeth. It lays the mouth over the dark film at a warm seam and waits until the folded pockets open against it. Afterward, a little of the film is gone. A little remains on its lips.

The creatures are not equally gray. Some have a violet cast; others are almost white along their backs. Old ones carry dark lines in the folds of their joints. These lines open and close independently of the limbs.

A resting animal is therefore less still than it first appears.

Something is living in its creases.

***

There are many kinds of the dark film.

The one nearest the surface is brittle because it is exposed. Deeper in the seams, it becomes supple and hangs in overlapping sheets. Where a seam opens into a chamber, those sheets can extend across the walls, meeting behind pillars and passing through openings too narrow for an animal to enter.

They have no roots. Their attachment is distributed over the whole underside. To remove one would require separating it from the stone at every point at once.

In some rooms the film has lifted slightly away from its support. A very small distance—less than the thickness of a fingernail—but enough for warmth to pass beneath it. Then the entire wall moves with a slow, shallow undulation.

These are the richest places for the animals. They approach them with their limbs folded close, and feed only at the edges.

They also bring things.

Not objects. Nothing here has much use for an object that can fall.

An animal carries dust packed into the soft hollows behind its head. It carries fragments of film in its joints. It carries warmth for a little while after leaving a seam, and sometimes the warmth lasts much longer than its body should permit.

When it enters a cold chamber, it may remain against one wall until a new dark stain appears beneath its mouth.

It does not wait to see whether the stain grows.

***

The young are difficult to distinguish from defects in the walls.

At first they are pale, flattened things in the shallow niches, each with six grooves running outward from its center. There is usually a dark fringe around them. A young one may remain in this state through several meetings of the streets.

Its limbs become free one at a time.

The first limb explores the wall without helping the body move. The second holds the edge of the niche. For a long while the other four remain part of the smooth mineral surface, or look as though they do.

When the animal finally pulls itself loose, it leaves a hollow with no broken edges.

It is soft then. Its head pad has not divided into plates. It cannot cross an edge safely. It moves along the warm seam in short, awkward advances, dragging two limbs that have not yet learned how to bear it.

An older animal sometimes accompanies it.

There is no visible tenderness. The older one does not carry it or offer food. But when the young animal approaches a drop, the older one places a limb across its path. When a light region passes overhead, the older one shifts its body between the brightness and the young.

The dark fringe that surrounded the young is left in the niche. Much of it closes after the animal departs.

Some of the fringe moves after it.

***

Their private downward direction is not entirely private.

Near another animal, a creature’s body changes subtly. The limbs on one side shorten their reach. The head turns. Two animals passing on a narrow surface may both lean toward the same empty space, balanced against a direction that belongs to neither alone.

This is why they meet on broad floors.

Three can usually find a direction together. Four take longer. A fifth may wait beyond the court until one of the others has left.

During a meeting, the bodies remain still while the limbs occasionally adjust. Dust caught beneath them draws into tiny lines. The pale bands along the walls become more distinct.

Afterward, each animal leaves carrying something it did not bring.

You can see the effect when one reaches a familiar corner. It hesitates, tests an apparently empty place, and then takes a route it has never taken before. Sometimes it starts down a wall long before the terrace below has arrived.

The meeting has given it knowledge of a way.

Not necessarily a way that exists yet.

***

This is the beginning of what makes them inhabitants rather than merely creatures surviving in ruins.

They return.

A particular animal with a white scar across its head visits the same court whenever it can. Its body is missing the last joint of one limb. It has learned to brace that limb against a low ridge while the others rest.

There is no food in the court. The nearest warm seam lies several streets away.

Sometimes the court passes beyond reach for months. When it comes back, the animal is waiting along the approaching edge.

It crosses before the stone seems thick enough to carry it.

Other animals return to other places: a niche, the underside of a stair, a stretch of wall facing nothing but distance. They stand there in recognizable postures. One spreads its limbs very widely. Another folds all six beneath its body and rests directly on the floor.

Their attachments survive the rearrangement of the city.

Yet an animal may come to its place and find another already there.

Then both press their heads to the stone.

Sometimes one leaves.

Sometimes the visitor remains, and the returning animal settles beside it.

Sometimes they leave together, although neither takes the street by which it arrived.

***

There are inhabitants you cannot follow from place to place.

One first becomes apparent in a sequence of small accommodations.

A sheltered chamber stays warm after the seam outside has cooled. On the far side of a gulf, another chamber does the same. Between them, a narrow strip of film opens in a passage where no animal is feeding.

An animal crossing that passage stops and turns its head.

Below, on a terrace almost lost to distance, several others turn as well.

For a few moments these scattered places behave as parts of one attention.

Then the warmth passes, and they cease to agree.

Such an inhabitant has no continuous body. A wall may be part of it for an afternoon. A patch of film may belong to it for centuries. An animal can participate in it while feeding, leave it at the next turning, and enter it again much later without having returned to the same district.

There are several of these presences. Possibly many.

They differ in their habits.

One makes connections briefly and often. Wherever it is active, the animals hurry. New curbs form in slender loops, and chambers lose their walls before those walls have become thick.

Another keeps a few rooms warm for very long periods. Dust accumulates there despite the wind. Animals entering those rooms lower themselves close to the floor.

A third is known chiefly by delays. Streets almost join, remain almost joined, and move apart. Young animals in its niches take unusually long to come free.

None occupies every part of the city.

They encounter one another at the courts, where the animals place their heads against the floor.

Perhaps this is what the courts are for.

***

They can be injured.

A terrace separates too early. A warm seam fails to continue on the opposite wall. A chamber grows thin while something is still using it.

After such a loss, animals may spend days visiting an edge. They set their head pads against the last remaining stone, then move a short distance and try again.

The film nearby closes even while the seam is warm.

Elsewhere, far away, a room remains cold.

The damage does not stay where it happened.

Nor does repair always begin there. A niche thickens on another terrace. An animal chooses an unfamiliar street. Dust gathers in the air along a line that has no obvious connection to what was lost.

A bridge may eventually form at the damaged edge.

It will not be quite the old bridge. The animals approaching it take longer to cross. For years afterward, one of them may stop halfway and press its head to the new stone.

***

There are old inhabitants whose remaining parts are very far apart.

You might sit in one of their rooms without anything happening. The seam under your feet gives only enough warmth to make the cold bearable. No animal visits. The niches stay empty.

Then a low note comes from below.

The stone beneath you answers so faintly that you feel it through the bones of your wrists.

Across the gulf, an empty window brightens.

Farther down, beyond any ledge you can clearly see, something else answers.

For a little while the room is occupied.

It does not become less empty. Nothing takes shape beside you. But the warmth moves around the place where you are sitting, reaching the broad step, the doorway, the worn recess behind your shoulder.

If you stand, it continues beneath your feet.

If you leave, it follows as far as the next turn.

There it stops.

Long afterward, when that district has passed out of sight, you may find a small animal waiting at another court. It tests the ground near your shoes with its two foremost limbs. Then it lowers the flat side of its head onto the place where you have just stepped.

Behind it, in the sheltered wall, the dark film opens.

The animal waits there until you move on.
