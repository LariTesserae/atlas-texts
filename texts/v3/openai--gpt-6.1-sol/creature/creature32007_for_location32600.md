What lives here has no front.

There are surfaces turned toward the opening, surfaces pressed against ceramic, surfaces that have never faced anything except another leaf. None is more properly the outside of the living thing than another. Even the pale blade at the broken lip, which could be mistaken for a face looking out, is occupied chiefly with the ledge beneath it. Its underside follows the ceramic closely enough to preserve the impression of a flaw in the glaze.

It is not a single plant. Where two stems touch, they remain two stems. A transparent trunk passes through a mat of jointed growth without joining it; the mat makes a narrow sheath around the trunk, and each keeps its own tissue intact. The blue-black fronds have roots distinct from those of the white stalks. Their differences continue underground, through the dust and into the pores of the insulator, where branching threads lie side by side in passages scarcely wider than themselves.

Yet it is difficult to say where any one inhabitant ends.

A tree whose highest branch rests against the roof is supported halfway up by the hooked edge of an unrelated leaf. That leaf is held at its angle by a bundle of pale stems. The stems grow beside the regulator, whose enamel roof prevents them from leaning farther. Remove the regulator and the stems would shift; the leaf would lose its purchase; the tree would settle against a different part of the ceiling. Nothing here has the space to possess its shape alone.

The transparent trees are not glassy. Their trunks have a faint inward cloudiness, like clear material folded many times. Within them run closely laid bands of conductive tissue. At a branch junction the bands spread, divide, and close again, each enclosed in its own waxy sheath. There is no hollow channel through which a liquid rises. The entire trunk is occupied.

Their leaves are held in shallow fans. Some of the fans are visible only through other fans, so that the eye would assign a complicated crown to one tree where three small trees have interleaved their branches. Under green light, their boundaries nearly disappear. Under violet, the separate layers show as fine, dark lines.

They are among the largest things living in the hollow. One has a trunk shorter than the thickness of the bolt head.

The smallest inhabitants make no recognizable branches. They form the velvet on the ceramic: upright scales with a dark conductive base and a paler upper rim. Each scale is a leaf, or serves as one. Along the edges of the regulator’s old enamel, they occupy tiny discontinuities that larger roots cannot use. They do not spread across the metal beneath. Their border records every surviving fleck of coating.

A patch no larger than a pinprick contains a great many of them. It would be possible to look straight at the patch and see only a softening of the surface.

The inhabitants differ most clearly in what they cannot endure.

The thick fronds cannot fit between the ceiling pleats. The pleated growth cannot establish itself on the bare alloy. The white stalks require the protection of the shelf above them; their square blades are stiff in one direction and delicately yielding in another. Each species occupies a place that would deform or starve some other species. Their close packing conceals these exclusions. Seen together, they appear extravagantly successful. Seen separately, each is confined to a precise allowance.

They live on the charge that passes through a structure built for something else.

At the spindle, their roots flatten into minute contact pads. The pads are darker than the threads that lead away from them, and violet light gathers at their margins. From there, conductive layers run outward into trunks, stems, and blades. Carbon taken from the thin air is fixed into the same close arrangements that already exist. Material is repaired where it stands. A membrane keeps its boundary; a rib keeps its stiffness; the wax between two layers remains supple.

All of this happens beneath the threshold of visible movement.

The hooked leaves do not reach. The trees do not lift their crowns. The velvet does not advance over the next grain of ceramic. Living tissue replaces living tissue without altering the crowded outline. A blade can persist in the same narrow interval between two other blades while the substance of its surface is maintained, little by little, from within.

The great sound passes through these bodies continuously. A thick rib carries it down to a root. The root carries it into the ceramic, from which it enters another plant. Some of the thin leaves have curved edges held clear of their neighbors by distances too small to judge. Others lie against one another over most of their area. The difference matters: a free edge and a supported edge bear the vibration differently. Every surviving form has its own way of remaining intact beneath the note.

There is no listener.

At the bolt head, the leaves overhang the six-sided clearing. Their tips stop above its surface, leaving a sliver of dark air beneath them. Here the forest’s many boundaries are unusually easy to see: a combed edge, a blunt edge, one almost transparent point extending beyond a heavier blade. The bare metal remains bare.

Beyond that little clearing, there is no passage through which anything could walk, even if something small enough existed. There are only occupied intervals.

Near the entrance, the pale leaf holds its place against the ledge. Its exposed edge receives the faint light of the stars. The rest of it receives the green light of neighboring tissue and the vibration of the tower through the ceramic below.

It lives in both, without turning.
