At first you would take it for a piece of mortar loosened by the rain.

It lies at the foot of a wall, just where the damp line turns downward around a broken corner. Its back is pale gray, flecked with darker grains. One end is blunt; the other narrows into something like the edge of a leaf. Water gathers along its sides without quite passing over it.

Then the blunt end lifts. Beneath it, two small feet uncurl and touch the stone.

There are four more feet farther back. They are almost transparent, jointed close to the body, each ending in a broad pad divided by fine grooves. The animal moves by setting these pads against the paving and drawing itself forward. Its back scarcely changes shape. Only the wet border around it tells you how far it has gone.

An adult is about the length of a finger. The body is wider than it is thick, with a low ridge running from the head to the tail. Along either side hangs a soft fold of skin. When it rests, these folds spread against the ground. They hold a little water beneath the animal even where the street appears merely damp.

It can spend most of an afternoon in that narrow reserve.

Its eyes are set far apart, close to the edges of the head. From above they resemble two dark grains embedded in the gray surface. The mouth is underneath. You see it only if the animal climbs a wall, exposing a pale underside and a small muscular opening that presses to the stone, releases, then presses again.

It feeds on the soft growth that coats the wet paving.

Behind it, a cleared track appears, narrower than its body. The track lasts until water carries fresh sediment across it. In a busy gutter there may be dozens of these trails, crossing and fading under the thin current.

Most days the animals remain beneath loose slabs, inside the lower joints of walls, or in drains where there is room between the water and the ceiling. They favor places with two ways out. A blocked passage can become a deep pool in an hour, and although they swim, they do so poorly. Their feet trail uselessly; the broad tail flexes from side to side and pushes them toward the nearest rough edge.

They are better at remaining attached.

If a current strengthens, the side folds draw inward and the feet spread. A small animal can hold to a stair riser while runoff passes over it. The head turns away from the flow. Fine slits behind the jaw open and close.

After the rain has gone, it may remain there for a long time, with its tail hanging down the step.

There are more of them in the city than you would guess from the streets. A single displaced paving stone can uncover six or seven adults, loosely arranged around the wettest part of its underside. They do not flee at once. Light reaches them; their skin contracts; slowly they begin to move toward the shadow of your hand.

The young react more quickly. Some are no larger than a grain of barley. Their backs are smooth and almost colorless, so that the dark line of the gut shows through. They slip into joints too narrow to admit an adult’s head.

Each neighborhood holds a slightly different population. On the sunnier terraces the animals are darker and spend longer beneath the stone. Along the cold northern walls they have broader folds, stained brown by the deposits in the seepage. In courtyards with clean, fast runoff, their backs remain pale.

They carry very little of the city on them, but enough that an animal from one street can look out of place in the next.

At certain times of year they travel.

The first mild rains bring them out from the drains in the late afternoon. They follow the shallow channels, stopping where the flow divides. There may be three at a threshold, then twelve along a retaining wall, then a close, irregular line ascending a stair.

They go uphill slowly. A step that takes you one movement may occupy an animal for several minutes. It climbs the riser, pauses beneath the projecting lip, then reaches over with its forefeet. Sometimes the stone dries before it has crossed. It folds itself into the angle and waits for another shower.

Some never get farther.

The gray birds find them there. A bird working a gutter turns its head, takes two quick steps, and pulls one from the stone. Usually it eats the animal at once. Occasionally a folded strip of skin remains attached to the paving, moving briefly in the water.

Those that reach the upper terraces gather in shallow places where the current slows: roof hollows, settling basins, rooms whose floors have sunk beneath their thresholds. Their eggs are laid in flat patches under stone. Each egg sits within a clear rim, and the rims join, making a thin sheet with water held between its layers.

The young emerge facing in all directions.

For a few days they remain close to the empty eggs, feeding on growth too fine for you to see. Then they enter the outflow. A roof may release hundreds into a lane over the course of one wet evening. They catch in rough joints, disappear beneath silt, wash onward into another courtyard.

The city’s failures make new places for them. A fallen lintel can create a sheltered pool. A tilted slab can retain enough water to carry a small population through a dry week. Elsewhere, the collapse of a wall opens an old refuge to sunlight, and the animals leave it within a season.

They continue living among these changes without keeping to the shapes of rooms.

In winter they retreat below the reach of ordinary frost. Where a drain passes deeply beneath a stair, they gather against the damp ceiling, their feet hooked into its pits. Bodies overlap. The side folds draw close, and a fine film of water runs among them.

They scarcely feed. If a warm day sends meltwater through the passage, a few loosen their hold and move toward it. Most remain in place until the stone above them has warmed for several days.

You could cross the city in winter and see none at all.

In summer, you might sit beside one for an hour without noticing it. It would lie in the shadow of a threshold, its back drying to the same gray as the stone. Now and then one foot would shift. Beneath it, the stored water would diminish.

Then a thread of runoff would arrive from a distant shower.

It would touch the tail first. The animal’s skin would darken along the edges. Its feet would unfold, and it would turn, slowly, until its head faced into the current.

By the time the water reached the next street, it would be moving.
