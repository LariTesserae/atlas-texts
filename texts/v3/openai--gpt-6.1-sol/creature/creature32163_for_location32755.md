The inhabitants are people, as far as their neighbors have any reason to ask. They have chilblains, toothaches, favorite cups. They lose needles in the joints between floorboards. A woman in the western row has been trying for eleven years to persuade her brother to move his workbench away from a damp wall.

He agrees that it ought to be moved. He has measured the other walls.

Most of them are rather pale. Their hands redden easily, and the skin over their knuckles is rough from cold metal and repeated washing. Outdoors they wear several close-fitting layers instead of one heavy garment: a bulky coat catches on pipes and leaves too little room for someone coming the other way. Even young people tend to turn sideways before passing a doorway.

Their clothes are repaired neatly, often in colors that do not quite match. Cloth arrives through a cupboard in the house of a woman called Enna. She cannot order particular shades, only quantities and weights. For a period of nine years, almost everything that came through was blue. There are still blue linings inside gray coats, blue bindings on blankets, a blue wedding dress folded in a drawer.

Enna has never met the person who packs the cloth. She knows that their left thumb is stiff. The knots have changed gradually, becoming easier to untie but less secure.

Once, she sent a pair of knitted thumb supports back in an empty wrapping.

For several deliveries afterward, the knots were unusually careful.

***

There are more households than surnames.

The district keeps records, but addresses are considered more useful than ancestry, and the oldest records describe families by the fixtures they tended: the copper return, the third bread cupboard, the south stair. Some of these families have moved several times without changing their names. A man may be called South Stair even though he lives in an upper room beside the northern wall.

People know whose children they are. They are less certain how long their families have been here.

Paper survives well in the dry rooms, but dates do not always reconcile. A household ledger may include four generations of expenses and only twenty-seven years of clock maintenance. Nobody takes this to mean that the people in it did not live full lives. The discrepancies are copied faithfully when the books are renewed.

There is a clerk who can distinguish a genuine discrepancy from a careless entry. She works beside a heating disk with her sleeves rolled back, using three rulers and a small set of brass weights. Most of her work is mundane: births, leases, tools lent and not returned. She dislikes being consulted about the south road.

“How long will it take?” people ask her.

“Who is carrying it?” she replies, although she knows that makes no difference.

***

Children are taught the district by touch before they are allowed to walk it alone.

They learn the rounded screwhead outside their own doorway, the warm joint in a particular pipe, the notch beneath the rail at the turn of a stair. Adults lead them along passages with their eyes closed. A child who can get home by counting doors is still too young to be trusted; doors can be repainted, replaced, or, in certain weather, counted twice.

They are also taught the ordinary things adults elsewhere teach: do not put wire in your mouth, do not hit your cousin, do not leave food where it will spoil.

Their games are noisy indoors. The absorbing walls make a room full of children seem almost silent from the passage, which has led more than one visitor to open a door and receive a startling blast of shouting.

They race nutshell boats in the gutters after rain. They collect scraps of colored wrapping and trade them according to elaborate, changing values. They argue about the bird. Some maintain that its shadow comes to see whether the district is safe. Others say the bird is late and always has been.

A little boy once put a crumb on the parapet where the shadow had settled.

The bird arrived, ate it, and left.

This settled nothing.

***

The maintenance workers are not a separate class. Nearly everyone is responsible for something, and most people have an ordinary trade besides.

A baker checks the junction beneath her oven. A shoemaker tends three street posts. The clerk measures the crack beside the glass clock shelter every sixth morning, provided that the rain has stopped. More difficult work belongs to engineers, but nobody can afford to live in a house whose basic habits they do not understand.

The engineers carry very little. A padded case of instruments, chalk, thin metal shims, a brush. Their work clothes have knees worn smooth.

They spend much of the day listening.

Not for messages. A pipe that is preserving a staircase develops a slightly different tremor when the landing begins to belong to the floor below. A cellar panel can be entirely still and yet feel wrong beneath a measuring foot. The engineers compare readings, wait, and take them again. They are reluctant to call anything an emergency until they know what an intervention will put at risk.

There is an old engineer who has a particular gift for young stone. He can rest a fragment on his palm and say whether it is likely to split into plates or form a dense, rounded mass. He is wrong often enough that his judgments are written in pencil.

When asked how he does it, he describes its weight and temperature. These descriptions do not help his apprentices.

He has arthritis and cannot open his own cupboard latch on cold mornings.

***

Most meals are eaten close to the walls.

Tables are narrow, sometimes hinged, with room for dishes along one edge and knees beneath the other. People sit beside the heating disks and pass plates rather than ask someone to get up. Soup is common. So are steamed puddings, thick porridge, bread warmed on a metal rack.

Fresh apples are prized but never bitten immediately. They are cut into quarters and set near the disk until the cold has left their centers.

Guests are given the warmest seat. This is a substantial courtesy; the host may be uncomfortable for the rest of the evening.

There are quarrels, and some go on for years. The districts’s silence does not make its inhabitants gentle. They resent unfair allocations of cupboard deliveries, careless repairs, the smell of someone else’s cooking. A family above a workroom once refused to speak to the tenant below because he adjusted a support without telling them. Their sleeping alcove had become three inches narrower.

He argued that the street had remained where it was.

They argued that he did not sleep in the street.

Eventually he made them a chest of drawers fitted to the altered space. They still do not like him, but they greet him on the stairs.

***

There are residents who seldom appear outside.

In a room behind the second machine-parts cupboard lives a woman whose legs have become too weak for the staircase. Neighbors bring her water and take away her laundry. She repairs the small fogged panes in cupboard doors, a job requiring patience and good eyesight.

She has excellent eyesight.

Through some of the panes she has seen light, movement, or the corner of a parcel before the second depth was properly available. She does not watch for these things. Her task is to remove the damaged glass without disturbing the ceramic bed.

Once she saw a hand holding the other side of the pane while she loosened its frame.

She waited until it had let go.

Afterward she checked the replacement glass particularly carefully for sharp edges.

***

The drain creatures are better known than anyone admits.

Different covers have different inhabitants. Beneath the laundry stair are narrow ones with many short joints; under the square, broader creatures with translucent plates along their backs. They feed on something in the accumulated damp. No one has established what.

The repair workers can recognize several individuals by missing limbs or discolored shells. One has survived beneath the same cover for at least forty years. It retreats only far enough to allow work and returns while the tools are still being packed.

People do not pet them. They do avoid dropping hot solder into the drains.

The copper-colored moss also receives small accommodations. Heating disks are wiped around it. When a disk has to be replaced, someone usually scrapes a little moss into a dish and holds it against the new plate until its fine threads catch. This is not required by any maintenance rule.

It takes so long to grow that bare metal looks wasteful.

***

Among the residents, a few have become slightly difficult to locate.

This is usually first noticed at home. Someone calls from an alcove that appears empty. A hand reaches from behind a curtain whose lower edge hangs flat against the wall. The person concerned may be embarrassed, particularly if visitors are present.

Knocking on the wall generally restores an ordinary view of the room.

Doctors check circulation, sleep, and appetite. Engineers check the surrounding pipework. Sometimes a repair helps. Sometimes the condition persists without worsening.

A retired seamstress has lived this way for fourteen years. She keeps a little wooden striker beside her chair and knocks when someone brings her tea. Her nephew has suggested moving her to another room.

She will not leave a room she knows so well.

***

The rising earth in the courtyard belongs to no household.

Those whose windows face it take turns recording its height. The task has passed through several hands. The first observer hoped to see it become a hill before he died; the present observer, his granddaughter, writes down the measurements without forecasting anything.

Occasionally someone brings a chair to the courtyard and sits beside it.

The houses block almost all the sky. There is little to watch. Frost thaws on one side of the mound and remains on the other. A drop gathers on a bent pipe. Above, a window opens and somebody shakes out a cloth.

The residents who sit there are not always the old ones. A young father comes when his baby will not sleep. A cupboard keeper comes after a delivery has failed to arrive. The clerk once spent an entire afternoon there with her rulers in her lap.

They leave the patch untouched.

When the sunlight reaches it, they sometimes put out their hands, not over the earth but beside it, into the same narrow warmth. Then they go indoors, where there is food to prepare and a latch that needs attention, and where someone has saved them a seat against the wall.
