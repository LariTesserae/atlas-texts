The animals come closest to the service huts in bad weather.

They gather beneath the landing stages, where the wind cannot get at the water so easily, and lie with their backs pressed against the undersides of the planks. Anyone standing above them feels an occasional soft lifting underfoot. It is easily mistaken for a wave until it happens after the waves have stopped.

The boat crews call them bolsters. A large one is about the length of a person, though considerably wider through the middle. On land it seems almost shapeless: a low body, four short legs, a head carried close to the ground. In water the shape becomes intelligible. The broad chest parts the stems; the hind feet open into paddles; the neck extends farther than one expects. An animal that appeared to have no neck at all can reach into a crack between rocks without moving the rest of its body.

Its coat is dark at the roots and pale at the tips. Out of water it rises into uneven ridges, brown-grey, like wool pulled from an old mattress. Submerged, those ridges flatten and hold a bright layer of air. In clear shallows a swimming bolster has a silver outline that breaks apart when it turns.

They are not equally shaggy all over. The muzzle is bare, as are the palms of the forefeet and a narrow patch beneath the tail. These exposed parts are nearly black and have a tough, finely creased surface. An old animal’s muzzle is usually crosshatched with pale scars from pushing through rushes.

Their eyes are small but not dull. They watch hands. A person can walk along a landing stage without much response, but kneeling to untie a rope causes the nearest head to turn.

They live chiefly in the flooded meadows.

There they eat the old turf, cropping it close beneath the streaming water plants. The long leaves are less important to them than what is underneath: pale growing shoots, root crowns, the thick bases of sedges. They work at these with a divided upper lip and broad lower teeth. From a boat, one sometimes sees a patch of vegetation moving against the current. Then the animal comes up to breathe, with a tuft of roots protruding from one side of its mouth.

They also eat snails, freshwater mussels, and the soft-bodied things they uncover while tearing at the bottom. The sound of a bolster feeding under a stage is surprisingly domestic: small crunches, chewing, an occasional snort.

Most feeding takes place in water shallow enough for their feet to touch. They can cross the deeper channels, but they dislike doing so. Before a crossing, several will assemble on a submerged shoulder of stone and wait. One lifts its head. Another noses downstream, testing the pull. They may remain there for half an hour while the weather changes around them.

When they finally go, they go close together.

The young travel inside the group. They are born narrow-bodied and almost smooth, with disproportionately large feet and a coat that does not yet hold air well. For their first weeks they ride on an adult’s back, gripping the long hair with their forefeet. Usually this is the mother, though not always. The crews have seen one old male carrying three young while the females fed nearby.

A carried youngster lies flat during a squall, muzzle buried in the adult’s coat. Only its ears show: two small rounded cups, closed at their lower edges against spray.

On calm mornings the young stand up.

They balance badly, changing their minds about which foot to move. Sometimes the adult continues grazing while a youngster walks the length of its back. Sometimes it turns its head and folds the youngster firmly down with its chin.

Their sleeping places are not quite nests.

A family pulls loose sedge roots into the lee of an island and wedges them among anchored plants. More stems are dragged in over several nights. The result is a thick, saturated platform, held together partly by living roots and partly by the animals’ weight. They sleep with their bodies in the water and their noses resting on it.

These platforms are responsible for some of the plateau’s deceptive shores. Moss takes hold on an abandoned one. Seeds lodge there. After a few seasons, willow may begin to grow, and the whole thing looks like a small piece of ordinary land.

A bolster knows which parts will hold it. A person generally does not.

The families maintain several sleeping places and move between them as wind and water dictate. They do not defend every one. Two groups may crowd into the same sheltered bay for a night, breathing and shifting against one another in the dark. But a platform with newborn young is another matter. An approaching animal is met by open mouths and a low vibration that carries through the water.

Their voices are easier to feel than to hear.

At close quarters there is a sound like someone humming with their lips shut. Farther away, a hand resting on a boat’s hull detects it as a brief tremor. The animals use it while feeding out of sight of one another among the plants. A mother calls; somewhere beneath the leaves her youngster answers.

When frightened, they make a much sharper noise, a bark with a wet catch in it. Birds lift from the surrounding reeds at once.

The plateau’s machinery has altered their lives.

Before the low barriers joined the lakes, the bolsters moved over wet ground between feeding places. Older animals still come ashore at some of the former crossings. They climb a few metres, sniff the slope, and turn back toward the water. In other places the raised level has opened broad new pastures. Drowned lawns and roadside verges grow soft shoots that the animals keep cropped almost as neatly as the geese keep the island turf.

The intake bays are more dangerous.

At first, an opening gate merely loosens food. Roots lift from the bottom. Small creatures tumble out of the weed. The bolsters come to feed, and the current strengthens around them.

They are strong swimmers over a short distance, but their strength is in pushing through vegetation, not pulling free of a steady suction. Their coat makes matters worse. Loose strands catch in it. An animal struggling toward shelter can arrive wrapped in a moving skein of weeds.

The gate keepers have learned to clear the bays before drawing heavily. They strike the landing-stage rails with a spanner, then start the boat engine and run a slow circuit through the shallows. Most families leave at the first knocking. One female at the western intake has to be followed almost to the channel mouth. She stops repeatedly to pull up another mouthful.

There are scars on her right flank where her coat never grew back properly.

People know some of the animals individually without considering them tame. They are distinguished by torn ears, pale brows, the way they enter the water. One male lowers himself backward from a particular rock. Another sleeps with a forefoot hooked over the lowest rung of a ladder.

They sometimes climb into unattended workboats.

Not for food. An enclosed engine retains warmth for hours, and the animals discover this quickly. A bolster squeezed between the engine housing and a bench is difficult to remove. It braces all four feet, lowers its head, and becomes extraordinarily heavy. The crews now close the canvas covers before leaving a boat overnight.

The animals show little interest in the huts themselves. A concrete floor offers nothing worth eating, and a roof magnifies the rain. But outside, beside a warm exhaust pipe, there may be a shallow hollow worn into the moss by repeated use.

On rare bright afternoons they come completely out of the water.

They choose bare, gently sloping rock and lie where the sun has had time to warm it. Water runs from their coats into the cracks. The ridges of hair slowly open. Youngsters explore the space between the adults, biting lichen, inspecting boots, retreating at the movement of a finger.

An old animal may sleep deeply enough to let its jaw hang loose.

Then the light goes. A gust presses the willow flat, and rain begins dimpling the channel below. The first bolster rises, stiffly on its short legs, and noses the nearest sleeper awake. One after another they slide down the rock.

The last is often the animal carrying young. It waits while they climb into place, turns its head to check them, and enters the water without a splash.
