The first inhabitant becomes visible when the tool is moved.

Not because it has been watching the tool, or because some alarm has been disturbed. A hand is simply there to receive it. The hand occupies the water a little above the unfinished foundation, palm upward, with four long fingers bent in anticipation of a handle that is no longer where it was left.

For a moment you can see the fingers very clearly. They are pale at the joints and transparent between them. There are narrow spaces inside the hand that do not appear to contain water.

Then the rest of the person becomes distinguishable.

It has been standing beside you.

Its body is tall, though height is difficult to judge in someone whose feet do not quite meet the stone. Its legs end in broad, divided surfaces, suitable for gripping an edge or resting against a wall. The torso is narrower than a human torso and has no obvious ribs. Two arms hang from high shoulders. Around the head is a faint, irregular outline, rather like the edge of a clear object submerged in clear water.

There is a face. It takes time to find.

What first seems to be the mouth is a seam beneath the chin. The actual mouth is higher, small and closed. The eyes sit far apart, not on the front of the head but at the ends of two shallow ridges. They are the same pearl color as the walls. When the person looks down at the empty hand, one eye changes before the other.

It does not reach after the tool.

It waits for you to put it back.

Only after you do so does it bend, close its fingers around the handle, and lift the loop from the sockets. The action is quite ordinary. Nothing shifts in the mountain. No brightness comes from the square.

The person examines the loop. Something in it may have been damaged by your handling, or it may always have required examination before use.

While it works, you notice that its wrist is not connected continuously to its forearm. A thin interval separates them. There are similar intervals at the elbows, beneath the jaw, across one thigh. The parts remain aligned when it moves. They do not knock together or drift apart.

The intervals are not wounds. Their edges are smooth and old.

You begin to find the other inhabitants by looking for these small separations.

One is sitting in the house whose wall has become partly transparent. It occupies one of the shallow floor recesses, with its knees raised and its hands resting on the table. Another stands in the vertical shaft, lower down than the room, only its head and shoulders visible above the opening. They seem to be engaged in a conversation.

Neither moves its mouth.

At irregular intervals, the seated one puts two fingers against the tabletop. The other raises a hand and touches the side of the shaft. A narrow line appears across the table, travels to the wall, and disappears. There is no accompanying disturbance in the water.

The lines do not persist long enough to read, even if you knew how.

Their faces move more slowly than their hands. The seated person’s mouth gradually becomes uneven. When the other answers, it smooths again. It might be smiling. It might have been considering an unpleasant matter and found the answer acceptable.

Their attention is unmistakable, though. They turn toward you when you enter. The one in the shaft comes up into the room, placing its broad feet against the sides rather than swimming. It examines the light you carry, then your wrists, then the place beneath your jaw.

It is particularly interested in your uninterrupted neck.

Its hand rises almost to touch you. Before contact, it stops and lays that hand against its own collar instead. There is a gap there, narrow enough that you had missed it.

This appears to be an introduction.

***

They are not equally easy to see.

Some have a cloudy density to their bodies, especially around the hands and face. Others become apparent only while passing a wall, where the light changes at their edges. One inhabitant is recognizable chiefly by a row of dark inclusions down its back. You learn to look for these before approaching a doorway. It is discourteous here, as elsewhere, to walk through a place someone is occupying.

Their clothes are difficult to distinguish from their bodies. Most wear something fitted close around the torso, with loose extensions below the arms. The material becomes visible where it folds. Small objects are kept inside these folds: a flat piece of black stone, several pale rods, a loop like the one at the foundation but much smaller.

One person carries a chipped cup.

The cup remains opaque. It gives this otherwise elusive resident an appearance of particular solidity, and you sometimes see it moving through the square before you can find the hand that holds it.

They do not seem to need lamps. Yet several pause beside the luminous circle on their way between houses. They lower a hand toward it, stop short of touching, and continue. Some do this each time they cross. Others only occasionally.

The youngest does it twice, glancing back toward an adult after the second attempt.

There is at least one child in the town.

It is small, with a disproportionately wide head and short arms whose separate portions do not always remain exactly aligned. When excited, its fingers shift sideways at the joints. An adult brings them back into order with a patient stroke.

The child spends much of its time beside the suspended chip near the rail.

It puts a finger between the chip and the rock. Withdraws it. Puts two fingers there. Once, it tries the edge of its foot.

The adult stops this last experiment.

Nothing in the gesture suggests terror. It is the practiced intervention of someone who has explained a rule several times and will have to explain it again.

The child then goes to trouble the animal.

***

The pale ray belongs to the town in the uncomplicated way an animal can belong to a place without owning anything or understanding its arrangements.

It knows the inhabitants. When one passes near the northern foundations, its fins rise a little. With certain people, it leaves the threshold and accompanies them for several meters, moving through the water in brief, uneven efforts. It never follows you.

The six dark spots on its underside are soft. You see one deform when it settles against a resident’s palm.

An older inhabitant comes to feed it.

The person brings nothing you can identify as food. It crouches in the water and holds both hands under the animal, one near the mouth and one beneath the central body. For a while they stay that way. The fins loosen. A little warmth reaches your fingers if you stand close enough.

Afterward the older person rests on the threshold.

Its hands are more visible now, almost white. It folds them into its clothing and remains still until they begin to clear.

The child is permitted to help, but only with one hand. It is impatient to finish and visibly proud afterward. The animal follows it halfway across the foundations.

There are marks along the unfinished threshold where the ray has rested. They are not wear in the stone. They are faint differences in brightness, shaped like overlapping bodies.

This is the oldest trace of habitation you have found.

***

The municipal chamber is not deserted either.

Someone is almost always down there, moving between the frames. The surfaces show more when touched by a resident. A view of the rail acquires a second, slightly displaced edge. The pieces inside the mountain change in clarity. Sometimes one of the fine dark intervals briefly broadens, though the actual stone above does not move.

The work involves comparison.

One person touches a surface while another watches a neighboring frame. They wait. They exchange places. A pale rod is brought out and held against the frame, never against the image within it.

They sometimes disagree.

You recognize this before you understand anything else about their government. A hand remains raised. An answer comes sharply across the wall. Someone turns away from a surface, then returns to it. There is a long consultation involving four residents and the person with the cup.

Eventually a mark is made on the floor beside one frame.

It is very small.

For the rest of that day, everyone entering the chamber looks at it.

The work is shared, though not evenly. The foundation worker spends little time below the square. The older person who tends the animal comes frequently but rarely touches the surfaces. The child is brought down once and shown where to stand.

There are no uniforms. You cannot tell which of them has authority until someone else accepts a decision.

Above ground, ordinary movements continue while the inspection is underway. A resident crosses to another house carrying two rods. Someone adjusts the edge of a doorway. The person seated at the table draws its feet from the recesses and rubs the broad ends together, as if they ache.

The town’s maintenance does not require every inhabitant to attend to it constantly.

It requires that someone attend.

***

There are departures.

A resident approaches one of the rail’s gates and waits with a hand resting on the fastening. Another comes from the square. They touch their wrists together, carefully, on the solid portions below the gaps.

The gate opens.

Beyond it there is nothing but the drop and the deep water. The departing person steps out, turns until its head points downward, and descends close to the rock. Its body remains visible longer than you expect. Then only its hands can be distinguished. Then nothing.

The other resident closes the gate.

Later—much later—the fastening is opened again from the town side, just before a pale hand appears beyond the rail.

You never discover where the inhabitants go. They return carrying objects, but not always. Sometimes they return exhausted. Once, two go out and three come back, the third supported between them. That person has lost part of an arm. The remaining pieces are held in alignment by a thin loop.

A room is made ready.

For several days, someone sits there with the injured resident. Visitors touch the wall outside before entering. The child is quiet in that house.

Recovery, if it is recovery, is slow.

***

The empty foundations are visited often.

The worker with the loop spends hours at the rectangular sockets, lifting the tool, lowering it, checking something that remains invisible to you. Other residents stop nearby but do not interrupt. The work sometimes produces a short, pearl-colored upright. By the next visit it is gone.

No one treats this as an astonishing failure.

One afternoon the upright remains.

The worker sets down the loop and goes to fetch someone from the nearest house. Together they inspect what has been made. A third resident joins them. The child arrives and is kept back with one gently extended arm.

The new piece is no taller than your thumb.

They stay around it for a long time.

Afterward, the tool is left across the sockets again, within reach. The worker goes home. The animal resumes its place above the threshold.

You can see the upright from the square if you know where to look.

There is still almost nothing there: a small beginning at the northern edge, seven houses, a gate closing after a return. Below them, the mountain’s separated pieces remain held in their places.

In one house the child is being put into a floor recess. It objects, reaching for something on the table. The adult gives it the smaller loop, but takes away the rod.

Another resident comes to the doorway and waits until the child settles.

Then the two adults cross the square together and descend to take their turn in the chamber.
