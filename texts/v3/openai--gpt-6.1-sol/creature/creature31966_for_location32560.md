The person carrying groceries has bought something that is beginning to hatch.

It is meant to do this. Inside the paper sleeve, a cluster of hard green fruits has warmed enough for their inner membranes to separate. Each gives a little click. By the time she reaches home, the edible flesh will have drawn away from the bitter rind, and the pale structures at the center will be searching for a surface to attach to. She will put them in the kitchen’s propagation tray. For three or four days they will make a faint dry scratching sound.

She carries the sleeve against her hip, away from the other bag, which contains cold things. At a fork in the path she stops to adjust her grip. One handle has cut a red groove across her fingers.

Her name is Sera. She works in the hospital, repairing the soft fittings that keep beds, cabinets, and patients from receiving too much of the city’s vibration. She has spent most of today on her knees beside a man who complained that his bed felt wrong. The instruments showed nothing outside tolerance. Eventually she took off her gloves and laid her fingertips against the frame.

He was right. One corner was transmitting a small, irregular knock.

Now she is walking home because she has spent the day inside, and because her daughter has a visitor, and because there are times when arrival should take longer than selecting a destination.

She passes three people in the first kilometer. She knows two of them.

That is a fairly crowded evening.

The inhabitants of this city are not especially solitary. Many have large households; many belong to clubs, professional associations, quarrelsome neighborhood assemblies, choirs. A supper table may hold twelve people who have come from buildings too distant to see from one another’s windows. They arrive almost together, bringing dishes whose lids release different warm smells into the same room.

What the distances give them is the ability to end an encounter completely.

Someone leaves a party. A door closes. A moment later they are home, with kilometers of vegetation between themselves and the thing they should not have said.

This can be a comfort. It can also make apologies difficult. People compose messages, delete them, and look across the plain toward lights they cannot reliably identify.

Their bodies are ordinary enough, though their habits can make them seem otherwise. They stand with their knees slightly loose. When waiting, they prefer to touch a wall, a rail, another person’s shoulder. Much information arrives through contact.

A resident can often tell whether a building’s dampers are working before noticing the quiet. The jaw unclenches. The soles of the feet cease to itch. A spoon resting against the rim of a bowl becomes merely a spoon.

Out on the paths, people speak close together. Intimacy has consequently acquired a practical ambiguity. A face beside your ear may belong to a lover, a stranger asking directions, or a municipal inspector explaining that the hollow ahead is closed.

They have conventional gestures for this. Two fingers lifted before approaching means a question. An open hand held briefly beside the mouth means something longer. Children forget the gestures and shout from a distance until an adult comes to fetch them.

Some residents hear very little. Others hear selectively, with devices tuned to voices and alarms. There are people who cannot bear the strongest underground reports even through protection, and people who sleep badly when they travel somewhere quiet. The city makes accommodations imperfectly. Its public notices arrive as light, sound, and a pulse through the handrail, but a broken handrail can remain broken for weeks.

Nobody who lives here thinks of the noise as one thing.

There is the westward rolling sound, which usually means an uneventful afternoon. There is the brittle series of impacts that makes maintenance workers check the time. There is an insect chorus Sera dislikes because it resembles a failing suspension bracket. There is the sudden broad silence during which everyone discovers what they have been doing with their shoulders.

The oldest inhabitants sometimes hear water in the machinery.

They remember the former coast, although memory has made its dimensions unreliable. One remembers a pier that another insists was a service platform. One remembers waves breaking beneath a dining room. One remembers only the wet ropes outside a school.

Their arguments are preserved in recordings. So are the measurements. The two archives are maintained separately.

A few old people still call an inland district the harbor. Delivery workers learn the name because addresses given by elderly customers sometimes depend on it.

There are families who helped raise the plain and families who arrived after its first plants had already spread. This distinction matters greatly to certain people and scarcely at all to their children. It reappears whenever a new ledge emerges. Who has the right to name it? Who will inspect it? Can someone put a house there? Must everything that happens be dedicated to somebody’s grandfather?

The engineers do not always enjoy being asked what is happening beneath the ground.

Often they know precisely. Sometimes they have a range of estimates. Their profession requires them to distinguish the two in public, while everyone around them would prefer either reassurance or a reason to be afraid.

They shop at the same market as everybody else. They attend weddings during which the deepest mechanical note changes, and half the guests look at them.

At home, they teach their children not to touch humming stone.

Children have a more immediate geography.

The hospital is far away. The market is far away. The hollow where a six-legged animal lost a leg and nevertheless survived is nearby, even if reaching it requires a considerable walk. A certain slab is the boundary of a game. A broad leaf is a roof. Three dark seed cases beneath a creeper mean someone has already claimed the path beyond.

Adults try to keep these little territories away from breeding chambers and fresh fractures. Success varies. A child who can recite every warning color may still follow an animal into a cleft because it is carrying something blue.

Their pockets fill with mineral chips, discarded skins, and husks that remain alive longer than expected. Schools have shallow quarantine drawers for discoveries. Teachers occasionally open them to find that the contents have fastened the drawer shut.

The animals learn the human routines as well.

A small grazer lives beneath the lowest step of Sera’s building. It comes out when the entrance light brightens, not because she feeds it—she does not—but because her arrival frightens a thin hunting animal away from the path. The grazer has learned to take four or five mouthfuls during this interval.

On days when she transfers directly upstairs, it receives no such opportunity.

The long-bodied hunter waits nearby anyway. It is patient in a way that makes patience seem unpleasant.

Inside the buildings live less conspicuous companions. Tiny animals eat the wax that accumulates around window seals. Rootlike growths enter drainage fittings and must be discouraged with particular salts. In old upholstery, something sometimes begins to answer the district’s insect choruses. The furniture then goes into a treatment chamber, and the household sits on borrowed chairs for a week.

Cleanliness here is a matter of choosing what may remain.

People disagree about that too. One household keeps a little damp basin under its washroom floor and enjoys the breathing of the creatures that occupy it. Another regards the practice as intolerable. Both submit petitions when a new housing standard is proposed.

The city’s great stretches of growth are cared for by workers whose interventions can be almost invisible. They move a fallen crown so that it shades a fissure. They remove one plant from a crowded root mouth. They leave another to die because its husk will retain more water than its living leaves.

These workers come home with adhesive discs stuck to their sleeves and resin in the folds of their hands. They are frequently accused of doing nothing. After rain, when an incorrectly cleared hollow dries too quickly, they are accused of doing everything wrong.

They have their own pleasures. Seeing a dry patch survive. Finding that an animal has reopened a ventilating throat without damaging the roots around it. Taking an apprentice back to a site after several months and letting them notice the change unaided.

Sera’s daughter wants this work. Last month she wanted to maintain transit chambers. Before that she wanted to leave.

Sera has not yet decided which ambition worries her most.

By the time she reaches her building, the groceries have become uncomfortable to carry. The fruit sleeve is clicking steadily. A yellow line surrounds the transit door.

She sets the bags down.

Above her, the upper rooms spread darkly against the gray sky. Her own kitchen window is bright. She can make out two figures moving inside, though not which is her daughter. One of them approaches the glass and looks down.

Sera raises a hand.

The figure raises a hand back.

For a while, that is all they can do. The person upstairs is perhaps thirty meters away. The system that ordinarily makes the hospital close and the market convenient cannot carry her this last distance.

She sits on the entrance bench. Its surface is warm through her clothes. Beside her shoe, the little grazer emerges and begins cropping the translucent edge of a leaf.

The machinery has not yet shifted. There is time to open one of the bags, take out a cold drink, and hold it against the groove in her fingers.

Upstairs, somebody brings a second chair to the window.

Below, Sera waits with the animal, the ripening groceries, and several kilometers of neighbors doing much the same thing.
