The paired knocking comes from an animal that seldom shows more than its nose.

People call it a root-hare, although it has neither the legs nor the ears of a hare. The name is old enough that no one expects it to be accurate. A grown one is as long as a person’s forearm, with a narrow head, a loose-skinned neck, and a body that changes shape considerably when it moves. At rest it looks almost plump. Pulled into a passage between roots, it becomes a length of shoulders and ribs, its hindquarters following after a slight delay.

Its fur is the color of wet straw near the skin and pale at the tips. Seen through parted leaves, it is difficult to tell whether you are looking at an animal or at old fibers catching the light.

The forefeet are its most noticeable feature. Each has four long fingers and a short, thick thumb. They are not digging feet. The nails are blunt, and an animal confined on bare soil can do surprisingly little with them. In its own country, it slips those fingers between strands, takes hold, and pulls. It climbs sideways through the plateau.

There is room for this movement beneath almost every place where a person can stand.

The living cover is not solid. Old stems arch over newer ones; roots leave gaps when they die; a buried shrub may hold open a chamber long after its leaves have disappeared. Some spaces are no wider than two fingers. Others could contain a sleeping child. The root-hare uses the narrow passages between them, sometimes squeezing through with its head turned completely on its side.

Its ears lie against its skull beneath folds of fur. Long hairs grow from its cheeks, elbows, and the outer edges of its feet. These touch the walls ahead of the body. An animal moving quickly through darkness carries information about the passage along its whole length: where it narrows, where the fibers are loose, where something else has just gone through.

It stops often.

Then it puts its lower jaw against a root and knocks twice with one forefoot.

The sound is made by a hard pad at the base of the thumb. Up close, it is scarcely louder than a fingernail tapping wood. Through a dry, continuous runner it travels much farther. Other root-hares feel it through their feet or hear it with their jaws against the same growth.

The answer may come promptly, or after a long pause. It may be two knocks, three, or a rapid scraping.

People who spend their lives gathering fiber can recognize a few of these exchanges. A repeated double knock usually means that an animal is approaching through a narrow passage. The scraping often comes when two meet and neither will move aside. Beyond that, interpretations vary from household to household.

Children are more confident. They claim to know which knocks mean food, which mean a snake, and which belong to an animal that has lost its way. They spend long afternoons lying with one cheek on the ground, tapping a stem with a bit of horn.

Sometimes something taps back.

The root-hare eats what accumulates out of sight. It takes insect eggs from the undersides of leaves, pale grubs from rotten wood, fungal growth from the seams of wet fibers. It also cuts young runners with its small orange incisors. It is particular about these, eating the soft inner portion and leaving a clean sleeve of outer tissue behind.

A place occupied by root-hares consequently has better passages than an empty place. Their repeated journeys keep channels open. Their feeding removes some of the growth that would close them. Water passes through these routes during rain, then remains in the lower chambers when the surface dries.

This is useful to the people, though not always convenient.

Under a house, root-hares will gradually find the darkest, quietest part of the foundation raft. There they bring seed wool, shredded leaves, bits of discarded cord, and whatever soft material they can detach from the walls. They favor ear stuffing. A plug left on a sleeping ledge may be gone by morning, with a narrow trail of fibers leading toward the floor.

Most households tolerate one nest. More than one produces too much damage, and the animals begin to quarrel under the sleeping chambers.

They are moved with a woven trap containing a piece of damp, decayed stem. The trap must be checked before sunrise. In daylight, a trapped root-hare presses itself into a corner and becomes dangerously hot, even in shade. People carry it wrapped in a wet cloth to a hollow well away from the house, set the opening against the vegetation, and wait.

It leaves backward first, testing with its hind feet. Then it turns in the mouth of the basket with a motion that seems impossible for an animal with bones, and is gone.

For several minutes afterward the leaves may move in a thin, wavering line.

A root-hare has little use for the open surface. Its eyes are small and its vision poor. Without stems against its sides it hesitates, reaching for support that is not there. When a patch of cover burns or is stripped by grazing animals, root-hares sometimes become stranded beneath the last few clumps. Birds learn to watch these patches.

Yet at dusk they come up voluntarily.

First a nose appears. It is broad and dark, divided by a deep groove, with droplets caught along its edges. Then the head rises, followed by the shoulders. The animal keeps its hind feet anchored below and rests its forefeet on the leaves.

It remains like that for a long time.

A person standing nearby can hear it sniffing, even through the evening insects: short breaths, each followed by a faint wet click. It is sampling the air for other root-hares. Their scent changes with age, food, breeding condition, and the part of the plateau where they live. To an animal accustomed to enclosed passages, the wind carries news from an immense distance.

Occasionally it lifts its chin and calls.

The voice is unexpectedly low. It begins as a breathy vibration, steadies, and breaks at the end into two rough notes. Across a shallow hollow, another animal may answer. Where there are many, the calls overlap until it is difficult to locate any one throat.

This is one part of the dusk noise that people recognize clearly.

When the calls begin early, the animals have often smelled rain coming across the rim. When they continue far into the night, young root-hares are usually moving between territories. But sometimes they call on a clear evening, with no change of weather afterward and no strangers found in the traps.

People open their curtains to listen anyway.

The young are born in a chamber lined with dry fibers, usually above a wet passage but below the layer warmed directly by sunlight. There are two or three. Their mother can carry one beneath her chest, held between her forearms, and still climb with her hind feet and teeth.

Very young animals have a strong grasp. Put a loose cord against one’s palm and it closes its fingers around it before it opens its eyes.

They learn the paths by following their mother with one hand on the fur above her tail. For several weeks a family travels as a small connected procession. The mother stops; the young compress against her. She moves; they lengthen behind her. At a difficult crossing she turns, takes a youngster by the shoulder skin, and places it where its feet can hold.

Gatherers sometimes uncover such a procession while peeling back a layer of growth. They lower the leaves carefully and work somewhere else that day.

There is no rule requiring this. Root-hares are neither sacred nor especially beloved. They steal, bite when handled, and occasionally undermine the edge of a water pocket so that a person puts a foot through it.

But anyone who has watched the young holding on finds it hard to cut across their route.

An old root-hare may occupy the same few hundred paces for years. It knows the dependable chambers, the stems that carry sound, the deep pockets where moisture lasts. Its paths bend around stones no one above has seen. Some pass through buried masonry; others follow wood laid down before the nearest ruin was built.

Above it, a house is shifted, a marker stake falls, a band of horned grazers crops a hollow silver-gray. The root-hare encounters these changes as pressure, scent, warmth, and the interruption of familiar passages.

It investigates each one with its nose and hands.

Beneath a newly placed foundation, it waits until the weight has settled. Then it finds the edge, grips a strand, and begins making a way through.
