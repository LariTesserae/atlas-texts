The one who lives here comes out after the rain, when the ground has begun to lift but the water has not yet found where to go.

At first you might mistake it for something washed loose from beneath a ridge. It is long and low, the color of wet ash, with a darker strip running along its back. Water gathers on that strip and travels toward the tail. Its legs are folded close against its sides, so that its first movements seem to happen without limbs: a shortening, a pause, a lengthening onto the next patch of stone.

Then it reaches a pool too wide to cross that way, and stands.

There are six legs. The rear pair are broad and short, well suited to pushing. The middle pair end in narrow, divided pads. The front pair are longer and can turn their joints in either direction. When it raises them, they become arms.

Standing, it is about as tall as the shelter’s lowest roof panel. Its head hangs forward between its shoulders. There is no neck to speak of, only a loose fold that opens when it looks up. Small dark eyes sit far apart beneath a translucent brow. A row of fine slits runs down either side of its chest.

It waits at the edge of the pool with one forelimb in the water.

Something is trapped beneath the new mineral growth. You can see the movement, not the animal itself: a little disturbance inside a cloudy branching structure.

The resident presses two fingers into the soft rim. Its fingers separate into narrower tips. Slowly, with frequent pauses, it pulls the rim apart.

A winged shrimp comes out backward.

For a moment the shrimp hangs from one finger, folded wings tight against its back. Then it springs away. The resident withdraws its hand and holds the divided fingertips together until they have joined again.

It does not watch the shrimp go.

It is already testing the next pool.

***

Its body does not have a hard outline everywhere.

The back and shoulders are covered in overlapping plates, thin enough that their edges flex. The plates are dark when wet and pale when dry. Under the arms, around the mouth, and along the inner surfaces of the legs, the skin is soft and faintly granular. Grains come away when it rubs against the stone. In certain light, those grains resemble the particles gathering beneath the pools.

It leaves prints, but not consistently. On firmer ground its divided feet make marks like small bundles of reeds. On softer surfaces the feet spread until the resident’s weight scarcely dents them. Sometimes it moves with all six limbs down. Sometimes it carries its front half upright, freeing its hands. When the ground drums sharply, it lowers its entire belly to the stone.

That is how it listens.

It may lie still for several minutes while animals step around it or climb over its back. The birds are less cautious with it than they are with you. One lands between its shoulder plates and probes an old seam there. The resident rolls a shoulder, gently enough to dislodge the bird without frightening it.

Under that plate is a pale scar.

There are many such scars. One hind foot lacks its outer division. A patch beside the left eye is opaque. The tail has healed crookedly, with a blunt bend halfway down.

This is not a creature that survives by always knowing what comes next.

***

It occupies the shelter, though not exclusively.

Beneath one of the panels it has accumulated things the basin does not make: three dark metal pins, a curled sheet of flexible material, a narrow tool with a white handle, and several pieces of cord stiffened by damp. These are pushed into a gap above the ground. Whenever the gap becomes too shallow, the resident moves them.

The cup belongs to it.

It drinks by lowering the underside of its face into the water. A small opening unfolds there, surrounded by four soft lobes. The water level falls with no audible swallowing.

Afterward it rinses one forelimb in what remains.

The folded seat fits it poorly. It sits with its rear legs tucked beneath the frame and its middle legs extended along the ground. The front limbs rest on either side of the cup. As the seat adjusts, its body shifts to accommodate the movement, but its head stays almost perfectly still.

It spends a considerable part of each day there.

Not asleep. Its eyes follow the birds. A fingertip occasionally touches the floor. When the ridge behind the shelter gives a deep knock, the chest slits open and close.

Sometimes it raises a hand to the scratches on the panel.

At the lowest row, its fingers fit.

At the highest, they do not quite reach.

***

It eats the gray vegetation, but only from the underside.

A front hand slips beneath the lifted mat and pinches off a little of the wet root fringe. The resident holds this against its mouth for a long time. It takes so little that you could watch the same patch for days without being certain anything had been removed.

It also eats the flat translucent animals.

Not often, and not while they are running. It waits beside a seam until the drumming stops them. Then one hand descends with surprising speed. The captured animal disappears beneath the face. The others resume their rush.

The resident does not interfere with the long predator under the mat. It approaches the feeding ground from the other side and keeps its fingers out of the water there.

Once, the predator’s jaws clap against its wrist.

The resident jerks back. Its chest slits close. For several minutes it holds the injured limb folded against its body, touching the wound with the tips of the other hand.

After that it gives the gray mat a wider berth.

***

When the posts sing, it leaves whatever it is doing.

It approaches the buried disk on four legs, carrying the narrow white-handled tool in one hand. The last few steps are slow. Water on its back rises into tiny points. Its shoulder plates lift at their edges.

It does not enter the ring of posts.

Instead, it finds a place outside them and pushes the tool into the ground.

Sometimes it has to try several places. Sometimes the tool goes in easily, and the resident holds it there with both hands while the note climbs.

A line appears in the stone.

The line may run toward a pool, or away from the shelter, or along the base of a ridge. Growth hesitates on one side of it. On the other, the mineral threads continue knitting together.

This gives the small animals time.

They emerge in brief, dense streams, crossing the line while the resident holds the tool steady. The birds hover above them, feeding. The resident makes no attempt to drive the birds away.

There are occasions when nothing emerges.

It waits anyway.

Once the line closes around the tool. The resident pulls until its rear feet slide forward. The white handle bends. For a few seconds its whole body trembles with effort.

Then it lets go.

The tool remains standing in the new stone, tilted slightly toward the shelter.

For the rest of that day the resident visits it, touching the exposed handle each time it passes.

By morning only the white tip remains.

***

It knows the door.

That becomes apparent before you ever see it open one.

When the steady knocking below changes rhythm, the resident lifts its head. Whatever posture it is in, whatever distance from the ridge, it turns one eye toward the frame.

Usually it goes no farther.

But on certain evenings it climbs the ridge and rests its front hands against the dark metal. The frame carries vibrations too faint to hear. They travel through its arms, lifting the loose skin at the shoulders.

It stands there until the sky has dimmed.

Then it opens the door.

The stale warmth spreads over its face. Its chest slits draw tight, as though the air is unpleasant or difficult to breathe. It leans into the opening but does not cross the threshold.

Below, something knocks.

The resident answers by striking the frame with a fingertip.

Two taps. A pause. Another.

You cannot tell whether the knocking changes in response. You may think it does, until you hear the same sequence the next day when no one is near the door.

Sometimes the resident leaves a piece of gray root on the red floor.

By the time it returns, the piece has dried into a thread.

It removes it carefully.

***

There are smaller versions of it beneath the shelter.

You discover them only because one moves while the adult is away. What looked like a stack of pale flakes inside a wall gap unfolds a head and two minute forelimbs. The young creature is scarcely longer than your thumb. Its back plates are clear, showing a dark cord underneath.

It lowers itself toward the floor.

Before it reaches the stone, the adult returns.

One broad hand closes beneath it. The young creature clings to a finger, feet spread wide, and is carried back to the gap.

The adult remains there for some time, placing its mouth close to the opening. Its chest moves, but you hear no sound.

There are at least four young. Perhaps more. They fit together so closely that their folded bodies are difficult to separate by sight.

They do not all grow at the same speed.

One has broad rear feet already. Another stays curled, its plates cloudy and its limbs seldom moving. The adult brings water to the gap in the cup and tips it carefully until a bead catches on the lip. Small mouths reach for it.

When the ground tilts, the cup spills.

The adult catches what it can in its hand.

***

At night it is harder to distinguish from the basin.

The amber light beneath the stone shows through the soft parts of its body. Its legs glow dimly where they touch the ground. The dark strip along its back remains unlit.

It moves from hollow to hollow, occasionally lowering its face to a breathing hole. It clears a little stone away with its fingers. At another hole it waits, then moves on without touching anything.

Near the open edge, it sometimes stands upright and looks into the cloud.

There is no visible country then. Only whiteness rising past the shelter, wetting the panels, gathering along the resident’s brow.

Behind it, the young shift in their gap.

The posts begin their low note.

It turns immediately. One hand reaches toward the place where the tool used to be kept, stops, and closes.

Then it goes out onto the changing ground.

At the first pool, it kneels and puts both hands into the water.
