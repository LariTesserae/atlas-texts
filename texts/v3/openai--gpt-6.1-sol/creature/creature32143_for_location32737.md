What lives here is easiest to see by looking away from it.

Watch the silver edge of a pool, then let your attention settle on the underside of the stone beside it. Something there interrupts the water. Not enough to make a wake. Only a small thickening, a place where the transparent sheet divides and joins again.

At first it looks like calcite.

There is a pale rim, irregular and slightly raised, following the shallow hollow beneath the stone. Within the rim, the surface is smoother than the surrounding limestone. A few grains adhere to it. Water passes over them without carrying them away.

Then one of the grains moves uphill.

The animal is about as long as the last joint of a finger, though its length is difficult to establish. Most of it is spread thin against the rock. Its margin tapers almost to nothing, and water covers the whole body, so that the boundary between flesh and current has to be found by watching how each responds to an obstruction. Water divides around a grain. The animal slowly incorporates it into the contour of its back.

It is not white in the way the plateau is white. It is translucent, with a faint milkiness where its tissues are thickest. Through those tissues, the limestone remains visible. On a smooth surface it nearly vanishes. Across a dark mineral seam, it becomes a small, pale blur.

There are no eyes.

Along the forward edge is a fringe of short filaments, usually folded beneath the body. When extended, they do not wave freely. Each lies against the stone, testing the thin layer of water that clings there. Their movements are minute and independent. One retracts while another reaches into a pore; several gather around the mouth of a crack no wider than a hair.

This is where the animal feeds.

The spring water rising through the limestone is not quite the same water that has crossed the open plateau. Where the two meet, microbes make a living from the difference. Their films are too thin to give the stone a visible color. The animal removes them with a soft, finely ridged mouth on its underside, advancing by fractions of its own length as it grazes.

A hollow that looks bare may sustain it for weeks. A neighboring hollow, equally wet and apparently identical, may offer nothing.

It knows the difference before it enters.

Its body is sensitive to dissolved substances, to pressure, to the slight warming of water that has traveled over an exposed face. It follows these changes under ledges and into seams, sometimes disappearing for months into places too narrow to admit a human fingertip. The open pool is not its principal home. It is a crossing between feeding surfaces.

Crossing is dangerous even without a predator.

The animal holds itself to the rock with a broad muscular underside. Its outer margin makes a seal, but not a perfect one: water passes beneath it through shallow grooves. Where the current strengthens, sections of the margin lift and settle in succession, releasing pressure without releasing the whole body.

An overturned specimen would be almost helpless. It could fold, contract, and make slow paddling motions, but these would do little against the water. Most animals washed loose are carried into the deeper passages. Some find another surface there. Others are pressed into gravel, where the water continues through them as readily as through everything else.

On the exposed rock, they keep close to roughness. A ridge scarcely higher than a grain can shelter an entire animal.

They are not entirely soft. Over time, mineral gathers on their upper surface. The first deposits are scattered points, then small plates, each separated from the next by a clear line of living tissue. Older animals carry a broken mosaic of limestone-colored armor. It is thickest at the rear, where the body has grown least recently.

This coating makes them harder to distinguish from the stone, but it also burdens them. Periodically they loosen one plate from below. The plate remains where it was, attached to the rock by its mineral edges, while the animal draws itself out from under it.

Some of the little scales beneath the springs are abandoned coverings. Most are only stone. Even held in the hand, the two are difficult to tell apart.

The animal feels the plateau’s noise through its whole underside.

A person standing above it hears one immense, continuous rush. Against the rock, that rush is divided into local vibrations: the tapping of drops on a hollow slab, the lower tremor of water passing through a throat, the fine irregular disturbance made by gravel shifting in a slot. Its tissues respond especially strongly to changes in these patterns.

A steady fall does not trouble it. A new vibration makes its fringe withdraw.

If a stone settles upstream, the animal tightens against the surface before the altered current reaches it. If a blind crustacean picks its way over the same ledge, the animal stops feeding until the small sequence of contacts has passed. It has no need to identify what moved. Remaining still is sufficient.

Its life proceeds slowly, but not uniformly. There are brief periods of abundance when a change deep in the mountain sends richer water through a seam. Then the feeding fringe stays extended, the transparent body thickens, and small rounded swellings appear beneath its rear edge.

The young leave as clear specks. For a short time they swim, using a band of fine hairs that is lost when they settle. Most enter the dark drainage beneath the basin. A few lodge against wet stone near a spring, where they flatten and begin to feed.

There is no nest and no gathering of adults around them. Several animals may share a productive seam, their margins nearly touching, each occupying a slightly different part of the mingling waters. When the seam weakens, they separate so gradually that the ledge seems to empty without anything departing.

One beneath a rounded slab has been there long enough for its discarded plates to form a pale trail behind it. The trail is shorter than a handspan. Across it, a transparent fringe is feeling into the stone.

Above the slab, the plateau remains open and apparently uninhabited. Water shines in every slot. The nearest fall keeps up its forceful, hidden pouring.

Underneath, the animal finds a patch that has not yet been grazed. Its mouth settles against it, and the forward edge of its body advances.
