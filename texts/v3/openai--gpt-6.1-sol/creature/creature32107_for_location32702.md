The first one you meet is holding something shut.

You do not see what. There is only a small strain in the air beside a basin, as if a cord has been pulled tight at waist height. The drizzle parts around it. Beneath it, the clay bears two shallow hollows and a third, narrower impression that might have been made by a heel, a knuckle, or the end of a walking stick.

It stays there all afternoon.

Once, you hear a wet little adjustment. One of the hollows deepens. Water runs into the other and immediately runs out again, although there is no channel.

By evening, the strain is gone. In its place stands a low wall, already firm enough to lean against. The top has been smoothed except for one spot where the smoothing stopped. If you put your hand there, you feel a warmth different from the general heat: concentrated, receding.

Whatever stood beside the basin has left before you learned how to look at it.

Others are easier to notice, though never because they are more complete.

Under a workbench, something draws its knees out of the way of your foot. You see the movement in the hanging cloth rather than a body. Beside a channel, the rounded head of a mallet lifts, hesitates, and returns to precisely the same place. A cup rises from the ground with its handle facing inward. For an instant there is a depression in the wet surface that could accommodate a seated person. Then the cup is set down and the depression relaxes.

They do not seem especially interested in concealing themselves. They make no effort to cover their tracks. Sometimes a tool is passed across the space in front of you with the plain inconvenience of someone reaching across a crowded table.

But the eye wants an outline before it will admit that someone is there, and these inhabitants seldom provide one.

A hand is the most likely part to appear.

Not abruptly. First there are creases in a wet slab that no longer lie flat. Then the clay between them rises. A thumb separates from what you had taken for a rounded corner. The hand rests a moment, palm down, gathering purchase.

Its skin is neither clay nor flesh, although both comparisons remain useful. Fine particles lodge in its folds. Water makes it swell a little. Where a scraper has nicked it, the cut closes slowly and leaves a pale line. It has a worn patch beneath the first finger, a small dark mark near the wrist, details so particular that you begin to look for that hand again.

You sometimes find it.

Or you find the same worn patch on a hand large enough to rest across a canal.

They are not giants who happen to become small. Size seems to enter their bodies only when required by the work. A finger smoothing the inside of a cup may continue beyond the wrist into an arm whose elbow lies past the horizon. Nothing in the movement acknowledges the difference.

At rest, even the number of their joints is uncertain. Their tools offer the clearest evidence. One handle has been polished by a palm and four fingers. Another has six separate places where pressure has darkened the wood. A third is smooth all the way around, as if grasped from within a closed ring.

Each is comfortable for someone.

You begin to distinguish them by how they leave things.

One rounds every corner twice. Its work has a soft extra lip at the edges, easily damaged before drying. Another leaves a narrow ridge wherever two strokes meet. A third sets cups into shallow recesses so they cannot overturn during a change in the ground.

The one with the marked wrist works very slowly. It lays out its wedges before starting, even for a little repair. When interrupted, it places the scraper edge upward on a folded scrap of cloth. Its footprints, when they occur, are close together.

There are disagreements.

Near a broad basin, a groove is filled in each morning and cut again each evening. The filling is careful. So is the cutting. Neither worker damages the other’s tools.

Eventually a small vessel is placed at the disputed point. Water gathers in it instead of entering the groove. For several days nothing changes. Then someone makes a recess for the vessel, and the basin acquires an outlet elsewhere.

You never witness a council or hear a voice. The things they leave seem sufficient to keep an argument going.

They also leave work for one another. A stack of slabs may include one with an awkwardly lifted corner. A scraper beside it has been freshly dressed. On the next visit, the corner is flat and the scraper is gone. Farther away, under another shelter, a new handle is being fitted to it.

Some inhabitants appear to spend their whole lives holding.

You find them near unsupported ceiling sheets and at the rims of pools that stand higher than their banks. Their presence is a pressure against your chest, a sense that the air has become difficult to pass through. Occasionally a shoulder shows beneath the haze: broad, bent, with water running down its hollow.

They cannot reach for a cup while bearing that weight. Another must bring it.

The cup tilts in the empty air. Its water diminishes. A few drops fall, and a patch of the ceiling lifts almost imperceptibly, as if the holder has taken a fuller breath.

Later the vessel returns to the ground.

This is the nearest thing to eating you see.

There may be other needs. Along the underside of lifted slabs are places worn smooth by prolonged contact. Narrow hollows in the darkness hold the shapes of bodies curled around themselves. Sometimes, while crossing a newly finished floor, you feel a slow movement beneath it. It stops when your steps stop.

You learn to go around such places.

The white growths belong to the inhabitants too, but not as markings on their bodies.

Watch an unfinished wall closely enough and you may see a pale branching figure break away from the others. It runs ahead of a bead of water, splitting where the bead will divide. When the water fails to follow, the figure pauses. Several branches withdraw. It tries again.

These little beings are easiest to see when they make a mistake.

Most vanish into the work as it is completed. Their paths become cracks, drainage lines, the fine texture left by a cloth pressed against wet clay. A few survive longer. They gather on tools waiting to be used, outlining chips that have not yet broken from an edge. They crowd the hands of workers who are hesitating.

You cannot tell whether they help or merely arrive early.

One spends several days tracing the rim of a cup. Each time you look, the cup has become a little less lopsided. On the fourth day the pale figure lies inside it, almost round. On the fifth there is only a white deposit in the bottom.

You do not wash it out.

Not all the work proceeds toward a finished landscape. Beyond one lean-to you find a row of abandoned attempts: a bank too steep to hold water, a ceiling prop whose bundled limbs have opened under their own strain, a slab with an impression pressed through both sides.

Something visits these at intervals.

It sits beside each piece for a long time. You know it is sitting because the wet ground gives slightly beneath its weight. Sometimes a fingertip becomes visible, following the edge of a fault. Nothing is corrected.

When it rises, the failed piece is wrapped in damp cloth.

There are many such bundles beneath that shelter. They remain workable. None is broken up for clay.

At the dark boundary, you can watch a new slab appear and mistakenly believe you have seen a birth. But the hands that bring it forward are already worn. The square carries a scraped place where a previous surface has been removed. Water beads in old finger marks beneath the new ones.

The beings come from farther in.

If you wait without looking directly into the darkness, you may glimpse one approaching. It has gathered enough of itself to carry a basket. There is a bend where a back might be, an elbow braced beneath the load, several feet taking care on ground not yet able to hold them.

It puts the basket down.

For a moment afterward it remains assembled. Heat passes from it in a slow, damp wave. One hand rests on the basket’s rim while the other searches for the cloth. You can see how tired it is in that small search.

Then it finds the corner, covers the clay, and loosens.

Its back sinks into the dark. The supporting elbow disappears. Last of all, the hand leaves the rim.

The basket stays where it was placed, within reach of whoever comes next.

Once, after a shift in the plain, you return to a shelter and find a second cup beside the first. It has an ordinary handle. The opening is large enough for your fingers, and the lip has been smoothed where your mouth would touch it.

A low bench stands beneath the cloth roof.

Neither object was there before.

The ground around them is firm, except for two shallow hollows at the bench’s far end. While you sit and drink, one of the hollows deepens slightly. The cup beside it lifts.

You keep your eyes on the water.

After a while, you hear it being set down.
