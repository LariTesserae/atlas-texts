The permanent inhabitants live in the thickness of the ridges.

Their rooms are not deep. In some places, less than a hand’s breadth of stone separates a chamber from the open air. You can stand above a sleeping household without knowing it, watching the grazers move across the basin while, below your soles, someone turns over and draws a membrane across their eyes.

The entrances are usually on the north sides of rocks. They look like gaps left when a slab settled badly: low, horizontal openings, often partly obstructed by a stone that cannot quite be moved. Inside, the passage widens. Its floor bends downward and then levels, with no step at which rainwater could gather.

There is no loose dust beyond the first bend.

An inhabitant coming out must flatten itself considerably. First the narrow head appears, then the forelimbs, folded backward along the body. The shoulders come through separately. For a moment the creature looks caught, but the rest follows in a smooth, patient movement, like something being drawn out of a close-fitting sleeve.

Standing, it is about the height of a grazer’s shoulder. Its body is longer than it first seems. Two pairs of limbs support it; a third, smaller pair are held beneath the throat. These small limbs end in fans of pale, blunt digits. They are almost always touching something.

The skin is gray where exposed and faintly pink in the folds. It has neither fur nor scales. Fine creases divide it into irregular patches, and dust adheres to the creases rather than the skin itself. An old individual acquires a map of the plateau’s colors: rose in the shoulder folds, white along the flanks, dark gray around the feet.

Its eyes are set low on the sides of the head, beneath translucent ridges. From above, you might think it had none.

It does not look toward a distant sound as you would. It raises one supporting foot and holds it just clear of the ground. The other three remain planted. Sometimes it does this for several minutes.

They spend the brightest hours inside. Their chambers are cool, but not uniformly cool. A sleeping ledge may be warm at one end and cold at the other. Young lie across this boundary, heads on the warm side, their narrow abdomens in the cold. Adults change position during the day as the boundary moves.

The rooms contain shallow cups, thin stone partitions, and objects like short lengths of root. There is no furniture that has to be assembled. The ledges rise directly from the floor. The cups can be lifted, although their undersides fit particular hollows so exactly that it seems improper to put them elsewhere.

A cup placed in the wrong hollow remains dry.

Much of household work consists of carrying these things a short distance, waiting, and carrying them back.

One individual takes a black nodule from a recess beside the sleeping ledge. It holds the nodule between its throat-hands until the ticking stops. Then it sets it in a cup and leaves the cup near the entrance.

Later another individual moves it a little farther into the shade.

By evening there is water around it.

They drink by touching the liquid with the underside of the jaw. A thin edge opens there, takes up the water, and closes again. Eating is slower. Pieces of the pale feeding film are folded into damp packets and held beneath the head while the small hands separate them strand by strand.

They also eat the waxy cups of certain plants, but only after the cups have emptied. They leave the stems standing. Around their entrances, old stems occur in dense little groves, each neatly shortened at a different height.

The grazers are neither livestock nor strangers. They come close to the dwellings and sometimes put their narrow faces through an entrance. An inhabitant may push a grazer away, or move aside to let it drink. There are individuals that repeatedly permit this and others that do not.

The grazers appear to remember the distinction.

Young inhabitants are pale enough for the dark contents of their bodies to show through. They have difficulty keeping their middle limbs off the ground. When tired, they settle onto all six and become nearly indistinguishable from the creatures clustered beneath loose slabs.

For some part of their early life, they live among those creatures.

An adult opening a nursery stone does not immediately collect its young. It rests the stone at an angle, waits for the crowded animals to adjust to the light, and extends one small hand into the crack. Several bodies approach. Some touch the hand and retreat. Eventually one climbs onto it.

The young are carried against the underside of the adult, gripping the creases of the skin. There may be two or three, although from a distance the additional legs look like a fringe.

They are not kept away from the gray seams. Instead, an adult places a young one just beside a seam and nudges a pebble onto the smooth surface. Both wait while it slides uphill.

Sometimes the young reaches after it. The adult catches the limb before it touches the gray.

Sometimes the pebble does not move. On those occasions the adult may bring more stones, or return another day. A particular seam can occupy a household for weeks.

The inhabitants leave little in the way of marks. They do make arrangements: three pebbles at the edge of a wet hollow, a length of dry stem resting across a crack, two empty cups placed mouth to mouth. Such things occur too often beside changes in the ground to be accidental.

Other inhabitants inspect them. They touch the objects, touch the soil nearby, and sometimes alter the arrangement.

There are disagreements. Two individuals will sit facing the same group of pebbles, repeatedly making and unmaking a pattern. Their supporting feet remain still. Only the throat-hands move, briskly and with increasing precision.

Once, after a long dispute, one carried a pebble several meters away and buried it.

The other waited until it had gone, recovered the pebble, and put it back.

Their dealings with the upright panels are less casual. They approach from the side, where the surface disappears, and pass a hand slowly through the space it occupies. Then they stand before it.

An inhabitant can wait there much longer than the birds do.

What it sees is not always useful. A patch of ground may appear wet and remain dry all day. A grazer visible through the panel may arrive, or another animal may come instead. Sometimes the discrepancy becomes very slight: one curled leaf shown open, a shadow reaching a little farther beneath a stone.

They bring objects to compare against these differences. A cup. A stalk. Occasionally a young one, held firmly underneath the body.

They do not seem to expect the panels to answer every time.

Nor do they seem to control everything beneath the plateau. When a damp patch fails, they investigate it. They press their jaws to the soil and listen through their feet. One may remain there while another goes to fetch a nodule from a dwelling.

If the patch stays dry, they abandon it. A circle of misplaced stones may remain around it for years.

There are hollows they avoid, even when water is present. There are doorways into which they will reach but not enter. The roofless square is one of these. Now and then an inhabitant stands outside its narrow opening and lays a small quantity of food on the cool floor. Nothing visibly takes it.

The food is not there the following morning.

Their households are small and change gradually. An individual may sleep in one ridge for most of a season, then begin visiting another. At first it carries nothing. Later it brings a cup. Eventually its old sleeping ledge is empty, and the cup has found a place in a different floor.

Others travel between several dwellings and seem welcome in all of them. They arrive with dust in unfamiliar colors caught in their folds. Their visits cause much touching of hands and objects. A nodule they have brought may be passed from one inhabitant to another until it begins to tick again.

At evening, a few come to the central depression.

They do not take the best places. Usually they remain near the margins, where the dark lines are faint or broken. While the animals drink, an inhabitant lowers its throat-hands to the soil and spreads every digit.

It waits.

If moisture rises, it lifts its hands clear. If nothing happens, it shifts a little farther along the line.

Occasionally one stays after the gathering has dispersed. In the last light its body is difficult to distinguish from the low stones. Its hands remain on the earth; one foot is raised.

Then it goes home.

Inside the ridge, the young are waking. Cups have to be checked. A sleeping ledge has cooled at the wrong end, and someone has moved the smallest bodies away from it. Near the entrance, a grazer breathes into the darkness, waiting to see whether it will be allowed to drink.

The returning inhabitant squeezes past its face. Dust rubs from the folds of its shoulders onto the stone.

For a while, from outside, you can see the small hands moving in the chamber. They carry a black nodule from one hollow to another, hold it, put it down, and wait for the first quiet tick.
