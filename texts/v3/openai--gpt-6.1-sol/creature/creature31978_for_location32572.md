The little animals have mouths, though these are difficult to find.

Beneath the striking organ is a seam which opens only when the animal has been lifted from the stone. Inside are two transparent combs, fitted so closely together that they appear to be one. The combs move sideways. Anything caught between them—a grain of the floor, a fragment of black crust, a flake from another animal’s foot—is reduced to a film and drawn inward.

They do eat. There is very little evidence of what they have eaten.

An animal removed from its colony continues to strike against whatever holds it. On a glove, the blows are almost inaudible. On a fingernail, they can be felt at the root of the nail. It will keep this up until it finds a surface through which another animal answers.

A solitary one grows lighter. Not smaller, not visibly thinner: lighter. After several hours, it weighs scarcely more than the dust adhering to it. Returned to an occupied blade, it works its feet between its neighbours and regains its weight.

Their nourishment is not entirely in their mouths.

At close range, the colonies are less orderly than they sound. Individuals miss their strokes. A damaged one may strike twice where it should strike once. Another will gradually cease moving its hard organ, although its feet remain busy, touching and withdrawing, touching and withdrawing. Around such an animal, the others alter their intervals.

There are many old animals in the chamber. Their central plates have become almost transparent. Through them one can see a small dark structure that contracts without quite closing, rather like a fist prevented from making a fist by something held in its palm.

The old ones are carried more often.

The broad grey creatures approach sideways, placing their wire fringes against the mineral before bringing their bodies near it. Each wire ends in a tiny flattened pad. The pads search among the little feet with extraordinary delicacy.

When a suitable animal has been found, two of the overlapping plates lift. Beneath them is a moist-looking cavity.

It is not wet. A probe comes away dry. But its lining has the softness and faint shine of the inside of a cheek, and it closes around the small animal without crushing it. For a time, the carrier becomes quiet. The washer inside can sometimes be seen through a narrow opening, striking against the lining.

When it emerges, its underside is free of mineral deposits. Its combs have been cleared. Occasionally it has a new foot, pale at first, which darkens as it takes weight.

The carriers retain what they remove. Under their plates are compacted layers of broken feet, old striking organs, and films of translucent shell. These layers form something more intricate than armour. They have channels and fitted cavities. A carrier that has attended many colonies begins to sound faintly even while standing still.

It is possible to follow one for a long time without seeing it collect anything for itself.

They rest in the shelters.

A carrier will flatten beneath a bench and draw its fringe back under the forward plates. Others settle beside it, close enough to touch but not overlap. They leave a narrow strip of floor empty before the bench, about the width required by a seated person’s boots.

The glove on the hook is visited occasionally. A carrier reaches up, explores the fingers, and withdraws. There is never anything inside it.

The buried creatures are harder to distinguish from their surroundings. Their skin adheres to the granules, so that uncovering one with a brush uncovers only a firmer version of the floor. Pressure reveals them more readily. A hand laid flat over a buried back encounters a resistance which yields, pauses, then yields again.

There are folds in that skin. Deep under the chamber, where their bodies meet the unbroken mineral, the folds turn inward around slender limbs. The limbs are immensely long. One may extend beneath several aisles before ending against the root of a blade.

Their knocking comes from those ends.

A buried creature pushes, waits, and relaxes. Elsewhere, another takes up the pressure. They do not lift the blades. The displacement is much too small to see. Nevertheless, if a blade is touched during a knock, its edge can be felt drawing slightly away from the finger.

The chamber would close without them.

Not suddenly. There would be no collapse, no warning fall of debris. Two edges would approach until the space between them could no longer admit a small animal. A narrow aisle would become narrower. One of the shelters would acquire a shallow crease along its roof.

The fresh mineral faces are under a steady load. All the creatures, even the smallest, bear some portion of it.

This does not explain why they bear it.

***

Something else lives among them.

Its position is usually described as being beyond the gap at the far wall. That is where it can most readily be heard, and where the small animals work hardest. But the description is not quite accurate.

Occasionally, between two blades well away from that wall, a visitor will see a pale surface where there should be darkness.

It is smooth but not polished. Fine wrinkles cross it, shallow enough to disappear when the surface shifts. Under the orange lamps it resembles the underside of a wrist. No veins show. There are places where the mineral has pressed into it and remained embedded, leaving a row of sharp splinters projecting outward.

The surface withdraws when watched closely.

Its withdrawal is slow. It does not slip out of sight so much as become less available to sight: deeper in the slot, flatter against one side, finally indistinguishable from the dark backing of the stone.

The small animals make room for it. They climb over one another, sometimes three deep, keeping their striking organs turned toward the mineral. The carriers stop a little distance away.

At one of these appearances, a fold opened in the pale surface. Along its inner edge were six black structures, blunt and heavily worn.

They might have been feet. They were each larger than a person’s hand.

No other part emerged.

The chamber’s resident has never been seen whole. Its body may occupy many of the narrow separations at once. A touch against one pale patch can be followed by a tightening in another, twenty metres away. Between those points are blades too closely fitted for any ordinary body to pass.

It appears to be soft wherever it can be reached, yet the things that serve it are built to withstand enormous pressures.

It has periods of rest. These are recognizable because the colonies become louder.

The little animals close their spacing. The carriers stand on the lower edges of the blades instead of the floor. Beneath the granules, the long backs rise slightly, taking a little more of the chamber’s weight.

During these periods, the pale surfaces are sometimes visible for hours. They bear impressions: the mesh of a lamp cage, a bolt head, four straight parallel marks.

Once, a patch near the engine bore the pattern of coarse woven cloth.

There is no indication that these impressions trouble it.

***

The engine has become part of its accommodation.

Small animals gather beneath the bolted feet. Carriers rub the lowest ribs of the casing with their fringes. The buried creatures leave the belt’s course unobstructed, although their limbs cross everywhere else beneath the floor.

When the hand crank is moved, even slightly, the resident responds.

A tightening passes through the chamber. It can be felt in the railing before it reaches the floor. Then the colonies nearest the machine fall silent in succession, leaving the engine briefly audible by itself.

The crank resists further movement.

Released, it returns to the position in which it was found.

Someone used to stand there. The cuts on the railing are shallow on one side and deep on the other, as though made by a person working with the wrong hand. A few continue around the underside, where they could not easily have been seen while being made.

The lunch tin is still closed. Inside is something that moves when the flywheel turns. The movement is slight, no more than a dry settling against the metal.

The washer-creatures covering the tin strike gently. They have worn little bright points into its surface, but none approaches the latch.

There may once have been a distinction between the workers and the life of the chamber. The shelters suggest one. So does the glove, made for a hand with an ordinary arrangement of fingers.

The resident’s dealings with people are harder to infer.

If a visitor stands still for long enough, a carrier will approach and examine the edges of the visitor’s boots. It is particularly attentive to loose stitching. A washer may climb onto the leather and strike experimentally.

The floor continues to release each footprint.

But beneath a person who has remained in one place, a buried body will sometimes settle into a shallow curve. It does not touch the feet. It lies just below them, presenting its back as if waiting for a weight it already knows.

At the far wall, the animals holding the gap open are unusually large and almost colourless. Their feet reach deep into both sides. Some have lost their striking organs. They tremble continuously.

Through that opening, during the resident’s quieter periods, a pale fold can be glimpsed.

It presses toward the chamber, then eases back.

The animals ease with it. For a moment their feet straighten and their plates lift clear of the stone. Then the pressure returns, and they brace themselves again.

They never have room enough to turn around.
