The oldest crab lives beneath the third submerged step, in a hollow that fits it badly now.

It fitted better several molts ago. There is still room for the shell, but not for all the legs unless some are folded beneath the body and others laid sideways along the clay. One claw rests outside the hollow. Water passes over it constantly, and a small snail has settled near its hinge. When the crab moves, the snail shuts itself against the shell and waits.

The crab is not white everywhere. Its upper surface is pale, but the underside has the yellow-gray color of old mortar. Brown collects where one plate overlaps another. There are faint scratches on the broad back, most too shallow to feel, and a deeper groove near the edge where a piece of falling masonry struck it. That wound survived the last molt as a slight irregularity in the new shell.

Its eyes stand on short stalks. They are small and milky, still movable, though there is little here for them to distinguish. A shadow crossing the upper vents may cause the stalks to withdraw. So may a change in pressure, or something touching the water far away. The crab’s more exact knowledge comes through its legs.

It knows the step by the places where the stone presses against its joints. It knows the floor outside by a crack, a slope, and the narrow strip of warmer water that crosses two plates before dispersing. It can find a dead fish before the fish has settled. It can feel a shrimp landing on its back and usually does nothing.

Several young crabs live beneath its rear legs.

They are not all its offspring. Their shells are thin enough that the moving parts within them show as dull shadows. They shelter there because the larger body breaks the current and because most predators hesitate to reach beneath it. When the old crab shifts, they move with it, keeping close to the floor. Occasionally one is caught and eaten. The others return.

The chamber does not offer much separation between refuge and danger.

A fish holding beside a pillar may be feeding, hiding, or waiting to feed on something that is hiding there. The grazers have soft, rounded lips that touch the clay repeatedly as they work their way across it. Their bodies are narrow, with translucent fins and a little silver deep inside the head. Most of their color lies in the food passing through them.

The hunting fish look nearly the same until they open their mouths.

They wait at the edge of a warm flow, facing slightly downward. Their fins make corrections too small to see from far away. When a grazer enters reach, there is a brief bend of the whole body. The water carries the disturbance to the next pillar. Shrimp lift from the seams, settle again, and resume combing.

There are places a finger-length fish never goes. Beyond a certain lintel, the current is colder. Under one bench, a slab has dropped low enough that its belly would brush the stone. Those boundaries can hold for a lifetime. A fish may inhabit only a few alcoves of the chamber, turning through the same openings until the openings change.

The shrimp travel farther.

At close range they are less like glass than like wet scraps of very thin horn. Their tails have a faint yellow edge. The white line through the body swells and narrows as they feed. Their forelegs end in brushes so fine that the separate bristles vanish against the stone.

They work especially carefully at the mortar seams.

A shrimp braces itself with the rear legs, spreads its antennae, and draws one foreleg across a patch of film. It brings the brush to its mouth, cleans it, and begins again. Sometimes it encounters a filament too firmly attached to pull free. Then the whole animal leans backward. A little tuft comes away, and the shrimp tumbles half a body-length before finding its footing.

Around the shafts, the films grow faster than they can be eaten.

They are not one kind of life. Some cling directly to the clay lining, living on substances brought up in the water. Others grow on those first layers or consume what they release. Still smaller things move among them, dividing in pockets between grains of mineral. The visible skin is their crowded surface.

Where a snail has grazed, a narrow clean track exposes the red-brown clay. Within days the track dulls. Its margins disappear before its center does.

The snails themselves are flat enough to enter spaces that seem closed. Their feet spread beyond their shells in colorless rims. On the undersides of arches they hang with their bodies pressed tight to the stone, mouths moving against it. A loose flake may carry a snail down to the floor. It remains withdrawn for a while, then extends itself and feeds wherever it has landed.

Many never make the journey back up.

Their shells collect near the outlets, among shrimp skins, fragments of crab shell, and powder from the building. Worms pass through this deposit without coming fully into the open. A pale segment appears between two shells and disappears beneath a third. Their burrows admit water into the layers below, where life continues more slowly.

Nothing large lives in the air pocket.

There is warmth there, and moisture, but almost nothing to eat. The upper steps receive only what splashes onto them or creeps too far. A snail reaching the wet boundary may stay until its exposed flesh begins to contract. Then it turns, leaving a shining curve across the salt.

Below that boundary, the water is crowded with young animals too small to recognize without bringing them close to the light. Some have legs they cannot yet use for standing. Some are little more than a gut between two transparent surfaces. They gather in the round hollows of the benches, where the flow turns back on itself and keeps them from being carried toward the drains.

When a crab passes, the larvae shift together. For a moment the depression looks empty. Then they become visible again, suspended at a slightly different angle.

The old crab sometimes visits the split bench.

It approaches along the low partition, moving one leg at a time until the others have secure contact. At the gap it stops and probes. Shell fragments have lodged there, and more have accumulated against them. The crab lifts some, turns them in its claws, scrapes off the edible coating, and puts them down without care for their original position.

Often they fall into the crack.

Water rich in dissolved minerals moves through the packed fragments. Their surfaces acquire crusts. A piece that was loose one season resists the crab the next. It pulls harder, braces itself, then abandons the attempt.

The gap is becoming narrower. Shrimp still enter it, but the largest can no longer turn around inside. They back out with their antennae laid against their bodies.

The crab eventually returns to the step. Its journey takes longer when it stops to feed. Once home, it wedges its shell into the hollow and folds the legs that can be folded. The small crabs gather beneath it again. The snail near its claw hinge opens.

There is no resident that knows the room as a room.

The fish know passages and resting currents. The shrimp know surfaces that yield food. The crab knows the fit of the step, the reach of its claw, and disturbances arriving through the floor. Even the long lives here are lived among a few immediate things.

Above them, a drop gathers on the vault.

When it falls, the sound passes across the water. For an instant, fins stiffen, antennae rise, and the old crab draws its exposed claw a little closer to its body.

Then the shrimp lowers its brush to the seam again.
