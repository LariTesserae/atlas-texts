At first, it is another length of root.

It lies across the lip of a pool, dark above and pale where the water touches it. Fern stems lean over its back. There is a shallow groove along its upper surface, filled with rain, and two small plants have rooted near one end. Nothing about it requires attention until the pool begins to rise.

Then the groove tilts. The plants move together. A forefoot comes out of the moss and rests against the rock, spreading four blunt digits over the wet surface.

The animal has been awake the whole time.

***

They live chiefly where water changes its manner of moving: at the transition from a sheet to a seep, from a buried river to a pool, from cold surface drainage to the faintly warmed flow coming out of the stone. There may be none in a broad stretch of seemingly suitable forest, and then several within a few paces of one unremarkable spring.

An adult is about as long as a person lying down, though much of that length is tail. Its body is low and heavy through the shoulders. The hindquarters taper gradually, without the obvious division between trunk and tail seen in most animals. On firm ground it walks with deliberate, almost awkward steps. Under a root mat it becomes supple. The ribs draw inward; the shoulders turn; the animal enters a passage that looked too narrow even for its head.

Its outer coat consists of coarse, flattened hairs, overlapping closely enough to shed moving water but not to keep the animal dry. Beneath them is a dense layer of fine fur. Water caught on the outer coat freezes readily. Water does not easily reach the skin.

The back is usually brown-black, streaked with the rusty deposits of whichever watershed it inhabits. Algae grow on the older hairs. In the sheltered hollows behind the shoulders, liverworts sometimes establish themselves. These are not disguises the animal has arranged. They are consequences of its habits. It spends so much time lying still in wet places that other lives begin upon it.

When it moves, a small part of the hillside goes with it.

The head is broad, with little external ear. Its eyes are set high enough to remain above water while the mouth is submerged, but sight is not its principal means of finding things. Along the muzzle, and in narrow bands beneath the jaw, are stiff pale filaments. Some are long enough to touch both sides of a root passage. Others barely extend beyond the fur.

A resting animal spreads these filaments against the ground.

It can distinguish water moving through open stone from water passing through roots. It can feel a chamber filling below a pool and the irregular flutter produced by a fish holding against a current. The footsteps of the broad-hoofed browsers arrive as slow compressions. A predator approaching through a drainage tunnel makes a different disturbance: a traveling pressure in the water, interrupted whenever its body presses against the walls.

The animal cannot perceive the whole plateau. Its attention has a reach, and beyond that reach the country is unknown. But within it, much that appears motionless is moving.

***

They eat slowly.

At the margins of warm springs they graze the tender undersides of vegetation, lifting a mat with the muzzle and taking the pale growth beneath. They search submerged roots for small shell-bearing animals. With their forefeet they turn loose stones and scrape away some of the dark film below, leaving other patches untouched.

The mouth has a hard front edge and broad grinding teeth farther back. An adult can sever a tough root, but usually works at it from several angles, pausing between bites. Young animals are impatient and tug at things they cannot yet cut. They are often seen with a mouthful of fiber, braced backward while the plant holds them in place.

Their digestion is sustained by dense communities of smaller organisms. A youngster acquires these while feeding beside its elders, licking wet surfaces they have disturbed and swallowing scraps from their mouths. For its first season it smells strongly of milk and clean moss. Later it takes on the mineral odor of the family’s resting pools.

Their colonies differ from one watershed to another. An animal moved far from its native water may feed adequately and still grow thin. Those that travel tend to follow connected drainage systems, stopping for days at intermediate springs. They eat sparingly in new water. Sometimes they turn back.

***

They are social, but a group is seldom gathered in full view.

One lies at an opening beneath the bank. Another rests upstream with only its nose exposed. Two young animals sleep in the warm hollow under a tree whose roots have grown around an old spring. An elder may be thirty paces away, apparently alone.

Through water and saturated ground, they remain in contact.

Their calls, when audible, are low and brief: a closed-mouth hum, a rough exhalation, a note felt more readily against the breastbone than heard. At close quarters they touch muzzles and shoulders. A youngster returning from an excursion presses its jaw beneath an adult’s chin and holds it there.

They also tap with their forefeet.

The taps travel poorly through dry peat and well through stone. A short sequence may bring another animal to a feeding place. A repeated, widely spaced pulse keeps the young from crossing an unstable mat. When frightened, an individual strikes too quickly and too hard, producing a muddled signal that often alarms its companions before they know the cause.

The regular knocking from the plateau’s depths is familiar to them, but they do not mistake it for one another. They wait through it. An adult listening beside a pool may lift its jaw after each knock, then lower it again to feel what follows.

Their own exchanges occupy the intervals.

***

Certain channels remain open because these animals use them.

A narrow passage beneath a slope may have carried generations between a sleeping hollow and a feeding pool. Their bodies polish its lower stones. Their claws loosen roots that press too far inward. Leaves entering it are pulled apart or pushed into side pockets.

They do not keep every passage clear. A family may abandon a route for no visible reason, allowing sediment and roots to close it. Years later, the old tunnel remains recognizable only as a softer band beneath the moss.

At springs, their feeding has larger effects. Removing a mat of growth releases a small flow. Digging after buried food exposes a seam. Water enters the disturbed place, and the disturbance becomes permanent.

An animal will sometimes work at such a spot long after it has stopped eating. It braces its hindfeet, catches a root between its teeth, and pulls steadily. Then it rests with its muzzle against the opening.

Other members of the group may join it. They work in turns, leaving room for the water. There is no neat edge to what they make. A loosened stone remains where it falls unless it obstructs the flow. Plants take hold between the claw marks.

Some of these places are older than any living member of the group. The young learn them by following adults. They learn where to lie, where to drink, and which submerged openings can admit a body but will not let it turn around.

An old animal carries much of this knowledge. It has crossed ground that is now a lake margin, slept beside springs that no longer rise, and waited through winters when an ordinarily warm passage became cold.

When familiar water changes, it is often the oldest that leaves first.

***

One such animal might remain beside a failing spring for several days.

There is still water in the basin. The vegetation is still green. To an observer, little has happened. But the flow beneath the basin has weakened, and each pulse arrives a little later.

The animal feeds nearby and returns. It lays its jaw on the same stone. The younger ones become restless. One ventures into the outlet and comes back carrying the smell of stagnant water.

Eventually the elder climbs uphill.

It moves slowly, stopping often, pressing its muzzle into the wet ground. The others follow unevenly. They wait while it doubles back across a patch of shrubs, then descend toward a place where there has never been an open pool.

Here the moss has lifted slightly.

The elder lies upon it until evening. Beneath its chest, trapped air passes through a seam. The young crowd against its flanks. Later, one begins pulling at the moss, and another noses into the opening.

By morning there is a shallow hollow full of cloudy water.

They may stay. They may continue farther down the slope. Not every change leads them to a better place.

***

Their young are born in chambers above the usual water level, lined with torn grass and the dry inner fibers of dead wood. They are small, dark, and almost bare along the muzzle. For several weeks they know the plateau mostly through warmth, pressure, and the bodies around them.

The first time a youngster enters a stream, an adult lies downstream of it.

The current takes the youngster a short distance. It scrambles, finds no purchase, and comes against the adult’s shoulder. There it clings with all four feet. Later it tries again.

Old animals become less willing to enter cold water. Their coats grow uneven; the plants on their backs become larger. They spend longer at warm seams, shifting position when a companion arrives. Sometimes an old one sleeps while a youngster rests its jaw across the same stone, feeling the flow beneath them.

At dusk, when cloud enters the trees, the group begins to move between its scattered resting places. Their passage is quiet but not soundless. A wet foot releases from rock. A branch lifts and settles. Water drains from a tail onto the leaves below.

One pauses at a dark opening under the roots. It lowers its head, waits through the next knock from below, and slips inside.
