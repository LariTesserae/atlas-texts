The people are easier to see when they move. Standing beside a wall, a person in a pale hide coat can disappear into the limestone, leaving only the dark band across their eyes and the slight pink of a lower lip. Then a hand rises, or a shoulder turns, and the wall gives up a woman carrying a jar.

The dust does not make everyone the same color. It gathers differently on each occupation. Stonecutters have white creases behind their ears. Those who cure hides keep a darker, greasy dust along their wrists. Pool workers have clean shins and mineral crusts on their sandals. Children are dusty everywhere except where they have recently been licked by an animal.

Most people wear their hair short, but there are old men with thin braids wrapped in cords and women who tuck long hair into soft caps. A few shave their heads with flakes of hard stone. Clothing has many small fastenings rather than one large knot. A sleeve can be taken off without removing a coat; a torn panel can be exchanged; a child’s garment can be enlarged one strip at a time.

Their bags are often more carefully made than their houses.

A good household bag opens flat. Its pockets show everything at once: needles in a bone case, scraping flakes, a coil of cord, a folded drinking skin, grease wrapped in a membrane, a little packet of salt. People disagree about how much should be kept ready. One woman packs her sleeping mat every morning and is teased for it. Her brother leaves his possessions spread across three rooms and is teased more severely. Neither considers the other’s habit a moral failing until something has to be moved.

They are strong in ways that are not immediately impressive. They can hold a slab just above the ground while someone clears its bedding. They can carry an awkward jar against the same hip for a long distance. Their hands know how little force will keep a load balanced, and how quickly that force must change when another person loses their footing.

A child helping to lift a threshold is not asked to bear much weight. The child is asked to feel when the weight changes.

“Now?” the child says.

“Not yet.”

“Now?”

The adults lift.

There is pride in learning this, and impatience with those who never do. Someone may be generous, funny, and good at curing meat, yet remain the person nobody wants on the far end of a large stone.

***

They attend to the ground, but they do not spend their lives solemnly listening to it. Most movements are familiar. A spoon falling from a shelf does not empty a district. A cracked lintel may remain cracked throughout someone’s marriage.

The old mason who sits with his hand on a doorstep is also waiting for his daughter to return a borrowed mallet. He has heard the floor settle twice that morning. He has heard his neighbor explain both sounds incorrectly. He does not correct her until she proposes moving a load-bearing stone.

Knowledge of movement is unevenly distributed. Some people are very good at reading dust. Others hear a change in the sound of a jar placed on the floor. One herder notices that animals have stopped sleeping beside a particular wall. A woman who has no reputation for building has nevertheless predicted three failures because she remembers exactly where the winter damp used to reach.

These observations are traded along with goods. A buyer turns a bowl over, tests its rim, asks its price, and mentions a hollow sound under the seller’s eastern stair.

Warnings have consequences. A trader who spreads a false report can empty a row of stalls and acquire the best position for himself. A householder who conceals a spreading crack can leave a neighbor sleeping beside it. There are disputes in which nobody doubts what happened, but everyone argues over when it became possible to know.

In such disputes, children are sometimes useful witnesses. They remember where they put their little bones.

***

Households are larger than the rooms they occupy.

A household may include two sisters, their partners, a father who sleeps poorly, three children, and someone who has lived with them since a wet season ruined his own rooms. They need not all sleep together. One person has a dry corner across the court; another uses a room belonging to a cousin. What joins them is where their tools return, who prepares their food, and whose possessions they are expected to carry when called.

People ask, “With whom do you put down your things?”

It is a practical question, but it can become an intimate one.

Courtship involves a great deal of borrowing. A young man lends a very good needle and must find reasons to ask whether it is still needed. A woman offers to help adjust a drip jar that one person could easily move. Lovers develop private signs from ordinary ones: the gesture for *wait* made against the chest, the gesture for *come through* performed with two fingers inside a sleeve.

There is privacy, despite the doors that must remain passable. A lowered hide means something. So does a pair of sandals turned inward beside it. Neighbors pass through quietly when they must and explain afterward. Someone who regularly claims necessity when there was another route soon finds fewer doors willing to open for him.

The law prevents a person from blocking an escape. It does not prevent embarrassment, gossip, or a long quarrel over muddy feet.

***

Their wealth is portable, but it is not all light.

A numbered set of building stones can make a family prosperous. So can several dependable drips, a breeding group of grazers, or the obligation of other households to help with heavy work. These possessions carry very different risks. Stones must be lifted. Animals must be fed. A drip can migrate into a difficult place.

Water owners employ people to watch vessels through the night. The watcher adjusts the jars, wipes grit from the rims, and listens for the hollow note that means one is nearly full. It is cold work. Wealthier owners sleep while someone else sits beneath the wet ceiling.

There are families who have watched other families’ water for generations.

They know the seams more intimately than the owners do. When a wet line divides, or a drip wanders beyond the boundary recorded in an old agreement, that knowledge becomes troublesome. The argument may begin with a finger held against a ceiling stain and end before witnesses, with jars, tally bones, and three different accounts of where a vanished wall once stood.

The city has poor people who possess almost nothing that cannot be worn. They move readily, but readiness is not comfort. When everyone is carrying possessions, a person without a load can be asked to carry someone else’s. Sometimes this is paid work. Sometimes it is a favor that will be returned. Sometimes the distinction is allowed to remain unclear for years.

***

The grazers are not regarded as particularly beautiful animals.

Their flexible mouths make a wet scraping sound at close quarters. They push through loose household arrangements in search of damp stone. An old one may develop the habit of lifting a hide door with its snout. They can smell a freshly exposed surface through a wall and will stand facing it for hours, obstructing a passage with apparently limitless patience.

Nevertheless, people become attached to them.

Each herd contains animals known by the shape of an ear, a scar on the muzzle, a way of turning before lying down. Children name them more extravagantly than adults do. A slate-gray female officially recorded as belonging to the third breeding group may be called Grandmother-With-the-Bad-Ideas by everyone who feeds her.

A herder rests a foot against a sleeping animal and feels its jaw working. Another kneels to examine the faint coating on a new bank. Grazing is managed with temporary barriers and careful timing. Too many mouths leave the rock bare; too few allow an exposed patch to dry before it can be used.

People scrape some of the coating for themselves. Fresh, it is slick and faintly bitter. Dried in thin cakes, it thickens broth. There are favored surfaces whose growth is said to taste cleaner, and there are people who maintain that nobody can tell the difference once enough fat has been added.

The animals eaten at feasts have usually been known to those eating them. Children are allowed to be upset. They are also given work to do.

***

There is music, though little of it carries far.

Small clay whistles produce clear, narrow notes. Strings braided from hair are stretched across rib frames. Some instruments are played against the body so that the player feels a vibration stronger than anyone else hears. In the quiet cooking courts, a singer can use a very soft voice and still gather listeners.

Songs remember routes. A verse turns left at a washing place that no longer exists; another descends stairs buried long ago. Singers preserve the turns because they belong to the rhythm. People argue about what the words originally meant.

Children make newer songs about adults who slipped, lovers who chose unfortunate meeting places, and a market keeper whose best jar was stolen by a grazer. Those songs travel quickly.

There are carvings too, mostly small enough to hold. A sleeping animal. Two figures carrying a slab. A hand closed around something that has broken away. Wear gradually removes the details, and an heir may treasure a smooth piece of bone without knowing what it depicts.

***

The dead leave awkward possessions.

A well-made coat can be altered. A drip right can be divided, though not always fairly. Stones can be passed down with their numbers intact. The harder things are arrangements everyone followed because one particular person was alive: which room was kept quiet in the afternoon, whose bowl was filled first, who knew where the spare wedges were.

For a while, the survivors continue these arrangements.

Then someone needs the quiet room. A child uses the bowl. The wedges are found in a bag nobody thought to open.

There are burial chambers at the dry margins of the hollow, some still entered, some known only through records. Families keep small tokens outside them so that remembrance does not depend on a passage remaining open. Even so, an elderly person may ask to be carried to a chamber that can no longer be reached. The answer requires care.

The inhabitants are capable of staying too long. They love particular rooms. They refuse to abandon walls their parents built. They become angry when someone suggests dismantling a threshold polished by generations of feet.

At dusk, the mason calls his daughter over.

She has brought back his mallet. She sees the wedges already laid beside him and sets it down.

For several moments they examine the threshold. A shallow crescent has opened beneath one end. He touches it, then gives her the tool.

They work from opposite sides. Once the stone is free, they stand with it between them, judging its weight.

He tells her where to put it. She says that place will obstruct the jars.

He looks across the room, considers, and chooses another.

Together they carry it inside.
