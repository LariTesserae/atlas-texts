The first one you notice is carrying a door.

It comes along a polished path between two ribs, the green slab tilted across its back, the root handle knocking softly against something beneath its shoulder. At first the carrier seems to be a bundle of material advancing on the floor’s own machinery. Then it pauses while a band of leaves changes direction, lifts one end of the door to let the band pass, and continues.

It has four walking limbs and two shorter limbs folded beneath its chest. Its body is narrow enough to enter a maintenance slot sideways. Most of its apparent bulk is a loose coat of white fibers, worn smooth where the door rests against it. Through the coat you can see plates overlapping along its flanks. They are not quite bark. Each has a translucent margin, and several have been replaced with plates of a slightly different color.

Its head is blunt and almost featureless. A seam runs around the front of it. When the creature stops at a survey marker, the seam opens, and a small fan of filaments touches the three printed distances in turn.

It spends a long time on the return distance.

Then it takes the door down into a hollow, where you lose sight of it.

Others appear over the following days. There are more of them than there seemed to be, but they are seldom standing where a visitor would think to look. They wait under conveyor growths, sit within the divided roots of pillars, or lie with their limbs inserted into sockets in the floor. In that posture they are difficult to distinguish from mechanisms left out of use.

None is quite the same shape as another.

Some have long hind limbs and travel with a slow, vaulting gait. Some have bodies so flattened that their coats trail on both sides like strips of peeled tissue. Those living around the newer mills are usually pale and symmetrical. In old districts, individuals carry mismatched joints, dark repairs, additional hands. One has a walking limb branching halfway down into two feet, both of which it uses. Another drags a slender length of cable that enters its back and disappears into a leafy seam. It can travel several hundred yards before the cable tightens.

These are not defects that the mills seem disposed to correct.

They eat by fastening themselves to things.

A hand closes around a stem. Fine hairs emerge from its palm and enter the stem’s microscopic passages. The creature remains still while a little of the stem’s chalky color passes into its fingers. Sometimes a throat plate trembles. Sometimes the leaves immediately around it lean away.

Feeding takes hours. It looks much like listening.

There are public feeding places at certain crossings: short, thick columns with polished constrictions where hands have rested for generations. The columns renew their outer tissues continuously. In a productive district they are warm enough to soften the joints of an old individual. After flowering, they become cold, and those waiting around them hold their limbs close to their bodies.

The residents also collect loose grains. They keep them in pouches made from folded leaf margins, separating them by touch. A pale grain may be food, a dark one replacement material, a nearly transparent one something to place beneath a ceiling panel and leave there for a year.

They are careful with these distinctions. A pouch spilled on the floor brings everyone nearby down onto their knees.

Much of their time is spent opening the place.

A factory may manufacture a ladder perfectly and still grow across the opening the ladder was meant to reach. A wall panel, once awakened, begins thickening toward the best available light. A duct sends a root through its own bore. The residents trim these tendencies back, persuade them elsewhere, or give up and mark a new route.

They carry short tools whose working edges resemble folded leaves. The tools do not cut until held against living tissue for a while. Then the edge stiffens, and the tissue parts without smoke.

One individual may spend an entire day beside a seam, removing the fine growth that repeatedly closes it. Another visits the same seam, touches the floor on either side, and makes a small adjustment with its feet. Afterward the first one can leave.

They have arguments about such things.

An argument can last several days and involve no audible sound. The participants stand with their hands on the same conducting root. Their coats rise and settle. Leaves around them alternate between two angles. Other residents stop to inspect the pattern, then go away or add a hand of their own.

Occasionally the root is abandoned by everyone. It remains warm for a long time afterward.

They make sounds chiefly when they cannot touch.

A hollow plate beneath the head produces two or three dry clicks. A longer call is a fine rattling, like seeds shaken inside a narrow casing. It carries surprisingly far through the nearly silent mills. The listeners turn, but not always toward the caller. They turn toward whatever nearby surface has repeated the sound most clearly.

Their speech through the floor is much richer. It can be seen, faintly, in the translucent leaves of old hollows: small changes of brightness moving down veins, branching, meeting, becoming too complicated to follow.

A resident can speak to someone on the other side of a district without knowing where that other person is. The reply arrives under its feet.

Travel nevertheless troubles them.

Before a long journey, a traveler takes a strip of tissue from a place it intends to return to. It winds the strip around a forelimb and feeds it occasionally from its palm. While the strip remains alive, something at home continues to recognize it.

The strips are not infallible. There are individuals with whole wrists hidden under them.

At the crossings, travelers compare their strips with the survey leaves. They stroke the printed distances, then walk a short way down a route and come back. Sometimes they do this many times. Companions wait without impatience.

One of the flattened residents has been observed returning to the same crossing for years. Each time it brings a different object: a jointed pipe, a roll of dormant tissue, a small wheel still trying to turn. It puts the object beside the marker, examines the returning distance, and leaves by another path.

A little structure has accumulated there. Other residents maintain it. None has moved into it.

Their homes are usually smaller than their bodies would lead you to expect.

They live in gaps kept from closing: beneath a root flare, inside the thickness of a mill roof, in the space between two layers of floor. A dwelling may have no room for standing. Several bodies fit together in it with limbs tucked into the intervals between their neighbors.

Inside, the leaves are rubbed smooth. Conducting veins have been encouraged to emerge in convenient places. There are pouches, tools, bundles of spare coat fiber, and objects that have no evident use.

Many keep the seed stamps from vanished mills.

These are small flower heads, dried hard, their faces bearing the impressions they once pressed into bark. A resident may take one out and hold it for a long time without examining the mark. Its filaments touch the worn edges instead.

Others keep lengths of defective material: a stem that divided into five unequal ducts, a sheet whose veins describe a shape with no manufacturing purpose, a pulley grown with a cavity in its center. Such pieces are passed from hand to hand. Sometimes they are set out in a clear patch of floor, and several residents gather around them without feeding or working.

Their coats change while they watch. A ripple travels from one body to the next.

The young have little coat at all.

They begin as pale, folded things in shallow cradles attached to feeding columns. Their limbs are packed against them, and their head seams remain sealed. Through their skin you can see grains traveling in orderly streams, stopping where a joint is being made.

Several adults tend each cradle. They open passages, remove grains that have stalled, and rest their palms against the developing body. No single adult appears to own it.

The young are allowed to unfold unevenly. One limb may be usable long before the others. It reaches out of the cradle and catches whatever passes within reach: fibers, tools, an adult’s coat. Once it has caught something, it is reluctant to let go.

For a while, everything it touches attempts to feed it.

Later this stops. The young individual must learn which surfaces admit it and which do not. Adults take it to warm columns, then to cooler ones. They let it grip a harmless dormant stem until it grows frustrated and rattles beneath its sealed head.

The first journey away from the cradle is very short. The return is supervised closely.

They play in the shallow hollows.

Young ones press their feet into selected veins and make lines of leaves fold. Two or three can send a pattern around a basin, each trying to intercept it before it reaches the next. Some patterns continue after the players have gone. For days a little square of ground will open and shut at irregular intervals.

Adults occasionally join them. An old, cable-bound resident may contribute one pulse from a resting hand, and the entire hollow changes its game.

They also build things that the floor cannot use.

Small arches. Spirals. A ladder with every rung joined to the next. These are made from shed fibers moistened with gas from the mouth seam and allowed to harden. The objects last until the floor notices them and draws nourishment from their bases.

Sometimes a young one carries its construction from place to place to keep this from happening.

The six-legged seed eaters come into their dwellings.

They slip beneath the residents’ coats and pick defective grains out of the folds. Individuals being cleaned remain motionless, even when the little animals crawl over the open seam of the head.

There are varieties of seed eater that live nowhere else. Some are flat as flakes and occupy the spaces beneath body plates. Others have long grasping hind feet with which they hold on during travel. A resident returning from a distant mill can bring a population of unfamiliar animals with it.

The newcomers are usually tolerated. Occasionally the residents gather around one, touch it with their filaments, and carefully carry it to an isolated feeding column. The column is then removed from the network.

What they recognize in it is not visible.

The ceiling creatures receive much less attention.

When one drifts down, the residents become still along with the plants. They close their head seams. Those feeding release their hands.

The descending creature may pass directly over them. Its wings have no opaque parts; it is visible through the distortions it makes in the light. Beneath the wings hangs a narrow body, darker than one expects, with several folded appendages.

Sometimes it settles on a resident.

The wings drape across the coat. A limb that has been held awkwardly for years relaxes. A body plate changes color. A short train of pale grains emerges from a joint and hangs in the air before the ceiling creature gathers it.

At other times the resident does not move again.

The others wait until the wings have risen out of sight. Then they approach. They touch the still body, not quickly and not all at once.

Whatever happened does not seem to surprise them. That is not the same as welcoming it.

An ordinary death takes longer.

The coat loosens first. Feeding becomes difficult. A hand remains attached to a column after the rest of the body has tried to leave.

Companions move the dying individual into a shallow depression and keep the leaves from growing across it. They bring warm roots to its palms. They lie alongside it, communicating through whatever passages remain open.

Eventually they stop bringing food.

The plates separate, revealing the fine conduits within. These are carefully extended into the floor. For a few weeks the depression becomes unusually responsive. It opens beneath familiar footsteps. Tools laid beside it are drawn within reach of a hand that is no longer there.

The responses diminish. Leaves grow over the place.

Residents still visit it. Some sit there with nothing visibly occurring around them.

In the oldest quarters, a few inhabitants have become too entangled to move.

A body rests within a pillar, with a face seam opening between two sheets of bark. Hands emerge from a polished path. A row of what appear to be small pulleys turns slightly when a traveler approaches, and only then can you see the limbs beneath.

The mobile residents address these individuals slowly. They wait for replies through the warm veins, sometimes overnight.

Some of the embedded ones are still busy. They regulate a feeding place, keep a chamber open, or hold a route at a dependable length. Others have no apparent occupation. Young visitors climb over them and are gently shifted into safer positions.

It is possible to live near one for years without knowing how much of the surrounding structure belongs to it.

They have no obvious authority over the mills. Belts continue moving when a resident is in their way. New floor can close a dwelling while its occupants are elsewhere. They work to prevent these things, and often succeed, but the immense production does not appear to be waiting for them.

Nor are they waiting for it to finish.

They settle into it. They maintain particular gaps, particular warmths, a handful of routes whose return distances they have learned to trust. Their attention is intensely local. A shift beneath one square foot of floor can bring three neighbors out of their dwellings; a new plain opening along the horizon may receive scarcely a glance.

When the old surface begins rolling toward the concealed apparatus, they do not all leave at once.

Some depart well beforehand, carrying tools and folded young. Some remain to detach conducting roots and mark the last reliable crossings. Others travel slowly along the rising edge, testing the fresh leaves below with one foot while three remain on the old floor.

The door carrier appears among them.

Its green slab has acquired a thin fringe of roots. It lowers the door onto the new plain and waits while the fringe searches for purchase. Then it lifts the handle, opens the door a little, and inspects the space beyond.

For some time it stands there, halfway between the two surfaces.

Behind it, the floor turns upward. Its white leaves fold, showing the dark roots underneath. Ahead, the fresh growth brushes against its legs.

At last the carrier lets the door close. It picks it up again and walks farther out, looking for somewhere else to put it.
