What lives here has no need to be seen all at once.

You might first notice a change in the way the moths settle. They crowd the cool crack until their wings overlap, but leave one small place bare. There is no obvious obstruction there, only a pale swelling of limestone, smooth at its center and rough around the edges. When the air begins to move, the swelling draws inward. A slit appears beneath it.

The moths lift their wings. The slit closes.

Elsewhere in the hollow, behind the stone shoulder that catches the noon light, something has shifted its weight.

It is not the hill, though the sound is of a little grit being crushed between heavy surfaces. A fern frond rises where nothing should be touching it. Then a length of what seemed to be root withdraws from the leaves, bending at two joints.

The inhabitant spends most of the day folded into a recess between a pillar and the wall. There is barely room for it. Its back fits the stone so closely that dust accumulates across the meeting of the two, and a vine has grown over one shoulder without finding cause to turn aside. The vine is not attached. When the creature moves, it slips beneath the stem with the care of something accustomed to putting itself back afterward.

Unfolded, it would be longer than a person is tall. It seldom unfolds.

Its body is narrow and deep, with a low ridge running along its back. Four limbs are tucked against it, the front pair longer than the others. Their ends divide into broad, soft digits, each edged with a hard crescent that looks less like a claw than a chip of translucent horn. These can grip the faintest irregularity in limestone. They can also lift a wet leaf without tearing it.

The skin is not quite the color of stone. In the dry places it is gray-brown, finely creased, and powdered with whatever has settled there. Under the limbs it is almost yellow. Around the mouth and along the throat it becomes thin enough to show slow changes beneath it: a darkening, a clearing, the passage of blood.

There are small mineral scales embedded in the older skin of its back. Some have cracked. Some have grown a green margin. They give it the appearance of an object that has lain here for a long time, but the warmer, softer skin between them is plainly alive.

Its head is difficult to distinguish when it rests. There is no projecting muzzle, only a broad blunt front with a mouth set low and two eyes placed far apart. The eyes remain covered through much of the day by translucent lids. Behind those lids they have a dull golden color. Open, they are nearly black.

It sees movement well. Whether it sees the shape of a motionless person is harder to tell.

Its attention seems to depend as much on contact as on sight. Resting, it keeps the tips of its forelimbs against the floor. A beetle crossing a leaf several paces away may cause one digit to lift. A footstep above the hollow makes the loose folds at its throat tighten. During rain it raises its head before any sound of water can be heard below.

The crack beneath the fig is one end of its resting place. A passage behind the wall connects that opening to the deeper recess where its body lies. On the hottest days it presses its mouth to the passage and draws air from farther inside the hill. The cool breath that reaches the moths is the small portion it lets out again.

It does not always use the crack. Sometimes the air from below is wrong, and it turns away after a single breath. Then it remains low on the floor, throat working, until evening.

Heat troubles it less than dryness.

This distinction governs almost everything it does. It will rest against a stone still warm from the afternoon, but avoid a cooler ledge whose dampness has vanished. It knows which roots bring water after a shower and which merely darken on the outside. It knows the shallow basin beside the slabs, the pockets behind the mineral rims, and a place under the fern mat where moisture persists after the visible trickle stops.

At dusk it makes a slow circuit of these places.

Often it does nothing more than touch them. One digit enters a basin, stays there, withdraws. Its mouth opens against a wet root. The throat pulses once or twice.

It can drink a film of water too thin to bead.

When a leaf has fallen across the little basin, the creature sometimes removes it. It lays the leaf nearby, usually with its wet side down. On other nights it leaves the obstruction in place. The leaf reduces evaporation, and the creature can still reach beneath it. Whatever distinction it makes is too fine to be obvious to someone standing over the basin.

The frogs do not flee from it unless it moves abruptly. One occasionally climbs onto a forelimb and sits in a crease while the creature rests. The geckos are less trusting. They flatten themselves into the narrow spaces behind their slabs when its head passes close, though it has never been seen trying to reach them there.

It eats beetles, fallen fruit, and the soft bodies of moths. It takes the beetles one at a time, turning a piece of bark and waiting for the disturbed insects to stop moving. Its tongue is short and pale. There is none of the sudden snapping associated with a lizard or frog: the mouth approaches, closes, and remains still while the jaws work.

With fruit it is patient. An intact skin may be too tough for it. It waits until insects have opened a way, then presses its mouth to the break. The polished pits on the floor include some it has cleaned. After feeding, it rubs its face along a particular rounded projection of the wall, leaving a faint stain that beetles visit later.

It is not harmless. A snake entering its recess would meet a stronger animal than the folded body suggests. The horn edges of its forefeet can hold with considerable force, and the mouth has teeth far back, small and numerous. Yet its usual response to disturbance is to make itself fit the space more tightly.

A person could spend an afternoon in the hollow and never know it was there.

A second visit might reveal that the rounded shape behind the pillar had changed. A third might find the recess empty and a long, low animal crossing the floor, its belly just above the leaf litter. It would pause at the visitor’s movement, placing all four feet flat. The throat would close. Only the eyes would continue to move.

If given time, it would resume its round by another route.

It does not come into the amber patch at noon. When that patch reaches farthest across the floor, the creature draws its nearest limb into shade. At night it sometimes approaches the sloping throat and stays just inside, where the smell of the surface comes down. Its head lifts toward passing animals. Fruit may be lying only a short distance beyond the entrance, but it rarely goes out to fetch it.

There are old abrasions on its back, healed smooth between the mineral scales. The narrow passages account for some of them. Others may date from a time when it was smaller and used routes it can no longer enter.

No young have been seen. There is no second resting hollow worn to its shape, though there are places deep behind the roots that cannot be examined.

By the end of the night it returns to the pillar. It lowers its body gradually, easing the limbs underneath, making small adjustments until its back meets the wall. The vine settles across its shoulder. A gecko ventures onto the slab above it. Dust disturbed during the night remains briefly visible on the warm air.

Just before becoming still, the creature extends one forefoot toward the nearest shallow dish.

It rests two digits in the damp leaf matter there.

When the next drop falls beside the shelter, the digits tighten slightly. Then they loosen, and stay where they are.
