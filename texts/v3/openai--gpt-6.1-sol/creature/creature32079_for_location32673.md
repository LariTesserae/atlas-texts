The animal is easiest to see just after the hollow sweats. For a little while its body contains more green than the wall behind it. The fluid gathers in two long chambers along its sides, leaving a clear interval through which you can see the surface it stands on. A vein in the wall seems to pass through its body without interruption.

Then it turns, and the vein breaks.

It has six legs, but it rarely puts all six down. Four hold it against the living surface while the front pair handle the thorn. When it needs to move, it transfers the thorn to the underside of its body, where a shallow fold closes around it. There is no sheath. You can still see the point protruding, dark against the green fluid.

Its feet are broad for so small an animal. Each ends in a transparent pad that settles onto the wall with the completeness of a drop of water. When a foot lifts, the pad comes away from the edge inward. Nothing tears. Nothing is left behind.

You might expect it to travel quickly, with the abrupt, busy movements of insects. It does not. A leg reaches, rests, accepts weight. Another follows. Sometimes the animal remains between steps for so long that a new rib forms under its raised foot.

It puts the foot down on the rib.

What looks at first like a narrow head resolves into several surfaces that move independently. Two small plates cover the place where it drinks. Above them is a cloudy bead, not quite an eye. Light enters this bead and stays there briefly. When a bright current passes beneath the floor, the bead shines after the rest of the animal has gone pale.

There are no feelers you can readily distinguish. But when it approaches a fresh opening, something finer than a hair extends from each side of its head. These threads touch neither wall nor air, as far as you can tell. They remain outstretched while the animal waits.

Then one withdraws.

The animal goes the other way.

Its thorn is not always the same thorn. One has a reddish base and a hooked point. Another is nearly straight, with a white line running through its center. You begin to recognize them before you become certain that you recognize the animal.

There may be more than one animal.

You never see two together. Yet sometimes the one at the damp seam has a broad green belly, while the one you find soon afterward beside the closed-eye crease is almost empty. The distance between those places is unhelpful. So is the interval between sightings. You have walked toward it for several minutes and found it still beside the same groove; you have looked away to steady yourself and looked back to find only a thorn taking root.

Nothing in the hollow gives you a reliable way to count its inhabitants.

The animal feeds at wounds, but it is particular about them. A split made by the chamber’s own growth may be full of sap, and it will pass without stopping. It chooses an intact surface beside the split, lowers its body, and works there.

The first scrape leaves no visible mark.

The second lifts a curl so thin that it vanishes against the wall.

After the third, a dark green point appears. The animal places its mouth over it and waits. Its side chambers fill slowly, beginning near the head. You can watch the green advance in little pulses, with clear spaces between them.

The wall does not remain passive. A lip rises along the groove. Fine cells gather over the mouth of it. The animal moves its thorn a fraction sideways and keeps the opening clear.

Sometimes it loses. The lips meet under its head, and it is left standing over an unbroken surface. It presses once with its front feet. Then it cleans the thorn, drawing the point between the plates of its mouth, and goes elsewhere.

At other times it leaves the thorn wedged in place.

Beside such a groove, its body settles nearly flat. The green within it becomes still. Its legs remain spread, their pads firmly attached. You can bring your face close enough to make out a row of pale dots along its flank, but you cannot see it breathing.

The hollow is never louder than when you are trying to decide whether this small thing is alive.

A crack opens overhead. Sap whistles through the wall. The floor booms beneath your knees.

Immediately around the animal, the tissue barely moves.

Only after watching several of these resting places do you notice the difference. The ribs nearby rise unevenly; a thin flange curls away from its neighbor. Beneath the animal, the surface stays smooth. Even the groove closes more slowly there.

When it leaves, that little patch catches up. The scar tightens. A ridge lifts through the place where its feet have been. Sometimes this happens at once, with a dry snap. Sometimes it takes most of the light.

It sheds its skin beside the central stalk.

You almost step on the empty covering. It lies fitted to a joint, six legs spread, so clear that only the shadows beneath the feet give it away. A slit runs along its back. There is no green inside.

For some time you take it for the animal at rest.

Then you find the animal on the other side of the stalk, soft and faintly milky, gripping a thorn too firmly. Its new feet have not yet flattened into pads. They curl around the stalk instead. When the great rushing begins, it holds still with its whole body pressed to the pale stem.

The discarded skin does not blow away. Moisture enters it through the feet. By the next dark interval, its legs have become six tiny channels carrying light. Later you cannot find it, though the joint has acquired a delicate web of veins.

You do not see it sleep elsewhere. It has no sheltered corner, no place you can call its own. When the wall becomes the floor, it stays where it is. When a new sheet opens above it, it may follow the edge, or remain beneath the fold until the light reaches its feet.

Once you hold a finger near its path.

It stops. The cloudy bead darkens. One front leg rises and remains raised.

You wait long enough for moisture to gather in the print your other hand has left on the wall. The animal lowers its foot. It steps onto your finger.

Its weight is imperceptible, but each foot makes a separate cool point. Through its belly you can see the lines of your skin. It carries the thorn under itself, the tip turned carefully away from you.

Halfway across your nail it stops again.

There is nothing there for it to scrape. No vein beneath the surface, no pressure of sap, no new tissue opening. It extends its fine threads, withdraws them, and turns back by the same route.

Afterward, you cannot find it for several cycles.

Then the new sheet peels from the wall and stops beside your shoulder. Light fills its veins. At the far edge, the drop begins to gather.

Before it falls, the animal appears beneath it.

It is paler than you remember. The thorn rests against its underside. Four feet hold the thin edge of the sheet, while the front pair reach into the drop.

They move delicately inside the water.

The minute folded leaf turns. One of its points catches against a foot, opens a little, closes again. The animal draws it toward the surface and holds it there while the drop swells around them both.

For the first time, you see it without a tool between its front legs.

When the water breaks free, the leaf remains. It lies against the animal’s clear belly, its folds loosening slowly in the warmth.
