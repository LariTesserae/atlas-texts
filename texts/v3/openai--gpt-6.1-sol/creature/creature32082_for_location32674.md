The inhabitants are easiest to find at the thresholds.

A door may be dead across most of its width and alive along one edge, but the life in that edge is not always the life of the surrounding wall. Sometimes the strip swells while the wall contracts. Sometimes it keeps a small reserve of oil after the channels on either side have emptied. If the pressure changes, it responds according to something other than the pressure alone.

There is a little history folded into it.

The oldest inhabitants occupy several such places at once: a door strip, the underside of a shelf, a junction beneath the avenue. Between these places run filaments scarcely distinguishable from the fibers that hold the buildings together. Much of an inhabitant’s body is structural. To remove it would be to unmake a room.

But the room is not the inhabitant. A room can remain after its occupant has died, and an occupant can withdraw from a room without leaving it empty.

What persists is a particular manner of passing things onward. Oil arrives at a junction. One fraction is retained; another is changed and released. A vibration travels through a partition. Certain fibers tighten, diverting the next vibration elsewhere. Over years, these small decisions maintain a pattern that the general growth of the plant would otherwise erase.

Each inhabitant is a pattern that has learned to keep a little of the plant for itself.

They have no faces. They have places where they are especially attentive.

These are thin, taut patches, usually sheltered beneath shelves or stretched across the backs of sealed doorways. A patch receives movement through its attachments. It distinguishes the slipping of a distant root fiber from the settling of its own roof, and the settling of its roof from a tendril pulling nearby. The distinction may take a long time to establish. Some reports are so large that everything capable of attending is pressed into the same response.

During such a crash, the city briefly has fewer separate inhabitants.

Afterward they recover their differences. A membrane relaxes at one rate, another at a different rate. Oil is released from private chambers. The old routes reassert themselves.

Recovery is not always complete. Two neighbors who have endured enough of the same shocks may cease to be separable. Their channels join. Their reserves become available to one another. An alteration begun in one reaches the other without meeting a boundary.

There are districts inhabited by someone who was once many.

There are also single chambers containing several inhabitants, each occupying a different depth of the same partition. They may have shared a wall for centuries without ever exchanging nourishment. One attends to the hollow; another to the mineral rind behind it. They know different things about the same address.

Their names, insofar as they have names, are changes they cause in what passes through them.

One leaves a slight bitterness in the oil. One removes a constituent that most others retain. One answers a prolonged strain with three releases of pressure, separated by intervals so unequal that no accidental settling is likely to imitate them. These signatures are recognized downstream, often long after their makers have changed.

An inhabitant can receive news from someone already absorbed into the root.

This is ordinary enough that receiving a message does not establish that its sender still lives. It establishes that they lived when the message entered the channel. A reply is made anyway. Somewhere along the route there may remain a branch, a closed chamber, a trace of the sender capable of taking it in.

The combs and wedges belong to their more vigorous ancestors.

In those times, an inhabitant could extend itself into a jointed tendril and work at a distance from its settled tissues. It could test the grain of a strip, hold one end while another inhabitant pulled, and preserve a tension through the long interval needed for a knot to take its shape.

The knots were often made by several inhabitants together. Each held a different part. None could feel the whole arrangement directly; the finished knot was something they had to achieve through one another.

Some bundles still bear the marks of disagreement. A fiber has been loosened, drawn through a second opening, tightened again. Beside one bin, a rejected loop has hardened into the wall. The work took so long that abandoning an error was sometimes more expensive than building around it.

There are inhabitants who remember making these things, though their memories contain little that could be called a picture.

They remember the pull.

They remember where resistance increased, and which neighbor yielded first. They remember a useful substance arriving while a task was still unfinished. Such an arrival could sustain a tendril for years. Its timing remains associated with the task after the substance itself has been consumed.

The oldest memory in one chamber is the relief of a fiber becoming slack.

Nothing survives there that would explain why this mattered. The fiber is gone. The chamber has been shortened by the thickening shell. Yet whenever a similar tension reaches its living lining, a small passage opens and releases a nourishment the inhabitant would otherwise have kept.

Something was helped once. The disposition to help remains.

Not all the inhabitants are old.

Beneath the avenue, within the advancing pale sheet, new centers of life have begun to form. At first they differ only slightly from the growth around them. A patch retains more oil. A fold closes over a useful grain. A network of fibers answers a strain together, then continues answering together after the strain has passed.

Most of these beginnings disappear. The sheet consumes their reserves and resumes its even advance.

A few persist.

One lies beneath the worn place where an ancient groove enters a sealed door. It has encountered the groove from below. Along that line the paving is thinner, and traces of old transport remain in its cells. The young inhabitant has been following those traces, holding its soft leading tissue against the underside of a route that no longer carries anything.

It has no knowledge of an avenue.

It knows a sequence of favorable differences. It knows that growth becomes easier in one direction, and that something ahead repeatedly resists it.

Beyond the sealed door lives an inhabitant whose remaining body is mostly a bin lining and the membranes beneath three shelves. It receives so little nourishment that it has closed almost every route into itself. Its door has not opened since the oldest surviving tendrils were supple.

When the new growth first touches the threshold, the older inhabitant contracts.

The contraction reaches the young one as a hardening in the material it is entering. It stops. Much later, it advances along the edge instead.

This is their first exchange.

Neither has intended it as a greeting. The older one is protecting a reserve. The younger is finding a way through. Nevertheless, each has changed because the other is there, and the changes are preserved.

Over the next long interval, the elder releases a minute quantity of altered oil into the threshold. The younger takes it in. A part of its leading edge stiffens.

It now grows around the doorway rather than through it.

Perhaps the elder has taught it the boundary. Perhaps it has merely made that particular tissue difficult to digest. The distinction will depend on what happens when the young inhabitant reaches the next sealed room.

The towers contain another kind of resident.

Their lives are spread along the stitched seams, where fibers cross and hold one another under tension. They consume very little. Most of what they receive is spent maintaining the differences between one segment of membrane and the next.

They are sensitive to the air in their shafts. A distant movement of the root draws them tight; the hollow carries back the resulting sound. The return reaches each tower altered by all the structures it has crossed.

Thus a tower can attend to places where none of its tissue grows.

A roof sinking into a partition changes the returning vibration. A door opening introduces a new yielding surface. The loss of a neighbor leaves a small, persistent difference in the sound.

The tower inhabitants know the city by such differences. Their knowledge is broad and imprecise. They may notice the disappearance of a room without knowing which inhabitant occupied it. They may continue responding to a structure long after it has become part of the wall.

Occasionally one alters its seam in answer.

It cannot produce a sound whenever it wishes. It must wait for the root to move. But when movement comes, its response bears the alteration, and across the vacancy another tower receives it.

A conversation can depend on a crack opening far above them.

None of the inhabitants knows the shape of the plant. The rind is a source of substances and pressure, the root a succession of immense events. The hollow is known through what returns across it. No one has reached its center.

Some of the older ones once extended tendrils inward. These grew unsupported for a little distance, spent what had been allotted to them, and withdrew or dried. The attempts survive as stiff threads projecting from otherwise careful buildings.

The space offers nothing a filament can use.

Still, in several separated districts, tissues retain the disposition to extend toward it. Given an uncommon surplus, they begin again. A tendril uncurls above a shelf. Its tip makes a small searching movement in the dark.

For years it may touch nothing.

When the root settles, the tendril trembles with the rest of the city. The inhabitant feels a pull without the familiar resistance of a wall, a tool, or a neighbor. It holds the tendril there a little longer.

Below, the new growth reaches another threshold. An old door strip slowly thickens. Oil passes through a junction that has almost closed.

The curl of rind fallen onto the avenue is drawn into living tissue. Its constituents separate and enter different channels. A little reaches the young inhabitant beneath the paving. A little is retained by someone older, farther along.

For a time, both have enough to continue.
