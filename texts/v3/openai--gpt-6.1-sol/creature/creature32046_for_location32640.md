The people who live on the plain do not usually stand still with both feet flat on the ground.

One foot rests on a raised joint, or against the base of a shelter, while the other tests the surface ahead. Even indoors, they settle their weight unevenly. A person listening beside a doorway may seem to be preparing to leave, although they have no intention of moving. They are separating the vibrations: what reaches them through the structural rib, what comes through the newer floor, what passes through the water around the threshold.

Their boots have thick soles, but not uniformly thick ones. Beneath the heel is a firm insert. Beneath the ball of the foot, the material is thinner and more yielding. The boots are repaired often. A badly placed patch can make a familiar route feel strange.

They are not all alike in their ability to read the machinery. Some can distinguish a pump changing load from a chamber beginning to flood. Others recognize only the large, dependable changes and travel with someone more experienced. Children are sometimes unusually good at hearing a particular fault through their bones, then lose the ability as they grow heavier. An old woman may know the behavior of one buried conduit so well that people come from several shelters to ask her to walk across it.

She does not put her ear to the ground. She walks slowly, carrying nothing, and stops where the others would have continued.

Afterward, she may say that the route is safe.

She may also say that she cannot tell.

This second answer is respected, though it is not always obeyed.

***

Their clothes make them look broader and less articulated than they are. Outer coats overlap at the throat, wrist, and ankle. Closures are hidden under folded flaps; loose cords are cut short. Anything that can catch on a moving hatch eventually will.

The material is dark where it has absorbed oil and pale where mineral deposits have dried into it. Along the shoulders, drops gather on stiff ridges of old repairs. The hood is large enough to turn the head inside it without turning the whole garment.

Faces are often visible only when someone comes close. They are ordinary faces, with the differences ordinary faces have. Some are thin, some swollen from poor sleep, some younger than the coat suggests. The cold reddens noses and leaves the skin around the mouth cracked. People who work near warm discharge channels may have clear, almost delicate complexions beneath the grime on their brows.

Their hands are less uniformly rough than one might expect. Work gloves take much of the wear. Inside the shelters, a great deal of effort goes into drying fingers, cleaning under nails, and treating the small splits that refuse to close in damp air. There are jars of grease beside the doors, and narrow cloths hung above pipes. A visitor who is offered a warm cloth is being welcomed.

A visitor who is asked to show their hands is being assessed for exhaustion.

Cold people make mistakes with fasteners. Very cold people insist they have finished fastening things when they have not.

***

Most shelters hold fewer inhabitants than their number of beds would suggest.

Some beds are kept for workers overtaken by a change in drainage, or for travelers who arrive after the safe route has disappeared. Others belong to people away in another district. Their cups remain on the padded shelves. No one takes this as evidence that they will return soon.

The interiors are small but carefully divided. Wet clothing hangs in an entrance compartment. Beyond it is a warmer room where the floor is covered with fitted pieces of matting, each weighted at the corners. Lamps are shaded downward. Bright light reflected from a wall can become tiring when the wall never quite stops trembling.

Beds hang from springs, but there are restraints as well, broad straps that keep them from acquiring too much swing. Infants sleep in shallow suspended boxes with padded rims. A parent can lie with one hand resting on the suspension line and feel the child moving without opening their eyes.

The machinery is always present indoors. It trembles in spoons, in the surface of soup, in a thread held ready for a needle. People choose their moments for delicate tasks. A certain valve phase may be good for sewing; another makes it easier to pour.

Meals pause when the building shudders. The people at the table steady whatever is nearest, wait for the usual rhythm to return, and continue. An unfamiliar movement is different. Then someone touches the wall, someone checks the door, and no one resumes eating until they have compared what they felt.

***

Speech is used freely inside, though voices are low and conversation proceeds in short lengths. Outside, they use lamps, gestures, and contact.

The contact signs are precise. Two fingers on the upper arm mean something different from a palm between the shoulders. Pressure matters. So does duration. A hand that merely rests is not issuing a direction, though newcomers often move as if it were.

People who live together develop additional signs without agreeing to them. A thumb pressed once into the back of a glove can mean that a route looks wrong, or that the person ahead is walking too quickly, or that there is something worth seeing beside the channel. The meaning comes partly from where they are and what they were doing a moment before.

Arguments outdoors are difficult. A person can refuse to look at a lamp. They can step out of reach.

Consequently, those who know one another well do not withdraw a hand casually on a dangerous crossing. They wait until there is somewhere safe to stand.

Affection has its own practical forms. Someone cleans the mineral crust from another person’s boot seam. Someone leaves a dry lining warmed beside a pipe. Someone waits at a doorway long after the expected return, pretending to be occupied with a hinge.

There are songs, but most are sung very close to the listener. A child may know a song as breath against one ear and a faint movement felt through the cheek.

***

Some inhabitants maintain the visible machinery. Others carry supplies, mend clothing, cultivate the warm basins, or tend the machines inside the shelters that turn pale bulk material into food.

That material arrives in sealed drums from service openings. The labels, where they exist, give handling instructions rather than origins. Different batches swell differently in water. Good cooks recognize them by the way a knife drags through the softened mass.

The plants along the pipes provide little food. Their tender growth is used sparingly, for flavor and for certain deficiencies that become visible first in the mouth. The stiff rectangular mats are harvested for fibers. The pale scales from sheltered basins are dried on fine mesh.

A person tending a patch spends much of the day adjusting its water. They scrape a shallow groove clear, fit a strip of metal beneath a dripping joint, or move mineral felt from one edge to another. Their plots may be only a few paces across. Some have been tended by the same household for generations.

When a warm channel closes, the keeper removes the harvest frames and leaves the plants where they are. There is little else to do. Every few months someone visits, lifts an edge of the flattened growth, and checks its underside.

Sometimes warmth returns while no one is watching. The first new shoots then appear beneath the old stems, cramped and nearly white.

***

The maintenance workers carry tools wrapped separately so they cannot strike one another. They also carry tags, wedges, spare lamp cells, and lengths of cord that resist the process water.

Their knowledge is extensive in some directions and abruptly absent in others.

They know which inspection covers must never be fully removed. They know how long a particular housing takes to rotate, which pressure readings are unreliable in cold weather, and how to free a retaining pin without letting it fall into the opening below.

Asked what the installation produces, they may name what emerges from the nearest outlet. Asked what happens to it afterward, they point toward another district.

There are records. Dry cabinets hold old diagrams, maintenance lists, and copied observations. Many pages describe equipment that can no longer be reached. A plan may show a broad chamber where a recent probe finds only dense layers of fibrous material. Elsewhere, water still runs through a passage absent from every available drawing.

Workers add what they have found without crossing out the older account.

Repairs generally begin with small things. A lamp is cleared. A lip is scraped so water can pass evenly. A hatch is prevented from settling crooked. Large failures often first become noticeable as an inconvenience somebody has tolerated for months.

The workers distrust an opening that has suddenly become easier to use.

***

Children learn the routes close to home before they are allowed to carry anything.

They learn where the ground is rough enough to grip, where a narrow dark line is harmless, and where an apparently identical line is an intake. They learn to retreat along the exact path they took. They are not told simply to avoid the water. Water is everywhere, and much of it is shallow. They are taught which kinds of uncertainty require another person.

Their games use sheltered strips of pavement, empty fittings, and the intervals between recurring changes. A small wheel is set rolling along a joint. Children follow it until the ground pulses; then everyone must stop without letting it fall.

Some children become fascinated by the places where plants survive. Others prefer the hatches, especially those that open infrequently. They wait for hours to see an edge rise out of the water.

Adults remember doing the same, though they discourage it.

***

People disappear here.

Sometimes an emptied pool exposes something that explains a disappearance. More often it exposes nothing useful. Searches follow the direction of flow, then the passages connected to that flow, then the records of which passages were open at the time. These can extend well beyond the district.

For a while, a missing person’s lamp pattern is shown at distant shelters.

Their clothing is kept dry. Their work is taken up by others. Someone continues to inspect a patch of pale growth whose keeper has not come back.

After enough time, belongings are used where they are needed, but this happens piece by piece. A pair of boots may be repaired for another wearer while a cup stays untouched on its shelf.

Those born on the plain grow old with habits outsiders find excessive. They check a familiar threshold before stepping over it. They secure a cup they have already secured. They wake when the vibration changes, even if the new rhythm is quieter.

There are evenings when several shelters become occupied at once after weeks of isolation. Coats crowd the drying compartment. Food is stretched, spare beds lowered, lamps trimmed. People bring news of a reopened route or an installation that has stopped accepting material. They compare repairs and ask after absent acquaintances.

Later, someone goes outside to examine the waterline.

The door opens briefly, admitting mist and the full weight of the noise. Those inside pause while it closes. The person who has gone out stands on the structural rib with one hand against the wall, waiting through a whole pump cycle before deciding whether anything has changed.
