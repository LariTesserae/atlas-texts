The inhabitants are easiest to see where a shelter has almost ceased to be one.

At the foot of two upright stones, beneath the place where a roof might have rested, there is a gray shape you take for a third stone. It has the same rounded back, the same darkening along its lower edge. A little moss grows against it.

Then a hand draws the moss closer.

The movement is small and practical. Four fingers slide beneath the brittle edge of the cushion and turn it so that its wet center faces the seam in the ground. The hand remains there afterward, palm down, as if there were no reason to withdraw it.

You can now distinguish an arm, a shoulder, a head inclined toward the knees. The inhabitant is sitting upright in a space too narrow to accommodate you. Its body is folded closely, though without the tension of someone crouching. You have the impression that it could remain this way without becoming uncomfortable.

It is somewhat smaller than a person. Its shoulders are broad, its chest shallow. There is little separation between the neck and the head. What first looked like a ridge of weathered stone above its eyes is a heavy brow, covered in short, colorless hair.

More hair grows along the outer surfaces of its arms and back. On the inside of the wrist, the skin is bare and dark. The fingertips are pale.

It wears nothing you can distinguish as clothing. Nevertheless, the wet does not reach it. Moisture beads where its body touches the stone, collecting in a thin line that follows the curve of its hip. The skin above that line remains dry.

Its eyes are open.

They are not large, and they do not catch the light. You see them because it looks at your hand.

You are still rubbing away the grains from the basin.

When the last grain falls, its eyes follow it. Then they return to the moss.

***

There are others.

Once you have seen the first, some of the shelters become occupied without any visible arrival. A low chamber contains two backs pressed together beneath its roof. A recess that seemed empty holds an inhabitant lying along the rear ledge, one arm hanging down to a pool. Under a slab near the rain, you make out a face at the height of your ankles.

They are not concealing themselves. Their bodies settle so completely into the available spaces that your attention passes over them as it passes over a stone that fits its socket.

Their faces differ. One has a longer jaw. Another has a pale patch extending from its mouth to one temple. The inhabitant beside the rain is missing two fingers from its right hand, and the remaining fingers have grown thick at the joints.

There are young ones, though at first you mistake them for more distant adults.

A small head rests between an older inhabitant’s shoulder blades. Beneath it, a narrow body follows the older body’s spine, held in place by its own hands and feet. The young one’s hair is darker. Its ears are still soft enough to fold against its head.

It watches the strip of falling water.

After a long time it reaches out.

The older inhabitant lifts a hand and places it beneath the small wrist. It holds the wrist there, short of the rain, until the young one draws it back.

Neither looks at the other.

***

They eat the closed-leaf plants, but sparingly.

One lies on its side beside a fissure, with its mouth close to a stem. It touches the leaves with the backs of two fingers, testing each in turn. Eventually it takes the outermost leaf between its lips. There is a slight pull, a pause, and then the leaf comes away.

The remaining leaves stay shut.

Its jaws work slowly. You see a row of small, broad teeth, dark at their roots. A little fluid gathers at the corner of its mouth. It wipes this onto the stone above the plant.

At another shelter, an inhabitant lifts the dry margin of a moss cushion and finds something beneath it. Whatever it takes is too small for you to see. It eats with its head bowed over its palm, then smooths the moss back into place.

They also drink.

A bare wrist laid against wet rock darkens gradually from the fingertips upward. An inhabitant can remain beside a water seam for an hour with its mouth closed. When it rises, the seam is fainter and the skin of its hand has become slightly fuller.

The pale animals share these places with them.

One sleeps against an inhabitant’s thigh. From time to time the inhabitant passes its fingertips through the dusty fur, separating it near the skin. It finds a tiny black speck and holds it on a nail.

The animal does not wake.

The speck is placed in a crack beneath the ledge. Only then does the hand return to the fur.

***

You rarely see them walk.

They can do it. Their feet are long, with broad pads under the toes, and they place them carefully on the dry portions of the shelves. An upright inhabitant is taller than you expected. Its arms hang nearly to its knees. Its back remains curved, its head held forward as though it were still beneath a low roof.

It crosses six paces of open stone and lowers itself into a hollow.

The crossing seems to cost it something. Once seated, it presses its palms flat and waits until the fine hair along its forearms lies down.

For longer journeys, they use the shelters.

You see one enter a chamber carrying a young inhabitant against its chest. Far away, at the base of another ridge, the same pale patch on its face becomes visible in an opening.

You cannot say when it passed between them.

There is no crowding at these entrances, no dispute over which hollow belongs to whom. But particular bodies leave particular traces. A darkened crescent where a shoulder rests. Four parallel grooves beneath a ledge. Moss arranged with its brittle edge inward.

The oval on the rear ledge is bedding.

Later you see an inhabitant settle its cheek into it. The fit is exact. It closes its eyes, leaving one hand against the wall.

***

Their hands do most of what you can recognize as work.

They press loose grains into a shallow depression until its floor is level. They lift a plant stem away from a place where stone would pinch it. They move a flake no farther than the breadth of a fingernail, changing the steady click it makes against its ledge.

This last task occupies one inhabitant for much of your stay.

It holds the flake at one edge, releases it, listens. Holds it again. A young one sits beside its knee with both hands resting on the ground.

You cannot hear a difference in the clicking.

Eventually the older inhabitant removes its hand.

The young one touches the flake.

Immediately the hand returns and steadies it. There is no haste in the correction. The young one waits, then tries again with a lighter touch.

This time the older one leaves it alone.

***

They have voices, though you do not hear one coming from a mouth.

In a shelter containing several inhabitants, the thin tones between the rocks become distinct. One rises slightly. Another continues beneath it. An inhabitant at the entrance turns its head; the one lying farther in opens its eyes.

Their throats move.

You place your hand against the wall and feel a brief tremor. A second tremor follows through the threshold under your foot.

Outside, the noise remains enormous.

You speak to them once. The nearest looks at your mouth, then at the stone beside your hand. You repeat yourself more softly.

It waits until you have finished. Then it lays two fingers against the wall.

You feel nothing.

After a while it returns to what it was doing.

***

You do not find their dead in any chamber.

There is, however, an unoccupied hollow with a rounded depression along its rear wall. Its floor is clean. No moss grows there, although a water seam passes close enough to sustain it.

An inhabitant enters while you are standing at the threshold. It goes to the depression and rests the underside of its jaw against the stone.

For a long time it stays there.

You move away before it does.

***

When you return to the first shelter, someone has placed a small cushion of moss against the wall where you stood.

The cushion is imperfectly oval. Its center is damp, its edges folded under. Beside it, a flat stone has been turned so that the smooth surface faces upward.

There is room for one of your hands on it.

The inhabitant from the broken shelter sits just outside, with its back toward you. It is picking damp grains from the crease of its palm. Each grain is set into a hairline opening at the base of the rock.

You sit down.

The noise is no quieter here. Your knees ache, and you cannot arrange your shoulders comfortably beneath the overhang. After several attempts you find a position that you can hold.

You put your hand on the flat stone.

Outside, the inhabitant pauses in its work.

It does not turn around.
