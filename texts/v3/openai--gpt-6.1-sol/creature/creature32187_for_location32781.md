It lives behind the things that still look attached.

Behind the ladder brackets, under the lip of the inspection plate, in the narrow separation between an inlet collar and the masonry, there is a layer that would be easy to mistake for softened packing. It is gray where the water reaches it freely and cream-colored farther in. At its thinnest it is almost transparent. At its thickest it has the appearance of a wet strip of leather folded several times into a space too small for it.

These are parts of the same animal.

There is no body in the open center of the chamber. Nothing waits there for a light to reveal its outline. Its largest portion occupies a failed mortar joint behind the lowest ladder bracket, a cavity roughly the size of a closed hand. From this, narrow extensions follow the joints in several directions. Some end after a few inches. Others pass behind bricks and emerge a yard away. One reaches the underside of the inspection plate. Another has grown along the outside of the southern pipe, well below the floor.

The masonry hides most of it. Even if the chamber were drained, much of the animal would remain inside the walls.

Its flesh is soft, but its exposed edges are tough. They have a fine, closely ridged surface that lies flat under a steady current. When the pressure falls, the ridges separate a little. Water enters between them. When pressure returns, they close, retaining a minute quantity of whatever the water carried.

It feeds this way. Bacteria, fragments of other animals, oils dispersed so finely that they pass unseen through the chamber: some adhere, some are enclosed, some are rejected hours later as a clear mucus. The cream and orange skin under the inspection plate grows particularly thick around its margins. The animal grazes this skin from beneath, extending a delicate surface into it and withdrawing that surface when it has absorbed enough.

The translucent crustaceans live beside a part of it. They are neither its offspring nor its attendants. They have found a useful shelter in a crease of living tissue that resembles a crack in the bracket’s bedding. Their feet irritate it. Sometimes the crease tightens, slowly enough that they can retreat. Sometimes it remains open for weeks.

It has no eyes.

Along the exposed margins are paired depressions, each smaller than a grain of sand. A membrane covers each depression, and beneath the membrane a dense particle rests against a tuft of sensitive fibers. The particles move when the iron moves, when the brickwork moves, when water presses differently on one side of a joint than on the other.

There are thousands of these little organs.

Through them the animal receives the chamber in extraordinary detail. It distinguishes the southern pump from the others, and distinguishes changes within that pump: the slight irregularity before a bearing is replaced, the altered beat after replacement, the gradual return of looseness. The remote valve has a recognizable sequence of knocks. The washer at the grille supplies a much finer signal, felt most strongly through the extension beneath the inspection plate.

All of this arrives together. The animal has grown within it. Its sensitivity is adjusted to the continuing roar as the bodies of other creatures are adjusted to gravity.

A stopped pump produces a much greater response than a running one. Tissue around the corresponding inlet stiffens. Farther away, small internal chambers contract, moving fluid through the animal’s branches. These contractions travel slowly. It may take several minutes for a change sensed beneath the floor to reach the thick portion behind the ladder.

By then the pump may have started again.

The animal retains both events. Its response to the second is affected by the first, and its response to the next stoppage is affected by the interval between them. Over years, repeating sequences alter the sensitivity of particular branches. Some become quick to contract. Others scarcely respond.

The working week has left a physical pattern in it.

There are regions that loosen before the southern flow increases, having been drawn into that rhythm through innumerable repetitions. During the quieter circulation late at night, it extends feeding edges that remain concealed through the day. On certain mornings it does this too early and loses a little tissue to the strengthening current.

It has no means of accounting for a holiday.

Its oldest living tissue is close to the warm pipe beneath the floor. That tissue has been replaced many times, but a small framework of secreted material remains inside it: pale, flexible tubes around which new flesh forms. Some tubes have been abandoned and sealed. Some have become channels. Others hold dark grains that entered the chamber decades ago and were never expelled.

Age is unevenly distributed through the animal. A feeding margin may be only a few days old. A sensitive patch beneath the plate may have persisted for years. Deep inside the floor joint are structures built while the chamber’s ladder still had all its rungs.

There is no secure way to establish when it became one creature. Several small organisms may once have occupied adjacent seams, met, and joined. Parts of it can still live separately. When grit severs a narrow connection, the isolated tissue continues to feed and contract, occasionally growing back toward the surviving network.

One such portion lives behind the opaque pressure gauge. It has been separate for eleven years.

The main body still responds to it. A contraction beneath the gauge passes into its mounting, through the brick, and reaches sensitive tissue near the inspection plate. The separated portion responds in turn. The exchange is faint and easily submerged in the pumps’ vibration, but in the simpler circulation of the night it sometimes continues for hours.

During heavy rain, the animal closes itself.

The first warning reaches it through a small extension near the western grille. Pressure there rises before the flow reverses. Exposed ridges flatten; feeding margins withdraw; the crease occupied by the crustaceans deepens. Fluid is pulled out of its finer branches and stored behind the ladder bracket, where the flesh becomes firmer and slightly swollen.

Then cold water descends the wall.

Sometimes it is ready. Sometimes the reversal comes too quickly, and a margin is torn free. Loose grit cuts the exposed tissue. The brass fragment strikes the ladder and sends a violent signal through the largest part of its body. For a while, contractions pass between branches in sequences unrelated to any ordinary pump rhythm.

When the warm circulation resumes, damaged surfaces begin to seal. New growth follows whatever shelter remains. A scoured joint may offer access to a cavity it could not previously enter. A shifted stone may cover an edge that had fed well for years.

After each severe storm, its shape is a little different.

The fish that occasionally enter are immense disturbances. Their tail strokes reach several sensitive patches at once. A fish resting behind the blind plate presses a complicated series of small movements into the water: gills opening, fins correcting, the occasional abrupt flex of the whole body.

The animal contracts away from these movements at first. After some hours it becomes less responsive. Its feeding margin extends again beneath the plate, close enough that a resting fish may brush it.

Whether the fish leaves or dies, the margin eventually receives a brief abundance of material. The animal grows. A new pale edge appears along the iron, remains exposed for a few nights, and is gradually worked back into shelter by the current.

Light has little effect on it. The weak stripe on the north wall passes over one of its branches without producing a response. Rain-brightened water, however, often accompanies a pressure change, and traffic overhead sends distinguishable loads through the vault. The animal is sensitive to both.

A tram stopping above the chamber compresses certain joints by an amount too small to see. The branch behind the north wall responds. As the tram leaves, the compression eases, and the response travels toward the ladder.

Feet are less distinct. Many steps disappear among the machinery’s vibrations. A person standing directly on the sealed shaft produces a steadier change, especially when the pumps are running low. The animal’s uppermost tissues tighten slightly and hold until the load is gone.

It has never followed anything upward. Its growth ends where the joints become dry.

Below that limit, it continues finding room. It advances into loosened mortar, withdraws from exposed iron, feeds along warm surfaces, and rebuilds after damage. The oldest intersection in the harbor district gives it a changing supply of heat, food, pressure, and shelter.

Tonight the pumps are steady. The washer rattles against the grille. Behind the ladder, several crustaceans leave their crease and pick across the bracket’s rough edge.

Beneath their feet, the gray tissue loosens. A line of minute openings admits water, then closes. Farther inside the wall, a contraction begins its slow passage through the animal.
