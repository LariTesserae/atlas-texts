The people who live there are not especially small, though visitors remember them that way. They are usually seen kneeling, crawling, or sitting with their legs folded beneath them. Inside a house, where a person can stand, they straighten slowly. An old woman rising to take a bowl from a shelf may turn out to be taller than the traveler she has admitted.

Their clothes are close-fitting at the shoulder and loose through the body, so that a little warm air can be carried beneath them. At the knees and elbows they sew patches of smooth, dark fiber. These are replaced often. A person’s good coat may have scarcely any of its first cloth left at the joints.

Children learn to hold on before they learn to walk. There are braided loops along the inner walls of the houses, and a child going from bed to hearth travels hand over hand, solemnly, without much need of them. Outside, the same habit serves another purpose. The first excursion from the entrance is made with a cord tied around the waist. An adult sits nearby holding the other end, mending something, apparently inattentive.

The children are allowed to fall. They are not allowed to let go.

Much of their education concerns what can bear weight. A green surface may be firmly rooted, loosely rooted, floating over water, or stretched across a hollow by stems that will hold a lizard but not a foot. An apparently solid shelf of soil may be the roof of an animal’s passage. There are particular plants children are taught to test with the backs of their hands: one stiffens when dry, another becomes brittle, another looks unchanged but loses its grip on the stone.

By adulthood they can move through a basin with their eyes on someone across it, feeling the ground through their soles. Visitors find this unsettling. They assume the people are careless until they try to follow.

***

The most numerous of the larger animals live beneath the green.

They are about the length of a forearm, with bodies that look unfinished when exposed: blunt heads, almost no neck, forelegs set wide, a long supple middle, and hind feet which can turn far outward. Their fur is brown near the skin and pale at the tips. A dry animal is the color of old root fiber. A wet one becomes nearly black.

The people call them seam-runners. The name describes where they go, but not how slowly they spend most of their lives.

A seam-runner works through the underside of a plant mat by pushing its head between two stems and waiting. Gradually the stems separate. It advances, stops, and pushes again. Behind it, the vegetation closes. Its passage makes little more disturbance than a hand passing beneath a blanket.

The animal has conspicuous whiskers, some short and stiff, others so long they lie back over its shoulders. Its eyes are small, but not blind. If lifted into daylight it squints with an expression of patient offense, then begins to search the lifting hand for somewhere underneath.

It eats berries, soft roots, moths, and the pale grubs that live in decaying wood. The heaps of shells beside certain roots are its work. It brings the hard-shelled seed fruits to the same place, holds them against a stone, and wears through one end with its teeth. A favored stone becomes smooth in the middle and surrounded by fragments. Some are used for years.

A person clearing a new sleeping pit may discover such a place and move it a little way aside, stone and shells together. Usually the animal finds it. Sometimes it spends several evenings bringing the stone back.

Seam-runners are seldom hunted. Their meat is poor, and they help keep the grubs from hollowing the house roots. More importantly, their passages show where a mat has begun to loosen. A household that sees its familiar animals coming indoors with bits of dry soil in their fur goes out to inspect the roof anchors.

This is useful knowledge, not an infallible warning. A seam-runner may also enter a house because someone has left berries uncovered.

***

Their young are born in chambers woven from stripped leaf ribs. The chambers are remarkably clean. The mother carries out wet bedding and replaces it with fibers warmed against a fissure, making many trips with a tuft protruding from either side of her mouth.

For the first few days the young cling to her belly. Later they hold the long fur along her flanks. A mother moving beneath the plants can look impossibly broad, with six or eight little feet searching for purchase as she squeezes between roots.

People sometimes find a whole family in a field torn loose by the wind.

If the field has caught in another hollow, the animals may survive without assistance. If it has landed upside down on bare stone, the mother tries to burrow upward through it. This is one reason travelers carry a short, curved cutting blade. A stranded mat is turned green-side up if possible; if not, a shelter is cut beneath its downwind edge.

No one follows a mat to the cliffs.

After a great wind, children search the newly lodged vegetation for unfamiliar shell heaps. There is pleasure in knowing that something has arrived from a basin no member of the household has ever visited.

***

In the warmest hollows there is another animal, difficult to distinguish at first from a fleshy stalk.

It has a narrow, translucent body and moves on a broad foot. Two dark folds run along its sides. At night these open into thin, ruffled surfaces which collect beads of moisture. Before dawn the folds close again, carrying the water inward. The creature retreats beneath a leaf and remains there through the bright hours, slowly rasping the softened surface.

They grow no longer than a finger. Their eggs, laid inside dead stems, look like drops of milk.

Children keep them in bowls with pieces of damp root. Adults discourage this because most bowls are too dry, and because the creatures have a talent for leaving. In a house they travel toward the warm wall and settle in the seams between slabs. A lamp may reveal one there months after everyone has forgotten the bowl.

Their trails are visible only at certain angles. A basin seen from the right place at dawn can be threaded with silver. As the sun rises, the threads disappear one after another.

The small pale moths feed on these trails.

***

The households raise more food than a traveler notices. What looks like an undisturbed tangle may have been tended for generations. Bitter shoots are cut back. Berry stems are bent into sheltered pockets and pegged down until they root. Fibrous plants are encouraged along the exposed rim, where their interlaced stems protect softer growth behind them.

Their fields are irregular because the shelter is irregular. A few steps can separate a place where seed ripens from one where every flower is torn away.

Work is done lying on one side as often as kneeling. Farmers carry little wooden wedges to lift layers of vegetation without breaking them. Beneath the lifted green they inspect roots, remove pests, and push handfuls of dark compost into places where the soil has thinned. Then they lower the layer and press it down with their palms.

The most painstaking work is the tending of new edges. After a section tears away, the exposed border of the remaining field must be weighted, stitched, and covered before its roots dry. Whole households come out with baskets of wet fiber. They work in shifts, keeping their tools tied to their wrists.

A person may spend three days saving a strip of plants narrower than a bed. Later, that strip will shelter the growth advancing across the bare patch.

People remember who came to help.

***

There is an older woman in one basin whose duty is to keep the sound paths clear.

These are hollow roots leading between the chambers of the settlement. She feeds a slender cord through them, pulls a tuft of fiber from one end to the other, and listens afterward while someone taps at the far opening. A blocked root can sometimes be opened with warm water. A split one must be replaced or carefully bound.

She knows voices altered by passage through wood. A man who sounds thin and distant through one root is full and unmistakable through another. Children like to whisper into the openings and wait for her to identify them. Occasionally she pretends to be mistaken.

The sound paths are not used for everything. A private conversation is still held close, after touching the other person’s arm. Bad news is usually brought by someone crawling all the way into the room.

The woman has a seam-runner that sleeps beneath her drying basket. It is not tame enough to be held. It tolerates the movement of her feet and steals the pith she uses to clean the roots. When she has been away, it sometimes comes out to smell her cuffs.

She has given it a name which no one else can pronounce quite as she does. The name includes a small click made far back in the mouth.

***

The long-winged birds have no nests that anyone has found.

Their feathers are kept in narrow cases, where light and damp cannot reach them. Some are traded between households. Others belong to everyone in a basin and are brought out when news arrives from far away.

Up close, the feathers are less splendid than people expect. They are gray, long, and extraordinarily light, with shafts that bend almost double before resisting. Along one edge the barbs have tiny stiff hooks. Run a finger against them and the feather produces a faint dry sound.

The people disagree about what the birds eat. Someone once saw one rising with a dark object in its feet. Someone else has watched them linger over a drifting mass of uprooted vegetation. There are stories of birds sleeping against the undersides of cliffs, held there by the upward wind.

Nobody treats these stories as settled knowledge.

A child finding a feather is asked exactly where it lay, which way its tip pointed, whether it was dry, and what plants were nearby. The questions can feel disappointing after such a discovery. Later the child is allowed to carry the case to the neighboring basin.

***

At night, beneath the houses, the warm cracks shelter lives that almost never reach the surface.

The narrow lizards descend there. Small white insects graze on films along the stone. Water passes slowly through chambers crowded with roots. Some roots are living; some have lost their connection to anything green but persist for a long time in the wet darkness, being eaten from the outside inward.

When a well is cleaned, the bucket sometimes brings up a blind, soft-bodied creature with too many legs to count while it is moving. It folds tightly around the cord. Left beside the opening, it finds the dark and lowers itself out of sight.

The well cleaners dislike drawing water immediately after this. They wait, let the sediment settle, and argue about whether the creatures mean the passage below is narrowing or widening. Both claims have evidence. Neither has enough.

In certain houses, sleepers hear a slow scraping through the warm wall. It stops when a lamp is lit, or continues, or was only noticeable because someone happened to be awake. The owners check their stored food and the roots supporting the roof. If both are sound, they go back to bed.

***

In still weather the inhabitants become briefly easier to see.

Doors are opened. Bedding is spread across the stone. People sit upright outside and speak at an ordinary distance. The moths rise from the foliage in pale, uncertain numbers. Seam-runners venture out along the edges of the fields, keeping one flank against the stems. A lizard lies across a warm seam with its feet relaxed.

Such intervals may last no longer than the time required to empty a basket.

Then a grass blade leans. Somewhere beyond the basin, leaves turn their undersides to the light. A person reaches for the bedding while another puts a hand on a child’s shoulder. The seam-runner slides backward into the green. The lizard is gone before anyone sees it move.

By the time the wind arrives, most of what lives there has found its way beneath something else.
