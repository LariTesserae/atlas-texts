Up close, the pale animals are not quite the color of the stones. Their outer hair is translucent, flattened, and split at the ends. It holds dust in the way a frayed rope holds flour. Beneath it is a short brown coat so dense that a finger pushed into it would leave a hollow for several moments. The calves have only this undercoat. They acquire their pallor gradually, first along the spine, then over the shoulders, with the belly remaining dark through a second winter.

An adult lying down comes nearly to a person’s waist. Standing, it seems scarcely taller. Its legs are short, but much of their length is concealed inside the coat, and its feet are broad enough to carry it across wet gravel without breaking through. Each hoof is divided into three blunt lobes. These spread under weight and close when lifted, shedding the stones caught between them.

There is a joint above the foot that can turn farther than seems reasonable. On a slope, all four feet may point in different directions while the body stays level.

The head hangs low between the shoulders. It has no horns. What look like horns from a distance are the rims of the ears, thickened with cartilage and folded tightly against the skull. In still weather an animal opens them a little. The interior is black and finely furred.

Its eyes are larger than they appear, hidden behind a crease of skin and a fringe of stiff hairs. They look outward and somewhat down. A standing animal can watch the footing ahead, the feet of its neighbor, and much of the country behind it without lifting its head.

The muzzle is almost hairless. Along its lower edge are small, firm projections that feel their way over rock. When the animal encounters a useful crack, these press against either side, steadying the mouth while the tongue enters.

The tongue is dark, narrow, and much longer than the head. It can flatten to the thickness of a leaf or curl around a strand of root. Its upper surface is rough; underneath, it is smooth and wet. An animal working a crevice will draw the tongue out slowly, close its lips around whatever it has found, and remain motionless while swallowing. Sometimes it tries the same crack repeatedly. Sometimes it leaves after one attempt and walks directly to another, several body lengths away.

They remember ground at the scale of a mouth.

A particular animal may return for years to a fracture that no observer would distinguish from the hundred fractures beside it. When stones shift after a thaw, it searches the displaced pieces, nudging them with the side of its face. If the opening has closed, it may lie nearby for part of a day before moving on.

Their teeth are broad and low, with dark mineral stains in the grooves. Most food is crushed rather than cut. The roots they pull from beneath the gravel are tough, fibrous, and scarce; the films they lick from stone contribute salts and living cells. Digestion continues through long periods of rest. A herd that appears to have done nothing all afternoon has been processing the morning’s few mouthfuls, returning warmth to its extremities, and storing enough energy to stand again.

They are not all asleep when they lie down.

Within each gathering, a few keep their heads raised. These animals are difficult to pick out because they rarely turn or twitch. Watch long enough and one will lower its chin to the ground while another, some distance away, lifts its own. The change passes without a sound.

They are not taking turns in any exact order. A mother with a small calf is more likely to remain watchful. An animal that has recently fed will often settle deeply, its nostrils disappearing into its coat. An old one may keep its head up even while its eyes are closed.

The herds contain much smaller associations: a dozen animals that rest together, several females and their offspring, an aging male followed by two younger ones. Such groups can separate for weeks within the same basin and find one another again at a spring. Recognition begins with the breath. Two animals stand muzzle to muzzle, exhaling into the space between them. Then one presses its cheek beneath the other’s jaw.

There are voices, although a visitor might spend days among them without hearing one. The sound is low and brief, more readily felt through a resting hand than heard. Calves make a finer noise when searching for their mothers. A mother answers by breathing sharply through her nose, and the calf turns toward her through a crowd of nearly identical legs.

A newborn is very small for the size of the adult. It can fit beneath the mother’s chest while she lies down. For its first days, this is where it spends most of its time, tucked against bare skin hidden deep within the coat.

When the mother rises, the calf must already be on its feet. She lifts herself slowly, pausing halfway, and it steps out from under her. Others nearby give it room. In a narrow passage, the adults behind a calf will stop rather than urge it forward. There may be hundreds waiting, their backs disappearing into mist, while one dark youngster tests a rim of ice with its foot.

At the springs, the small animals beneath the grazers’ jaws become visible.

They are flat, jointed creatures, each about the length of a thumbnail, with a hard upper shield and a soft underside crowded with hooks. Several may occupy the warm fold behind a lower lip. Others cling to the throat, where the hair is sparse. They move whenever the grazer drinks, descending toward the mouth and then climbing back with their bodies swollen.

They feed on the loosened crust and the organisms in it. Their young are released into shallow water, where they spend a brief stage among the mineral grains. Many never reach an animal. Those that do may travel across half the plateau before dropping into another spring.

A grazer sometimes scrapes its jaw against a boulder and removes most of them. It seldom removes all.

The warm footprints have their own inhabitants. Threadlike worms lie in the mud at the bottom. Tiny, clear-bodied creatures work along the edges, consuming what the hoof has stirred up. When the water begins to freeze, some burrow downward. Others contract into hard specks that remain attached to grains of sand until another warmth releases them.

The spring films are harder to describe as individuals.

Lift a piece of black crust and its underside shows a pale, wet mesh. Some filaments follow the stone’s grain. Others cross it, joining into knots around minute particles of metal. There are several kinds of organism in the mesh, growing against and through one another. One opens the mineral surface; another uses what has been released; another carries charge along a sheath scarcely thicker than a hair.

The visible patch may be no larger than a palm. Its connections extend below the reach of a hand, down a fracture, into a wet seam, and out beneath another outlet.

Parts die continually. A passage dries, a crystal grows across it, or the water changes course. Nearby filaments thicken along the remaining routes. In some springs, a crust that appears unchanged from one year to the next has been replaced almost entirely underneath.

The grazers favor particular outlets. They may pass an open pool to crowd around a smaller one, waiting while each animal sets its lips to the same dark rim. At such places their muzzles are stained nearly black. The young learn the route by following, but old animals notice changes first. They taste the water, stand back, and move uphill to a seep that has only just begun to wet the gravel.

The predators watch these departures.

They are longer-legged than the grazers, though they carry their bodies close to the ground. Their dark coats have little loose hair to catch the wind. The tail is thick at its base and rests along the hind feet when they lie down. From above, a resting predator resembles one more exposed seam in the ridge.

Its patience is partly necessity. There is little cover, and an unsuccessful pursuit may cost more food than several quiet days require. It studies the herds at crossings, where careful movement becomes hesitation: a calf approaching water, an adult favoring one foot, an animal that has risen slowly and fallen behind.

When it finally runs, the speed is startling.

The pursuit is usually short. It turns the victim away from the others rather than trying to overtake it across open ground. A second predator may be waiting below a bank. Sometimes the two have lain out of sight of each other since morning.

After feeding, they bury what they can under loose shale. The covering is shallow and seldom conceals the smell for long, but cold does most of the preserving. Small scavengers arrive beneath the stones before the larger ones arrive above them. A carcass gradually acquires tunnels, chambers, dark feathers, shed skins. Meltwater carries its richness downhill. Several summers later, a grazer may stop there and pull a length of root from soil deeper than the soil around it.

Much of what lives here is encountered in this way: through a place that has become a little warmer, softer, darker, or more nourishing than its surroundings.

Turn over a stone and mites retreat into the impression it has kept in the earth. Look through clear ice and something inside changes direction. Touch a cushion of leaves and find that the stems continue into a crack beyond sight. Wait beside what seems to be an empty spring, and the black lip of it becomes crowded with minute moving bodies.

Farther out, a calf stands alone on the pale gravel. It looks too small for the country. For a while nothing approaches it.

Then a low shape lifts nearby, shedding frost from its back. Another rises beyond it, and another. The calf has been surrounded all along.
