The first sign of an inhabitant is a door that remains closed after its walls have gone.

It stands a little way inside the pumping station, beyond the reach of the blue light from the windings. Its lower edge is buried in silt. Roots pass through the holes where its hinges should be, but not through the gap beneath it. Water flows around the frame with unusual smoothness.

On one side, the wood is swollen and black. On the other, it is warm to the touch.

Occasionally something presses against that other side. The wood bows outward by a few millimeters. The roots tighten. After a while the pressure eases, and a small quantity of dark liquid emerges from the keyhole. A leaf immediately folds over it.

There need not be anything resembling a room behind the door. Often there is only a brick pier, or a dense stand of stems. Once, when the currents drew the vegetation aside, the back of the door was plainly visible. It was unfinished wood, furred with a pale growth. Whatever had pressed against it had done so from somewhere that did not occupy the space behind it.

There are other signs.

A cable enters a thicket and returns with one more strand than it had before. The additional strand is soft, has no metal core, and contracts when the engines falter.

Three bolts lying in the mud gradually turn until their heads face the same direction. Days later, a plate of new ground arrives with three empty holes along its edge.

In the northern shed, a patch of condensation forms on the inside of a wall, although the shed is full of water. The droplets run upward. They collect beneath a narrow seam and remain there through several cycles of the walls.

What lives here takes time to become distinguishable from what happens here.

Its smallest visible parts resemble pale cuffs fitted around the stems of plants. Each cuff is made of thin, overlapping material, neither quite leaf nor skin. It can remain motionless for weeks. Fine red filaments grow across it; silt obscures its edges. Then, during a particular combination of engine noise and current, it opens.

Inside is a dry fold.

The fold may contain nothing but darkness. More often there is a short length of some ordinary interior surface: painted plaster, worn linoleum, the corner of a wooden step. These things are proportioned for a body that never appears whole.

The cuff turns on the stem. Its opening faces the sound.

Farther along the same root, another cuff opens, then another. For a few seconds the exposed surfaces join. A strip of floor continues through several meters of forest. The underside of a stair passes through a pipe and emerges among the blades of a different tree.

Something moves along this continuity.

It is visible chiefly in the way the surfaces yield. A floorboard bends under a weight. Dust lifts from a step. A hanging cord is pushed aside and slowly swings back. Sometimes there is a glimpse of a narrow, jointed part, the color of porcelain before firing, drawing itself through an opening too small to admit it.

Then the cuffs close. The forest is only forest again.

The inhabitants are not confined to these passages. Much of each one is spread through the wet country: in roots, in the soft interiors of ridges, in the extra strands of cables. These portions grow and take nourishment without ever entering the dry folds. The part that moves gathers them into an arrangement it can use.

An individual may have a broad territory and a very small accessible body. Around the pumping station, one has established several dozen openings, yet can bring only a few into continuity at once. It spends long periods assembling a passage from the station door to a stand of sideways-growing trees. A change in current can undo half its work.

When that happens, it does not begin again immediately.

The cuffs remain open on the broken ends. From one comes a faint tapping. Another answers, farther off. The taps travel through roots and metal until a new route is found. Occasionally the route passes through a buried structure. A patch of mud rises, cracks, and reveals the edge of a tiled wall.

This accounts for some of the smaller sounds beneath the pounding. Not all of them are mechanical. There are careful knocks, the scrape of something being drawn over a threshold, the repeated soft impact of a surface tested before weight is placed upon it.

They feed in both conditions.

The dispersed parts take the dark liquid from the iron through the enclosing leaves. Within a fed cuff, the pale material thickens and develops shallow ridges. An unfed one becomes translucent. Its dry interior shrinks until there is barely room for a line of light.

The moving part needs something else.

When a rectangular bubble leaves the broken outlet pipe, nearby cuffs turn toward it. Roots lift from the ground. They do not seize the bubble; they lay themselves along its edges and travel with it.

Inside, the yellow-lit corridor remains empty for a time.

Then its closed door opens a fraction.

The bubble darkens. The corridor grows shorter, though the bubble itself does not diminish. Tiles slide together without breaking. The lamp approaches the door. At last only a narrow vestibule remains, occupied by a folded pale shape whose surfaces fit too closely together to follow.

When the bubble ruptures, the shape is gone. The attending roots have thickened. Several new cuffs open downstream.

Not every corridor is taken. Some travel untouched through the vegetation, their lamps burning steadily until they burst. The inhabitants appear to distinguish between them. They will abandon a nearly captured bubble to follow another, smaller one emerging from the same pipe. What they recognize may be beyond the closed door.

Their young are easier to see.

They begin in pockets where a root has passed through a loop of metal and returned through it again. The enclosed water clears. A skin develops across the opening, then a second skin, with a tiny dry interval between them.

For several days the interval contains a single pale object, usually resembling a knuckle or a tightly rolled petal. It turns whenever a loud sound passes through the ground.

Roots gather around the loop. Dark droplets are brought to it. Sometimes a mature cuff opens nearby and exposes a floor no larger than a coin. The young object extends a fine projection onto that floor, withdraws it, and tries again.

Its first successful passage leaves the pocket empty.

Afterward, small openings appear through the surrounding vegetation. They are poorly made: tilted doorways, steps that meet ceilings, corners with no space between their surfaces. Many close permanently. Others persist and grow.

Whole patches of new terrain sometimes arrive carrying these immature arrangements. Beneath their cooling skins are short passages worn smooth by something that has already been moving there. The roots threaded through the fresh ground belong to a life older than the ground around them.

The inhabitants seldom cross open clearings. They extend into them cautiously, sending roots through mud and waiting for driftwood, a fallen girder, a bank of silt—anything that offers another folded edge. A broad exposed surface seems difficult for them to occupy.

During a shutdown, their passages multiply. The softened vegetation allows cuffs to spread wide. Openings that usually reveal only a narrow seam become deep enough to show furnishings: a low shelf, a basin fixed to a wall, lengths of pale material hanging in an orderly row.

There are resting places inside.

One lies beneath the pumping station floor. It can be seen only while the foundations are raised. Roots hold apart a horizontal slit, and beyond it is a shallow chamber filled by the inhabitant’s gathered body.

At rest, that body is almost comprehensible. Long, flattened parts lie folded against one another. Hard joints rest in soft sockets. Along one side, thin membranes open and close around lengths of dark, fibrous tissue. Several of its extremities continue through the walls. Others have been tucked beneath it.

It makes no attempt to hold a fixed outline. As the chamber changes, it rearranges itself to remain in contact with the floor, walls, and ceiling. A narrow part draws inward; another unfolds into the space left behind.

When the gauge reaches the red sector, the resting chamber begins to contract.

The inhabitant rises through it slowly, gathering its folded parts. A small door opens near the ceiling. Beyond that door another opens, farther into the forest.

By the time the pumping station settles, the chamber is empty.

Outside, the roots around the brickwork loosen one at a time. A pale cuff closes on a stem near the entrance. Another opens beside the broken pipe, facing the next bubble to emerge.
