One of the knots beneath the hanging roots has changed position.

It was there when you passed in the morning: a pale swelling, no larger than a bundled coat, caught where three roots crossed. Now it hangs lower. A narrow part of it rests against the trunk, and two dark points have appeared on the side facing you.

For a while, that is all.

Then a hand loosens from the knot. It has four long digits and a broad, divided palm. Each digit unwraps separately, without disturbing the root it held. The creature lowers itself until its underside touches another strand of wood, pauses, and transfers its weight.

It does not jump. It seems unwilling to be unsupported even for the duration of a fall.

There are six limbs, though they are difficult to count while the animal is folded. The foremost pair are slender and end in those deliberate hands. The middle pair bear most of its weight when it travels over the surface. The hind pair turn almost backward, gripping whatever it has just left until the next hold is secure. On open ground it can move surprisingly quickly, its body passing through a succession of supports without much rising or falling. Among roots, it proceeds one attachment at a time.

Its body is long and soft-sided, covered with short, colorless hair through which the skin shows faintly amber. Darker patches lie over the shoulders and spine. Seen at rest, these resemble the stains on old root bark.

The face is broad at the mouth and narrow above it. Its nostrils close when it presses its muzzle into wet vegetation. The eyes are deep-set, sheltered by clear inner lids. It watches your hands more closely than your face.

Fully extended, it would reach a person’s chest. It rarely extends itself fully.

Most of its attention is directed through its palms.

Along the heel of each hand is a bare, ridged pad. The creature presses this against a supporting root before moving, and often remains still for several breaths. It is feeling more than the firmness of the wood. Somewhere farther along the strand, water is flowing. Something small is feeding. A load has shifted, or another creature has settled onto a branch.

The information travels poorly through some plants and very well through others. This animal knows the difference. It will cross a broad, solid-looking trunk without stopping, then listen for a long time on a vine no thicker than your wrist.

When it reaches the ground, it spreads all six feet.

Its weight scarcely dents the leaf cover.

These are the inhabitants of the chambers.

Not every chamber belongs to them, and not every one of them lives in a chamber. Some occupy places no person could enter: narrow galleries beneath a ridge, slits between descending root walls, warm pockets reached through submerged passages. Others sleep in the crowns for years before returning to the surface.

They do not make themselves conspicuous. A occupied dwelling may look less inhabited than an abandoned one. There are no heaps of discarded shells at its entrance, no smoke, no cleared approach. The leaves over its doorway are arranged to shed water without revealing the opening beneath.

Inside, the differences are small but persistent.

One root bears the polish of hands. Another has been persuaded to grow a shallow hook. A row of living bulbs hangs where a visitor might expect a shelf; each bulb contains something dry. The floor has several firmer patches, placed not at regular intervals but where a body naturally rests while reaching, feeding, or listening.

An empty chamber loses these distinctions. The hook lengthens. The bulbs split or take root. The firm patches begin to sprout.

The creatures maintain their homes by using them, but use alone is not enough. They pinch off buds. They rub particular shoots with substances carried in folds beneath their arms. They bind young stems together with strips of leaf that decay after the stems have joined.

Sometimes they get it wrong.

A roof grows too heavy on one side. A useful plant flowers and dies earlier than expected. A channel closes during a season when everyone who knows how to reopen it is elsewhere. The inhabitants leave such places, carrying what can be carried, and later generations may treat the resulting hollow as a natural feature.

They have small tools.

These are easy to overlook because most are made to resemble nothing in particular. A curved splinter probes a drainage passage. A hard seed casing scrapes the feeding surface from a root. There are wedges of dense, old wood, valuable because so little of the surrounding material will hold an edge or resist pressure for long.

A good wedge passes from hand to hand for many lives. It is stored above the damp floor, wrapped in a leaf that is replaced whenever the leaf begins to soften.

Their food comes chiefly from the pale layers.

At certain places they open the surface with their hands and uncover growth that has not yet met daylight: swollen stems, fungal fans, tender roots, translucent eggs laid by burrowing animals. They eat a little and close the opening again. Some feeding sites remain productive for decades. Others are exhausted, infected, or found by creatures less inclined to leave anything behind.

They also hunt.

A patient individual can lie with its palms against the ground for most of a day, shifting only when the vibrations beneath it change. When it strikes, the forelimbs vanish into the root felt almost to the shoulder. What it withdraws may be a long pale animal, still winding around the wrist.

The young are less patient. They chase surface grazers, overturn leaves, and dig indiscriminately. Adults stop them with a firm hand laid across the back.

There are places where they feed alone, and places where they will not permit another to feed at all.

Their quarrels are usually quiet. Two individuals face one another on a taut root and press, release, press. The messages travel through the support between them. If neither yields, they grapple. The hands that can arrange a new shoot so carefully can also pull a digit out of joint.

Old individuals often have crooked fingers.

Their speech is difficult to notice from outside.

They possess a few low vocal sounds, useful when there is no continuous structure between speaker and listener. At close quarters they touch one another’s wrists, throats, and flanks. Much of what passes between them goes through the house.

A message sent into the doorway root reaches the chambers in different forms. The nearest listener receives the sharp beginning; farther away, someone feels only a softened sequence. Responses return along another strand. Several inhabitants can communicate at once if they know the structure well.

A visitor leaning against the wrong wall may interrupt a conversation merely by absorbing its vibrations.

The houses therefore have places for strangers to sit.

Those bare, warm patches in the center of certain chambers are not beds, although someone may sleep there. They keep an unfamiliar body clear of the more sensitive supports. The leaves turning inward are responding partly to warmth and partly to the settling of the floor. In an inhabited house, that motion announces that the visitor has become still enough to approach.

A hand may then appear at the edge of the opening.

It will not necessarily offer food. More often it offers a piece of firm fiber, short enough to hold between two palms.

What follows feels at first like hesitation. A pressure. A pause. A slight turning of the offered piece. Another pressure, adjusted to the strength of your grip.

They are finding out how much you can feel.

Their children learn this before they learn to walk.

Newborns are small, dark, and nearly hairless. They cling beneath an adult’s body, enclosed by the six limbs whenever the adult rests. For a long time they have no reliable sense of where their own weight ends and the supporting growth begins. They reach for things that cannot hold them. They cry when placed on dead wood.

The first nursery lessons take place on a single broad root. An adult moves along it while the young remain in a cluster, learning the difference between a familiar footfall and water, between water and a feeding animal.

Later they learn the house.

Only much later do they leave it.

There is no single age at which one is grown. A youngster that has mastered a wet district may still be helpless in a dry crown. An old inhabitant, carried into unfamiliar growth, may spend days testing surfaces that a local child crosses without attention.

Their knowledge is closely attached to particular places.

This is one reason their departures are slow.

When a flowering front approaches, several households may begin moving years before it arrives. They visit the next district repeatedly, bringing young, testing food, learning which supports transmit clearly. Tools are carried in stages. A former dwelling may remain occupied by one or two adults long after the rest have gone.

Sometimes those adults never follow.

Age makes the limbs stiffer, but it does not always diminish the hands’ sensitivity. An individual that can no longer travel may still distinguish changes in water flow beneath an entire chamber. Others bring food and ask it, by touch, whether a new tremor matters.

Eventually its accustomed place is empty.

The body is carried into a feeding hollow and enclosed beneath fresh growth. For a time the household avoids drawing food from there. Later it returns. The plants have uses, the hollow has warmth, and the living still need to eat.

By then, a new resting place has usually been made elsewhere in the house.

At evening, the creature you first mistook for a root knot reaches a low opening beside the path. It presses one hand to the arch and waits.

Something inside answers.

It enters backward, hind limbs finding holds in the darkness, its eyes remaining on you until the leaves cover them. A few moments later, a smaller hand parts the leaves from within.

The hand stays there, palm against the threshold.

When you shift your weight, its fingers tighten slightly. When you stand still, they loosen.

It is young. It has been allowed to listen.
