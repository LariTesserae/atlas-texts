The people who live here have broad feet and very little wasted motion.

They are not especially small, though they often look small because of the way they stand: knees loose, head lowered, one shoulder turned against the wind. Their soles are covered by several layers of woven frond, with thin mineral scales slipped between them. A damaged sandal is taken apart immediately. Even an old person who has ceased to mend anything else will reach for a loose sole and turn it over in their hands.

There are eleven people in the hollow, at present.

This is not a number anyone expects to remain fixed. One woman has been away beyond the open edge for most of a season. There is a child whose place on the sleeping shelf has only recently been made. In the narrow shelter, an old man is dying slowly enough to be irritated by the attention.

They live among three structures, but not as three households. The windbreak is where they work when the gusts come from a favorable quarter. The roofed shelter holds their stores and most of their sleeping places. The recess is for illness, births, delicate repairs, and arguments that should not have an audience.

Nothing said there is entirely private. The stone carries voices. It is nevertheless considered private, and people elsewhere in the garden make an effort to occupy their hands.

Their clothes are pieced from tough, flattened strips of plant skin. Fresh strips are amber and almost transparent; old ones become brown, supple, and polished at the elbows. The seams are rubbed with wax. Cloaks have weights along their lower edges and loops inside for the wearer’s wrists, so that a sudden gust cannot turn a garment into a sail.

Everyone has burns. The burns are mostly on the hands, where gloves would make certain tasks impossible. Pale, tight patches run across knuckles and along the inside of wrists. The worst injuries are not displayed or hidden with any special care. A man with two fingers fused together has a scraper made to fit the hand he has.

They eat more often than visitors expect.

Pith is gathered after a stem bursts, while it is still soft enough to squeeze between finger and thumb. Some goes straight into leaf packets on the cooking stone. Some is kneaded with powdered mineral and the crushed bodies of tiny grazers, then sealed beneath wax. The resulting cakes keep for a long time and smell faintly of heated metal when opened.

The larger leaf-grazers are eaten too, but never all from one plant. Their work keeps the wax from closing the breathing pores. Children learn this while they are still being carried: take from several stems; leave the ones with swollen bellies; never strip the underside of a leaf merely because it is easy to reach.

There is no well, no cistern, no hidden vessel of precious water. Their nourishment comes in substances that seem dry until they are broken down inside a living body. They eat slowly, and close their mouths between bites. Nothing wet is left on the stone.

One of the oldest inhabitants has the task of feeding the dying man. She holds a little piece of warmed pith against his lips and waits. Sometimes he accepts it. Sometimes he complains that it has been scorched.

She tells him to cook his own.

This is an old exchange between them, and still useful.

***

The gardens are not given names according to where they stand.

Places would make poor names. The seven islands are known by features that travel with them: the Forked Crown, the Copper Belly, the One That Clicks. One is called Grandmother, though there is disagreement about whether the name first belonged to a person or to the largest plant in it.

Each island contains hundreds of separate lives, but the people speak to it as a whole when they work.

“Lift.”

“Leave that.”

“Not this way.”

The plants do not understand these instructions. Saying them helps the workers agree on what is happening.

They can influence an island’s movement, a little. A strip of warm mineral is laid near the roots, or a cold slab is moved over a crack to keep its breath from rising. A hook can be wedged temporarily. These are slow arguments. The plants follow heat and food, and occasionally drag away a tool someone was certain they had secured.

The people do not try to hold all seven in convenient positions. They maintain room around the shelters and access to the cooking seam. Beyond that, they accommodate what moves.

A woman with a cloudy left eye is particularly good at this work. She carries two wedges and a forked pole, and seems to spend most of the day leaning on the pole without doing anything. Then she shifts a stone, cuts a tendril, lifts a cushion of copper foliage, and by evening a passage that had been closing is open again.

Her name is Eren.

She has a habit of resting her palm against thick stems. Sometimes she does this to judge pressure. Sometimes she does it after the judgment is already made.

She favors the island called the One That Clicks. It is neither the largest nor the most productive, but its inner stems have an irregular dry ticking that can be heard through the floor of the roofed shelter. She slept beside that sound as a child.

When it travels too far away, she sleeps badly.

***

The little animals exposed beneath a moving island are almost colorless.

They have flattened bodies, many short legs, and a fringe of stiff hairs that holds dust above their skins. While the roots cover them, they browse among the debris. In the open they become frantic, not because light hurts them, but because the wind can roll them over. Their legs cannot right them quickly on polished stone.

The youngest child has appointed herself their rescuer.

Her name is Tal. She carries a shallow scoop made from an old leaf sheath and deposits the animals beneath the nearest cushion. She is not always gentle. She wants to save every one of them and becomes angry with those that run away from her scoop.

Some are eaten by the grazers before she has finished rescuing the others.

She has learned to choose a place without grazers.

For several days she tried to keep one in a folded leaf inside the shelter. It escaped each night and was found near the same crack. On the fourth day an older boy showed her the narrow comb beneath its body, and explained that it ate something which grew only where the stone’s warmth met the wax dust.

She put it back.

Now she checks that crack whenever she passes. There is usually a pale animal beside it. Whether it is the same one matters to her, though she has no way to know.

The burrowers are harder to know.

Sometimes a plate of pavement gives way and reveals one: a dense, blunt creature with digging shoulders and a naked, delicate face. Its eyes are smaller than the nostrils on either side of them. It holds still when exposed, pressing its chin to the floor of its tunnel, while the people hurriedly lay a slab across the opening.

They do not kill burrowers for food. The tunnels distribute the mountain’s breath. An abandoned passage fills with dust and eventually closes; an occupied one is kept open, enlarged, diverted. A burrower under the cooking seam is troublesome. A burrower that leaves the whole hollow is worse.

The knocks are not all warnings or messages. Some are the noise of work. The people distinguish several patterns, and are frequently wrong.

A person can spend an afternoon lying with an ear against a cool stone, trying to decide whether a sound is approaching.

***

The plated animal by the heat seam is older than anyone in the hollow.

Or rather, there has always been a plated animal there. Its armor changes so slowly, and the seedlings on its back disguise so much, that no one can say with certainty whether it is the same one.

It has a name, but the name is also used for its kind.

The people call it the Basket.

Small cleaners pass through its armor in streams. They take loose flakes from its skin and scraps from between the white plates of its mouth. In return they shelter inside it when a pressure gust comes. There are chambers beneath the great overlapping scales, dry and warm and surprisingly spacious.

The Basket eats fallen pith, dead roots, and occasionally a grazer that comes too close. Its mouth closes slowly. The grazer has time to escape, unless it is injured or occupied with feeding.

The children put shed hooks on its back and watch the cleaners carry them away.

Eren stops this when she sees it. Sharp hooks can lodge between the scales.

Once each season, two people lift the larger seedlings from its shell. The plants grip hard, and the animal objects by lowering itself onto their toes. They leave the low copper growth, which seems to cause no harm.

Afterward it walks to another seam.

Its movements are deliberate and immense in their effect. Creatures depart ahead of it. Stems lean away. Behind it remains a clean, broad track, scattered with things that had been lost under the foliage.

A clasp. A blackened cooking weight. A child’s scraper with a handle too small for anyone now living.

***

The hunters above the garden sometimes nest in the walls.

Their young begin as bundles of claws and folded wing. Before they can fly, they climb. They descend headfirst to take grazers from the cliff plants and are often driven back by older hunters. No human climbs after their nests. The meat is bitter, and the parents will strike at an exposed ear.

The adults learn the household movements. They recognize a basket of pith, the opening of the store shelter, the posture of someone distracted by a damaged sandal.

They have also learned that Tal looks upward before throwing a scrap.

For this reason she is forbidden to feed them near a doorway.

She obeys the location of the rule.

***

When the wind reverses, each person has somewhere to go, but no one assumes they will reach it.

They take whatever shelter is nearest. Eren goes down flat behind a stone rib. Tal gets her hands into the loops of an adult’s belt. Someone catches the dying man’s door before it can slam against his shelf.

For the minute of the mountain’s breath, the hollow is full of bodies holding on.

Afterward they count one another before they collect anything.

Then the work begins. Pith must be gathered. Broken stems must be checked. A blocked valve may need opening with a needle. The hunters reclaim their ledges, the Basket tests the air, and the first grazers unfold beneath the torn leaves.

Tal finds her scoop half buried in red powder.

Eren discovers that the One That Clicks has shifted toward the back wall. Its outer hooks have crossed the marked path. She stands beside it for a while, with one hand on a stem, and then fetches her wedges.

From inside the narrow shelter, the old man asks whether anyone intends to feed him.

Someone answers.

The sound is lost in the wind, but he knows the voice.
