It lives where the mud thins over stone.

Not on the bare pavement, where the grit would wear it, and not in the deep deposits, where a single strong tide could bury it beyond recovery. It occupies the uncertain edge between the two: a place where sediment accumulates against a rounded shelf, then drains away. Often there is no visible edge at all. The animal finds it underneath.

At first it resembles a fold in the bottom. One side is pale and dusted with sand; the other disappears into the gray deposit. There is no head raised to look into the water. Nothing about its outline distinguishes it reliably from a softened fragment of skin, or a strip of weed that has lost its color.

Then the pale edge tightens.

A few grains slip from it. Underneath is a surface with a faint, close-set pattern, like the marks left by cloth pressed into wet clay. The pattern shifts as the animal contracts. It is not skin lying loose on the bottom. It is a muscular rim, spread thin enough to meet the sediment without making a step.

Most of the body lies beneath that rim.

It is broader than it is thick, though neither dimension stays fixed. In quiet water it loosens and spreads into the mud, occupying a shallow space between deposit and rock. When the current strengthens, it draws inward. Its underside holds to the stone through many small areas of contact rather than one strong attachment. A part can let go without the whole animal being lifted.

This is how it moves. One margin releases, reaches a little farther beneath the sediment, and adheres again. The rest follows slowly. A journey of a body length may take several tides. From above, its passage is visible only as a slight settling of the mud, or a line that appears beside a pebble and is gone when the water turns.

Its back retains fine sediment in a thin layer of mucus. Shell dust, clay, and fragments of dead plankton adhere there, making a covering that the current can renew. Large grains are shed. When the covering grows too thick, small contractions loosen it in patches.

An old animal may carry several colors at once: dark mud from the channel, pale grit from a recently exposed shelf, a narrow rusty stain where it has rested against an iron-bearing stone. None is its own color. Beneath them it is a dull translucent gray.

Along the rim are openings too small to see at a distance. At slack water, threads emerge from them.

The threads do not stand upright like the branches of the sea pen. They lie across the bottom in loose curves, reaching into pits and around grains. Their tips are adhesive. They gather the material that has just settled, selecting fragments from among much greater quantities of silt. Each thread returns separately, drawing its small burden toward a groove beneath the rim.

Sometimes a thread remains extended too long. Sand begins to move around it. The animal releases it before the drag can tear the margin of its body. The lost thread travels down the channel among pieces of plankton and shell.

It can grow another, but slowly.

There are long intervals when it feeds on almost nothing. It closes the grooves, keeps a little water moving across its sheltered breathing surfaces, and waits beneath the sediment. Its reserves are spread through the body. Lean periods make it thinner rather than shorter. The rim becomes translucent; the muscular folds beneath it grow shallow.

It has no eyes. Light would tell it little that the water has not already announced.

Changes in flow reach it through the tension of its feeding threads and the pressure along its exposed edge. Within the body are small organs sensitive to tilt. These help it keep its underside against the rock when sediment pours into the spaces around it. It also responds to vibration. A nearby fish settling into a hollow produces a brief contraction. A pebble knocking repeatedly against its shelter keeps the nearest part of the rim closed.

It has no permanent forward end. When the tide reverses, one set of feeding grooves closes and another opens. The body need not turn.

During the strongest flows, it withdraws entirely beneath its covering. Water still reaches it through short passages held open by folds of tissue. These can clog. A deposit of unusually fine mud may seal them all, and an animal that has survived for years can die under a layer scarcely thicker than a shell.

Others are exposed when the sediment is stripped away. For a while they show plainly against the stone: low, irregular bodies with scalloped margins, too soft to belong to the pavement. Small crustaceans approach the damaged edges. A fish may lift one side and tear away a mouthful.

The animal contracts around the wound. If enough of its underside remains attached, it survives. The missing margin reforms unevenly, leaving a notch that persists through many seasons.

Its young spend little time in open water. They develop in small recesses beneath the parent’s rim, held away from the grinding surface. When they leave, they are no larger than coarse sand. Some crawl directly under the same shelf. Others are carried off, making brief contact with the bottom, losing it, and making contact again.

Most find no suitable place.

One may catch beneath a stone newly uncovered by the turning flow. It flattens against the lower face, where the grit cannot reach it directly. Over the next few days, mud gathers around it. Its first feeding threads extend into that mud.

Above, the shell in its hollow tips onto its edge and starts down the channel. Sand runs along the floor. The stone begins to disappear again.

Beneath it, the young animal holds on.
