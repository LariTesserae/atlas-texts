The first inhabitant to find you is likely to mistake you for a place.

It settles in the fold of your sleeve while you stand listening at a junction of paths. It has a flat, yielding body, six short limbs, and a fringe that explores the cloth without seeming to move. Against your wrist it feels like a piece of soaked bark. It wants the warmth held between your skin and the waterproof outer layer. If you remain still, it begins fastening itself there with a secretion that will later have to be rolled away under a thumb.

The station workers call these creatures cuffs. They live on the sheltered sides of grazing animals, in the crooks of roots, beneath pipe insulation. There is usually one behind the handle of an outdoor door. A cuff eats the thin growth that develops wherever warmth and moisture remain together. On a person it is harmless until it finds a scrape.

It does not know you have arrived. It knows that a favorable surface has become available.

Much of the plateau’s life encounters other life this way.

The combs beneath the trees contain small, jointed animals that spend their entire lives moving along a few inches of wet tissue. They scrape microbial films from one strand and carry their waste to another. The trees grow more vigorously where they feed. In a dry interval the comb folds inward around them, leaving narrow passages through which water continues to creep.

Other creatures live inside the folds of leaves. Their bodies are long enough to bridge the drainage channels, and their eggs adhere in rows to the undersides. When a leaf closes, they flatten. When it opens, they resume feeding. A visiting animal may brush against an entire colony without disturbing it, then dislodge half its members by breathing too warmly across the opening.

Here, disturbance is a matter of pressure, heat, chemistry, vibration. A large body can pass unnoticed. A drop of unfamiliar sweat can empty a branch.

The broad-winged animals above the marshes have faces that are mostly listening surfaces.

Their mouths sit low, under pleated folds of flesh that extend along the neck. They make a soft pulse, then leave an interval in which the answering air can be heard. Close to foliage, the pulses shorten. Over open water they become so widely spaced that a person below may hear only the wings.

They feed with their feet. Each foot opens into a loose basket of hooked digits, lowered through the canopy to gather the smaller clicking fliers. Sometimes a basket catches a twig. Then the animal hangs briefly from one leg, beating the air with ponderous strokes while it works itself free.

They are not graceful in every circumstance. Around a pumping house, with its abrupt walls and unexpected railings, they blunder often. Workers stretch cords across dangerous approaches. The cords sing in the draft from the ventilation outlets, and the fliers learn to turn before reaching them.

The herds are harder to divert.

An adult grazer is broad enough that two people meeting it on a narrow path must both retreat. Its head hangs beneath a muscular arch of neck; the horns project sideways and slightly down, useful for parting stems and lifting mats of growth. Along its flanks, deep skin folds hold warm air and support a cargo of cuffs, comb-feeders, small scavengers, and at least one kind of worm that travels from host to host when the animals sleep pressed together.

Its feet have spreading rims. They open under weight and close when lifted, though never quickly enough to prevent the familiar wet sucking sound.

The herd keeps in contact by touching as much as by calling. A youngster follows the brush of its mother’s tail against its muzzle. Adults lean into one another at uncertain crossings. The low throat sounds tell a straying animal where the others are; pressure along the ribs tells it which way they intend to go.

The routes they maintain are inherited in the bodies of the herd. Older animals pause at places where a bank once collapsed, although roots have long since made it firm again. They avoid an abandoned intake for generations after its machinery stops. Elsewhere they force their way through saplings toward a warm seep that no longer rises.

A herd can be wrong about the ground. This is one reason people do not simply follow them.

The people live mostly beside the stations, but not inside the engine rooms.

Their dwellings occupy shelves cut into the warmer rock, or stand on piles above ground that has proved, over several lifetimes, to drain reliably. A settlement may be only seven houses, a drying room, a food chamber, and a workshop connected by covered handrails. From outside it can be difficult to distinguish from the surrounding growth. Water runs over the roofs. Roots enter the spaces beneath the floors. The inhabitants keep certain approaches clear and allow the rest to thicken.

Inside a house, the first impression is of boundaries.

There is a dry place for sleeping. There is a wet place for removing boots. A shallow groove in the floor marks the beginning of the cooking area. Objects that must not be knocked over stand in recessed shelves, each shaped differently under the hand. The floorboards have edges a bare foot can read without looking down.

No one puts a chair in the middle of a passage.

Children learn the houses before they learn the forest. They crawl along warm pipes enclosed in protective sleeves. They discover which walls carry the noise of the station and which merely tremble in sympathy. They are taught not to put a finger into a hole just because something inside has tapped back.

Their first journeys outside are made along a cord attached to a parent’s belt. Later they walk with one hand on the rail. Later still they are sent to bring something from a neighbor and return by a different route.

The hardest lesson is to keep listening while frightened.

A frightened child hears the nearest sound as if it were the only sound. A frightened adult can do the same. The training is practical: stop, loosen the jaw, identify the surface under both feet, listen for water, then for something beyond the water. Only then move.

The inhabitants have eyes, though these contribute little to finding their way. Faces are known by voices, by the shape of an embrace, by fingertips passed briefly across brow and cheek. A person who has not been touched in years may still be recognized immediately by the rhythm of their steps.

Visitors often speak too much. They mistake silence for absence and keep announcing themselves until no one can hear what is approaching.

At first, a local guide answers every few paces. Eventually the guide rests two fingers against the visitor’s wrist and lets the place speak.

Food comes from managed beds near the warm springs.

The most important crops are thick sheets of living tissue cultivated on lattices in mineral water. Their undersides house organisms that feed on what rises from the stone; their folded upper surfaces exchange gases with the moving air. Harvesters cut away a portion and leave the supporting layers intact. Different springs yield different flavors. Some crops need long washing. Others are boiled until their fibers separate, then pressed into cakes that keep well in the drying rooms.

There are root bulbs, cultivated combs, trapped fliers, and herd animals taken at particular crossings. Cooking is done in closed vessels because an uncovered pot attracts things from the walls.

People argue about food, especially about how much washing is enough. A newcomer may be told solemnly that a certain basin produces the finest crop on the plateau, then discover that everyone outside that basin describes its produce as tasting of old pipe.

Work at the stations requires similar local knowledge.

A keeper knows a turbine through its whole range of loads. The housing’s pitch, the beat transmitted into the handrail, the interval between governor movements: together these disclose its condition. A replacement part can be within specification and still sound wrong for months. Keepers complain about this while admitting, eventually, that nothing is failing.

Their listening rods are personal tools. Some have short handles fitted to the palm; others can be braced against the jaw. The tip goes against metal, masonry, or ground. Through it, a worker separates a damaged bearing from a root pressing on the casing, or water hammer from something alive striking an intake grille.

Every station keeps records in raised notation. There are service histories, pressure cycles, routes to neighboring installations, births, deaths, and accounts of dangerous weather. The oldest records include corrections made by several hands. A crossing described as firm becomes unreliable; later a new line gives the location of the rail built above it.

There are disputes over those corrections. People who maintain one basin do not readily accept that someone from another understands its water.

Beyond the settlements live families who visit the stations only occasionally. They keep herd routes, tend distant food beds, salvage metal, and trade knowledge of crossings. Their arrivals are heard first in the heel plates at the door, then in the careful setting down of wet equipment.

They bring news that takes time to become clear: a suspended grove has lowered; a salt spring has begun pulsing; a familiar path now ends at moving water. Someone asks when. Someone else asks how warm the water was. A meal is served while the account is told again.

They also bring young people who want station work, and station workers who have had enough of the noise.

There are predators in the inland forest that none of them willingly approach.

One kind lies with its body distributed among several root hollows. Its limbs are long, flexible, and lined with pressure-sensitive bristles. It remains still while small creatures move over it. A footfall traveling through the roots makes it draw its limbs inward, comparing the arrivals of the vibration. It does not strike at every large animal. Often it waits through a herd’s passage and reaches for the last, separated body.

People notice it most often through an absence: a stretch of combs with no feeders moving inside them, or a patch of reeds in which the ordinary small noises have ceased.

They go around. On returning, they enter the location in the route records, with the date and the conditions under which it was heard.

An old keeper, resting between shifts, may sit with one palm on the wall beside the bed. Through the stone comes the station’s familiar motion. Farther away, a herd works along the bank. Something is feeding beneath the floor and will have to be removed in the morning.

Then the deep water begins to move.

The keeper wakes fully, waits through the first long tremor, and listens for the change in the turbines. In the next room, another hand settles against the wall. Neither person speaks until they know whether they need to get up.
