The first inhabitant you meet may appear to be part of a doorway.

They stand with one shoulder against the wall, a long, narrow figure in a coat of overlapping gray plates. The plates are shedding at the edges. So is the wall. For a while the only obvious distinction is that the figure has two pale hands folded at its middle.

Then a passing freight husk makes the avenue click, and the hands separate.

One goes to the jaw. The other rests flat against the house.

The gesture is habitual: feeling the disturbance in two places, inside the body and outside it. When it has passed, the inhabitant turns toward you. There is no visible white in their eyes, only a dark, finely pebbled surface that catches the sky in several small reflections.

They have been waiting for the house to finish waking.

***

The people are taller than they seem at a distance. Their usual posture conceals it. They carry their heads low, shoulders drawn forward, elbows close to the body, keeping the soft joints sheltered from wind. Indoors, with their coats loosened, they unfold into surprising height.

Their bodies are narrow through the chest and broad at the pelvis. Four thick toes spread inside each shoe. The soles have enough give to distribute weight over brittle scales, but the people themselves are not particularly light. Under their clothing they have a close, dense covering of fibers, dark near the skin and pale at the tips.

At the wrists, throat, and corners of the mouth, the fibers give way to smooth tissue. These exposed places are faintly violet.

A person who has been working outdoors all day develops a powdery bloom across them. Before entering a house, they wipe it away with a strip of felt and place the strip in a little recess beside the door. By morning the wall has drawn most of the useful salts out of it.

They do not sweat. When overheated, they open narrow vents along the ribs, revealing folded membranes that look black until light strikes them obliquely. The membranes are easily damaged by grit. A strenuous job therefore requires someone else to stand nearby with a wind screen.

Their mouths close very tightly. Speech comes from chambers farther down the throat, and often continues while the lips appear sealed. It has a low, carried quality: murmurs, clicks, short harmonic notes. You can learn to hear where one word ends by watching the small movement beneath the jaw.

They breathe less often than you expect.

When frightened, they breathe much more often, and hate being noticed doing it.

***

Their hands are what you remember.

Each has three long fingers and a broad opposing thumb. The fingertips are covered in concentric ridges, pale enough to look worn smooth. Between the ridges lie sensory pits that respond to pressure, charge, and the slow chemical traffic of the plants.

A hand laid against a wall receives much more than warmth.

It can feel whether the rooms beyond are occupied, whether the nearest freight stem is ready to accept a load, whether a structural root has begun to pull in the wrong direction. Some people can distinguish individual neighbors through several connected houses. Others need to press both hands down and concentrate.

Children are notoriously poor at this. They ask the pavement for destinations with so much enthusiasm that they receive contradictory instructions and end up in the wrong square.

You sometimes see a child standing perfectly still, palm planted on the road, while an adult kneels beside them and says, with increasing patience, that asking harder is not the same as asking clearly.

The child’s coat will be bright along its seams. Children are allowed to wear conductive thread exposed, so that anyone can find them during a discharge. Adults cover theirs. To go on shining after maturity is considered either theatrical or a sign that one’s family worries too much.

***

Their clothes are cultivated separately from their bodies.

A coat begins as a sheet of tough tissue in a shallow frame. It is fed, folded, and persuaded to grow hinges at the elbows. Once mature, it is detached and allowed to become almost entirely dormant. Good coats retain enough life to close small tears.

Old ones lose this faculty.

There are people in town wearing coats repaired so many times that nothing remains of the original surface except a little patch under one arm. The patches may come from relatives, former lovers, an employer, a dead person’s wardrobe. They differ slightly in color and flexibility. On a windy day, an old coat rattles unevenly.

This is not necessarily a sign of poverty. A badly fitted new coat can be much more humiliating than a well-maintained old one.

Shoes matter more. A sole that transmits too much pressure can trouble a nervous stretch of avenue; one that insulates too thoroughly leaves its wearer unable to feel a warning. Shoemakers keep trays of living substrate in their workshops and make customers walk back and forth over them.

Visitors are usually given temporary soles before they are given directions.

***

The town is sparsely built, but its people are not solitary.

Several households may use the same kitchen while sleeping in buildings a square apart. A workshop can have its tools in one depression, its workers in another, and its accounts kept by an elderly person who rarely leaves a bench on the intervening ridge.

Distances are measured partly by the town’s willingness to shorten them.

Two houses can be socially close and inconveniently far apart. A disagreement between their occupants may persist as a reluctance in the road: the avenue will still carry each person where they ask to go, but it stops making the helpful little adjustments that once brought them together.

Nobody agrees on how much of this is the town’s own judgment.

Some insist that it merely responds to the habits of those who walk it. Others speak to their thresholds before bed.

People in a hurry take routes along the oldest ridges, where the grain is stiff and the distances remain dependable. The walk is longer, but at least it stays longer. Messengers know these paths well. So do impatient people who claim not to be impatient.

***

Food is kept in shallow sealed boxes.

There are dense wafers made from the inner tissue of selected stems, translucent curls of cultured membrane, mineral-rich pellets produced by the small grazers, and soft portions wrapped in their own tough skins. A meal is opened one piece at a time. Anything left exposed acquires a chalky bitterness.

The people eat slowly, pressing each mouthful between plates inside the cheeks before swallowing it.

They can digest much of the vegetation around them, but casual feeding from the street is discouraged. Public surfaces have been asked to bear weight, carry signals, and resist wind. A person gouging nourishment out of an avenue would leave a poorly placed wound for someone else to repair.

Children try it anyway. The youngest peel little curls from stair edges and hide them inside their sleeves.

There are specialists who cultivate food by interrupting electrical flow through a stem at precise intervals. The resulting tissue swells, stores rich compounds, and stops growing structurally useful grain. These specialists are treated with respect and watched closely. A productive crop can make a neighborhood’s doors sluggish for days.

At a shared meal, complaints about doors are usually directed at whoever brought the best food.

***

Much of their work consists of noticing a small change before it becomes a large one.

A maintenance crew crosses a square carrying no machine you would immediately recognize. There are wedges wrapped in insulating felt, spools of dull wire, jars of powdered crust, a tuning frame held carefully between two workers.

They stop beside a raised threshold.

One kneels and puts an ear against it. Another fits the frame over the doorway. The others wait.

Then they begin a sequence of pressure, vibration, and charge, coaxing new growth into a shape that will carry the old load differently. The work can take hours. Someone nearby brings food. A resident leans out to ask whether the door will be ready before evening.

Occasionally the threshold drops level all at once, knocking the kneeling worker backward.

Everyone laughs except the worker.

Some damage cannot be persuaded away. In those cases they cut.

A major cut brings neighbors out of their houses. The released solvent clouds at the wound, and the new white crust smells so strongly of resin that people cover their throat vents. Waste tissue is caught in sealed sheets and carried to nurseries. There is no casual pile of damp sawdust here, nothing left to dry later.

The town’s oldest scars have names. Directions include them.

Turn at the Bad Repair. Keep going past the Split That Held.

***

The weather towers employ people whose bodies are unusually sensitive to differences in charge.

They spend long apprenticeships learning to distinguish a dangerous gradient from the sensation of their own alarm. This proves harder than learning the instruments.

Inside a working tower, the air presses at the teeth. Fibers hang from the ceiling in dense curtains, each attached to a neighboring possibility of wind. The operator touches them with insulated tools, opens a connection briefly, listens to the response, and closes it again.

A stronger wind brought into the street must come from somewhere. Sometimes it arrives carrying dust the local plants cannot identify. Sometimes the whole town smells, for an hour, of a resin no one grows.

Tower workers are examined after each shift. Their jaw ridges are checked for whitening. Fine burns on their fingertips are recorded. They are forbidden to work alone, though some do.

An experienced operator may become distracted by weather no one else can feel. They pause during conversation to listen to a gust that never quite reaches the square.

Retired workers prefer rooms with thick walls. Several keep winged animals, which settle calmly around them even on difficult evenings.

***

The animals live close to the people without becoming entirely domestic.

Small channel creatures nest beneath warm kitchen floors. They take crumbs, shed scales, and conductive dust. Their movement can be soothing until a colony discovers a resonant chamber and begins running through it at night.

Householders discourage them by changing the floor’s charge. The creatures retaliate by moving into the doorway.

Long-backed browsers are marked with tiny clips along the spine. The clips identify which keeper is responsible when one opens a feeding seam in a public wall. These animals recognize people by the pressure of their footsteps. A familiar keeper approaching across a basin can cause several pale heads to rise at once.

Predators occasionally enter the town.

When that happens, there is no shouted alarm. A warning passes beneath the avenues. Doors open for anyone nearby, then close and stiffen. People still outside stand with their feet apart and their clothing held clear of the ground, making themselves easy for the substrate to distinguish.

For several minutes the squares become very quiet.

Afterward, everyone discusses how close it came. Someone always says they felt it first.

***

Age changes the inhabitants’ relation to the ground.

Young people feel sharp local events: a seed case snapping, a door loosening, a freight transfer. Older people become more sensitive to slower things. They can sit for half a day and describe the distribution of a building’s weight, or say that a basin will need attention within a season.

This is useful, but tiring. An old person may avoid a busy avenue because every passing instruction arrives as a disturbance in the fingers.

Their benches are carefully placed. The single rooted seat outside a tower may occupy the least communicative patch of ground in the square.

Toward the end of life, they often sleep in rooms detached from the common root network. Family members visit on foot, over distances the town no longer shortens. The journey gives everyone time to prepare.

After death, the body is opened in a sheltered chamber. Its scarce internal fluid is recovered; salts and useful compounds are separated; the fibrous tissues are laid into a prepared nursery bed.

The family keeps something dry.

Often it is a section of hand, sealed and mounted where visitors can touch it. The sensory ridges survive long after sensation has gone. Over generations, these pieces become smooth from handling.

***

At evening, people emerge while the rooms rearrange behind them.

They gather in the open squares, leaving space between their bodies as they do between their houses. Meals arrive in warm husks. Children are called down from the low freight stems. Someone tests an instrument against the great chord moving through the hills, trying to find a note that will remain audible when the wind changes.

Conversations pause whenever the ground shifts.

One person reaches out to steady another. A bowl is lifted before the bench beneath it tilts. Across the square, a maintenance worker notices a new ridge and makes a tired little mark on a strip of recording tissue.

When the discharge comes, the people lower their heads. Their exposed throat patches gleam violet. Children’s seams shine white.

For a moment every household is visible: figures standing well apart, hands on benches or walls or one another’s sleeves, waiting while the immense living country takes the charge beneath them.

Then the light passes. Someone retrieves a fallen piece of food. The musician begins the note again.
