The red animal is not red all over. Its back is the color of brick dust after rain; its underside is pale, and the joints of its legs are almost clear. In the threaded hole of the nut, these distinctions disappear. Only a little red thickness shows, tucked into the lowest turn of the brass.

It has six legs when walking. When it works, it seems to have four, because the foremost pair lift and remain lifted, holding something or feeling ahead. There are short, blunt claws at their ends. The claws fit the scratches in the brass better than they fit the mineral below.

Its body is flattened, but not evenly. One side carries a slight bulge, as though an internal organ has been packed too close to the skin. To pass through a narrow opening it must turn the same shoulder forward each time. This makes its departures from the nut recognizable: a hesitation, a sideways adjustment, then the quick drawing-through of the rest of itself.

It lives inside the threads rather than in the hole as a whole. The central shaft is too exposed. Along the spiral, lint has been packed into three of the deeper grooves. One patch is dark with moisture. Another contains pale oval objects, each attached by one end. In the third, which receives the last evening light, the animal sleeps.

There may once have been a fourth occupied groove. A fringe of fibers remains there, high above the others.

The animal spends much of its waking time removing white material from its claws.

It does this carefully, bringing each foot to a mouth too small to see clearly, then drawing it through. A foot neglected for a day becomes stiff. A foot neglected longer would acquire a shell. This is a problem for several of the inhabitants. The ants bite deposits from one another’s knees. The centipede rubs its sides against the copper tube. The little snails cannot clean the backs of their shells and therefore carry low, chalky fins, delicate until they catch against something.

The red animal is unusually clean. Only its left rear foot has a permanent white cuff. When it climbs glass, that foot makes a tiny audible scrape.

It does not eat the grains it keeps on the nut.

It eats what gathers beneath them: a soft bloom, almost colorless, that develops where grit rests on warm metal. With its forelegs it lifts a grain, holds it away from itself, and works its mouth across the damp spot. Then it puts the grain back. Each has its place. One sits beside a nick in the rim, one above a darkened thread, and one wherever there is room.

The third is the grain that falls.

Sometimes it is pushed after being inspected. Sometimes it is touched several times and left until the following evening. The animal will stand over it with its forelegs raised while its rear legs make small adjustments on the brass. If an ant appears on the column below, it waits for the ant to pass.

Once dropped, the grain is not abandoned.

After the machinery has been quiet for a while, the animal descends. This takes much longer than walking around the rim. Yesterday’s mineral growth gives good purchase; today’s is soft enough to retain the marks of its claws. It travels down the shaded side, pauses under the nut, and then enters the broken country at the column’s foot.

Here its color is a disadvantage. Against lime it is conspicuous. Against iron stains it vanishes.

It keeps to those stains where it can.

The other creatures do not make way for it, except when touched. An ant goes over its back with a fragment of bread. A mite, startled from beneath a leaf, knocks it sideways. The red animal rights itself and continues. At the fallen grain it tests the ground, puts its head beneath the grain’s edge, and remains there for some time.

By the next evening there is usually a fresh grain on the brass rim.

How it gets there is difficult to observe. The climb happens during the laundry’s busiest hours, when moving shadows and vibration make a small body easy to lose. On one afternoon the red animal can be seen halfway up the column, facing downward, with something pale held beneath its chest. It climbs backward for the remaining distance.

There are others like it, though none lives so high.

One occupies the fold where the glove’s smallest finger meets its palm. This one is darker and has no white cuff. Two very small ones shelter beneath the drain cover. They come out after the local rain to drink from depressions that are barely wet enough to shine. Their abdomens swell visibly. Afterward they can no longer fit through the crack by which they emerged and must wait beside it, pressed flat, until they have diminished.

These smaller animals do not keep grains. They turn over husks, scraps of paint, the thin circular flakes that fall from the pipe. Beneath each object is a little softness to feed on.

They are not yet good at judging what can be lifted.

The swept tile belongs to a larger inhabitant.

It is the same general shape as the red animals, but pale brown, with a back that has hardened into overlapping plates. It is broad enough that it must cross the wire ladder with its legs extended on either side. Under the platform, it hangs beneath a rung and rests there, its weight held by four hooked feet.

Its remaining two feet can turn the tap.

Not far. The movement is scarcely more than a twitch, followed by a long pause. Water gathers at the lip, slowly enlarging. Before the drop becomes heavy enough to fall, the brown animal touches it with its mouth. Sometimes it guides the water down one foreleg onto the tile.

The tile is swept with a piece of laundry fiber, gripped at its middle. The sweeping is thorough around the tap and less thorough at the edges. There, several minute bodies gather in the moisture left by the fiber. The brown animal steps over them.

It sleeps above the ladder, in a pocket of rust under the platform. Its back is dusted with particles from the metal ceiling. Its belly remains clean.

Below, the ground continues to rise around its route. The buried rungs have made the last step onto the tile increasingly awkward. For several mornings it has had to stretch down with one leg before releasing the rung above.

Soon it will need a different way.

Already, it pauses on a neighboring cable. Already, a narrow strip of that cable has been cleared of dust.

None of the inhabitants can keep the basin unchanged. They clear an opening and return to find it narrowed. They settle beneath a ledge that grows high enough to admit a predator. The spider’s lowest web has risen with its anchors until she can no longer reach it from her traveling cable; she has added a slack connecting strand and now approaches from underneath.

At evening, while the red animal attends to its three grains, the brown one waits above the tile. A snail stretches down from its pedestal. Beneath the white spur, a beetle carries an egg that has begun to adhere to the place where it was laid.

It stops once to pull the egg free.

Then it takes it farther into the dark.
