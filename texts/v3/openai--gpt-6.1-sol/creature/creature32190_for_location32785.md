Most of those who live here never see the green.

They know it from below: the resistance of a root across a passage, the softened fibers of a fallen stem, a change in the air where a wall meets living wood. Their bodies are pale and lightly armored. Taken out into the open, they lose moisture quickly. Even a few inches of bare ground can be too much.

A worker termite reaches the end of a covered gallery and stops against the last intact grains. Beyond them is a breach. Light enters there, along with hotter, drier air.

Other workers arrive behind it. They touch the damaged edge with their antennae, turn, return carrying material. A damp pellet is pressed into the gap. Another is fitted beside it. For a while the repair is porous, admitting thin points of daylight. Then the points close.

On the outside, it looks as though nothing has happened.

Inside, traffic resumes.

The colony occupies more than the tower suggests. Workers descend into chambers below the roots, travel sideways beneath a shrub, rise again inside a low ridge that could be mistaken for wind-packed earth. Some routes end at feeding places. Some reach soil that is cooler and less dry. Others enter rooms where young termites lie together in pale clusters, attended by workers that feed them, clean them, and carry them elsewhere when the conditions change.

There is no single darkness underground. A chamber close to the surface warms early. A deeper one retains humidity. A passage beside a thick root smells different from a passage through compacted mineral soil. The inhabitants respond to distinctions invisible to anything standing above them.

Soldiers wait where those distinctions become dangerous.

Their heads are darker than their bodies, their mouthparts enlarged. At a narrow opening, a few can occupy nearly all the available width. Behind them the workers continue to move. The soldiers do not pursue every disturbance. Much of their work consists of being in the way.

Ants test the margins. A scout follows a crack down the shaded face of a mound, hesitates at a place recently repaired, and turns along the seam. Elsewhere, ants already have a route of their own beneath a dead branch. Their traffic runs close to the termites’ traffic without joining it. Between the two is a thickness of earth that neither colony can afford to treat as permanent.

Deep in an inhabited mound, the queen lies in a chamber she cannot readily leave.

Her swollen abdomen occupies much of her length. Workers pass beside it, take up eggs, bring food, maintain the walls around her. The king remains nearby. Neither has any sense of the mound’s pale silhouette against the sky, or of the aloe whose roots have begun to press against its lower rooms. Changes arrive as altered temperature, interrupted attendance, vibrations through the chamber floor.

The insects repair what they can reach. They carry the next grain and place it.

***

Other inhabitants live at the limits of that labor.

A small lizard has a resting place in the broken upper wall of an old mound. It is the color of several things at once: dust, dry bark, the shadow inside a fissure. At midday it fits itself so closely to the cavity that only an eye is exposed. Toward evening it comes out headfirst and waits on the warm surface.

Its hunting ground is small but crowded. Flies settle on nearby flowers. Ants cross a ledge. A moth rests beneath a thorn leaf. The lizard watches each without moving toward all of them. When it does move, it is sudden enough to look like a slip of the eye.

After swallowing, it returns to stillness. Its throat works. One foot shifts off a hotter patch of wall.

Below it, a mouse emerges through a hole partly screened by fallen aloe blades.

The mouse has large ears, dark eyes, and a coat that keeps the color of the ground even when clean. Its whiskers extend into spaces before its body follows. It uses routes that offer successive places to disappear: a root hollow, the underside of a leaning stem, a crack behind a mound’s apron.

A seed is light enough to carry away whole. The mouse takes it into cover before opening it. The fragments remain there, mixed with older husks and small droppings, in a pocket of earth protected from the wind.

It is not the only animal that knows this pocket.

A narrow snake moves through after dark. Its body passes over the accumulated husks without much sound. At the mouse’s entrance, its tongue appears and withdraws. It waits with the front of its body loosely bent, leaving most of its length concealed beneath a branch.

For a long time, neither animal moves.

Elsewhere, the mouse has another exit.

***

The larger residents carry evidence of how little room there is.

A fox comes through the thicket with its ears above the lower leaves and its flanks brushing dry stems. Dust marks the fur along its sides. It cannot walk straight toward everything it smells. A mound intervenes; a thorn crown reaches too low; an opening that admits its head will not admit its shoulders.

It pauses, backs out, goes around.

Its paths are worn into a sequence of accommodations. Here it lowers its body. Here it turns sideways. Here it steps onto a hard roof rather than forcing through the plants. Its feet have polished a slight hollow in the edge.

The fox hears movement beneath that roof. It tilts its head and listens again. The sound is faint and difficult to locate through the branching chambers. After a while it continues, nose low, toward the sweeter smell of a fallen fruit caught beneath a shrub.

A tortoise uses some of the same passages, much more slowly.

Its shell is scratched at the edges where it pushes under branches. Dust fills the grooves between its scutes. Old plant fibers cling beside one hind leg. It feeds close to the ground, cropping tender growth where a stem has broken or a shaded shoot has escaped the worst of the heat.

When it comes to an obstruction, it may keep pressing until the obstacle yields a little. A dry branch rises on its shell, drags along its back, and drops behind it. The resulting noise is considerable in a place where so much movement is concealed.

By noon it has entered its scrape beneath an aloe. Soil touches the underside of its shell. Its head withdraws only partway; the eyes remain open for a time.

***

Above all this, the birds occupy a less continuous world.

They can cross in seconds what takes the fox several minutes to negotiate. But they too must enter the crowd to feed and nest. A small bird slips between thorn tips with its wings half-folded, lands, and works along a branch where tiny insects shelter beside the buds. Its nest is tucked into a fork that larger animals cannot approach without bending the surrounding thorns.

Sandgrouse keep to wider patches. Their feet leave neat tracks among the more tangled marks of mice and beetles. They pick, lift their heads, and pick again. Sometimes several look up together, although nothing visible has changed.

After one of the rare rains, the basin acquires inhabitants that have been present all along but not in this form.

Winged termites gather behind openings in the mounds. At the right hour, they emerge. Their bodies are darker and tougher than those of the workers; their long wings catch the remaining light. Birds turn toward them. Lizards leave their usual stations. Ants carry away fallen individuals and discarded wings.

Most do not travel far enough, or land in the wrong place, or enter a crack already occupied.

A few reach loose, damp soil beneath the outermost plants. They shed their wings. A pair disappears into a small excavation, and the entrance closes behind them.

There is scarcely anything to see: disturbed powder, perhaps a thorn leaf shifted aside.

Under it are two insects, a little retained moisture, and the first chamber.
