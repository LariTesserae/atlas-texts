At first the inhabitants are visible only by what they hold still.

A strand of drifting matter stops beneath a ledge. Several grains of shell hang against the current, caught on something too fine to see. Then a fan folds, bringing them toward a small dark mouth. The animal behind it is clear except for its joints and the food moving through its body. It has six limbs fastened to the ceiling of the ledge and two that reach outward. Each of those divides repeatedly, until the last branches are finer than the suspended grit.

When it closes its fans, it becomes scarcely larger than a crack in the shell.

There are hundreds beneath the ledge. They do not all face the same way. Some feed on water entering the shelter, some on the slow return along its ceiling. A few have their fans turned toward the wall, taking what rises in the small eddies around their own neighbors.

Their young settle wherever a limb can find purchase. For a time they wear no shell at all. Later, a white collar forms around each gripping foot. The collars broaden and meet. What appears to be a thin crust on the underside of an old chamber is a crowded attachment of living animals, each able to withdraw into a separate hole.

When the crust becomes too crowded, the new arrivals settle on the old.

Other small things move among them.

One has a narrow, jointed body and more legs than it needs for walking. On a flat surface it uses only the lower pairs. Inside a crack it turns several outward and braces itself between the walls. Its back is covered with overlapping translucent shields, through which a pale thread of gut can be seen. It feeds by lifting particles with two soft brushes held beneath its head.

It is sensitive to the feeding animals’ movements. When the fans close, it stops brushing. When they withdraw entirely, it presses flat and grips.

Often nothing follows. The fans open again, one after another, and the little animal resumes its slow passage. Occasionally the water strikes hard enough to peel the entire colony from the ledge. Then the jointed animal remains against the bare patch, its legs trembling under the load.

The processions in the grooves are a larger kind of grazer. Each carries a shallow shell with a heavy front edge and a thin rear lip. Their feelers are long enough to reach around the animal ahead. Where a groove forks, the leader may pause for several minutes, touching both openings while the others gather behind it.

They eat the soft growth that never becomes tall enough to resemble vegetation: films of minute life, settled fragments, the bodies of creatures too small to distinguish from the sediment. Their mouths scrape a narrow path through it. Behind a procession, the floor is briefly brighter.

The leader is simply the animal currently at the front. If a current lifts it away, the next one continues. If the groove ends, they spread over the terrace, lose contact, and travel separately until the slope gathers them into another hollow.

Some shells bear old chips that have been grown over. Some are pierced by neat round holes.

The makers of those holes live under the shell dust.

They are broad, soft animals with a fringe that lies flat against the floor. Their upper skin holds grains of chalk in tiny pockets, so that even close by they resemble a patch of slightly coarser sediment. Beneath the body are short muscular feet and a mouth enclosed by two hard curved plates.

They can remain in one place for days. A passing grazer may climb over one without noticing. The flattened animal waits until the grazer’s foot is above its mouth, then bends its whole body upward around the shell. For a while there is only a pale lump on the terrace.

The bore is made slowly. The animal holds its prey close and works through the shell with a small rasp and a clear secretion. When it leaves, the empty shell remains intact except for the hole. Soon the feeding fans grow inside it.

In open water, the upright swimmers keep apart from the floor. They have long bodies, narrow heads, and a translucent blade running down either side. The blades ripple almost continuously, though the movement is difficult to see. A row of dark spots beneath the skin registers changes in light. Short hairs along the jaw bend with changes in pressure.

Their posture varies with the water. In a gentle descent they hang head-up, intercepting whatever settles past them. In a rising current they turn nearly horizontal. A sudden reversal may scatter them among the pillars, but within a little while those still in open water have found the same angle again.

Inside each swimmer are two pale weights, enclosed in soft tissue. The weights shift when the animal turns. Even a dead one usually sinks headfirst.

They feed on transparent prey, opening their mouths much wider than their narrow heads would suggest. Sometimes a swallowed animal remains visible for hours, slowly losing its outline within the swimmer’s body.

The large inhabitants are harder to find.

A low dome may have three openings on its sheltered side. Two are dark. In the third, a sheet of tissue lies just behind the shell edge. Fine folds run across it, and along those folds minute particles travel inward.

Farther inside is a thick body attached to the floor. It has no obvious head. Several soft arms extend through separate passages, each ending in a fringed mouth. Most of the animal is occupied with moving water through itself: drawing it into one chamber, passing it over folded surfaces, expelling it through another.

The dome is its shell, but the animal does not occupy the whole dome. Old portions have been sealed behind thin partitions. Some contain silt. Some contain water that moves very little. Small scavengers live there, entering through fractures too narrow for the owner’s arms.

At the growing edge, the shell is almost translucent. A broad fold of flesh presses against it from within. The fold lays down a little material, withdraws, then presses against the next section. The new rim extends unevenly, farther where the current is weak, thickening where it must bear weight.

Two neighboring domes may grow together without their occupants ever meeting. A passage between them narrows over many seasons. Animals that once fed in its steady flow move closer to the entrance. Eventually the gap closes, and the water takes another route.

Some of the domes have been empty for a long time. Others contain a smaller occupant living in one corner of a dead animal’s shell.

The youngest builders are difficult to associate with these immense bodies. They swim for part of their lives, no larger than the grazers’ eyes. Each is enclosed in two clear valves and propelled by a ring of beating hairs. Within the valves, its folded feeding surface is already present.

They settle in hollows where fine particles accumulate. Most are eaten, buried, or swept away. A survivor fastens itself down, loses its swimming hairs, and begins to extend a rim. For years it may look like no more than a white blister on an older wall.

The animals that resemble scattered plates are different. Their shells never fuse into one piece. Each plate rests on a separate patch of tough skin, with flexible tissue between. The body beneath is broad and low, and its many feet fit into irregularities in the floor.

When resting, it spreads until shell grit collects between its plates. When disturbed, it contracts. The plates overlap, the grit falls away, and the whole animal becomes a compact armored shape moving surprisingly quickly down the terrace.

Along its underside are rows of soft fingers. These explore the floor ahead of the feet, entering cracks and lifting broken pieces. Small prey are passed inward. Useful fragments are sometimes held against the back while fresh shell grows beneath them. An old individual carries pieces of several structures that no longer stand.

Near the rim live smaller, flatter builders with gripping surfaces almost as wide as their bodies. Their shells are thick on the downstream side and low enough that overflow passes over them without finding much purchase. They feed only when the water eases. Then a narrow slit opens and a dark fringe appears.

During storms the slit closes. Their feet remain spread against the polished shell.

Afterward, some are missing. Others have been shifted a short distance and are already fastening themselves again. The pale outlines of their former positions remain visible until grit settles over them.

When the distant arch falls, the first movement comes from the open-water swimmers. They turn away from the descending pieces. The grazers crowd into grooves; the flat hunters lift their margins and slip under slabs. Beneath the ledges, fans fold into their collars.

Dust passes through the chambers of the great domes. Their outlet openings contract, then widen, pushing brief cloudy streams into the water.

As the fall settles, a broken piece comes to rest with its fresh inner surface exposed. It is ridged and pearly, very different from the worn floor around it. A transparent, many-legged animal reaches it first and brushes along the ridges. Later, several grazers find the edge.

In the new current through the opening, tiny shelled swimmers turn end over end. Most pass through. One touches the sheltered underside of the fallen piece and holds there. Its valves open slightly. The beating hairs slow, then stop, while a small pale foot tests the surface beneath it.
