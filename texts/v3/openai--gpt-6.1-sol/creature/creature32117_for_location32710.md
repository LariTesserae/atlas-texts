The first sign of an inhabitant is usually a wedge in the wrong place.

You leave a door propped open and return to find the wedge farther under it. The door still stands at the same angle. What has changed is the ground beneath the threshold, and someone has made the small correction before the movement could split the hinge strap.

Nothing else has been touched. Your cup remains on the bench. The ear covers are where you put them.

Later, beyond the third hanging, you hear a short scrape. A hand is drawing another wedge from beneath the bench. It has broad fingertips, pale nails, and a dark crease across the palm in which white splinters have collected. The hand pauses when you look at it.

The person attached to it is crouched where there did not seem to be room for anyone.

They are narrow through the shoulders and dressed in overlapping strips of leaf fiber. Their face is long, with a small mouth and eyes set well back beneath the brow. They look neither surprised nor welcoming. Their attention moves from you to the doorway, where the lower edge has begun to bind.

They turn the wedge over, reject it, and reach for a thinner one.

For some time, this is all you learn about them.

***

There are more people in the valley than the empty shelters suggest.

Not many more. A grove may have only one household near it, and several shelters along a traverse may belong to the same person. Some dwellings are used only when a particular slope is dark. Others contain tools for a repair that has not yet become necessary.

The inhabitants move slowly on good ground and almost without pause on bad ground. They do not test every step. They watch the intervals between the markers, then place their feet with an accuracy that seems excessive until you try to follow.

Their bodies are not entirely constant.

In the old clearing, a person has ordinary proportions. On fresh stone, the distance from hip to knee may become difficult to judge. A forearm reaching across an unfinished seam seems briefly too long, though the hand arrives exactly where it was intended to go. This does not happen to their clothing. A sleeve can pull tight around an arm that, for a moment, occupies more distance than the sleeve contains.

They dislike being watched during a crossing. Not because the change is shameful, but because attention distracts them. A stumble may leave someone with a shoulder that no longer agrees with the rest of the body. Such injuries are treated by sitting against finished stone while another person presses firmly at the joint.

Recovery can take a long time. The pressure has to be maintained, so visitors take turns. Beside the injured person there will be several small objects to handle: a smooth wedge, a length of cord with knots, a piece of leaf folded into a shape that opens and closes. These keep the sound from becoming the only thing present.

Their ears are small and folded close to the head. They can hear, but rarely depend on hearing. A stone hammer striking a bench is useful because it can be felt through a resting wrist.

Most conversation takes place by touch.

Two fingertips against the inside of the forearm ask a question. A drag toward the elbow gives the question a direction. Pressure supplies distinctions that you cannot at first detect. They can discuss a broken roof strap this way, or a journey, or someone whose conduct has become tiresome.

For a conversation involving several people, they sit around a slab with one hand resting on it. Movements of the fingers travel through the finished stone. A good slab is kept level and clear. Setting a tray on it while people are speaking is rude.

They have voices as well. In the smallest pockets of a shelter, behind enough hangings, you sometimes hear them use them. Their speech is low and surprisingly quick. They laugh with their mouths closed, producing a little disturbance in the throat.

Children find this particularly funny.

***

Their children are not often visible on the paths.

A young child is carried in a stiff cradle woven from pleated leaves, with a piece of old stone secured beneath the back. Without it, the child has difficulty remaining the same size. A foot may become too small to bear weight; a cheek may deepen under the pressure of a kiss.

Adults touch children lightly and often. They check the placing of the ribs, the firmness behind the knees, the way the fingers close around an offered stalk. These examinations are affectionate, though an outsider may mistake them for constant apprehension.

When a child can stand reliably on old stone, they are allowed to help with the path markers.

The first task is to clean white splinters from the bases. Later they learn to notice when a marker appears to have moved farther from its neighbor without either shard changing its position. They fetch an adult, who walks the interval several times before adding a new marker.

Children are forbidden to make thumbprints in soft rock near a dwelling.

They do it anyway.

There is a shelter with a row of narrow niches along its rear wall, each just large enough to hold a cup. The resident claims these were planned. An elderly neighbor, who remembers the shelter before it had a roof, says otherwise.

***

Their food is kept in trays under the benches.

It consists of thin pieces taken from the interior ribs of plants, folded together with shavings from particular white seams. Not every seam is suitable. They test a shaving between their back teeth and spit out most of them. The rejected pieces go into separate baskets; some can be used for straps, and some must be carried outside before they begin to branch.

A prepared portion has almost no weight. They hold it in the mouth for a long while. During this time they sit very still, hands laid flat against the thighs.

After eating, their skin is warmer.

They do not eat fallen leaves. These have already spent whatever the living plant could give. Nor do they cut freely from the luxuriant groves. An opening in a canopy changes the work of the roots, and a careless harvest can make a familiar approach unreliable.

There are individuals who know particular plants over great stretches of time. They remember which stems tolerate cutting and which cause trouble elsewhere in the grove. One woman tends a low, violet growth beside her shelter. It has never become large. She clips its inner fans, adjusts the stones around its roots, and complains that it is taking too much warmth from the wall.

She could gather better food a short distance away. She continues tending it.

No one regards this as strange.

***

Their work occupies much of their time, but they are not solemn people.

A well-fitted wedge is admired. So is a foolishly elaborate knot that serves no purpose beyond being difficult to make. Some hangings have pale undersides arranged into patterns visible only from one seat in the room. There are people who take immense trouble over these and people who consider them a misuse of sound-breaking material.

They keep grudges. A borrowed lever may be returned much later than expected, with its end shortened. The borrower insists the change was necessary. The owner continues to mention it whenever the two meet, pressing the complaint into whatever slab happens to be between them.

They also make journeys for company.

A visitor may arrive carrying nothing but a small piece of food and remain through the slow displacement of several branches outside the door. When conversation is finished, the two sit separately and repair things. There is no requirement to continue speaking merely because someone has come a long way.

Those who live alone prepare an extra pocket behind their hangings anyway.

***

Age shows first in their hands.

The palms become less responsive. Fingers that could once distinguish tiny pressures need a firmer touch. Old people rest both hands on the conversation slab and ask speakers to repeat themselves. Some pretend not to have missed anything. Their answers become abrupt and occasionally inappropriate.

Later, they find unfinished ground exhausting. They spend more time in the old clearing, where their bodies need make no adjustment. Younger relatives bring food and news. Wedges are brought for them to sort; their eye for thickness remains good long after they cease to trust their feet.

The shallow tool marks in the clearing have accumulated around these visits.

A person waiting beside someone very old will work a little at the stone: smoothing a place to sit, cutting a groove to hold a tray, flattening an edge that catches clothing. Much of the work is unnecessary. It gives the hands something to do.

When someone dies, they are wrapped in leaf hangings and placed in a recess cut into finished rock. The cutting is difficult and quiet enough to hear. People come to help who would ordinarily have little reason to meet.

Nothing is planted there.

The recesses face in different directions. Some look toward groves, some toward paths, and one toward the blank underside of a ledge. The inhabitants remember who wanted each view.

***

They know the gap above the valley is closing.

Their measurements are modest: a notch on a shard, a cord stretched between two established points, a sightline past the corner of a roof. No one tries to measure the whole mountain. It is enough to know that an opening has narrowed, or that a path now requires fewer markers.

Some have begun carrying stores toward the older end. Others maintain shelters beneath the descending stone. There are disagreements about how much time remains, and about whether a grove can be kept alive after its ground has hardened.

A few small plants have been moved into baskets. Most stop growing. One produces a new pleat so slowly that the household checks it every time someone returns.

The shelters nearest the gap are still repaired.

In one of them, a resident is making a second room. The roof slabs have been brought over many journeys and lie stacked beside the entrance. There is no immediate need for the room. It is intended for someone who has agreed to come from the far rim, when the crossing is easier.

Inside, behind the hangings, two cups stand on a level shelf.

Outside, the unfinished heights bear down. The doorway shifts slightly. The resident kneels, draws out the old wedge, and selects another from the tray.
