At first you take it for a piece of debris caught at the edge of the discharge: a short length of wood, dark at one end, turning slightly in the current. Then it raises its head. There is a bill where the splinter seemed to be, and beneath the narrow body two legs so fine that the water hides them.

It is a small wading bird, brown above and pale underneath. In flight it would show a clean stripe along each wing. Standing here, it has almost no distinguishing color. The wet feathers on its back resemble the older mud; its breast takes on the gray light of the pools. You find it by movement, lose it when it stops, and find it again several feet farther along.

It lives at an edge too temporary to appear on a map.

This afternoon the edge is a curve of sand below the active pipe. Yesterday it was beside a survey stake farther out. By tomorrow a fresh deposit may have covered both places. The bird follows the shallowest water, walking where the surface has just ceased to shine continuously and begun to break into little separate mirrors.

Its feet spread when they touch down. The toes leave delicate forks that fill with water almost at once. Where a person would sink to the shin, the bird makes scarcely an impression. Even so, it tests the ground. Sometimes a foot goes farther down than expected; it withdraws it, takes a short sideways step, and continues.

The bill enters the mud and comes out apparently empty. It does this again, and again, rapidly enough that the movements seem nervous until you watch for longer. Every few probes there is a pause, a small manipulation at the tip of the bill, a swallow. What it finds is usually too small to see from where you stand.

Some of its food has arrived violently. Worms, little crustaceans, and fragments of shell have come through the dredging system with the channel sediment. Others are already living in the older deposits. The bird works the boundary between the two: fresh material beside mud that has rested long enough to acquire holes.

A shrimp flicks away through water no deeper than the bird’s toes. The bird runs after it, stops abruptly, and lowers its head. For a moment its whole body is inclined toward the bill. Then it straightens and resumes the slower work.

The pump does not trouble it. Neither does the loose panel beating against the service shed. A truck climbing the embankment makes it look up, but it watches the truck rather than the noise. When the vehicle passes above it, the bird crouches, opens its wings, and flies low across the pool. It lands before the truck has gone much farther, shakes one leg, then the other, and starts feeding again.

It is more wary of a person who stops walking.

A worker following the service road can pass fairly close. Someone who turns toward the bird and stands still becomes an object requiring attention. The bird raises its head until its neck looks unexpectedly long. It keeps one eye toward the observer. After several seconds it moves away, not directly, but by feeding along a slanting route that increases the distance.

There are others like it throughout the basin. They arrive separately and gather loosely, each keeping a little space of wet ground around itself. Their disagreements are brief: a rush with the bill held forward, a wing lifted, a few hurried steps by the bird being displaced. There is enough shoreline here for all of them, but the richest few inches beside a draining pool can still be contested.

When one catches something substantial, its neighbors notice.

A worm comes out slowly, stretched between the bill and the sediment. The bird leans backward. The worm releases, and the bird runs several paces before swallowing it, followed by another bird that gives up almost immediately. Both return to probing. The rain has already softened their tracks.

By evening, their feeding places begin to contract. A depression fills; a long strip beside the sand fan disappears. The birds move upslope by increments so small that you do not see a retreat, only discover that they are standing somewhere else.

The nearest one reaches the newly exposed ridge where the gulls have gathered. It does not join them. It works along the foot of the ridge, passing beneath their larger bodies, occasionally pausing when a gull turns its head. Then it finds a shallow pocket on the sheltered side.

Here the current has weakened. Fine sediment settles around its ankles. It feeds for several minutes in almost the same spot.

Later, when the lamps come on, it leaves the water. It climbs onto a little shelf of firmer sand, folds one leg beneath its body, and settles its feathers. The change in its outline is considerable. The thin, purposeful animal becomes a compact oval with a bill projecting from one end. Rain gathers on the feathers and runs off.

It does not close both eyes for long.

Another wader lands nearby. Then two more. They stand apart at first, but as the light goes they draw closer, facing in slightly different directions. A bird on the outside lifts its head whenever something moves along the bank. One in the middle tucks its bill among the feathers of its shoulder.

They will not build here. There is too little cover, and the ground under them is still being delivered. Their stay may last days or weeks; some will leave sooner, and others will arrive after them. For now they know the basin in considerable detail: which margin drains first, which pipe is running, where the water remains shallow enough to feed, which scrap of raised ground is still above it at dusk.

During the night the discharge reaches the lower end of their resting shelf. The first bird wakes when water touches its foot. It puts down the other foot, steps higher, and pauses. Its neighbors stir and follow.

There is no general alarm. Five small birds shift along a ridge in the dark while the pumps continue running. Behind them, the places where they stood fill slowly with silt.
