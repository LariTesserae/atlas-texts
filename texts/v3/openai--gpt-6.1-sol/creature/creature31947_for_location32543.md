The inhabitant is easiest to see when it reaches for the cup.

Until then, it has been sitting in the niche behind the stool, where the wood does not quite meet the wall. What looked like a folded piece of cloth was its back. What looked like roots hanging beside the stool was the hair along its neck. It has been awake throughout your visit, with its hands tucked under its arms and its eyes open.

It waits until you have stopped looking directly into the niche. Then one hand comes out.

The hand is small enough to enter the cup without touching the sides. There are four fingers and a thumb, all much the same length, with broad, smooth pads. It lifts the dry leaf by its stalk, turns it once, and sets it on the stool. The leaf is a lid. Beneath it, at the bottom of the cup, is a little water.

The inhabitant leans forward to drink.

It is about the size of a sitting cat, although that becomes difficult to judge when it unfolds. Its limbs have more length than they first appeared to have. Its knees rise beside its shoulders; its feet can be placed almost beneath its hips. It comes out of the niche without shifting the stool or disturbing the fern that grows across its opening.

Standing, it still keeps its head slightly lowered, as though there were a shelf above it.

Its skin is pale brown, darker at the knuckles and along the edges of its ears. Fine creases cover it, but the creases do not suggest frailty. They look like the folds in something that has often been put away carefully. There is hair on its head, on its forearms, and in two narrow patches beside its mouth. The hair is nearly colorless. In the courtyard’s brightness, individual strands disappear unless a leaf lies behind them.

Its face is broad at the eyes and narrow at the chin. You may have seen that face between the stones by the entrance. You may instead have seen one of the small animals that share the passages inside the walls. The resemblance is close enough that it does not trouble the inhabitant.

It wears a garment made from pieces of cloth too old to retain their original colors. The pieces have been joined with very small stitches. Some are soft and thin; others still show the diagonal weave of somebody’s discarded coat. The elbows have been patched from within, so there is nothing to catch against a twig.

Around its waist is a braided cord carrying a wooden pin, a shallow spoon, and a sliver of slate with one edge sharpened. A pocket over its chest holds a little wool. Another holds several empty snail shells.

It takes an empty shell out while you watch. It examines the opening, rubs something from the rim with its thumb, and puts it back.

The living snails receive no such inspection. They know the pressure of its feet through the bridges and withdraw only when necessary. One remains extended while the inhabitant steps across, its feelers passing close to an ankle.

There are routes through the courtyard that fit this body exactly. A foot goes into a gap between two pots. A hand closes around the peg concealed beneath a shelf. A shoulder turns through a fork in the evergreen branches. It moves without making the plants yield very much.

At the pipe, it pauses.

The dark bead trembles against the clay. The inhabitant puts the shallow spoon beneath it, then touches the mouth of the pipe with the wooden pin. Water travels down the pin into the spoon. Enough gathers to cover the wood with a sheen.

This is carried to the pale flower.

It does not pour the water onto the roots. It slips the spoon between two stones and waits while the water draws away along an old fiber laid in the crevice. Then it checks the fiber with a fingertip.

Several such journeys occupy what feels like a long time. You begin to notice that it seldom carries anything empty. On the way back from the flower, it brings a loose grain of mortar. Near the steps, it exchanges that for a fragment of bark. The mortar goes into a depression beside a drain; the bark goes under one end of a pot that has begun to lean.

Its work is full of these small corrections. Most do not improve the appearance of anything. The leaning pot remains crooked. A dead fern stem is left where it supports the edge of a moth’s resting place. The grain of mortar holds a channel open barely wider than itself.

The shears are heavy for it. To use them, it braces one handle against its thigh and draws the other inward with both hands. You might expect such effort to be reserved for an important branch. Instead, it clips a single stiff fiber projecting from the plaited cord of the hanging pot.

The fiber would have caught the white rootlets beneath it.

Afterward it oils the hinge with something from an empty snail shell and returns the shears to the stool.

It does eat. Under the slab seat are small stores wrapped in leaves: seeds, dried berries, the pale interior of a nut. There are also things you cannot identify, thin and translucent, gathered into a bundle with surprising care. It eats sitting down, one piece at a time, and catches the crumbs in its palm.

The bird sometimes comes to the edge of the slab. It is not tame enough to be touched. The inhabitant gives it a crumb by leaving the crumb behind.

Its sleeping place is higher than its seat, inside the blocked doorway. A shallow cavity has been lined with shredded bark and a little of the wool from its pocket. There is room there for its curled body and a folded square of cloth. From the cavity it can see the pipe, the lowest stair, and most of the white wall.

You cannot tell how old it is. Its possessions have the same worn surfaces as the courtyard, and some bear repairs too small to see until it turns them in its hands. It knows which edge of the slate spoon will fit between two particular stones. It knows where to press the paving to release a trapped drop.

Yet it still examines things. A newly opened fern holds its attention. It bends close to a patch of lichen, waits, and shifts its head to see it from another angle.

When it finally looks at you, there is no surprise in its face. Its gaze travels from your boots to the loose stone above the entrance. Then it comes nearer and touches the wet edge of your sleeve.

A drop has gathered there, brought down from the city.

It holds the spoon beneath your cuff. With its other hand, it steadies the cloth until the water falls. Then it carries the spoon away, already looking for where to put it.
