The first one you notice is keeping a crack open.

You have passed several such cracks already: shallow splits in the outer crust, their edges raised just enough to cast a narrow shadow. This one seems no different until you see that the shadows are arranged in pairs.

There are eight of them on either side.

Between each pair lies a joint.

The animal has settled lengthwise into the opening, with its back flush to the surrounding surface and its limbs braced beneath the lips. Its upper plates are pale, scuffed, and edged with the same dark resin that gathers on old wounds. Loose scales have caught in the grooves between them. Something small and bright is feeding near its head.

It is not trapped. It is holding the edges apart.

Every few moments the crack tightens. The limbs yield a little, then extend. A clear film stretches across the opening and breaks against the animal’s flanks. Below it, warm air escapes in a thin stream.

You could mistake this for a contest between the animal and the ground. It goes on for hours, neither gaining much. But farther along the crack, the exposed tissue has begun to grow into smooth, rounded channels. The animal moves only when one of these channels is complete.

It advances less than the length of your hand.

The new passage stays open behind it.

***

In the upper dead layers, these animals are about the size of grazing beasts. Lower down, they become much larger.

That is partly because the young live near the surface and partly because an old one wears its surroundings.

Its body has a flexible central trunk enclosed in overlapping shields. Beneath those shields, dense bands of muscle pull against a framework of tough internal struts. The limbs are short, powerful, and broad at their ends, suited to pressing against tunnel walls. There are more limbs than you first count. Some remain folded into grooves along the belly, emerging only when the animal reaches a wide space or needs to hold several surfaces at once.

Its own armor grows slowly. Around it, the plateau lays down further layers.

A sleeping animal irritates the living wall just enough to make it thicken. It turns in its resting hollow, scraping the new growth smooth, gathering a sleeve of dead fiber around its body. Over years this sleeve becomes a traveling shell. Over decades it acquires scars, embedded scales, old conducting strands, and patches of tissue that remain alive.

A very old burrower may carry enough living growth to have a temperature different from the tunnel around it.

Mites inhabit its seams. Small grazers follow it when it comes near the surface. Sometimes a delicate fringe grows along the rear of its shell, sheltered from abrasion by the direction in which it moves.

There are places where you cannot tell whether a buried ridge contains an animal until it turns.

***

Its head is not recognizable as a head until it begins to feed.

The front shield lifts. Under it is a broad rasp, divided into many little working surfaces. Some scrape. Some pinch. Others press the fragments against a ridged palate, testing them before they pass inward.

The animal eats dead fiber, old sealing compounds, storm-burned tissue, and the dense residue left in passages where the plant no longer circulates air. It can spend half a day on a layer thinner than a fingernail, removing the useless material without breaking the living cords beneath.

It does not always succeed.

Fresh wounds are common in its tunnels. The clear, stiff secretion that beads from them is nutritious, and young animals sometimes keep scraping after the dead layer is gone. The plateau responds by hardening the wall. A feeding place that was warm and yielding becomes cold, close-grained, almost impossible to bite.

The animal may try elsewhere. It may wait.

In an especially lean season, it may dig through.

There are sealed hollows containing whole families that failed to leave before the tissue contracted around them.

The relationship has lasted a very long time. That has not made it gentle.

***

Water is held throughout the animal’s body, but almost none of it is free.

Its tissues are dense with substances that keep moisture bound even when a wound opens into the dry air. The lining of its gut folds around each mouthful until the available nourishment has been drawn out. Waste emerges as compact, nearly weightless pellets.

These pellets matter to the small creatures of the tunnels. They contain compounds the plant has gathered from dust over centuries, concentrated by a passage through the animal. Pale crawlers gather wherever a burrower has rested. They loosen the pellets, eat part of them, and drag the rest into minute fissures.

Around a long-used sleeping chamber, the living walls become unusually rich in color.

You might think this explains why the plateau makes cushions for the animals. It may explain part of it. The cushions also keep a sleeper from compressing the smaller conduits. They hold the animal away from the warmest tissue, and they allow it to shift without tearing the chamber apart.

Some are abandoned while still perfectly shaped.

Others are occupied by animals whose bodies have changed so much that they no longer fit.

***

The animal’s eyes are small and deeply covered. At the surface they distinguish the slant of light, the flash of a crown, the movement of a nearby body. Underground they detect the faint illumination carried down through translucent fibers.

Most of what it knows arrives through its feet and jaw.

Each limb ends in a hard rim surrounding a softer contact pad. Pressed to the substrate, the rim takes up fine vibration; the pad feels strain, warmth, and the slow changes of pressure within the tissue. Along the underside of the jaw are combs of stiff sensory filaments. The animal lays these against a wall before entering a new passage.

It does not attend to the whole enormous sound of the country.

The unchanging part disappears from its awareness. A tower’s regular pulse becomes something like the feel of a wall: present, useful, seldom noticed. What matters is a missed beat, a pulse arriving late, a faint vibration becoming stronger where it should weaken.

An animal may stop feeding because a tower fifty miles away has begun to drag against its own tissues.

Hours later, the air in its tunnel changes.

By then it has moved.

***

Their signals are easy to confuse with the plateau’s machinery.

A burrower braces its rear limbs and gives three short presses with its forelimbs. Another, somewhere beyond a bend, answers with a longer pressure followed by a scrape.

The messages are not carried chiefly through the air. They pass through the wall. Near a broad conducting band, a slight action can travel surprisingly far. In loose dead layers, even violent stamping may reach no farther than the next chamber.

They alter their signals accordingly.

They also leave marks. At junctions, an animal rubs the underside of its head against the wall, depositing a thin waxy smear. The smear holds its scent and changes the texture of the surface. Another animal tests it with its jaw filaments and may follow, turn aside, or wait.

Frequently used junctions become crowded with overlapping deposits.

The plateau eventually grows over them. A route used by several generations can become unreadable in a season of vigorous repair. When that happens, animals gather there and touch the new wall for a long time.

Some depart in the old direction and force a passage through.

Others accept the closure.

There is considerable variation between individuals. One may repeatedly reopen a route that the plant seals. Another may abandon a chamber after the smallest change in its temperature. A third will tolerate strangers in its resting hollow but attack them at a particular feeding seam.

Travelers learn these habits. They announce themselves before entering certain tunnels.

***

The young begin inside an armored capsule carried beneath a parent’s rear shields.

There are few at a time. For weeks after emergence they remain pressed against the adult, feeding on a soft secretion produced in narrow grooves along its belly. Their plates are already hard enough to resist the dry air, but their limbs cannot yet oppose the contraction of living tissue.

The parent takes them through old passages.

A growing tunnel is dangerous. A wall can close across a small body without rupturing any of its own fibers. A pressure pulse that an adult scarcely feels may throw a juvenile against a seam. Even a patch of nutritious exposed tissue can hold a young animal fast when its secretion hardens around the mouthparts.

The first journeys are short.

The parent advances, stops, and sends a pattern through the floor. The young follow the vibration. At a junction, they crowd beneath its body while it tests the walls.

Later, the adult sometimes goes around a bend without signaling.

The young remain where they are, touching the ground.

At last one moves. It finds the warmer branch, pauses, and presses a hesitant pattern into the wall. The others come after it.

Eventually the parent stops answering.

***

The small pale animals you see in surface cracks are not all juveniles. Some never descend far enough to become large.

They live between the outer scales, where temperature changes quickly and storms renew the supply of broken tissue. Their bodies remain narrow. Their armor stays light. They can cross exposed ground at a surprising pace, fitting their limbs into the corrugations and pulling themselves forward in brief, sliding motions.

The deep animals move less often.

An old one may spend several seasons enlarging a single chamber. It feeds along one wall, sleeps against another, and keeps a passage open with the slow labor of its body. Air moving through that passage supplies the plateau beyond it. The plateau, in turn, maintains the chamber’s warmth.

Other animals make use of the place. Surface grazers shelter in its upper entrances during storms. Scavengers wait where the burrower pushes out indigestible fragments. Thin, almost transparent creatures live near the ventilation slits and catch particles in spreading fans.

When the resident moves away, some of these followers leave with it.

Some stay in the chamber until it closes.

***

Before one of the great chambers opens, the deep burrowers often ascend.

Not all of them, and not by any route visible from above. But for several days there are more traveling ridges beneath the outer crust. Old seams lift. Warm air escapes from places that have been cold for years.

The animals gather near thick scars.

There they lie with their limbs spread, each foot testing a different part of the ground. When the immense opening begins, they brace. The pressure draws air from their tunnels; dust and shed scales stream past their heads. Smaller creatures shelter in the lee of their shells.

A burrower may remain motionless throughout the opening and closure.

Afterward it goes down again, stopping at every junction.

Some of the passages it knew are gone. Others have appeared. A familiar warm wall has become a pressure outlet. A sleeping chamber now lies on the wrong side of a taut membrane.

It explores slowly. It feeds where it can.

If you follow one, you may notice that it returns repeatedly to a sealed place, pressing its jaw against the surface. Perhaps another animal is beyond it. Perhaps the tissue still carries the vibrations of a route it used for a hundred years.

There is no way to ask.

***

Their deaths are usually hidden.

An animal that has carried a living shell for decades becomes difficult for the plateau to distinguish from its surroundings. Its resting place thickens. Fibers join the growth on its back. It can still rise, but doing so tears small connections that must be fed and repaired.

At some point it rises for the last time.

It may continue to move its limbs long after it ceases traveling. The mouthparts work against the nearby wall. Visitors find its wax mark fresh at the entrance. Young animals shelter alongside it.

Then the marks stop changing.

The plateau grows inward. The hollow fills by fractions, leaving the richest parts until last. Above, nourishment reaches the surface in unusual abundance, and the pale crust breaks into green.

The distant flying creatures arrive first. Later come grazers, mites, and animals that spend their whole lives within the shelter of one delicate frond.

Below them, a passage remains open for a while.

A younger burrower uses it. At the place where the dead animal rested, it lowers its head and tests the floor. Then it settles into the broad depression, which is still warm and still shaped to hold a body, though the body that made it was much larger.

For several seasons it sleeps there.

It leaves a small wax mark beside the entrance.
