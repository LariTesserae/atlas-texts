The people who remain sleep above the brick line.

Their beds stand on platforms fastened to the upper walls, with ladders that can be pulled up after them. Beneath each bed is an empty space, sometimes tall enough to stand in. A visitor might mistake this for a precaution against flooding, but water rarely rises so high. It is the heat they are avoiding. They sleep where the air moves, near an open vent or a missing roof panel, wrapped in thin blankets that are stiff with mineral dust.

Most households occupy only one room of a building. The rest is kept accessible.

A woman lives with her two sons in the upper office of a disused tile works. They eat at a desk whose drawers have swollen shut. Below them are four production floors, three settling tanks, and a passage wide enough for carts. Every sixth morning she walks through these spaces, opening doors, touching the walls at marked points, looking beneath the tanks. She owns none of the machinery. No one has paid her to inspect it for years.

Nevertheless, when one of the boys leaves a bucket in the passage, she makes him go down and move it.

There are perhaps a few hundred people in the city. There may be fewer. They are difficult to count because the fountains determine where they can be found, and because some use several houses without considering any of them a second home. A bed near the windward wall is for the hot weeks. A room above the baths is for the weeks when the high conduits knock. Somewhere else there is a cupboard of bowls, a stove, a coat that has never been brought downstairs.

They do not often gather.

The six chairs along the parapet belong to six people who come at different hours. One chair has a wooden seat, another a woven one, and a third is made entirely of iron but is always covered before anyone sits. The people who use them know one another’s arrangements well enough to leave a message under the appropriate chair. They may go months without meeting.

Some are employed by institutions that no longer have offices. Their wages arrive irregularly through a countinghouse on the lower road, in envelopes bearing names for districts that are now empty. Others receive grain, lamp oil, and replacement ceramic by weight. The deliveries are sufficient to keep people here, but not sufficient to explain why they stay.

Several have never been down to the plain.

They know its heat by the smell of the ascending roads. On certain mornings a dry, powdery current rises through the stairwells, unlike any air produced in the city. Doors are closed against it. Children are told not to lean over the last retaining wall, though the drop itself is not especially dangerous.

The children have narrow playgrounds in the middle of enormous squares. They mark them with chalk, avoiding pavement that has changed color overnight. Their games involve gates, crossings, and permissions. One child stands beside a gutter while the others approach with stones in their hands. Sometimes a stone is accepted. Sometimes it must be carried all the way around a building and offered again.

Adults interrupt only if the chalk crosses an inspection mark.

Most inhabitants wear thick-soled shoes and clothes without hanging hems. Their cuffs are sewn tight, their pockets high. A common jacket has a strip of glazed cloth across the shoulders, not for rain but for the water that falls from ceilings. The strip can be unbuttoned and replaced. In doorways, people turn slightly sideways to keep it from brushing the frame.

They carry small things that are hard to identify at first: a white tile, a folded length of wire, a wooden wedge darkened at one end. The tile is placed against a wall to test whether its warmth is changing. The wire is lowered into channels. The wedge holds a door open without resting on the floor.

A person may have an elaborate set of tools and no knife.

Their hands are often reddened, though less damaged than a stranger’s would be. They have learned which water can be touched briefly and which cannot be touched at all. They distinguish them without tasting, and sometimes without looking. A man carrying a ladder pauses at a crossing, listens to the gutter, and takes another street.

Asked why, he says the water has become heavy.

This is an ordinary expression here. It does not mean the stream is flowing more slowly.

The workers at the lower pumping stations are broad across the shoulders, with pale lines around their wrists where gloves end. Higher up, those who tend the roof reservoirs are leaner and tend to walk with one hand free. People who inspect the pressure rooms have a habit of swallowing before they answer a question. A few are missing the small outer edge of an ear.

Age is less readily apparent. The constant warmth keeps faces flushed, while the mineral air roughens the skin. A woman who looks old may still run lightly up an inspection ladder. A young man may sit for a long time with his fingers spread on his knees, waiting for feeling to return after a shift.

They do not speak loudly unless a machine is operating nearby.

In an empty square, two people standing far apart converse in voices intended for a small room. They wait through the echo before answering. Names are used sparingly, especially near open shafts, where a call can travel to the wrong terrace and bring someone a long way from their work.

A few residents are known only by the places where they are usually encountered.

The man beneath the west spillway has been given several names by people passing through. None has lasted. He repairs valve leather on a table made from an old door. His room contains a bed, a stove, and shelves of rolled leather separated by thickness. He also keeps a small framed drawing of a tree. The drawing has faded to the point that its branches are almost indistinguishable from scratches on the glass.

He knows what a tree is. He has seen them.

He does not speak of them with longing.

There are attachments here, but they settle around particular things. A sound made by a gate when it closes correctly. A stretch of wall that stays cool in the afternoon. The arrival of someone’s footsteps on a roof above the kitchen.

People marry, separate, quarrel over supplies, and nurse one another through fevers. They give gifts suited to the recipient’s rounds: a new lamp cage, a pair of soles cut from unusually light material, a cup whose handle remains cool. They keep useless objects too. A damaged musical instrument. Colored glass. A button from a uniform no longer worn anywhere.

These are stored high, away from the wet.

Below, other inhabitants occupy the parts people leave open.

The translucent creatures in the overflow tunnels are most numerous behind the ceramic linings, where there is a narrow continuous space between the finished wall and the rock. When a panel is removed, dozens may be seen clinging to its back. They remain still for several seconds, limbs extended, then begin to fold themselves into the seams.

Their hooks do not scratch the glaze.

Some are no longer than a finger. Others are long enough to bridge a channel. Size does not reliably indicate age; a large one can divide at a constriction in its body, leaving two smaller creatures to take different passages. Nothing spills from the division. For a while afterward, both move awkwardly, feeling ahead with feet that had previously been near the middle.

They eat something carried in the water. In a clean glass vessel they grow cloudy and cease moving. Given a chip of green-stained ceramic, they gather beneath it.

Children occasionally keep them for a day in covered bowls. The animals escape more often than the children admit. A mother lifting a basin may find one flattened beneath it, transparent except for a dark grain moving slowly through its body.

She puts it in the nearest drain. She does not crush it.

The chimney birds are less tolerated. They loosen mortar with their feet and pull fibrous packing from warm joints. Their nests, when found, resemble bundles of burnt wire. There are no visible eggs. A small bird is sometimes present beside a large one, standing in the same orientation, and later it is gone.

People tie loose metal strips to the chimney ladders to discourage them. The birds learn which strips make noise and which have become clogged with deposits. They favor the silent ones.

A dead bird weighs surprisingly little. Its breast is flat and hard beneath the feathers, and the folded wings cannot easily be opened. Dead ones are placed on the downhill roofs rather than buried. By the next rain they have usually disappeared.

The mineral crusts are treated with more care.

A wall is not scraped merely because a deposit has become thick. Workers first mark its edge and return after several days. If it has spread toward a warm pipe, it can generally be removed. If it has spread away from one, they consult someone who has been in the city longer.

Such consultation may produce no explanation at all.

An old woman comes, carrying a lamp even in daytime. She holds it close to the blistered paint, then turns the flame down. She asks whether a nearby room has been used recently, whether a chair has been moved, whether anyone heard dripping during the last shutdown.

Sometimes she gives permission to scrape.

Sometimes she puts her own mark beside the first and goes away.

There are houses in which deposits have been allowed to cross the entire floor. The people living above them descend by ladders whose feet rest on small ceramic islands. They bring nothing wooden into those rooms. In winter, if the cloud season can be called winter, the crusts become faintly luminous at their growing edges.

This light is too weak to illuminate anything else.

The keepers of the upper basin live nearest to something for which they have no common name.

They are not a separate order, though they work in pairs and are reluctant to accept substitutes. One brings food while the other watches the lamps. They rotate their duties according to a schedule written in pencil on the inside of a cupboard. The oldest entries are in several different hands. None has been erased.

They look at the water indirectly.

A keeper checking the far walkway studies the handrail reflected beneath it, or the line where a warning lamp ought to appear. If the reflection is displaced, the walkway is closed. No attempt is made to measure the displacement from above the water.

Their tools have short cords attached. Even so, things are lost.

Once, a woman dropped a wrench and watched it settle beside a white tile near the edge. It remained there for eleven days. On the twelfth it lay farther out, beside another wrench of the same pattern, so corroded that it could no longer have been used.

She entered both in the cupboard record.

She did not retrieve either.

When the warmth moves in long bands, the keepers sometimes hear a disturbance from the roofless chambers: bolts rolling, a length of rail settling against its fastenings, a door closing in a room that has no door. They remain at their posts. Afterward they walk the perimeter and check the surfaces people must use.

Their children are allowed near the basin only when it is still.

One of the keepers has a daughter who sits on the steps and tells stories to whatever she sees in the water. Her father listens from the walkway. He corrects her if she gives the wrong name for a district or describes a stair as leading somewhere it does not.

Otherwise he lets her speak.

At night, the scattered households become easier to locate. Above a wet workshop, someone turns a lamp toward the wall. A kitchen vent carries the smell of onions. Two people cross a square with their shoes wrapped in cloth, returning from a visit too late to avoid the hot paving.

In the pump houses, the gauges remain lit.

Somebody has filled each lamp, cleaned its glass, and set it where it can be read from the doorway. The person who comes after them will pause there, perhaps without entering, and then continue along a route that passes dozens of dark buildings before reaching the next occupied room.

Behind them, a channel creature tests the water at a junction. High above, a bird shifts its feet on a cooling cap. Beneath the upper walkways, something moves a dropped bolt a little nearer to shore.

By morning there will be these small changes to account for, along with the work already written down.
