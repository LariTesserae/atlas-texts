The larger animal is easiest to find during a replacement, though even then it is usually mistaken for part of the opening.

First there is a dark line beneath the descending plate. The line thickens at one end. Something pale turns inside it, presenting a surface that has neither the polish of a fitting nor the roughness of a worn seal. Then the animal draws itself out, slowly enough that its movement seems to belong to the mechanism.

It has two legs and a body held low between them. What leaves the brush mark is not a tail but the underside of the body, a dense fringe that trails lightly across the ground. On a small animal the fringe is the color of bone. On an old one it carries so much powder that the individual fibers cannot be distinguished.

Its feet are broad, divided beneath into many shallow cups. It sets them down without a sound. When it lifts one, several cups remain attached for a moment, stretching into translucent necks before releasing. The paired tracks are therefore deeper at the back than at the front. In fine powder, each indentation contains a pattern of little rings.

The animal may be as long as a person lying down, but it rarely presents its full length. Its body can contract into a thick oval or extend until it becomes almost ribbonlike. There is no obvious head. At either end, folds open to expose a row of short, ivory feelers. Usually only the forward row is visible. If something troubles it, both rows emerge, and it stands for a long time with its feet planted and its fringe raised clear of the ground.

It does not look toward the disturbance. It waits for information to arrive through the surface.

The white covering is not shell. Close inspection shows overlapping sleeves, each slightly loose around the one beneath it. Heat can enter between them. Dust cannot. Along the flanks, where the sleeves draw apart, the living tissue is a muted brown with small metallic flecks suspended in it. These flecks change position over the course of several days.

An injury does not bleed. The exposed tissue draws inward, and the neighboring sleeves slide together. A torn animal can remain in one place through several fronts before moving again. Sometimes its tracks resume with a new spacing, as though the repair has altered the distance between its legs.

It crosses hot ground because it carries its own cooler region. Heat passes through the fringe, enters the outer sleeves, and is moved into a pair of thick folds beside the legs. There it is stored briefly in a substance that softens as it warms. An animal near the end of a long crossing has swollen flanks and walks with its feet farther apart. It seeks a recently opened replacement site, lowers itself over the rim, and lets the folds hang into the cooler layers below.

This is why so many tracks end at new plates.

Most of the animals go in.

Some, however, come out.

Below the surface they feed on films that grow between parts no longer intended to touch. These films are almost invisible: a brown haze on a mesh, an iridescence on the inside of a duct. They live on traces of reactive material left by repairs, on the unequal charges of adjacent components, on gases escaping too slowly to register as leaks.

The animal’s forward feelers spread over such surfaces and become a soft comb. The comb moves once, very slowly. When it lifts, the film is gone.

It also feeds at the roots of the silver plants. A vigorous hedge may conceal a chamber large enough to hold several animals, their bodies pressed against the underside of the plates. Their feeding thins the roots without severing them. Above, the leaves become smaller and redder. The hedge persists for years in an apparently poor condition, while beneath it the animals prosper.

Their young begin life there.

They are not born with legs. Each is a flattened pouch, no larger than a fingernail, attached to the underside of an adult’s sleeve. For a long time it is difficult to tell them from loose pieces of the covering. The parent carries them through the warm phases and lowers them into feeding places. Gradually each pouch develops a seam. The seam opens, revealing its first feelers.

The legs are the last substantial parts to form.

A young animal leaving its parent must cross a short stretch of bare construction. Even a distance of a hand’s breadth can be fatal. Its heat-storage folds are small; its fringe is still sparse. Most wait until the dust moves, then travel beneath the advancing sheet, keeping within the minute interval of coolness that precedes it.

A collecting seam may receive hundreds.

A few find a fitting on the way.

There are places where these animals gather in greater numbers than the vegetation seems able to support. The low rising mound is one. Beneath it, adults lie in tiers, each following a different joint through the buried construction. Their bodies are long enough to span several components. When something below shifts, they withdraw their feelers together.

The vibration felt above is partly this withdrawal.

For a little while the mound contains room where there was none. A buried assembly expands into it. Later, when the assembly contracts, the animals return to the exposed surfaces.

Whether they impede the mound’s movement or make it possible has never been established. Fresh plates are brought up through their resting places without crushing them. A descending rod pauses when it touches a sleeve. Occasionally an animal is carried upward on a replacement panel, remains there through its installation, and steps off only after the rim has closed.

The maintenance system appears to recognize it.

This recognition does not extend to all living things.

The small foil-backed creatures are sometimes sealed into joins. Their gathered scales remain on the surface after the bodies have disappeared. Silver roots are clipped by repairs, and whole beds of translucent growth can be lowered into the ground without warning.

The larger animals are spared inconsistently. An adult may pass safely through machinery that destroys another of the same size. Some carry old punctures in exact geometric arrangements. Others have pieces of manufactured material fitted into their bodies: a pale collar around a leg, a narrow dark bar beneath the fringe. The tissue has grown closely around these additions.

Those with collars tend to survive the replacements.

They are not more numerous near the rising mound. They are more numerous near the empty spaces.

At the edges of these spaces, an animal moves with unusual care. It sets one foot inside, leaves the other out, and extends its body over the boundary. The forward feelers touch nothing. Nevertheless, they bend, each at a different point, as though resting against an irregular surface.

It may hold this posture until a bright front has passed.

The cooling that a traveler feels there draws heat out of its swollen folds. But the animal often stays after the folds have flattened. Its sleeves loosen. The fringe lifts from the ground. Tiny pale pouches become visible along its underside.

There are empty spaces where several generations come to rest. Their dimensions remain unchanged while the plates around them are replaced. The animals do not defend these places, but they return from considerable distances, following routes that can be traced in the dust only in scattered sections.

Occasionally one arrives at a space already occupied. It waits at the edge. Neither animal touches the other. After a while the first leaves, and the second takes up almost exactly the same posture.

Elsewhere, life is smaller and less readily separated from what supports it.

The translucent scales in the shallow basins are living colonies. Each scale contains several thousand cells arranged around a branching channel. The outermost cells are dry and brittle. Those nearer the center remain flexible, carrying ions back and forth through a gel so thin it cannot be seen without cutting the scale open.

During a cooler interval the upper scales tilt. Beneath them are dark attachment patches and tiny pale nodules. The nodules lengthen, flatten, and become new scales.

A basin’s colony advances by covering its own dead portions. It can be centuries old and less than a finger deep.

The foil-backed creatures eat the brittle margins, then carry fragments on their backs. Some fragments remain alive. In a favorable seam they put out attachment threads and anchor the bearer to the ground. The creature must pull itself loose before the threads thicken. Those that fail become small raised islands within the colony, still moving their limbs beneath the translucent layers for several days.

Other organisms follow the black granules through their slow circuits. These are fine white filaments with hooked ends. They attach to a grain, travel toward the basin’s rim, and release when it begins its return. Seen from above, an occupied basin has a pale margin that seems to breathe.

The filaments feed during the balancing of a load. A trapped boot can bring about a brief abundance: the white margin thickens, spreads inward, and gathers around the submerged object. When the grains finally release it, some filaments go with it. They survive in stitching, buckles, or the join between sole and upper until they find another place where mass is being transferred.

Most die on the journey. Their remains are a powder finer than the manufactured dust.

Even the apparently flawless stretches contain inhabitants, though almost none live on the surface.

Within the mineral fibers, colonies extend along single strands. Their growth is extremely slow. A branch may require a hundred warm fronts to reach the next strand, and a successful crossing may feed it for several thousand more. They respond to damage by thickening nearby fibers with substances taken from their own tissues. A small colony can be indistinguishable from an old repair.

Some are consumed when the fiber is recycled. Others survive the process and reappear in freshly made material.

A new plate can therefore arrive already inhabited.

For several days nothing shows. Then, in the cooler wake of a front, a pinpoint on its surface loses its shine. A faint circle develops around it. The circle may remain unchanged for years, or widen until the edge of the plate begins to support silver stems.

The longest-lived colonies produce no visible defect at all. They occupy stable chambers beneath the old ridges, where temperature changes arrive slowly and the dry gas is seldom exchanged. There they form soft, intricately folded bodies suspended between the chamber walls. Their folds open as the gas warms and close as it cools. They retain small quantities of whatever passes through: dust, fragments of membrane, the vapor of worn seals.

A chamber that contains one smells faintly sweet when opened.

These bodies cannot endure the surface. Exposure collapses their folds. Light draws a skin across them, and within minutes they resemble crumpled scraps of insulating material. Beneath the ridge, protected, they may outlast every plate above them.

The larger animals sometimes descend to such chambers and press their fringes against the folds. Afterward their metallic flecks have changed distribution. Their young grow more quickly. The folded bodies show little alteration beyond a few narrow depressions where the animals rested.

At the ridge during a cool interval, the visible life is slight: red leaf interiors, a few moving specks, a foil back disappearing beneath a stem. Most of it is within reach but hidden.

Under a resting hand, roots follow the join downward. Farther below, a two-legged animal has eased its swollen flank against a duct. Its young cling beneath the sleeves. In an older chamber, suspended folds open into the warming gas.

When the next front arrives, the surface leaves close first. The foil-backed creatures climb into the light. The larger animal withdraws its feelers from a film and waits while the duct shifts beside it.

Deep under the ridge, the warmth arrives much later. By then the upper ground is cooling again, and the folded body is only beginning to open.
