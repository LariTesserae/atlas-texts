What lives here is mostly folded away.

The visible leaves are the upper parts of it, the parts that can afford to meet the air. They are thick without being fleshy, reinforced by fibers that run lengthwise and gather at the blunt tips. Dust adheres to their upper surfaces. On the undersides, where copper-colored skin shelters the small openings, there is almost none. A leaf turned over feels cooler than it should. Its veins are raised, and the faint light in them stops for a moment where a finger presses.

The different masses of growth do not quite belong to different plants.

Under the dust, a pale stem from the pipe-side growth passes toward the nearest concrete footing. It branches before reaching it. One branch climbs the concrete, becoming narrow and hard; the other follows a shallow depression toward the shed. There it enters the sheet of roots beneath the ceramic tiles. Farther along, something very like the same stem emerges under the bucket.

There are breaks in this connection, too. Places where a length has dried, leaving two sealed ends. Places where a fallen stone has divided a once-continuous mat. The separated growth continues to open and close with the heat. It has enough stored water to persist on its own, and enough contact with the pipe to keep the same hours.

At the center of each mass, beneath the catchment leaves, are smaller leaves that never open fully. They stand in a damp, crowded arrangement, their edges tucked into one another. Some are nearly transparent. Through them you can see pale granules moving slowly along channels, stopping at a junction, then taking one branch rather than another.

These inner leaves do not gather water. They receive it.

A bead caught above travels down a groove, disappears between two overlapping surfaces, and spreads into a film. The film passes through layers too closely fitted for a drop to survive as a drop. By the time the outer leaf rises again, its basin is empty, but the weight of the whole growth has changed.

The smallest living things are in this interior.

They form a slippery gray lining on the submerged stems and a dark varnish where the roots enter the mineral seams. Removed from either surface, they would look like dirt in water. Here they grow in thin, distinct bands. One band occupies the cooler edge of a vapor opening. Another lies just beyond it, where the first band has altered what comes through. The roots press against both.

There is a red-brown layer beneath the white roots under the tiles. It is so thin that it remains visible only while wet. As it dries, it becomes indistinguishable from the stain on the ceramic. The roots lying against it have flattened sides, matching its surface precisely.

The green growth depends on these films. Its roots do not simply take up what the pipes release. Much of what comes out would injure them. At each seam, the fine root tips stop short of the hottest deposits, held in a damp sheath that the films continually remake. Where the sheath fails, a root blackens. The growth closes that branch and sends another beside it.

Old tips remain there, brittle and hollow, among the new white ones.

There is considerably more death here than the upright leaves suggest. It is tucked underneath, compressed into dark scales, or built into the outer walls of stems. The growth wastes little of it. A leaf that no longer lifts still shades the leaves below. A dead root holds a narrow passage open through a salt crust until another root finds the passage.

Some of the oldest stems are no longer pale. They have taken on the color of the deposits around them and can be mistaken for ridges in the floor. Inside the ridge is a thread of moist tissue no thicker than sewing cotton. Around it are years of hardened layers, although the chamber itself has been open for much less time.

The growth was here before the fall.

Not all of it, and not in this arrangement. There are old roots inside the pipe insulation, flattened between layers that were once tightly wound. On the underside of the shed’s detached roof, dry stem scars run toward bolt holes. The lowering ground pulled some of these connections apart and carried others down intact. Much of what now looks luxuriant began as a sheltered film, a small root, a leaf pressed into the dark behind a bracket.

The new chamber gave it room. It also exposed it.

The broad leaves are recent. So are the long crossings over open stone. At the edge of each mass, young growth advances with a low, close-fitting shape, keeping its tender surfaces against the floor. Only after several cooling cycles does it put up a leaf. Many of these first leaves fail. They lie as small brown tongues in the dust, still attached to stems that have withdrawn their moisture.

The bucket leaf is one that has lasted.

Its stem fills the hole in the metal. Around that hole, rust has been held in a damp ring and worked loose grain by grain. The leaf receives little water directly, but its roots have reached the underside of the nearest tile. Its bend toward the valve follows the cooler air there.

When the valve changes position, the wheel’s rim loses heat before the wall of the shed does. A small patch near the leaf tip becomes wet. The leaf extends farther during that part of the cycle, slowly enough that watching it gives little sense of movement. Later, as the wheel warms, it draws back.

A mark remains on the metal where the tip has touched. It is an irregular oval, clean in the middle and edged with powdery rust.

No one is tending the installation. The tiles were laid for feet, and the stool in the shed is still only a stool. Yet the growth has begun to alter the conditions that sustain it. Roots pack the spaces beneath the pipe, holding dampness against sections that once dried quickly. Leaves shelter joints from the moving air. A mat beneath one footing has thickened enough to lift a loose fragment of concrete, opening a narrow channel where condensation can collect.

The machinery still determines the larger changes. When a hot section stays hot too long, the leaves above it cannot correct that. They narrow their openings, relinquish their outermost layers, and wait. On one side of the trench there is a whole patch of collapsed growth from such a failure. Beneath its dry covering, three small leaves remain wet.

At the gathering drop, a leaf has extended beyond the safe edge of the growth.

Its tip is thin and faintly luminous. Heat has browned one side, but the central groove remains open. It does not reach toward the drop itself. It rests below and a little aside, at the place where previous drops have struck the stone.

For a long while nothing happens. The drop swells. The leaf holds its position. A few grains loosen from the roof and land on its dusty upper skin.

When the water finally falls, it strikes the groove and separates. Part splashes onto the hot deposit and is lost. The rest runs inward, beneath the overlapping leaves.

The extended tip stays where it is. Farther back, in the damp interior, a folded leaf loosens by a fraction.
