The largest of the ceiling animals has a pale patch behind its head where a molt failed.

The old skin came away from its feet and belly, but a piece remained caught across its shoulders. For several cycles it dragged this stiff scrap through the seams. Eventually the scrap broke off against a casting ridge, taking some of the new skin with it. The wound has closed. In the silver light it looks like a grain of wet salt.

It lives above the white mineral shelf, in a gap where two components no longer meet squarely. There is room there for its body and, farther in, for the folded skins of bodies it used to have. Its blunt head faces outward. Its rear feet remain braced against opposite walls even when it sleeps.

Seen closely, the toes are not sticky. Each has a soft pad bordered by minute hooks, and the animal changes how it grips according to what is beneath it. On enamel it spreads the pad. On mineral crust it curls the toe. Across a repaired crack, where the surface has risen into a smooth scar, it pauses and feels for purchase.

It has eyes, but they are small and recessed. They distinguish the entrance from the interior, a passing body from a stain. Much of what it knows arrives through its feet.

A grazer scraping below sends a rough, intermittent tremor through the shelf. Water moving through a groove makes a finer disturbance. Another ceiling animal approaching along the same rib produces a succession of loaded points: weight, release, weight. The resident can tell these apart while the whole chamber shakes around it. Sometimes it lifts one foot and then another, sampling a vibration from different places before deciding whether to move.

Its mouth is wider than its head appears to allow. At rest the edges are folded inward, leaving only a short pale line. During a strike they open around the prey, and the front of the head briefly loses its blunt shape. There are no visible teeth. Two hard surfaces inside the mouth meet and hold.

After eating, it returns to the gap. A dark bulge travels slowly down its transparent underside.

It is not always successful. The grazers have lived under hunters for a long time.

Their flatness is useful beneath loose enamel, but it also makes them difficult to seize. When a shadow crosses them, they settle hard against the ceramic, drawing the legs inward. A hunter may get only the edge of a back plate. That edge breaks readily. The grazer escapes with a ragged outline and seals the exposed tissue beneath a bead of cloudy fluid.

Several of the grazers on the dark corner are missing pieces. One has only five legs. Its paired tapping remains regular, though it has to stop walking to make it.

They tap for more than one reason. A grazer entering an occupied feeding patch knocks twice, waits, and knocks again. The nearest animal may shift aside. It may answer and hold its place. Along a good runnel, where fresh water has laid down a film of cells, the exchanges pass through several bodies before movement begins. Then a narrow lane opens, closes behind the newcomer, and resumes scraping.

The grazers’ eggs are difficult to distinguish from filter beads. They are laid in groups beneath flakes that water can enter but cannot easily lift. An adult sometimes spends hours clearing grit from such a flake, backing out with each particle held between its forelimbs. Once the eggs are attached, it leaves.

The young emerge almost without color. For a while their food can be seen through their backs, a dark crescent that advances and fades. They feed in pits the adults cannot reach. As their plates harden, they move out onto the broad worn surfaces, joining the small traffic of taps.

In the puddles, the transparent swimmers remain transparent throughout their lives. What looks like a fringe beneath the pipe is a crowd of separate hungers.

Their feet comb the water. Their mouths work continuously. A swimmer suspended upside down can remain almost motionless while its limbs sort particles against its belly. Most are rejected. A few disappear into the green-brown thread inside.

They gather most densely where condensation falls from the pipe, because each drop briefly opens the surface and turns over the water beneath it. The swimmers rise into that disturbance, tumble, right themselves. Some carry egg sacs so clear that only the curved row of embryos gives them away.

There is another animal among them, narrower and less numerous. It has almost no visible gut. It waits against the pipe with its limbs drawn close, then catches a swimmer and folds around it. For a few moments the two transparent bodies are hard to separate.

Nothing in the basin is quite safe merely because it is small.

The smallest inhabitants make their shelters inside the white shelf. In its pores live soft, segmented bodies that withdraw when the water cools. Their openings are ringed with fine bristles. During the warm flood, the bristles extend into the current and tremble under the burden of passing matter.

A newly broken piece of crust appears lifeless until it is left wet. Then points of movement emerge from its holes. A few bristles reach out. Something pale turns within a cavity scarcely larger than a bubble.

The shelf is eaten from inside as well as scraped from outside. Its overhang remains brittle because colonies soften the mineral behind it. When a piece falls, grazers arrive at the fresh underside. The ceiling resident usually comes down too, not for the crust, but for what has been exposed.

Its pale scar is easiest to see during these visits.

Before the manifold reverses, the chamber changes in ways too slight for a person to notice. The puddle lifts against its edges. A cooler groove stops cooling. Pressure shifts through the ribs.

The grazers begin climbing before the water appears.

The ceiling animals draw farther into their seams. Swimmers release the pipe and gather low in the puddle, limbs beating. Beneath the enamel flakes, hatchlings crowd onto the upper surfaces of their egg shelters.

Then the flood comes.

For a short time the swimmers can travel over the whole basin. They pass above feeding patches that are ordinarily dry, through the comb of bent green filaments, around the white shelf. Some are carried into wall cavities and return during the draining. Some do not return.

The five-legged grazer waits on the ridge with the others. Water touches its forefeet. It climbs a little higher.

When the flow subsides, small bodies remain in depressions too shallow to keep them alive. The ceiling hunters descend. Grazers return to their patches. The pore dwellers extend into the last threads of warm water.

The scarred animal takes a stranded swimmer from the foot of the shelf. It holds the prey for a moment while a drop forms on its head, grows, and runs across one closed eye. Then it swallows and climbs back toward its gap.

Below it, the paired knocks begin again. They travel through the cast ribs and worn ceramic, between cooling channels and beneath the immense pressure of the inhabited structure. Each reaches only a small distance. Across that distance, another animal shifts its feet and makes room.
