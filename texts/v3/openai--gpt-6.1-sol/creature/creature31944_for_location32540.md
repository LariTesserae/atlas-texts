At first it looks like a piece of the tree that has loosened.

It hangs beneath a sideways trunk, narrow shoulders drawn up around its ears, its belly hidden against the bark. The fur on its back is the color of wet seed shells. Along its flanks the fur thins to a dark, wrinkled skin that expands and contracts with each breath. Its eyes catch no light. Only the ears move, turning separately toward a falling fruit, a cough from the low ground, the faint tapping of pebbles somewhere under the roots.

Then it lets go with one hand.

The arm is longer than its body. Four fingers uncurl from the bark in succession, each ending in a blunt nail with a sharp inner edge. The fifth is short and set low on the wrist. It can oppose the others, but it is mostly used to hook into crevices while the long fingers search ahead. The animal touches a vine, pushes it sideways, waits. When the vine begins to draw taut, it takes its weight elsewhere.

It crosses without making a leap. For several moments it has a hand on one stem, a foot on another, the other foot spread against the trunk, and its tail curled around a root above its head. Each support carries too little of it to provoke a full tightening. By the time the leaves turn edge-on, it has passed.

There are six more behind it.

They are not a family in the sense of a single pair and their young. Two females carry infants pressed into the sparse fur of their bellies. A half-grown male moves close behind the leading animal, reaching for the same holds, sometimes finding that a stem has already begun to stiffen. The others range above and below the route, separating around difficult tangles and coming together again at forks.

The leader is an old female. One ear has a notch deep enough to divide it into two moving tips. Her left hind foot is missing its outer toe.

On level ground, these animals can run. Their backs arch, their long forelimbs plant far ahead, and their hind feet come down almost where their hands have been. But there is little level ground here. Mostly they descend only as far as they need to, then climb again. The soles of their feet are thick, with deep creases that let them fold around a rock edge. Even so, nearly every adult has a scar on a heel or a finger. The young sometimes leave spots of blood on the pale undersides of leaves.

The old female stops above a hollow trunk.

Her companions stop too, though not immediately. One continues another body length and is caught by the tightening loop of a vine. It makes a thin, furious sound. A nearby animal braces against the trunk and pulls the stem outward. The trapped animal slides free, bites the vine twice, and follows the others down.

Inside the hollow are husks, beetle wings, a mat of fur, and three stones.

The stones were carried up at different times. None fits especially well in a hand. One has a broad striking face; another is small enough to be brought down accurately on the seam of a pod. The third has been there so long that the animals step over it without using it. The hollow is a place they return to because seed shells can be broken there without rolling away, and because the sound does not travel as far as it does from bare rock.

Tonight there is no pod.

The old female puts her head into the hollow, sniffs, then reaches beneath the discarded shells. She finds a larva as long as her smallest finger. She folds it into her mouth before withdrawing her hand.

A younger female smells the same opening and stays to dig.

Their food is whatever they can reach and open. Soft fruit is taken first when there is enough of it. They eat the swollen bases of certain leaves, flowers before they unfold, eggs, small sleeping animals, and the white tissue beneath bark. Some seed pulp makes them salivate until ropes of foam hang from their chins. They still eat it, but slowly, rubbing pieces against leaves and dropping the bitter portions.

The infants learn which foods to take by reaching into another animal’s mouth.

An adult may turn away, cuff the reaching hand, or surrender a fragment already chewed. Thus the first taste of a dangerous fruit is usually a small one. The young learn the smell while it is mixed with the breath of an animal that has survived eating it.

Water is learned more carefully.

The old female lowers herself onto a root that enters a fissure. Her companions spread along the trunk above. She feels the stone with the inside of her wrist, where the skin is bare. Then she puts her muzzle close to the opening without touching it.

There is an odor here that was not present yesterday.

She retreats.

The half-grown male descends after her, reaches past the place where she stopped, and recoils with a cry. The others turn their faces toward him. For a while he shakes his hand. Later, as they travel, he keeps putting the burned fingers into his mouth.

No call names the changed fissure. The others have seen where the old female turned and where the young one cried out. Those who were farther away will approach it for themselves. By the next night they may all avoid it, or the smell may have changed again.

Their knowledge is carried in feet, noses, and repeated journeys. A young animal knows that a certain trunk leads to water because it has followed an older one along that trunk. It may continue to take the route after the water is gone. It may discover a new wet seam and visit it alone for several nights before another follows.

The old female has outlived three of the trees along her usual circuit. At the place where one fell, she still pauses before crossing the gap.

Below them a herd begins to pass. The first grazer presses its muzzle through a curtain of leaves; the rest follow the opening it makes. The climbing animals wait until the broad backs are beneath them, then descend into the disturbed shrubs. They take exposed insects and fragments of fruit knocked loose by the herd. One rides briefly between a grazer’s shoulders, parting the hide with its fingers to find the ticks clustered there.

The grazer tolerates this until a finger enters an old wound. It throws its head back. The rider springs into a shrub, which folds under the impact and leaves it clinging upside down near the ground.

Something in the litter moves toward it.

The animal climbs rapidly. Its companions give short, dry calls, unlike the rising cries they make during a quarrel. The sound follows the hidden movement for several yards. Then the predator stops, and they lose it among the roots.

They sleep above places where large animals have difficulty turning. Sometimes this is a fork surrounded by responsive vines. Sometimes it is a narrow trunk suspended across a collapse. They crowd together with their faces outward, infants enclosed between warm bellies. No individual stays awake all day. Their sleep is broken by the movements of the others, by heat, by flies, by a branch shifting under their combined weight.

A sleeping group changes shape continually. An animal on the exposed edge presses inward. Another wakes, bites it, and settles in the place it has vacated. A youngster climbs over three backs and is pulled down by the tail. Sometimes one falls. If it catches a lower branch, it returns noisily and keeps the others awake for a long time.

Tonight they do not reach their sleeping place before the small rain begins over the vents.

At the first drop striking a husk, the old female raises her head. Her ears turn forward together. She gives a low series of clicks and moves quickly, taking a route through stems that the younger animals use one at a time.

They arrive above the gathering grazers. Water hangs in beads along the leaves. The climbing animals pull the leaves toward their mouths and draw them between their lips. Infants lick the wet fur of their mothers. The half-grown male holds a broad leaf beneath a steady drip, but another animal pulls at its edge and spills what has collected.

For several minutes there is enough.

The old female drinks from a leaf tip until the flow weakens. Then she climbs down beside a vent where water has gathered in a shallow depression. She spreads her hands on the stone, lowers her face, and drinks without lifting it between swallows.

A grazer shoulders closer. She moves aside but stays low, feeling along the margin of the wet patch with her lips.

By morning the seven will be tucked into a fork farther up the basin. Their fur will dry. The burned hand will stiffen. One infant, woken by hunger, will pull at its mother’s jaw until she gives it something from her mouth.

When they wake again, the old female will descend first. At the next trunk she will stop, listen, and test the stem ahead of her before putting her weight on it.
