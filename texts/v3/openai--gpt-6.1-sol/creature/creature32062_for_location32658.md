The inhabitant is easiest to find at the exposed end of the slab, although it does not live beneath it.

There is a place along the band of fossil shells where the limestone looks slightly deeper than it should. The grain remains visible. So do the pale curves of the shells. But the light seems to have passed through an extra thickness of water before reaching them.

This is part of its body.

Another part lies in a groove on the inward-facing shelf. A third extends across the bare floor between two pondweed stems. These are not separate creatures. Between them, through water that looks quite ordinary, there is continuity: a broad, extremely thin body laid over the hollow’s lower surfaces. It follows the rock without quite touching everywhere. Above the silt it lifts a little, keeping its weight off the fine particles.

It has weight, though very little. It has edges, though they are difficult to locate. A leaf seen through an edge appears briefly displaced from its stem.

Most of the body is clear. Where it gathers into greater thickness, it takes on the faint grey-green of old window glass. It has no face on its upper surface, no arrangement of features to suggest which way it is looking. The portion near the slab is somewhat thicker than the rest, and several narrow folds converge there. That is as close as it comes to having a head.

Its underside is less simple.

Thousands of small, soft contacts meet the limestone. They are not feet. Each can shorten, spread, or withdraw, and each is sensitive to pressure passing through the stone. Some fit into pores too small to see. Others rest against the mineral coating, finding purchase without breaking it. The creature could move by releasing these contacts in sequence, but seldom does. When it changes position, the change is so gradual that the hollow still appears empty throughout.

It does not swim. Free water gives it nothing to hold together against.

The open side of the hollow is therefore a genuine boundary for it, though not an absolute one. It could travel across the level silt if necessary, contracting into a thicker shape and carrying its lower surface clear of the sediment. This would be costly and conspicuous. It has made that journey before. The body retains folds that are unnecessary in its present spread, tucked close to the slab.

Here, it can remain broad.

It feeds at the places where groundwater enters through the stone. The amounts are minute: dissolved substances, thin films of living matter, the small chemical differences between water in a crack and water over the floor. Its feeding surfaces are tucked into the limestone’s older channels. Nothing is scraped clean. Nothing is sucked out quickly enough to stir the sediment. Over many days, one little patch of mineral film becomes thinner while another develops beyond its reach.

The pondweed is neither its food nor its garden. It leaves room for the stems because rooted plants are troublesome to accommodate. A growing root presses with an insistence that smooth stone does not. At one seam, the creature has gradually withdrawn, leaving a narrow passage around the roots. Its edge there is folded back upon itself.

The vibration from the waterworks reaches it through almost every contact at once.

It can distinguish things in that low sound which would be lost to an ear: a slight mismatch between rotating machines, a bearing whose roughness comes and goes, the brief redistribution of pressure when a pump is brought into service. Across its body, the same event arrives at different strengths. The slab transmits one part cleanly. The shelf makes another part linger. Deep silt dulls almost everything.

This gives the creature a continuous sense of its own extent. A change at the distant machinery passes through the hollow, and the returning pattern tells it where its folds are resting, which contacts have slipped, whether a little water has entered beneath an edge.

During a shutdown, it becomes less certain of itself.

Then it thickens near the slab. The outer portions draw inward by a few inches. It presses more of its underside directly against the stone and waits, feeling for the weaker sounds that remain: water entering a fissure, a distant impact carried through the lakebed, the faint strain of its own body held between two surfaces.

It is not listening for footsteps. It has no expectation of a visitor.

Its awareness is occupied by things near enough to affect it. Cold means that its outer membrane tightens. A change in dissolved minerals opens some feeding surfaces and closes others. Daylight reaches through its body and alters a sparse scattering of sensitive cells, giving it a slow distinction between brighter and dimmer hours. It does not assemble these sensations into a view of the hollow. The farther rim, visible to an observer, is beyond most of its concern.

But it knows the buried end of the slab.

There the stone continues beneath the sediment, and the creature follows a little of it into darkness. That sheltered portion is denser, more opaque, folded in several layers. When the upper body is damaged, new tissue spreads from there. When conditions change too quickly, the rest can withdraw toward it.

The exposed body may eventually be lost. The part beside the buried stone can persist and begin again.

For now, nothing requires it to do so.

Its contacts hold. Water brings enough. The cold remains within the narrow range its tissues tolerate. Over the band of fossil shells, a slow contraction passes through the thicker folds, making the pale curves seem to lean and then return.

No sediment rises.

Across the hollow, the low vibration reaches a slightly different pitch. The creature loosens three contacts in a groove, settles four others against the slab, and remains spread beneath the daylight.
