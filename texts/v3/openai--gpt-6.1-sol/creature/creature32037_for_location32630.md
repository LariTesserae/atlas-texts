At first, the only sign of an inhabitant is that one of the thresholds stays dry.

Water beads on the lintel and travels down both sides of the door, but it does not cross the narrow strip beneath it. Something inside is holding that strip a little warmer than the surrounding pavement. When the pressure chamber empties, the door opens a finger’s width. After the third knock, it closes.

This happens several times before anyone comes out.

The figure that eventually emerges is roughly the height of a person, though the comparison becomes less useful as it moves. Its legs are short in relation to its trunk. Its arms hang close to its sides, ending in broad, divided pads rather than hands. Along its back, overlapping plates of the same mineral composite as the buildings remain slightly parted, with a pale, moist tissue visible between them.

It stands on the threshold until a heavy drop strikes the rail.

Then it crosses to the garden.

Each foot lands on the raised center of a paving block. Before lifting the other foot, it waits for the water around the block to settle. Its balance is excellent. What looks like hesitation is attention: the joints in its ankles make small corrections as the district shifts beneath it.

In the green light, its color cannot be established. The edges of its body are pale. Its underside seems darker, especially where the warm mist touches it.

It has a face, or at least a place where another creature would expect a face. There are two shallow depressions above a narrow fold. Water collects in the depressions without spilling. Occasionally a transparent membrane draws across one of them, carrying away a film of pollen.

The fold opens when it reaches the garden steps.

Nothing audible comes out. A little warmth does.

Others live behind the doors.

There are not many: perhaps seven, perhaps eleven. Counting them requires deciding whether a small body seen on one afternoon is the same as a larger one encountered several days later, and whether two figures leaving a service passage together have always been two.

Their bodies continue to change. Plates harden, loosen, and are shed inside the buildings, where the material can be taken up again. New growth shows pale bands. The smallest inhabitants have no rigid covering at all. They remain in the warm wall cavities, supported in recesses shaped almost like cupboards.

The cavities are not their homes in the ordinary sense. They are places where a body can be held safely while it is unfinished.

An adult will sometimes stop beside one and press the broad pad of an arm against the wall. It stays there through a full cycle of the pressure chamber. On the other side, something presses back.

They receive most of their nourishment while resting. A soft surface beneath the plates of the back lies against the wall, and fluids pass between the body and the narrow channels inside the composite. The process takes hours. During it, the inhabitant is not quite asleep. It can feel heat arriving from far above: bathing water, a kitchen cooling after use, many people gathered in a room.

These changes matter to them. A sustained warmth makes them restless. A sharp fall in temperature draws them toward the deeper service passages. They have no picture of the occupied rooms overhead, but they know their rhythms with an intimacy the people in those rooms do not suspect.

They also feed in the garden.

They kneel beside the low banks and open the fold beneath their faces against a leaf. The leaf pours into it. Sometimes they take pollen from the windows, using the edge of an arm pad to gather what the clearing pulse has left behind. They do this carefully, leaving enough that a visitor might think no one had touched the glass.

There is little waste. What they release is carried into the soil or accepted by the walls. This does not mean they are tidy. Tidiness would require the possibility of leaving something out of place. Most things they detach from themselves are still needed nearby.

Their work is harder to distinguish from their ordinary movements.

One stands beneath an ascending seam of water and lets the flow pass through the divided pads of its arms. Another rests its weight against a retaining wall while the root joints ease apart. A third spends an entire morning at a new runnel on the steps, lowering its face to the water and lifting it again.

They can close a leak, but seldom do so immediately. The edges must be touched. The water must be followed to wherever it disappears. Several of them may visit the same fault before any alteration is made.

They are young enough that repair and growth still look much alike.

The three deep knocks from the east façade bring them out together.

At the first knock, those in the garden straighten. At the second, figures appear at the thresholds. After the third, they place their feet more widely and open the plates along their backs.

For a few moments, all of them hold still.

The sound that follows—the apparent gravel dragged across a floor—is partly machinery and partly their answer. Each body makes a low vibration against the pavement. Through the joined foundations, the separate vibrations reach one another. The inhabitants learn where the others are, how heavily each is resting, which ones have hardened since the last exchange.

Then the plates close. Someone returns to a wall cavity. Someone descends the steps.

This is the nearest thing they have to a gathering.

There are preferences among them. One repeatedly chooses the threshold facing the shortest street. Another remains near the pressure chamber, although the impacts there are strong enough to shake droplets from its face. Two tend to occupy adjoining recesses and emerge within moments of each other.

They do not seem to possess objects. They possess routes, favored temperatures, particular places to put their weight.

The smallest mobile inhabitant has a route no other uses.

It crosses the garden diagonally, stepping between the low plants, and goes to the uncovered patch beneath the window with no room behind it. Its back is still soft. Only a narrow ridge has hardened along the middle, so it cannot rest against a wall for long without help.

The green points in the soil interest it.

At first it presses the ground around them, as it would test a new channel. On one visit it pushes too firmly. A shoot bends and does not rise again.

The next day, the inhabitant stops farther away.

It lowers itself onto the lowest step and remains there while warm water enters the patch. When the flow weakens, it reaches into the crack in the retaining wall and widens it slightly. When the flow becomes too strong, it holds one pad in front of the opening.

It has begun to regulate the water by staying beside it.

This occupies time it could spend feeding. The plates on its back develop slowly. When the others answer the pressure chamber, its vibration is faint.

Eventually a larger inhabitant comes down to the patch. It touches the small one between the shoulders, then presses a pad into the soil. A thin root has entered the drainage joint. The larger body holds the joint open.

For several cycles they remain together.

A new leaf separates from the shoot. It is small enough to fit in the hollow at the end of either creature’s arm.

Neither touches it.

Later, when the district adjusts its weight, the pavement tilts enough to send a shallow current toward the uncovered ground. The larger inhabitant leaves to meet the movement at the retaining wall. The smaller one stays. It spreads both arm pads across the approaching water, dividing the current around the shoot.

Water gathers against its arms. Its soft back trembles with the effort.

Behind it, a service door opens. Another inhabitant waits on the dry threshold, then comes down the steps.
