The animal comes out when the shadows of the fern stems begin to cross the channels.

At first there is only a change in the marsh: a floating leaf tilts against the current, and a narrow line travels through the duckweed. Then a head rises beneath the bank. It is blunt at the nose, small-eyed, with ears that fold so closely against the skull that they are difficult to distinguish. Water runs from a fringe of pale whiskers. The animal rests its chin on a submerged root and waits.

It is larger than the small mammals sleeping in the trees, but not very tall. Standing on firm ground, its back would come just below a person’s knee. Most of its length is behind its shoulders. The body is supple and heavy, tapering into a muscular tail flattened slightly from side to side. Its forefeet are broad, with separate fingers; its hindfeet spread into dark fans when it swims.

Wet, it looks almost black. As it dries, the fur opens into a close brown nap, with a reddish saddle over the shoulders and a pale, untidy patch beneath the throat. Older animals acquire orange stains on their legs from the springs. On this one, a rust-colored line runs down the left flank where she habitually squeezes past an iron-coated stone.

She stays beneath the bank until a bird moves above her. Her eyes follow its passage through the leaves. Then she pulls herself out, quietly except for the water falling from her belly.

She places her feet with care. The broad forepaws are not merely useful for swimming. Their fingers curl around roots hidden under the moss, and the bare pads can distinguish a firm branch from a floating mat before she commits her weight. In loose peat she spreads all four feet and lowers her belly. On stone she lifts it clear.

Her path is not the shortest route to anywhere.

She passes along one side of a pool, climbs a root, crosses a narrow strip of gravel, and doubles back through reeds until she is almost beside the place where she emerged. But she has avoided a deep pocket under the moss and a channel whose water is too hot. The turns are habitual. Some have been habitual for most of her life.

Where a spring has warmed since the last rain, she stops.

Her nose moves over the surface without touching it. The whiskers tremble in the vapor. She lowers one forefoot, withdraws it, and goes upstream to find another crossing. She tolerates warm water well, but the hottest channels would injure her as readily as they would any other animal. What distinguishes her is the attention she gives them. She tests shallow water before entering it and keeps to the edges of pools where a cooler tributary makes a narrow passage.

Her food gathers at such edges.

Small fish hold in the mingling currents. Snails graze the green film beyond the white mineral crust. Insect larvae live beneath stones where the water has cooled enough to carry more oxygen. She works these places in a particular order, returning to the reliable ones and investigating others after rain.

At the first channel she enters without a splash. For a moment her back remains above the surface, a low dark island, and then it disappears.

Underwater, she closes her ears and swims with her forefeet tucked against her chest. Her hindfeet and tail carry her forward. She does not remain submerged for very long. The water is warm, the air is thin, and after two or three passes beneath the bank she comes up to breathe.

A fish turns against her whiskers. She bends so quickly that her body makes a crescent around it.

She eats on a stone, holding the fish between her forefeet. The jaws are stronger than the small head suggests. There is a brief crushing sound, followed by careful chewing. A scrap slips into the current. She reaches after it, misses, and watches it pass beneath a leaf.

Later she will eat snails, a beetle caught at the waterline, and the soft interior of a fallen fruit. She is an opportunist within a country that offers many things in small amounts. Nothing here feeds her for long without requiring her to move.

There are three young waiting farther into the basin.

Their shelter is beneath the raised roots of a tree that leans over a warm seep. The chamber is above the flood line, though only just, and has two exits: one opening into a fern bed, the other dropping through tangled roots into water. She has lined it with fibrous bark and frond stalks stripped of their leaves. The material nearest the warm ground dries between rains. She pulls it into the sleeping hollow and pushes the damp bedding toward the entrance.

The young are nearly half her length. Their fur is softer, and each still has a broken band of cream-colored spots along its sides. One is broad-headed and cautious. One noses into every gap. The smallest has a pale forefoot and a habit of biting the tails of the others.

They hear her before they see her.

She makes a low sound with her mouth closed, hardly audible above the running water. The inquisitive one pushes out first. She touches its face with her nose, then catches it briefly by the loose skin of the neck when it tries to scramble over her.

They are old enough to follow her, but not old enough to choose a route.

At a warm crossing she enters first and turns back. The young crowd the bank, making thin impatient sounds. One steps onto a soft mat and sinks to the shoulder. She seizes it before it begins to struggle and draws it onto the root beside her.

For several breaths it stays pressed against her flank. Then it wriggles free and attempts the crossing again, this time using the root.

The marsh is full of hazards that do not look like hazards. A patch of green may float over deep mud. Clear water may scald. A crust strong enough in the morning may break after rain has washed out the sand beneath it. The young learn by following, by testing, and sometimes by getting hurt. Adults carry small records of these mistakes: pale scars under the fur, a split pad, a claw that grows crooked.

This female is missing the tip of her right ear. The edge has healed smooth. Whatever took it happened years ago.

Her range includes five feeding channels, two fruiting groves, a long shelf of dry stone, and the sheltered basin where she keeps her young. Another female lives beyond a bare crescent of sulfur gravel. They sometimes meet at the same cool tributary. Usually one waits while the other feeds. When food is scarce, they lift their heads, open their mouths, and give a rasping call that sounds surprisingly large among the reeds.

Males travel farther. A passing one leaves his scent beneath overhanging roots and rubs his shoulders on stones. She may inspect these places after he has gone, standing motionless with her nose close to the surface, her young climbing over her hindfeet.

Tonight no stranger has passed.

The rain has brought beetles down from the trees, and the family moves slowly through the flooded litter. The young pounce on drifting leaves. Once, all three crowd over something beneath the water and emerge carrying different ends of the same rotten twig.

Their mother leaves them to it. She has found a snail bed.

Above the plateau, the last light withdraws from the summits. The upper fronds remain visible, but the ground beneath them darkens. Warm mist begins to collect along the channels. It hides the animals’ feet, then their bellies, leaving four backs moving separately through the white.

They are more active now. The young run ahead along a fallen trunk and wait at its broken end. Their mother climbs beneath them, sniffing the air. A branch cracks somewhere in the grove. All four become still.

For a long time nothing follows.

Eventually she lowers her head, and the little ones move again.

At the rim, their channel approaches a shallow valley where the water gathers before going over the cliff. She does not take them that far. The banks grow steeper there, and after heavy rain the current can pull an animal out of its footing. Tonight she turns beside an old tree whose roots have entered the rock. One youngster continues downstream until her closed-mouth call brings it back.

By the time they return to the shelter, the plateau is dark. She stands at the entrance while the young squeeze past her. One turns around twice. Another pulls a strip of bark into the hollow. The smallest, overtired, bites her foreleg and is nudged firmly aside.

She settles last, curving her body around theirs.

Through the roots she can feel the steady warmth of the seep. The chamber smells of damp bark, fur, and the faint mineral odor brought in on their feet. Outside, water drips from the ferns. A bubble breaks beneath the floating mat at the lower entrance.

Her eyes remain open for a while. Each time one of the young shifts, her whiskers move against its back. Then they lie still, and she lowers her head onto her forepaws.
