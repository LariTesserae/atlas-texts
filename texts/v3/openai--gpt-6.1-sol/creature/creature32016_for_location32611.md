The people who live here are not especially quiet, although visitors often remember them that way. Voices do not carry well between the buildings. The pale walls take something out of a sound, and there are few hard surfaces to throw it back. A conversation across the square requires an effort. Usually one person walks toward the other.

Indoors, people speak at an ordinary volume. They argue over meals, call upstairs, sing badly while washing things. In winter, when the windows are shut and the walls have thickened, a household can make a considerable noise without anyone next door knowing.

Their hands are the first thing you notice at close quarters. Not large or strangely formed, but marked by tasks that are not quite those of carpenters or gardeners. Fine dark lines remain beside the nails. The palms have shallow scars from slips of the narrow blades used to reopen wiring grooves. People who work with cordage develop a smooth patch along the outside of one finger, where a moving strand passes under tension.

Most carry a small knife, including those whose work is clerical. It is used for cutting string, opening packages, and removing the pale shoots that sometimes appear inside cupboards. The blade is short, with a blunt tip. A pointed knife can pass through young bark into a water channel.

Children are taught this early, but they are not given knives early.

They begin with a flat wooden tool and a place where they are allowed to scrape. Usually it is a strip beside the back door. They learn the difference between a surface that can be pared and one that must be left alone. Under healthy bark the exposed tissue is faintly glossy. A dry, powdery patch means someone should be called.

There is considerable satisfaction in being old enough to say this with authority.

***

A woman named Sella makes the morning circuit of the collector trees.

She worked in the pressing works for twenty-three years, until lifting wet bundles began to trouble her shoulder. Now she carries a notebook, a little brass probe, and a folding stool with one leg shorter than the others. The stool was made for a particular sloping patch beside the western collector. Elsewhere she sets the short leg on a piece of packing wood.

Her work consists largely of waiting.

After mist, she sits near a trunk and listens to the tapping inside it. The first drops are distinct. Then there is a brief run of them, close enough to sound like something being shaken in a tin. A collector that empties all at once may have a constriction lower down. One that taps too long may be retaining water in a damaged fork.

Sella writes down times. She does not describe the trees as thirsty or tired. She finds those words unhelpful.

Still, she knows the western collector requires a little longer than the others before its stems relax, and she never leaves before it has finished.

People bring her information that may or may not matter: a damp floor, a bitter taste at a tap, a roof fan remaining folded after sunrise. She records almost all of it. Her notebooks occupy two shelves in a kitchen cupboard, wrapped against damp. The earliest volumes were kept by her predecessor. His handwriting slopes so severely that she must turn some pages sideways to read them.

She has a son who lives beyond the ridge, where houses are built of fired brick. When he visits, he brings her fruit and complains that his childhood room has become narrower.

It has. The outer wall was thickened after a cold winter. His mother has kept the bed in its old position, so there is scarcely room to pass its foot.

***

The town’s children are not as careful as its adults.

They bicycle over joints they have been warned about. They put their fingers into the small tunnels leading to old junction boxes. They discover that a pressed board, if left in a wet seam behind the school, may produce a leaf, and return every day to see whether it has done so.

The teachers confiscate such experiments but do not always destroy them.

In one classroom, a board stands in a tray near the window. It was stamped correctly and should have remained inert. A tiny branch has nevertheless emerged beneath the second mark. The teacher uses it when explaining that inspection is a record of what was observed, not a promise about everything that will happen afterward.

This distinction is less interesting to the children than the branch.

They also know places the maintenance crews do not. Behind the bicycle store, beneath the low joined ceiling, there is a gap where someone small can crawl between two roots. It leads nowhere useful. The ground inside is cool and smells stronger than the rest of the building. They go there to be out of sight.

A girl once left a marble in the gap. Over several years the roots closed around it, leaving only a blue crescent visible. She is grown now and works in the cordage factory. Occasionally, while fetching her bicycle, she bends to look.

***

Few people have a single occupation.

The man who maintains the weighing scale also repairs shoes. A factory worker may spend two mornings each month clearing the canal lining. The clerk who orders machine oil is one of the three people qualified to release pressure in a common rootstock.

There are specialists, but little work here can be separated entirely from other work. A badly repaired doorstep may divert water from a neighboring wall. A heavy cupboard placed against the wrong partition can change the way a room carries its roof.

Before moving a large piece of furniture, people generally ask someone to look.

This does not make them endlessly cooperative. There are old disputes about shade, drainage, and who neglected which section of a shared foundation. Some households prune too severely. Others let their roof leaves extend beyond the agreed boundary and insist the extra cover is harmless.

At municipal meetings, the same argument can recur for years with small changes in its vocabulary. Eventually a wall or a drain settles the matter in a way nobody wanted.

The municipal inspector has a broad face, thinning hair, and a reputation for staying too long after his work is finished. He accepts tea almost everywhere. He can remember which walls were injured in a storm forty years ago, but often forgets the name of the person currently standing in front of him.

When he certifies a batch of boards, he places the hollow-square stamp carefully away from the first mark. He likes both to remain legible.

His daughter says he stamps them as though they might someday be called as witnesses.

***

The house being lowered at the edge of town belonged most recently to two sisters.

One died. The other moved closer to the square, into two rooms where she no longer had to manage a staircase. She still visits the old house on the day the straps are tightened.

She does not supervise. She brings a flask and sits on an overturned crate until the workers stop.

The younger workers feel awkward about this. They straighten tools that do not need straightening and avoid discussing how much useful material remains in the roof. The foreman has known her for years. He tells her plainly what they intend to remove next.

Inside, there is a wall on which the sisters marked their heights as children. The lines were made on a strip of dead timber screwed into the living surface. The strip has been taken down and stored for her.

She was relieved when this was done. Until then, she had worried that asking for it would seem foolish.

She has requested currants for the raised bed. The water allocation has not yet been approved.

***

There are insects in the town, though fewer than its vegetation seems to promise.

The structural growth is dense and not easy to feed on. Much of it carries a sharp sap. Insects gather instead around pruning wounds, in stored fiber, and beneath the loose scales of old collector trunks.

A small pale moth breeds in the cordage works. Its larvae can ruin a spool from the inside while leaving the outer winding clean. The workers keep shallow traps under the combing beds. At the start of a shift, someone carries them outside and counts what has settled there.

Spiders live around the lamps and in the high wall slots where branches enter the factory. Their webs sometimes have to be cut before they draw loose fibers into the rollers. They rebuild at night.

In summer, tiny flies gather over the canal. The gray bird takes them when there is nothing larger to be had. It is not the same bird every year, although some residents insist that it is. One has a damaged foot. Another lacks the white line above the eye that people remember from childhood.

The canal keeper can distinguish them. He sees no reason to correct anyone.

***

The town has no general reverence for growth.

People complain about it. A door that fitted in autumn may scrape by spring. A pantry shelf becomes tilted. A cable disappears another fraction of an inch into its groove. Someone away for six months returns to find a bathroom window impossible to open.

Nor do they welcome every living thing. They pull weeds from the scale, treat moth-infested cordage, and kill the small burrowing animals when they cannot trap them. The waste heaps must remain sound enough to carry a person’s weight.

But a harmless shoot may be left beside a back step. Someone will keep a flowering weed in a pot, though it requires water and offers no useful material. There are kitchen windows with three or four such pots, each turned a little every morning.

A retired factory worker has persuaded his house to produce a single branch at shoulder height beside the door. He hangs his coat on it. The maintenance office has asked him twice to remove it, since it draws from a narrow section of wall.

He has cut it back twice. Each time he leaves enough for it to return.

***

At dusk, the residents become easier to see.

During the day their movements merge with the work of the town: carrying, inspecting, scraping, winding. In the evening they occupy doorways and windows. Someone sits on a bench without bringing a tool. A woman crosses the square with a covered dish held against her stomach. Two boys walk home slowly, pushing one bicycle between them because its rear tire has gone soft.

Lights show the thickness of the window recesses. In one kitchen, a man has opened a junction box and is holding a lamp while his wife reads the labels. In another, a child is being measured against the doorframe.

The mark will not remain at exactly that height. The adults know this. They make it anyway.

Later, beds creak, water moves below the floors, and a few sleepers wake when the factory machinery changes speed. Most turn over without looking at the clock. They have heard it happen nearly every night of their lives.

By morning, the streets will be yielding again. Sella will take her stool to the western collector. The woman who once left the marble under the bicycle store will ride past a swollen drain. At the edge of town, the old doorway will stand a little lower, still wide enough for its former owner to enter without bending.
