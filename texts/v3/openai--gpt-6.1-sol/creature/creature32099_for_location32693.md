The animal with the stone is easiest to find in the morning.

At that hour the rosettes are still holding their leaves above the ledge, and there is a little space beneath them. Look there, beside a root that resembles a length of wet thread. What appears to be a chip of limestone may be an animal waiting for its mouth to empty.

It has six legs, though the front pair seldom touch the ground. These are short and jointed, with broad tips that close against one another. The other four hold its body low over the rock. From above, it looks narrow; from the side, almost unfinished. Its belly is translucent. After it has fed, a soft brown line appears inside it, dividing gradually into smaller flecks.

There is no shell. A flexible plate covers its back, pale at the edges and faintly yellow toward the middle. When the animal presses itself down, this plate takes on the color of whatever lies beneath it. Against limestone it is cream. Against a root it looks like a thickening in the root. Over a wet seam, it becomes a small wet seam with legs.

Its eyes are not quite black. Each contains a dark crescent, turned inward, beneath a clear dome. It can see a stone close to its face with considerable precision. More distant things seem to reach it chiefly as changes in brightness.

A person entering the chamber is therefore not immediately a person to it. First, the ceiling dims. Then a vibration passes through the ledge. Then warm, moist air arrives from an unfamiliar direction.

The animal draws its forelimbs against its mouth and waits.

It can wait a long time without appearing distressed. Waiting is part of almost everything it does.

Its mouth is underneath its head, too small to see without magnification. Inside are combs of fine teeth. These gather the film that grows where a root keeps the limestone damp: minute cells, shed root matter, other life too small to have any visible shape. The film is richest in the shallow irregularities of the stone, where the mouth cannot quite reach.

This is what the grain is for.

The animal does not take the first one it encounters. It lifts a grain, turns it between its forelimbs, touches an edge to the rock. Sometimes it puts the grain down and tries another. A grain that is too round slides. One that is too thin breaks. The useful ones have a flat side for holding and a small rough corner.

Once it has found one, the animal braces its hind legs and draws the corner toward itself.

The scrape is less than a millimeter long.

It pauses, lowers its mouth, feeds, and scrapes again.

By the time the ceiling is fully bright, it may have cleared a patch scarcely larger than its own head. Its abdomen has become slightly fuller. One forelimb is wet where the loosened film has collected around the grain.

Then it drops the tool.

Occasionally another animal finds the grain before the first has gone far. It picks it up and tests it in the same manner. There is no resistance from the former holder, though if the second animal comes too close, the first may move its body between the intruder and the scraped place. Food can be defended. The stone, once released, is only stone.

There are perhaps forty of these grazers in the chamber, or perhaps twice that number. Several may occupy a single ledge without being visible together. A pale adult can lie along the underside of a leaf. A younger one may fit into a hollow where a root crosses the rock. Others travel the exposed cords, stopping wherever their feet encounter moisture.

Their feet have soft, divided pads. Each step leaves a mark that lasts only until the displaced dampness spreads back into place.

They do not make trails, but they remember routes.

An individual that has fed beneath one rosette tends to return to it. On its way, it touches certain ridges with a forelimb. Where a root forks, it may go a little way down the wrong branch, stop, and turn back. If a leaf has lowered across its usual passage, it waits beneath the leaf before attempting a detour.

The young are less patient. They climb surfaces that lead nowhere. They investigate dry grains. Sometimes they carry a grain farther than seems useful, laboring over a ridge with it held against their chest.

Most eventually abandon it. A few arrive at a damp place still carrying the stone and begin to scrape.

The smallest juveniles cannot lift tools at all. They feed along the margins of patches cleared by adults. An adult may move aside when one presses close to its mouth, though it does not always do so. The young animal then settles behind it and eats whatever remains.

When two adults meet, they touch forelimbs.

The contact is brief, usually one tip against another, followed by a pause. They may separate. They may remain facing each other while one slowly raises and lowers its head. Once in a while, both turn and proceed in the same direction, keeping far enough apart that their legs do not meet.

This is most often seen after rain.

Water alters the chamber on a scale the grazers cannot ignore. A shallow channel becomes a boundary. A low patch of stone becomes an island. The root that carried an animal yesterday may now end over open water.

They can survive immersion, but they dislike the surface. Water catches their limbs and draws them together. An animal trapped there bends its body upward until its back plate meets the air. It floats in that posture, making small, ineffective movements, until it reaches stone.

Some do not reach it.

After the pool withdraws, their bodies remain on the floor as nearly transparent husks. Fine growth enters the spaces between their plates. For a few mornings, the husks hold moisture better than the bare limestone around them, and the waterline animals gather there.

The grazers themselves seldom approach the pool. Their lives are concentrated around the three ledges, where dampness persists without becoming deep.

They lay their eggs beneath the rosette leaves.

Each egg is a flattened oval attached by a clear stalk. Seen against the leaf, it resembles a blister. The adult tests the place with its mouth before laying, then presses the egg into position with both forelimbs. It stays beside it for a while. Whether this is attendance or simply exhaustion is difficult to tell.

The egg darkens very slowly. Eventually a pair of tiny crescents appears inside. A few days later, the casing opens along one edge, and the young grazer steps out backward, pulling its last legs free one at a time.

For its first hour it remains beside the empty casing.

Above it, the leaf lowers as the chamber brightens. The young animal lowers itself too.

Its plate is still soft. Through it, the small workings of its body can be seen: a pulse, a contraction, something turning where the mouthparts join the head. By evening these have become harder to distinguish. The back has taken on its faint mineral color.

There are older grazers whose plates no longer match the stone so well. Their edges have thickened. A joint may carry a speck of dark material that grooming cannot remove. Some have lost a foot, and place the shortened limb carefully before each step.

They still use grains, though less neatly. A stone slips from their grip. They retrieve it. They rest halfway through a scrape.

One such animal may spend most of an afternoon beneath a single leaf, holding a grain without moving it. Then condensation reaches the root beside its head. A narrow line of moisture spreads toward its mouth.

It puts the grain down and feeds directly from the rock.

In the evening, as the light gathers into the last pale patches overhead, the grazers leave the exposed root cords. They move beneath leaves or settle into the shallow joins between root and stone. A few remain where they have been feeding, forelimbs folded, their bodies resting on four widely placed feet.

The chamber becomes dark enough that they can no longer be found by looking.

They are still there: one beside an egg, one beneath a leaf, one halfway across a ridge it will finish crossing in the morning. Near a clean patch of limestone, a grain lies with its rough corner upward.

When warmth returns through the ceiling, an animal will touch it, turn it once, and decide whether it is worth lifting.
