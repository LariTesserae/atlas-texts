For a long time, the hollow’s largest inhabitant looks like a place where the water is slightly deeper.

It lies beside a submerged root, broad at one end, narrowing toward the other. Silt has settled over its back. Three small plants grow there, their roots caught in a loose, pale covering that lifts a little whenever the animal breathes. Fish pass beneath its edges. Once, a bird lands on it and remains long enough to clean the narrow spaces between its toes.

Then a portion of the bank draws itself inward.

The movement takes perhaps half a minute. Mud wrinkles. One of the little plants tilts, rights itself, and is carried a hand’s breadth nearer the root. A rounded limb comes free of the bottom, bends at two joints, and rests on firmer ground.

There are other limbs. You find them gradually, by watching where the silt parts: four along either side, though the rear pair are small and usually folded beneath the body. Their ends divide into soft fingers. In water the fingers spread into fans; on land they gather around roots without quite gripping them. Each has a dark, blunt pad near its tip, worn smooth by use.

The animal lifts itself only high enough to let water pass underneath.

Its head is difficult to distinguish from its shoulders. It has no narrow neck, no conspicuous muzzle. Along the forward edge of the body are two shallow cups, set well apart. Something clear slides across them when a falling leaf touches the water. Beneath each cup is a patch of fine branching vessels, pale amber until the animal moves, then briefly red.

Those are its eyes.

They do not turn toward you together. One watches the pool; the other is angled toward the open ground beneath the shelter. Between them, a loose fold opens to reveal several short feelers. These test the underside of a floating leaf. A second opening, farther down, receives the leaf and closes over it.

You hear a faint rubbing sound.

The animal eats the softened parts first. After a while it releases a clean network of veins, which floats away with two small crustaceans still attached.

It is longer than the bench, though much of its length is a thin rear apron that trails over the mud. The apron has a scalloped edge and several old tears, all smoothly healed. Small creatures live in its folds. One carries a bright shell; another resembles a length of white stitching. When the animal moves, they withdraw into pockets along its sides. When it rests, they come out again.

Its upper surface is covered in overlapping plates so thin that they bend under a raindrop. You can see deeper layers through them: slow contractions, cloudy chambers, the faint movement of fluid. Near the center of its back is an opaque mass with a branching outline. It changes shape as the animal turns. You cannot tell which part of it is an organ and which part is the distorted image of something beneath.

Along either flank runs a row of little openings.

At first they appear to be injuries. Then, while the animal crosses a shallow pool, each opens in succession and releases a short stream of clear water. The body settles lower as they empty. On reaching the bank, it closes them and waits. Its skin becomes fuller, the limbs firmer. After several minutes it can carry itself over the roots.

It never travels straight across the hollow. Its route follows the same wandering margins as yours, with additional turns around ground you would have thought solid.

At one such place it stops and extends a forelimb. The fingers press into the vegetation. Beneath them the roots sink, allowing a small quantity of water to well up.

The animal withdraws its limb and goes another way.

You begin to recognize where it has been. There are shallow, paired depressions beside the pools, often filled with translucent eggs. Its broad underside has smoothed certain roots. The parallel grooves on the stone bowl match the spacing of the pads on its fingers, though that alone does not tell you how old the marks are.

It visits the bowl late in the day.

With one limb braced against the rim, it puts its mouth into the water and remains still. The tiny leaf-eater crawls across its face. A vessel beneath one eye darkens, then fades. When the animal withdraws, the bowl contains the same three leaves, shifted into a loose stack.

A little sediment is gone from the bottom.

This is how most of its feeding happens. It takes thin growth from stones, loose tissue from fallen wood, the cloudy material that gathers where a plant has died. Its mouth contains a soft rasp and a fringe that sorts whatever the rasp removes. Larger fragments are pushed aside. Living roots bend under the fringe and emerge unbroken.

Sometimes it finds a dead animal.

Then it stays until little remains beyond the hardest parts. Fish gather around its mouth, taking what escapes. Its sides flush with a slow, dull orange that is visible even through the silt on its back.

The birds show a similar color beneath their bills. One hops down to a low branch and answers with two brief patches of blue.

The large animal pauses.

A line of amber appears along its flank, moving from the forward limb to the rear. The bird smooths its feathers and turns away.

You cannot tell how much has been exchanged. Over the following days you see the same line appear when the animal approaches an occupied pool, when a grazer steps across its path, and once when it encounters a root that has grown through a passage it used before.

At the root, it waits longer.

Then it lowers its head and begins to clear the mud from underneath.

It does not seem to govern the hollow. The submerged disc continues its slow alterations whether the animal is nearby or asleep. Ground settles in places the animal has never touched. Warmth gathers under fallen branches.

But it notices the results early.

Before a bank sinks, it abandons a resting place there. Before a flush of new growth, it spends a morning feeding among the old stalks. When the disc’s marks shift beneath their covering of silt, the animal sometimes lays one soft finger against an exposed portion of its edge.

It may be feeling heat. There are fine sensory pits on the pads of its fingers, and it also touches stones, drains, and the joined trunks of trees.

The disc gives no visible response.

The shelter interests it more.

On dry afternoons it settles beside one of the supports, with most of its body still in the water. When droplets gather beneath the roof, it raises the forward edge of its head. The clear membranes draw across its eyes.

The droplets rise.

For a moment the animal’s branching vessels become visible all at once, a delicate amber pattern reaching down its sides. Then they pale again, and it lowers its head.

It does this even when no one is sitting on the bench.

During rain, smaller animals resembling it emerge from the ground.

They are not exact copies. Some are nearly round, with limbs too short to lift them clear of the mud. Others have long fringes around their mouths. The smallest are transparent except for their eyes and a dark loop inside the body. They travel toward the stone rim, stopping frequently to press their faces into the fresh runoff.

The large animal comes among them slowly.

Several climb onto its trailing apron. One disappears into a fold along its flank. Another is pushed away with the side of a finger after trying to enter an opening that is already occupied.

There is room for perhaps a dozen.

They stay there after the rain, visible as small bulges beneath the flexible plates. Over the next few days the bulges move toward the rear of the body. The large animal eats more often during this time and rests in warmer water.

Eventually the young leave.

They have changed. Their fringes are shorter, their limbs longer, and their backs carry the beginnings of the same overlapping covering. One remains on the apron for an additional day, repeatedly getting off and climbing back on. The adult waits beside a root until it finally goes.

Whether the young are its offspring, or simply find shelter on any adult they encounter, takes longer to learn.

There must be others somewhere. Yet the hollow’s resident has familiar damage: a missing pad on its third left limb, a pale healed notch behind one eye, those three plants on its back. You see it at the far rim in the morning and beside the shelter in the evening. It occupies the place thoroughly without occupying much of it at once.

On the day it first comes close to you, it has lost one of the plants.

The remaining two carry pale flowers.

You are sitting with your feet on the dry ground beneath the bench. It approaches through the shallows and stops just outside the roof’s shade. One eye faces you. The other looks down at the small runnel between its body and the support.

A forelimb lifts.

The fingers open, showing their worn pads. They remain suspended near your ankle, moving separately in the warm air.

When you hold out your hand, two of them rest against your palm.

Their surface is firm and finely ridged. You feel a slow pressure, then a slight cooling as moisture spreads over your skin. The amber line passes along its flank.

It releases you and reaches beneath the bench.

There is a fallen seed there, swollen with water and beginning to split. The animal draws it out, examines it with its mouth fringe, and carries it to the edge of the pool. With three movements it presses the seed into the mud above the waterline.

Then it settles nearby.

Its limbs fold. Silt begins to gather on the trailing apron. A narrow fish noses beneath its side, and the small creatures in its seams come out.

By evening you can scarcely distinguish its outline. The two flowering plants remain visible above the water. Beneath them, one clear eye is still open, facing the bench.
