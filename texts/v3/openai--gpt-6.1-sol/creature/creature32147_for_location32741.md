The people who live here are not especially small. This is one of the first things that surprises a visitor. The stair suggests a town of little bodies, something fitted between the roots, but the woman waiting at its foot is broad across the shoulders and has to turn sideways through her own kitchen door. The door has been encouraged to widen for eleven years. It is gaining on her slowly.

She carries a hooked knife, a lunch tin, and a pale moth on the upper sleeve of her coat. The moth is not hers. She has noticed it and judged it not worth removing.

Her name is Dessa. She tends the return channels beneath the mill, where sap that has passed through the machinery is cooled, filtered, and admitted to the living floor again. It is skilled work, although visitors sometimes mistake her for a cleaner. She can identify six kinds of blockage by the vibration of a pipe. She cannot identify all the creatures she encounters in it.

“Something with elbows,” she says of the animal that has been troubling the third return.

She has seen it twice. She has not seen its head.

***

There are forty-three people in the town this season, including a baby whose arrival has not yet been entered in the workshop ledger. The ledger records births because bodies alter the demands made on the chamber: water, food, warmth, door widths, the number of sleeping recesses that must be kept dry. It records deaths for the same reason. It is not the book in which either event is celebrated.

Several inhabitants belong to households elsewhere and stay only for the cutting season. A young man from an upstream settlement sleeps in the public shed beside stacked fiber. Two sisters have come to learn coil repair. An old pump specialist arrived for a fortnight eighteen years ago and still speaks of going home when the seals are sound.

People born here are called root-born, but the term carries no great dignity. It mostly explains what they fail to notice.

A root-born person can sleep through the mill starting. The unexpected absence of the mill wakes them.

They can hear a spoken sentence through a sound that makes a visitor flinch, but they sometimes lose words entirely when standing under an unfamiliar ceiling. Out in the open air, with nothing returning the voice to them, they ask one another to speak up.

Their own voices tend to be low and forceful. They do not shout often. Shouting wastes the part of a word that carries.

When the deep note comes through the floor, a conversation stops without apology. People keep their mouths shaped around the next syllable. A quarrel can pause this way. Two furious people stand facing each other while the chamber trembles, waiting for permission to resume.

***

Their hands are the most conspicuous thing about them.

Almost everyone has a dark, polished place on the heel of one palm from leaning against warm bark. The mill workers have pale crescents across their knuckles where fiber has whipped them. The graft tenders keep one thumbnail long and sharpened, for lifting a flap of rind without bruising the tissue beneath it. Those who work with copper have very clean fingertips. They scrub before touching a winding and again afterward, recovering the wash water through a fine cloth.

Children inspect adult hands with frank interest. Work leaves readable marks. A child can often tell which room a stranger works in before learning their name.

Other marks are less useful. A woman with three missing fingers makes the best roof grafts in the town. A man whose hands look almost untouched has spent thirty years measuring the growth of load-bearing roots. He says this is because he uses instruments correctly. His wife says it is because someone else always carries them.

Clothing is close-fitting at the wrist and ankle, without much loose ornament. Pockets close with overlapping flaps. A dangling ribbon will eventually acquire passengers.

People decorate what can be wiped clean: belt clasps, combs, the flat handles of tools. There is a fashion for scratches so fine they are invisible until grease settles into them. Dessa’s lunch tin bears a picture of a horse. She has never seen a horse, and the person who made the picture had apparently never seen one either. Its knees bend in too many directions. She is fond of it.

***

Children are taught where to put their feet before they are taught the names of the things they must not step on.

At first an adult carries them across the square, pointing down.

“White ones. Wait.”

Then the child is set on the ground and lifted again whenever the streams grow complicated. Eventually they learn to cross alone, with the absorbed, slightly staggering gait of someone following several thoughts at once.

They still kill things. There is no way not to. A shoe comes down, a hand closes carelessly, a bucket is set on the wrong ledge. The response depends on what has died. Nobody holds a funeral for a valve-cleaner. Someone may, however, make the child spend an afternoon picking residue out of a valve to understand what the insect had been doing.

There are also beloved animals whose usefulness is doubtful.

One dwelling contains a lizard called Hook, recognizable by an old kink near the end of its tail. Hook is not tame. It will bite anyone who tries to handle it and has eaten three broods of the lamp insects. Nevertheless, the family keeps a dry ledge for it near their stove. When it disappears, they accuse one another of leaving the door open.

A boy has named nineteen of the beetles near the mill. His older sister insists that at least seven of them are the same beetle. Neither can prove anything.

The hanging mammals in the rafters are treated with more reserve. They foul the beams, quarrel loudly, and sometimes descend through an open window. They also consume night insects that would otherwise strip the tender growth around the vents. People put trays beneath their roosts and complain while emptying them.

A household can be proud of the animals it tolerates and embarrassed by the animals it attracts.

***

Much of the inhabitants’ knowledge concerns distinctions a stranger cannot see.

The brown swarm below one window should remain undisturbed. The nearly identical swarm below the next must be removed before nightfall. One is eating wall-borer eggs; the other is laying them. A glossy patch in a doorway may be healthy new growth, or the beginning of a rot that will travel behind the bark for weeks before showing itself again.

They disagree about these things.

They disagree especially when a diagnosis requires cutting into a house.

A dwelling is warm tissue, but it is also where someone keeps their good cups, where a child has finally begun sleeping through the night, where an old person knows the distance from bed to latch without light. The workshop cannot simply pare it into the correct shape. Its workers must visit, explain, return with another opinion, and sometimes argue in the doorway while the troublesome swelling continues to enlarge.

One household has refused an alteration for so long that the street outside now rises in a noticeable hump. Their neighbors make remarks when carrying full buckets past it.

The town has rules about circulation, and it has people who resent the rules. These are sometimes the same people.

***

The copper keeper is named Elian. He is not the richest inhabitant, though outsiders assume he must be.

He works in a recess with a bright lamp and a shallow white tray. His shoulders have settled into the posture required by his lens. When he straightens, he does so carefully, one hand at the back of his neck.

Copper passes through his hands but belongs to the town. So do the oldest coils, the scraps in the locked drawer, and the tiny reddish grains recovered from the workshop sweepings.

He knows the histories of particular lengths. One strand came from a motor dismantled before his grandmother was born. Another was brought down the tree stair in a trader’s mouth after the trader lost the rest of the load in a flood.

People tease him for knowing these stories. They listen when he tells them.

He has twice refused material for a new lamp in the public shed. He approved a thinner winding for the pump that serves the upper sleeping rooms, then lay awake for three nights wondering whether he had allowed too little.

His daughter wants to leave.

This has nothing to do with copper, or very little. She wants a room with a window through which she can see weather arriving. She wants to walk without considering what is beneath her shoes. She has been offered work in a surface orchard, and everyone has advice about it.

Elian does not tell her she owes the town her skill. Instead, he keeps finding tasks he needs to show her before she goes.

She recognizes what he is doing. She learns them anyway.

***

Meals happen in the gaps between jobs, except on the rest evening, when tables are brought into the public shed.

They eat surface grain, pale roots cultivated in side chambers, oil-rich cakes, preserved fruit when trade has been good. The cistern fish are caught in fine baskets and fried whole. Certain insects are raised for food in screened boxes; this does not mean people willingly eat whatever falls into a bowl.

At table, they cover dishes between servings. A hand rests over a cup during a conversation. Food intended for someone who is late is put under an upturned tin.

Hospitality is measured partly by how often the host uncovers something.

There are songs, but most are sung by one person at a time. Sustained harmony is difficult against the machinery. The singer waits for a favorable rhythm and fits the opening line between two knocks. If the pump changes speed, a good singer adjusts; a bad one blames the pump.

Their funniest stories often depend on sounds. Visitors hear an imitation of a faulty bearing and do not understand why everyone is laughing.

***

Old people sit near places where the floor carries vibration clearly. Many are hard of hearing. They receive news through their feet before anyone comes to tell them.

One woman can distinguish the ordinary mill load from the heavier cut used for export fiber, though she has not entered the mill in nine years. If the heavier cut continues too late, she sends a grandchild to ask whether the crew intends to eat.

They have no expectation that the town will stop changing after they die. This does not make them indifferent to change while they are alive.

They object to relocated latches. They miss a particular bench after its supporting root is diverted. They describe corners that no longer exist and give directions by grafts removed decades ago.

The stair has replaced all its substance many times, but a woman ascending it still puts her hand where her father put his when he carried her home.

At dusk, when the moths lift, people pause under doorways or turn their faces aside. A few shut their eyes. The children who are still excited by it reach up and are told not to.

Then someone remembers a tool left at the mill. Someone calls across the square to ask for oil. Dessa comes out of the return gallery holding a torn filter sleeve, with a damp patch spreading down her coat.

She stops beneath a lamp to examine what has caught in the weave.

There are bits of fiber, a seed casing, the transparent husk of something long-legged. She separates them with her thumbnail. Behind her, through the open door of her house, a pot begins to boil.

She calls for someone to move it.

Nobody answers at first. The floor is sounding its deep note. She waits, the filter dripping between her hands, and calls again.
