The doorway cutter has six walking legs, two shorter limbs held against its mouth, and a shell so thin that a full stomach changes the shape of its back. In warmth, the contents show through as a brown crescent. In cold, they gather into a dark knot.

Its eyes are present but small. They distinguish the passing blue at the harbor from the darkness afterward, and very little else. Most of what it knows arrives through the feet.

A sound conducted through air may go unnoticed. The same sound carried through the living floor—a thorn dropped, jaws closing on a root, the scrape of a body turning in a narrow room—can bring it upright at once. When it is working, it keeps five feet planted and uses the sixth to test the wall. It cuts, waits, tests again. The pressure changes before the tissue visibly yields.

Its jaws are unequal. One is broad and notched; the other ends in a fine inward hook. Between them they can peel a shaving without severing it, leaving a curled strip attached to the wall. The cutter does this above a new doorway. Later, anyone finding the curl can feel where the entrance begins.

There are cutters whose curls turn the other way. There are old doorways that bear the work of an animal dead before the adjoining house was built.

This cutter is not old. Its shell has hardened only twice. A seam along one side remains tender, and it avoids passing that side against thorn pegs. It lives uphill from the market in a fold shared with three other adults, a clutch of eggs, and an animal no one invited.

The uninvited animal is flat, soft, and almost exactly the texture of the floor. It feeds on what the household leaves behind. At first they tried to lift it out with a husk flake. It tore, and both pieces moved away. Now they sweep scraps toward one corner and keep the eggs elsewhere.

During the coldest intervals it comes beneath the sleeping adults. They tolerate the faint pressure. There is less dampness under them when it is there.

***

The inhabitants do not all have the same kind of face.

Some meet another animal with antennae. Some touch mouthparts. The broad-backed grazers have a fringe around the whole front of the body, and an encounter with one feels like brushing against a wet, moving comb. A carrier greeting a grazer must hold still while the fringe passes over its feet. To withdraw too quickly is discourteous; to remain too long can result in the grazer beginning to eat the film from its shell.

Among their own kind, the carriers recognize individuals by a mixture of scent, gait, and the little irregularities of the body. A damaged foot is a name that can be felt from a distance. So is a particular hesitation before climbing a step.

The doorway cutter knows one market seller by the way its rear legs land together.

It knows a raft owner by a hollow knocking that passes from shell to deck to water to shore.

It knows one of the animals in its own household by the absence of a footfall. That animal lost a leg while freeing a drainage cord from beneath a drop. The missing beat has become so familiar that, when a visitor with six legs sleeps beside the cutter, it wakes repeatedly.

They have signs for individuals, but the signs are used mostly when someone is absent. In company, a body identifies itself.

***

The pale mites carry their eggs beneath them in clusters bound with secretion. They walk high when the eggs are new and lower as the young develop. Near hatching, they move with a deliberate rocking gait that gives every egg its turn against the warmer floor.

A mite carrying a clutch has difficulty crossing the pinched steps. It approaches sideways, hooks the front legs over the ridge, and waits for someone above to offer a fiber.

This help is usually given. Not invariably.

The young are not cared for in the same way by every species, and the town’s arrangements have grown around the resulting inconveniences. Some hatch ready to eat what the adults eat. Some must spend their first interval in water. Others emerge with mouths suited only to one soft layer of the bud, a layer that builders also prize.

A nursery can therefore be a chamber, a submerged basket, or a shallow patch of wall kept freshly scraped. What marks it as a nursery is the cord outside: a loose knot with several short ends. Passing animals slow there. Heavy loads are set down rather than dragged.

The mites themselves scarcely sleep before a hatching. They clean the eggs by touch, rolling them against the mouth one at a time. An egg that no longer yields under pressure is removed.

The removed eggs go to the market.

There is no common rule about this. Some carriers refuse to handle them. The flat grazers accept them without distinction, alive or dead, and so are kept away from the nursery thresholds. The mites trading an unsuccessful egg often remain beside it until the exchange is complete, resting a foreleg on the shell.

***

The long animals of the upper streets are patient with slopes and impatient with interruptions.

Each has more feet than it ordinarily uses. Several can hang free while the others hold the wall, allowing the animal to turn a length of root in its jaws without shifting position. Their bodies travel in waves. When one starts walking, the movement takes time to reach the tail.

They make good surveyors. A root measure can be laid beneath the body, held at many points, and drawn taut without being stretched. They remember dimensions partly as postures: how tightly they had to curve in a room, how many pairs of legs stood between two fixed thorns.

One surveyor returns often to the abandoned upper ward.

It goes there without a measure. In an empty house it settles into the place where the wall once supported both sides of its body. The room is wider now. Only one side touches.

After a while it leaves.

Its visits have worn a faint trail through the growth gathering on the floor. Smaller animals follow that trail, feeding along it, and the surveyor brushes them off before descending to the occupied streets.

***

Almost everyone in town is being eaten slowly by something that lives on them.

Most do not notice. A small burden of grazers can be useful, especially beneath a shell edge that the owner cannot reach. Too many make the joint itch. The carriers scrape such places against twisted fibers fixed outside the bath platforms.

The cutter’s household keeps a cleaning animal in a seed husk. It has a narrow head and a body swollen with stored food. It is allowed to climb over them during warm intervals, picking at the joints.

It must be watched around the eggs.

Once, while cleaning the cutter, it found the tender seam in the new shell and began to feed there. The cutter flung it against the wall. For several intervals afterward, it would not let the animal approach that side.

Now it does.

Under one market seller’s shell lives a cluster of smaller animals that emerge whenever the seller eats. They come to the mouth in a pale crowd and take fragments as the jaws work. The seller has learned to pass fine goods with its legs rather than carry them between the mouthparts.

Occasionally a customer receives one of the little bodies with a purchase.

The seller takes it back if it is still moving.

***

Citizenship is easiest to recognize at a cord.

An animal that follows the knots, stops at a warning, or leaves room for another body to pass is generally treated as belonging to the town. This includes several creatures whose origins no one knows.

One thin hunter lives beneath a market platform. It takes small animals from the drainage grooves and rarely troubles anyone larger. On crowded intervals it withdraws when tapped with a reed. The platform keeper considers it a resident.

The nursery keeper does not.

The hunter has no apparent understanding of their disagreement. It knows the reed tap. It knows the pressure of feet overhead, the arrival of scraps, the movement of prey beneath wet fibers. Once, during a flood, it climbed onto the platform among the traders and stayed there until the water fell. No one drove it off. No one touched it.

The animals hanging above the cistern are harder to accommodate. Their threads cross routes that cannot easily be moved. The town cuts some threads and leaves others. A hanging hunter will sometimes lower itself toward the cut end, gathering and testing, while a worker waits beneath with raised jaws.

There have been injuries on both sides.

***

The animal in the ceiling is known most intimately by a gutter worker with a scar across its back.

The scar is not from the claw. It came from a thorn driven into the worker by the rush of others escaping it.

The worker can distinguish several movements overhead. A slow pressure means the concealed body is turning deeper into the fold. A dry series of scratches means the hooked limb is coming down. There is also a faint tremor that has never been followed by an attack.

When that tremor passes through the ceiling, the worker continues cutting.

Others have begun to do the same.

The great animal sheds pieces of itself. A curved flake once fell beyond the thorn posts and lay there through several cold intervals. Eventually the worker drew it out with a hooked root.

Its inner surface was smooth. Its outer surface bore minute grooves filled with smaller living things.

The flake became a roof over a repair bench. Working beneath it, the gutter worker can feel the concealed animal moving through the wall, and the much smaller feet moving through the material overhead.

***

There are inhabitants who have begun preparing to leave.

A raft owner stores dry root in the highest part of its house. A mite has divided its clutch between this bud and the neighboring one. The surveyors keep separate records now: measurements of the present streets, measurements of passages that might serve after the first scales part.

The doorway cutter has made no store.

It has been asked twice to cross outside and inspect a wall in the neighboring bud. Both times the branch was cold enough for safe travel. Both times it went as far as the harbor comb.

Beyond the fibers, water pulled at its feet. There was no floor nearby carrying the movements of other animals. It could feel the branch overhead through the roots, but could not tell how far along it the next entrance lay.

It turned back.

Since then it has made a small cord from household to harbor. The knots are close enough that it can keep one against its belly while reaching for the next. It follows the cord during quiet intervals and remains at the entrance a little longer each time.

Inside, its work continues. A doorway narrows. A nursery needs an opening wide enough for a clutch. Two households want a passage between their rooms, though the wall is thickening faster than the cutter can remove it.

It braces five feet against the floor and sets the sixth to the tissue.

Behind the wall, sap moves. Behind that, something has begun to separate along a line too fine for the jaws to find.

The cutter waits with its foot against the place. Then it shifts a little to one side and begins the doorway there.
