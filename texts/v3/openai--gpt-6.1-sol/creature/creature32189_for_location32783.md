The people who live here are recognizable first by their shoes.

Visitors wear thick soles, assuming that more material must mean more protection. Residents wear shoes with a gap beneath the foot: a narrow construction of ceramic ribs, replaceable pads, and channels open at the sides. The soles make a hollow clacking sound on stairs. A person can put on a good coat, mend every visible tear, scrub the mineral dust from their cuffs, and still betray a bad season by the uneven sound of worn shoes.

Children are taught to notice this sound before they are taught to read a pressure gauge. If someone walking ahead begins to limp, you do not follow them onto the same paving.

Most residents carry a little water, even when they are going no farther than the next courtyard. Their flasks have two mouths, one for filling and one for drinking, so that the large opening need never be exposed outside. A household’s vessels are as familiar as its faces. There is the dented flask that belongs to the grandfather, the squat one for errands, the narrow one that fits beside a lunch tin. People recognize stolen water by its container more often than by any mark of ownership.

Theft still happens. So does lending. Someone who borrows a mouthful may return it a week later, poured carefully across a kitchen table while both parties watch.

They are not uniformly small, solemn, or spare. There are broad people who sweat through their shirts, women with loud voices, men who expend a scandalous amount of breath on arguments. There are children who race across bridges despite being forbidden to race across bridges, and old people who claim the air was thicker when they were young. But almost everyone has learned to interrupt an action without embarrassment. A sentence can wait while its speaker breathes. A load can rest halfway up a stair. Nobody mistakes stopping for finishing.

Visitors sometimes find this patience reassuring. They mistake it for temperament.

It is training.

***

In the eastern condenser district lives a woman named Sella, who has spent thirty-one years maintaining the same bank of cooling shutters.

Her work begins before sunrise. She climbs to the roof with a wrench, a folded cloth, and a small instrument whose needle moves when she presses its flat end against metal. The roof has forty-eight shutters. She touches each one, checks the hinge, clears the collecting lip, and records the temperature in a book protected by glazed boards.

There is little to see from close by. Her job consists largely of deciding whether an almost invisible film is water, oil, or the beginning of a deposit.

She knows which houses cook early because grease reaches the third shutter from the south. She knows when a particular family has guests because the fifth gutter yields more water overnight. She knows that a tenant below the western edge is washing cloth at a time when he claims his rooms are empty.

She does not report all these things.

The city has inspectors enough.

During fog, Sella becomes brusque. She sends children down from the roof, orders a neighboring shop to close its exhaust, and strikes a bell if anyone has left a warm cover open beneath her shutters. The weather may have crossed a hundred miles to arrive, but the last few feet are her responsibility.

Afterward she measures the catch. A good morning goes into the district tank. A poor one is discussed at length by people who did not get out of bed.

At home she keeps a plant made of hammered copper.

It belonged to her mother. Six leaves rise from a black pot, with a seventh that broke off and was repaired badly enough to remain recognizable. Sella wipes it twice a year. Her granddaughter thinks it is an embarrassing possession and has asked for an actual plant, something that smells alive.

There is room for one, perhaps, beside the sleeping alcove. The difficulty is the water. The child has produced several plans for finding it, all involving somebody else’s ration.

***

Families here often occupy the same address for longer than they occupy the same rooms.

A person may point to a low vent and say that it was where their sister used to look out while waiting for the food lift. Below that is a buried doorway. Below the doorway, another generation’s kitchen.

When a room is about to be lost, its contents come upstairs in an order that reveals more than anyone intends. Beds move early. So do tools and water vessels. Broken chairs remain surprisingly long. A heavy cupboard may be emptied and then argued over for months, while the ceiling descends toward its cornice.

The arguments are practical, but they are also about whose life gets carried.

A grandmother insists on saving a table too wide for the new stair. Her son proposes cutting it in half. His wife says the upper floor has no place for it. The grandmother answers that they have not tried.

Eventually the table is lifted out through a window, hangs for an hour over an astonishing depth of air, and enters the house again two stories higher. For the next twenty years everyone complains about it.

Some things are deliberately left below. A worn-out grinding wheel. A stove that would cost more to dismantle than replace. The chalk marks showing children’s heights, drawn on a wall that is becoming difficult to reach.

Before the last stair is removed, people sometimes go down alone.

They do not all have the same reason.

***

The largest profession is not pumping, but adjustment.

There are people whose entire working lives are spent shortening pipes, raising thresholds, moving brackets, enlarging holes, and persuading one structure to remain connected to another structure that has quietly ceased to be where it was yesterday.

A skilled stair fitter can tell from the polish on a tread whether its rise has become dangerous. A water-line mechanic reads pressure partly through her palm. Surveyors develop an irritating habit of staring past a person’s shoulder at corners and then asking whether the door has always shut that way.

Good workers are much in demand. Bad ones also find employment, because something is always changing and not every customer can wait.

The wealthy buy room to accommodate change. Their houses have deep adjustment spaces beneath the floors, spare lengths of pipe, columns that can travel farther before replacement. A rich household may go several years without moving its kitchen.

In poorer rooms, a rise in the ground is felt sooner. The stove is shimmed. The door is planed. The bed develops a tilt that someone tries to cure with a folded coat. Repairs take place around meals, sleeping children, and possessions that cannot be put anywhere else.

The unfinished shelf attracts people who want a new address. Young couples visit it in the evening and stand among the anchored bolts, imagining walls. Contractors show them drawings. From there the lower city looks crowded, old, and avoidable.

A few years later, they have their own inaccessible screw beneath the sink, their own crack that produces hot dust, their own landlord’s explanation of why the survey marks are probably wrong.

***

There are disagreements about the mountain.

Some workers consider it badly managed material. They believe sufficiently good valves, proper galleries, and honest reporting could make it behave.

Others have seen a pressure surge stop at a wall too weak to stop it, or a ridge form where the plans required an empty passage. They speak of these events reluctantly. They do not necessarily think the mountain is alive. They are less certain that *alive* is a useful distinction.

There are small observances at the works. On certain crews, the first stroke after a shutdown is watched in silence. Elsewhere, a scrap of bread is left on the pump housing and retrieved before it burns. One foreman allows neither practice, but always knocks twice on a particular plate before opening the main feed.

Most people have opinions about these customs without observing them consistently.

They are more unanimous about deep knocks.

When one passes through the city, a cook removes a pan from the stove. A lift attendant rests his hand on the brake. A woman stops combing her daughter’s hair, leaving the comb held midway down a braid.

They wait.

The second knock has occurred often enough that nearly everybody knows someone who was alive before one and not afterward.

When nothing follows, the cook puts the pan back. The lift resumes. The woman tugs too hard with the comb, and her daughter complains.

***

Children grow up with an odd distribution of knowledge.

They can name five conditions of newly set stone and may never have touched mud. They understand that a doorframe needs clearance but are surprised to learn that a tree becomes wider every year. They see clouds below them more often than rain falling from above.

At the produce hall, they are given leaves that have bruised too badly to sell. Some eat them immediately. Others carry them home to smell again later.

Valley children, arriving with a lift family, are valuable sources of information and unreliable ones. They describe ponds with animals in them, ground soft enough to push a finger into, houses standing directly on earth. A resident child may believe the pond and reject the house.

The river provides a game with rules that depend on how long the water lasts. A bit of bark, brought up in a timber crate, can be launched at one bridge and retrieved at another. Children run ahead to lift it past the measuring wheels.

The game becomes less friendly near the northern works.

Adults dislike finding bark in the tank.

***

The tank lice live on what people lose.

Water films behind bolts. Grease under a fitting. A skin flake caught against the edge of a housing. Their flat bodies allow them to enter spaces too narrow for a cleaning brush, and their pale legs move with a precision that makes some residents dislike them more than larger pests.

They are difficult to keep as pets. Children try, putting one in a tin with a screw, a crumb, and a drop of water. Usually it disappears. Sometimes it survives until it develops wings, at which point it becomes more frightening than beloved.

The birds are less intimate. Each has a circuit of outlets, parapets, and food-lift landings. Workers recognize them by damaged feathers and the pattern of soot on their heads.

One bird near Sella’s shutters has learned to strike the metal with its beak when the condensers are opened. Tank lice retreat from the vibration; it catches them as they emerge along the seams. Sella calls it a nuisance but leaves one housing uncovered a little longer than necessary.

No one knows where all the birds nest.

Eggs have been found in the insulated elbows of abandoned pipes. A fledgling once fell through the grating of the market and was recovered three terraces below, furious and unhurt.

For several days everyone knew about it.

***

In the evening the residents move toward the roofs, but not all at once. Work runs late. Someone has to mind a pump. Someone has promised to mend a shoe before morning. A child refuses to put on a coat because the floor is still hot and cannot understand why this is irrelevant.

On the balconies, people talk about shortages, weddings, an insulting price for onions. They lean where the rail is safe. Some look out at the older ranges. Others face inward, watching a neighbor hang laundry or trying to catch someone’s attention across a courtyard.

Sella brings her granddaughter upstairs with two stools.

The child carries the copper plant. She has decided it needs evening air, though she knows perfectly well that this is not true. Its leaves catch the remaining light. Below them, a cooling shutter closes with a small metallic complaint.

Their building has risen seven inches in the child’s lifetime. She knows this because she has measured it, and because every adult in the house tells her not to turn the adjustment screws.

She sets the plant beside the rail, moves it away from the edge, and asks whether the new floor will have a room of her own.

Sella says they will see.

Then someone on the opposite balcony calls to ask whether tomorrow looks like fog, and Sella gets up to look east.
