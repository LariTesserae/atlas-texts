At the edge of one pool, the mud has been pressed down in a shape that is not quite a footprint. There are toes, but their ends have dragged. Between them runs a shallow groove, interrupted where the ground becomes firmer. A few reed stems have been bent without breaking. One bears a smear of mud higher than a crab could reach.

The animal that made these marks is lying under the bank.

There is more room beneath it than the opening suggests. Water has removed clay from behind the roots, leaving a low chamber with a ceiling held together by fine, living fibers. The floor slopes upward at the back. Even when the creek rises, a portion remains above water. Here the animal lies with its belly against the clay, its limbs folded loosely beside it and its tail following the curve of the wall.

It is a large monitor lizard. In the chamber, only its head and the front of one shoulder can be seen. The rest is discernible as a thickness in the dark.

Its skin carries pale markings, clearer on the flanks than across the back. Mud obscures some of them. Others have become difficult to distinguish among scars and uneven scales. Near the end of its tail is an old injury, healed with a slight bend. When it walks over soft ground, that bend gives the dragged line a small, recurring hook.

The lizard has several resting places. This one is useful during dry weather because the clay remains cool enough to take heat from its body. Farther inland, there is a hollow beneath a fallen palm where it can shelter above the reach of the tide. It has also slept inside the lower portion of a broken tree, climbing into the cavity while the wood was warm and coming out the next morning through an opening on the other side.

It does not use all these places equally. A chamber can become flooded, a hollow occupied, an entrance narrowed by a fall of soil. For several nights it may return to the same bank. Then its tracks cease there and appear along another reach of creek.

Now it raises its head.

The movement is small. One eye catches what little light reaches beneath the bank. Its tongue comes out, touches nothing, withdraws. After a pause it comes out again.

Outside, a crab is feeding among the sedge roots. The lizard remains where it is until the crab moves nearer the water. Then its forefeet come forward, claws spreading against the slope. Its chest passes through the opening. For a moment the belly catches on a root, and it pushes with its hind legs.

Once clear, it stands still.

In open ground its size would be apparent: the long body, the heavy base of the tail, the neck capable of lifting the head well above the litter. Here these parts are separated by vegetation. A shoulder shows between stems. The head disappears behind a clump of sedges. The tail is still leaving the hole when the mouth closes over the crab.

There is a brief crack.

The lizard shifts its grip, swallows, and lowers its head again.

It eats what it can catch or uncover: crabs, eggs, frogs, fish stranded as pools shrink, animals already dead. Sometimes it spends a long time investigating a place from which nothing emerges. It pushes its snout beneath palm litter, backs away, approaches from another side. A rat can retreat farther into a tangle than the lizard can follow. A frog can escape in one jump while the lizard is still turning its head.

Its claws are useful for opening softer ground. At the base of a rotten trunk, it tears through wood that gives way in damp handfuls. Beetles scatter. Termites disappear into galleries newly exposed to the air. It feeds for a while, then leaves the excavation behind. By morning ants will be moving through it.

The broken end of its tail is not its only injury. One forefoot has a shortened outer toe. Along the neck, several scales have grown back smaller than those around them. These injuries do not prevent it from swimming. In water the legs draw close to the body, and the tail drives it forward with broad strokes. It can pass beneath an overhanging mat of roots with scarcely any disturbance above it.

During the driest weeks, its movements become concentrated around the remaining water. It encounters the same undercut banks, the same fallen branches, the same narrow gaps. At one crossing it climbs over a root polished by repeated contact. At another it enters the creek because the bank above has become too crowded with stems.

The hum from beyond the basin reaches these places too. The lizard sometimes lifts its head when the sound changes, but often it continues feeding. In an outer channel, where the water stays slightly warmer, fish collect near a shallow inflow. It visits that channel more frequently now. Some nights it catches nothing there. Other nights it leaves with its throat distended and returns to the clay chamber before the birds begin to wake.

Toward dawn it climbs onto the fallen trunk above the pool.

The upper wood is dry and firm enough to hold its weight. It settles lengthwise, with one hind foot hanging over the side. Below, the birds shift on their branches. A small fish breaks the surface beneath them. The lizard turns its head toward the sound but does not descend.

As the canopy becomes visible, so does the pattern on its back. There is dried mud along one flank and fresh mud beneath the claws. Its eye remains open.

Later, when sunlight reaches this portion of the trunk, it will move a little farther into it.
