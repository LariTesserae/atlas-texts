The first inhabitant you notice is kneeling beside a doorway, taking apart the outer sill.

It was not there when you looked a moment ago. Or it was farther away than the doorway, working in some portion of the room you could not see.

It lifts the crumbly stone in both hands, tips it carefully, and waits for three drops to fall from its underside. Then it sets the stone on edge. From within its sleeve it takes a little brush.

It is about the height of a small adult. Its back is broad beneath a coat of overlapping gray pieces, each hanging free of the next. Its head is uncovered. At first the head appears to be wrapped in pale cloth, but when it turns you see that the folds belong to it: fine ridges around the eyes, deeper pleats beside the mouth, a worn, smooth place above each cheek.

There is no hair. The exposed surface has the dullness of a stone that has been handled for years.

It brushes the sill, steadily and without looking at you.

Its hands have four fingers. At every knuckle there is a narrow dark interval. The fingers bend across these intervals without closing them. When one hand turns palm upward, sunlight passes between the pieces.

The brush is made of cord teased out at the end. It is nearly worn away.

There are others in the basin. In the brief, readable hour before evening, you may count nine. Later you will see eleven doorways occupied, though you have watched no one cross between them. One of the figures will be familiar by a broken corner of its jaw. Another will carry the same brush.

Counting them does not become much easier with acquaintance.

They do not hurry when the distances change. They become very particular.

The kneeling one clears a place for each knee before shifting its weight. It does not put a hand down simply because a surface looks dry. First it touches the surface with the brush, then examines the fibers. Crossing a channel, it follows the bridge notches with a precision that seems excessive in someone who must have crossed thousands of times.

If you offer your hand, it looks first at your fingertips.

A wet hand is refused. A dry hand may be taken, lightly, between two fingers. The touch is cool and slightly rough. Something inside those fingers yields, though the outside does not.

They can speak. Their voices are quiet, produced somewhere behind the small folds of the mouth. Against the waterfalls, you hear only the nearest syllables. They move close to be understood, but not so close that their sleeves touch.

Much of what they say concerns the next small action.

Lift that edge.

Leave the white one.

Wait until the shadow has passed.

They are willing to tell you their names, but a name often includes a pause you mistake for its ending. One person gives you a short syllable, stops, and then gives another. When you repeat the two together, it shakes its head. You must leave room between them.

Their bodies are built with similar care.

Under the loose coat, the chest is a stack of curved pale plates. The plates overlap without resting on one another. In the dark seams between them, something softer contracts. It resembles the gray material in the warming pools, but here it is drawn into close cords, each cord held back from its neighbors by a ridge of dry white grit.

The abdomen is less regular. There are patches that have been replaced, a blue-white piece among yellowed ones, a dark wedge beneath a rib. No two inhabitants have quite the same arrangement.

One has an ordinary left arm and a right arm so narrow that its sleeve must be tied around it. Another has a face assembled from many small fragments. Its expression changes slowly, with minute adjustments along the joins.

The eldest has no face you can recognize. A single smooth plate covers the front of its head. Two shallow hollows have been kept clear of deposit. It turns those hollows toward whoever is speaking.

They keep themselves much as they keep the houses.

Every shelter has a seat with a slotted back, a work surface, and shallow bowls. The rake beside the powder tray is also used on the body. Seated with its coat removed, an inhabitant draws the wooden teeth gently along the seams of its torso. Powder falls through the seat to the floor.

It inspects what has fallen.

Most is swept into a waste bowl. Some grains are lifted with a dampened point of cord and placed aside.

A damaged plate is not immediately discarded. It may be bound with two neighboring pieces while new material grows behind it. If it must come away, the owner holds very still while someone else loosens the wedges. The exposed inner surface is gray and faintly mobile. It draws toward warmth.

They do not like to be watched during this.

They will let you watch them prepare a hand. A hand is considered less private.

The replacement pieces are kept by color, but color is only the beginning of the sorting. Some stone drinks water too quickly. Some retains it under a skin that looks perfectly dry. Some will carry a wet connection through its whole thickness after a single touch. A useful piece must hold moisture on one face and leave the other face separate.

They test chips in the shallow bowls, turning them at intervals. The tests may take days. During that time the chip is referred to by the part it is intended to become.

The small blue thumb.

The second piece of the shoulder.

Your new cheek.

They eat, although there is little resemblance to a meal.

At the cold deposits beneath the displaced waterfalls, they collect the youngest mineral plates. These are carried home between layers of dry cloth and crumbled into bowls. A little water is added. An inhabitant sits over the bowl until the mixture has thickened, then takes it in small portions with the end of a flat tool.

The mouth opens farther than you expect.

Inside it there are shelves, not teeth. The paste is pressed onto these shelves. The person closes its mouth and remains still.

Afterward, there may be a faint, bright trace along one of the older cracks in its face.

Not everything they require is in the stone. Sometimes, in morning sun, one sits at the margin of a shallow pool and lowers a single finger into the gray sediment.

The branching lines approach.

They gather against the fingertip without being scooped or disturbed. The finger is lifted very slowly. A thin gray veil comes with it, disappears beneath the nail, and leaves the water clear.

You cannot tell whether this is feeding, tending, or taking someone home.

They do not answer that question directly. One tells you that this pool is too young. Another tells you not to shade the margin.

There are young inhabitants, though they are seldom outdoors.

The smallest you see occupies a broad shallow bowl in a roofless enclosure. Pale chips stand in the bowl with careful spaces between them. Gray threads join some of the chips below their edges. When sunlight moves across the bowl, the whole arrangement inclines toward it.

An adult turns the bowl a little.

Beside it lies a hand-sized coat of loose scales, not yet worn.

The young are given shapes gradually. A pair of pieces becomes a jaw. Several curved flakes are offered as a chest. The gray material accepts some and withdraws from others. Unsuitable pieces are removed, washed, and returned to the shelf.

For a long time, there is no head.

The adults speak to it anyway. They bring the bowl into the house before the evening damp and set it where it can hear the work. Sometimes the pieces knock softly against one another. An adult answers with a fingernail against the rim.

Later, it will learn to keep its own separations. Until then, someone is always near enough to lift it.

The danger for them is not simply being washed away.

You see this one afternoon when an inhabitant reaches beneath a ledge for a fallen tool. A thread of water runs from its wrist into the opening at its elbow.

The arm becomes long.

Nothing stretches. The upper arm remains where it was; the hand is still visible under the ledge. But between them lies an extent of pale, wet surfaces, tier upon tier, with the gray cords passing inward beyond sight. Its sleeve hangs loosely around a distance it cannot contain.

The inhabitant sits down.

Its companion does not pull on the hand. It takes powder from a pouch and works at the wet seam with the brush. Both wait.

Eventually the thread breaks. The elbow is an elbow again.

The injured one lifts its hand and turns it over several times. It leaves the tool where it fell.

Some do not come back so readily.

In certain pools, far beneath the visible bottom, you can make out arrangements that are too regular to be outcrops. A curved row of plates. Four slender pieces beside one another. A worn gray scale like those on the coats.

The inhabitants visit these places in dry weather. They clear the channels around them. They build tiny divided cairns on the banks and replace stones that have shifted.

They speak downward, pausing between the parts of a name.

Once, after such a visit, the person with the broken jaw returns to its shelter carrying a shallow bowl. There are only a few chips in it and a little gray sediment. It puts the bowl on the sleeping platform, where the light will reach it in the morning.

For several days it keeps a second coat ready.

This is why the buildings seem abandoned at noon. The residents are not all in their rooms, and those who are in their rooms are not always near the walls. The safe paths are kept for the hours when a body can remain a manageable thing. Much of the rest of their time is spent waiting, holding a separation open, or finding the small dry edge from which a familiar part can be reached.

Yet in the short evening interval they visit one another.

They bring a repaired tool, a promising chip, news of a channel. They sit on the broad sills with their feet outside. Someone taps a bowl to call across the valley. In the quiet after a waterfall moves away, another answers.

There is laughter, recognizable despite the unfamiliar voices. The one with the many-piece face takes a long time to finish laughing. Its companion waits, looking pleased.

As the western ridge darkens, they get up reluctantly.

Coats are shaken out. Bowls are carried inward. Each person checks the sill of the house it is leaving, even if someone else has already checked it.

Then the rooms close around their ordinary proportions: the seat, the shelf, the sleeping platform, the new cheek waiting in its bowl.

At the last open doorway, the inhabitant with the brush pauses to look across the basin.

The eastern roar draws near.

It listens for a moment, then turns the brush sideways and lays it carefully across two dry stones.
