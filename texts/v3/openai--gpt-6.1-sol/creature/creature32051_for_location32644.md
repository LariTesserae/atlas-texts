The figure beside the freight signal wears a coat made of narrow ceramic pieces threaded onto something softer. The pieces are brown where they overlap and gray at the edges, where the surface has worn away. One shoulder hangs lower than the other. This is not damage. The coat was fitted to a body that has never been quite symmetrical.

Its face is uncovered.

At first there seems to be very little face: a shallow ridge, two dark recesses, a mouth closed so completely that the seam is difficult to follow. But when it turns toward the track, several small surfaces shift together. A fold lifts beside one eye. The lower part of the face draws inward. There is an expression there, although it takes a while to recognize it as attention.

The hands are more legible. They have long fingers, broad at the tips, with closely fitted nails of a darker material. The left hand rests against the signal post. Its palm has polished a patch in the metal.

This inhabitant is waiting for a cart.

The cart is not late. It has not yet reached the place where it can be seen.

***

They are not numerous enough to keep every dwelling occupied. Nor are they so few that any one of them knows all the others.

Between the districts there are inhabitants who have never met, whose work has nevertheless passed through one another’s hands for longer than either can remember. A hinge made in the northern workshops opens a door in the brown ceramic quarter. The person who lives behind that door has repaired a gauge used by the hinge maker. Each recognizes the other’s workmanship. Neither knows the other’s face.

Their bodies are composed of materials that resemble the city’s without exactly matching them. The surface of a forearm looks like fine-grained stone until it bends. At the elbow, it gathers into shallow folds. The folds close slowly afterward, leaving no crease.

Under this surface is a dense, yielding substance. It holds warmth. If a hand remains on a tool for long enough, the tool takes on a slightly different temperature from the room.

They have no obvious pulse. Something moves within them, but not in a rhythm that can be found by holding a wrist. Occasionally, while an inhabitant sits at rest, a small adjustment passes through the throat or across the ribs. It resembles swallowing, though nothing has entered the mouth.

They do not need water.

What they need arrives in small covered dishes: mineral paste, shavings softened in heated vessels, a few dark grains selected from the dust separators. A dish may remain on a table through several factory cycles. Eventually a fingertip gathers some of its contents and presses them into the mouth.

Different households prepare these portions differently. In one dwelling, the paste is smoothed flat with a warmed blade. In another, it is left in little ridges. There is an inhabitant who will not take a portion unless a trace of pale kiln material has been mixed into it. The preference is older than the current kiln and has survived three changes in the composition of its output.

No one considers this peculiar enough to mention.

***

They can move quickly.

This would not be apparent to someone watching the streets. Most walk with their feet close to the paving, taking care over the slight inequalities that the eye misses. Their bodies advance, settle, advance again. A journey across a district includes pauses beside foundations, under conduits, at doorways where another inhabitant may eventually appear.

But a hand will close at once around a falling instrument. A worker will step out of the path of a slipping plate before the plate has made a sound.

Afterward, the worker stands still.

Rapid movement costs them something that must be restored quietly. For a while the joints remain warmer than the rest of the body. The mouth parts. One hand may be tucked inside the coat, pressed against the lower ribs.

They do not admire speed. They do notice competence.

At the rolling house, a narrow-bodied inhabitant can determine the wear on a bearing by turning it once between two fingers. Others require a fixture and a lamp. When work passes through that room, it is often left at the narrow-bodied one’s station, even when another station is nearer.

This has caused a small, durable resentment.

The resentful worker has never said so directly. Instead, finished components from that station arrive wrapped more carefully than necessary, with the inspection marks placed where they cannot be overlooked.

There are still such things here.

***

Their speech is quiet, but not because sound carries badly.

They speak with a little air held in chambers behind the mouth. The first syllable of a sentence is sometimes almost inaudible. The next comes more clearly, once the chamber has opened.

A conversation can include intervals long enough for a lamp to complete its adjustment. Neither speaker mistakes these intervals for an ending.

They have words for degrees of wear that would be difficult to distinguish by sight. They have separate words for an object that has become thinner through use and one that has become thinner through waiting. They can describe a familiar surface becoming unfamiliar beneath the hand.

Their words for distance are less exact in conversation than the figures posted at the junctions. A dwelling may be “past the second warmth,” meaning that the route crosses two foundations drawing heavily from the rock. A workshop may be “where the floor changes,” although the change occurred so long ago that only a few inhabitants can still feel it.

Names are spoken rarely. They are most useful when someone is absent.

In rooms where several people gather, recognition is usually accomplished by the angle of a head, a hand placed flat on a table, a slight opening made between two seated bodies.

A visitor may sit there for a considerable time before anyone speaks. During that time, a dish is moved within reach.

***

They live alone more often than together, but solitude is not the same as privacy.

An inhabitant may enter another’s room to adjust a wall contact, retrieve a shared gauge, or sit beside the heat when its own foundation is undergoing repair. The ceramic curtain makes a particular sound when lifted. Those inside know who has entered from the handling of it.

Some lift all the plates together. Some pass beneath one corner. One inhabitant, with a stiff shoulder, always catches the third plate against the door stone.

The sound is recognized across the court.

Affection has become attached to these small irregularities. A cup with an uneven lip is kept because someone made it that way. A route is lengthened to pass a window. Two inhabitants working in different buildings arrange their pauses so that their lamps are visible to one another across the empty ground.

The lamps do not flash. The arrangement would be easy to miss.

In one residential bar, two tables have stood close together for so long that their heated sides have altered each other’s surfaces. The inhabitants who use them are not always in the room at the same time. They leave objects for each other: a newly polished hand-piece, a portion from a different kiln, a smooth stone found in a dust channel.

The objects are handled, then placed on the shelves.

Nothing in the room indicates which inhabitant owns which shelf.

***

They wear out.

First there is usually a loss of precision. A finger no longer comes to rest at the intended point. A knee requires an adjustment before it bears weight. A familiar corridor feels longer, though its posted distance has not changed.

Some parts can be replaced. The replacements are made in the same workshops that supply the city, but at benches kept free of ordinary production. Their fittings are patient and exact. Once installed, a new joint may remain conspicuous for a very long time.

The inhabitant learns its resistance. The surface around it gradually changes.

Other losses cannot be repaired.

A person may cease to recognize a tool that has spent ages in its hand. It may remember the work but not the sequence. It may begin a journey and stop at a junction where it formerly turned without looking.

When this happens, others rearrange their routes.

They do not announce the arrangement. A worker begins passing the dwelling before each shift. A seat is moved closer to a wall. A task is divided so that its beginning remains with the person who knows how to begin it.

Eventually, some inhabitants no longer leave their rooms.

The room stays warm. Someone comes to lift the curtain, clear the dust, and place a hand within reach.

***

The small plated creatures are not kept as companions.

Nevertheless, certain inhabitants notice them.

One worker leaves the lower cover off an unused conduit because a creature has settled inside it. Another interrupts the sweeping of a stairwell when a gray foot appears beneath the lowest tread. The delay may be substantial. The creature does not understand that it is being waited for.

They have no agreed explanation for these animals. Their plates contain familiar minerals in unfamiliar proportions. The discolorations they leave are sometimes scraped up and examined, then returned to the separators with the rest of the dust.

There are inhabitants who say the creatures were always here.

There are others who remember a time when none were seen.

Both statements may be true within the limits of what each has noticed.

Sometimes a creature rests against an inhabitant’s bare foot. Neither moves. Their temperatures are nearly the same.

***

The watcher beside the freight signal has a room in the district beyond the elevated conduit. On its table lies a component from an earlier version of the signal: a small forked contact, worn too thin to use.

It was not retained for study.

The watcher sometimes holds it while sitting against the heated wall. One prong fits between two fingers. There is a faint depression where the thumb has rested repeatedly.

Out in the square, the signal changes.

The watcher removes its hand from the post and steps toward the track. Far off, the cart’s ticking has become intermittent as its wheels enter a stretch of dust.

There is time to go inside the signal house and take down the receiving tool. There is time to inspect its jaw and adjust the grip. When the cart arrives, the watcher will stop it, lift the new bearing from its cradle, and put the old one in its place.

Before that, it stands listening.

The sound comes closer, disappears, and comes closer again.
