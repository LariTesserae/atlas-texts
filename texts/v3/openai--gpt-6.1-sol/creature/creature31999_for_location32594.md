The pale creature has more limbs than can be seen at once. Some lie folded along its belly, so closely fitted that they look like seams in its covering. The two that grip the root are not its strongest. They are simply the ones that can reach.

Under the shell chips, its body is slightly flattened. There is a shallow dent in its back where the root has thickened against it. The dent does not trouble it. When pressure increases, the soft plates along its sides overlap a little farther, and a narrow band of darker tissue disappears beneath them. When pressure falls, the band returns.

Its mouth is underneath, among the folded limbs. It eats by holding something against itself.

Usually this is a strand of the fine growth that fringes the leaves. The creature draws the strand between two plates and keeps it there until the strand becomes clear. Nothing is bitten off. Sometimes the emptied strand remains attached to the leaf; sometimes it loosens and lies across the creature’s belly until new growth covers it. There are several such strands there now, in different stages of becoming part of its covering.

What it takes from them is insufficient to make it grow quickly. It has not grown quickly in a very long time.

Its eyes are not blind. Pressed against the wood, they distinguish the places where decay has reached the far side of a splinter. These places are a little different from the hard wood around them. Not brighter, exactly. Less complete.

The creature can remain with its eyes against such a place through an entire winter.

Behind the wood, something gives way. A seam admits water. The creature loosens one foot.

This is how most of its journeys begin.

It does not know the hollow as a collection of objects. There is no useful separation between a leaf and the growth upon the leaf, or between a root and the water held against it. There are places that yield, places that hold, places through which a change is already coming. Its feet can feel a fresh fracture before water has entered the full length of it. A folded limb beneath its body uncurls toward the fracture, though the creature itself may not turn.

The leaves tighten then.

For a while, the creature remains behind the shell, with one limb extended into a space too narrow to receive it. Far away by the hollow’s measures, a leaf begins to open. A small seam of soft tissue is exposed.

By the time the creature arrives, the seam may have developed a coat of fine growth. It presses its mouth there and waits. The leaf closes as far as the creature’s body allows.

There is no struggle. There is also no tenderness that can be separated from the pressure of the leaf.

Sometimes it remains enclosed until its covering splits.

The split begins beneath the head and travels backward between the plates. It is so narrow that water enters it without stirring the slime outside. The creature withdraws from its old covering a limb at a time. For several weeks, the empty limbs continue gripping whatever they held before.

Newly emerged, it is almost transparent. Its eyes seem too heavy for it. A small dark mass lies inside its body, nearer the back than the belly, and beside that mass is a pocket containing nothing visibly different from the surrounding water.

The pocket expands during a tide.

Afterward, the creature is pale again.

Its abandoned covering does not fall away. Leaves take hold of the thin edges. Fine roots pass through the empty joints. Eventually the back plate lies hidden beneath growth, and the limbs become difficult to distinguish from the little bent stems around them.

There are old coverings in the hollow that belong to no body of its present size.

One is lodged beneath the level patch of grit. It is broad and very shallow, with a row of openings along one side. The grit enters these openings during a tide and settles out again afterward. The creature sometimes puts a foot against the buried edge. It never pulls.

Another covering, if that is what it is, forms part of the enclosure at the back. The overlapping leaf ribs have grown through it. Its clearest remaining feature is a small curved plate beside the doorway, harder than leaf tissue and softer than shell.

The pale creature can reach the doorway with one of its longest limbs. It cannot put its head through.

On rare occasions, it leaves the limb there for so long that a translucent collar begins to form around the joint. When it withdraws, the collar is left hanging in the entrance. Cold water moves through it. The ring slowly narrows but does not close.

What lives beyond the doorway does not come out.

It has a surface. This much can be learned when the extended limb touches it. The surface yields farther than a leaf would, then resists. It is ridged in places, smooth in others. Sometimes the limb finds the same ridge twice in positions that should be too far apart.

The creature withdraws sooner on those occasions.

Behind the arch, the second pale body remains beneath the leaf.

Its back is covered, but something there still takes food. A filament laid across that place gradually clears from its middle outward. The leaf above it has become thick and faintly swollen. One edge is held away from the root by something smaller than a foot.

The visible creature seldom approaches. When it does, it grips the stems edging the grit and turns its head sideways until one eye rests against the leaf. It stays so until the pressure changes.

Once, a pale joint appeared beneath the leaf’s edge.

It was gone before the next winter. The leaf had not moved enough to account for its disappearance.

Elsewhere in the hollow, life has no particular outline. The translucent skins stretched between splinters thicken where two surfaces nearly touch. Along their thicker margins, small sealed folds form and gradually flatten. A fold may persist for years with a speck of grit inside it. If the wood breaks nearby, the speck is drawn toward the break.

The fine growth on the ribbons is not all of one kind. Some strands branch whenever they touch stone. Others run alongside those branches and turn only when the pale creature feeds. The darkest strands enter the deep water between the stones. Their returned tips are softer than the stems that went in.

Occasionally, one of these tips touches the creature.

It keeps all its feet still then. Even the limbs folded beneath its belly settle tightly against one another. The tip passes over its back, pauses at the narrow spaces between the plates, and continues toward the ceiling.

A little pale material adheres to it.

Whether this is taken from the creature or left upon it is difficult to tell. After such a contact, the creature’s next movement is usually toward fresh stone.

Now it has released one foot from the root beneath the white shell.

The shallow collar around that foot remains behind, holding its shape. The freed limb reaches past the grit, between two stems, and rests against a fracture that was not open last winter.

Within the fracture, the new pale tip divides.

The creature does not touch it yet. There is still stone between them, thin enough for pressure to pass through. Its other feet tighten. A leaf somewhere behind the arch opens a fraction, then holds.

For a long time, that is all that happens.
