The first person out in the morning does not necessarily have a reason to go far. Often she stands on the step with one hand still on the door and looks for something that ought to be there: the white head of a marker, the forked alder beside the path, the dark rise where the fuel shed stands. She has learned to look for absences before looking for damage.

Her name is Sen. She maintains six of the buried installations, though on forms she is responsible for five. The sixth is the abandoned station on the flats. No one has asked her to stop checking it, and she has not asked anyone to make the responsibility official.

She is broad through the shoulders, with a pale scar just inside her hairline and small, badly healed burns on two fingers. Her outdoor clothes make her seem larger than she is. Indoors she has narrow wrists and a habit of pushing her sleeves back even when the room is cold.

Before leaving the step, she raises two fingers toward the kitchen window. Someone inside raises two in reply.

The signal means she is going as far as the first junction. It does not mean the junction is still there.

Most of the people who live in the valley have some version of this exchange. They signal through windows, across workshops, from one end of a table to the other when a gust drives rain against the shutters. Their hands have become expressive in ways their faces sometimes are not. A palm lowered slowly means wait where you are. A closed fist held beside the shoulder means come back along your own footprints. There are separate signs for water over a route, water beneath it, and a route that merely looks wrong.

Visitors learn the first two quickly. The third takes longer.

Sen carries a probing rod, a small receiver, and a bag containing ordinary things: cable ties, clean cloths, a flask, biscuits wrapped twice against the wet. The tools for the buried machinery fit into a case no larger than a lunch box. The rod is almost as tall as she is.

She uses the rod more often.

At the first junction, the track has acquired a stream. Water runs through spruce roots that were exposed yesterday and are already collecting grit. Sen plants the rod, leans on it, and watches the water cloud around its tip. She waits long enough for a person watching from the window to become uneasy.

Then she steps back.

By breakfast there will be a new route on the kitchen board.

—

There are thirty-seven permanent residents, if the two children away at school are counted and the woman who spends every winter at the coast is not. The number is a matter of recurring disagreement. No one disagrees about how many sets of dry clothes should be kept in the waiting huts.

They are not all engineers. One repairs engines and hates the foundation systems because an engine can at least be taken apart where you can see it. One manages supplies and remembers what everyone ordered six months after they have forgotten ordering it. Two work the estuary traps. Several conduct surveys whose records are already full of places that no longer exist.

A man named Olan cooks for whoever is working near the western station. He came on a three-month contract nine years ago. He says he has stayed because freight costs make it uneconomical to move his ovens, although he owns only one oven and it is small enough to fit in a boat.

He keeps a towel over his shoulder and wears hearing protection while kneading bread. Flour settles into the creases around his fingernails. When someone arrives soaked, he does not ask what happened until their outer clothes are off. He points to the bench above the warm foundation, sets a mug beside them, and waits.

In his kitchen, people talk about people. They discuss an unanswered letter, a bad tooth, the likelihood that a son will return after training. The river is loud enough without being the subject of every conversation.

Arguments tend to happen close together. You cannot deliver a satisfying accusation from the opposite side of the room. You have to approach, bend toward the other person, and repeat yourself if they have not heard. Sometimes this prevents an argument. Sometimes it makes one worse.

—

The oldest inhabitant remembers the ice.

Not from a history lesson or a photograph. He remembers standing beside its dirty edge and hearing meltwater beneath it. He remembers a summer when the basin smelled mostly of wet stone, and the first spruce seedlings arrived in trays.

His name is Tavit. He now walks with two sticks, neither matching the other. He has a collection of photographs in which he appears progressively younger beside progressively emptier ground.

People ask him whether the valley has become what the founders intended.

He generally asks which founders they mean.

The first crews wanted stable pads and access to the estuary. The biologists wanted to see what would arrive without assistance. A later authority wanted a settlement large enough to justify its own transport schedule. Tavit wanted a house with a southern window. For several years the window looked directly onto a wall of ice. Now it looks into alder.

He has stopped cutting the alder back. There are birds in it, and he likes watching them from his chair.

He is not sentimental about the old landscape. In photographs, he points out the places where people fell through crusts into melt cavities, the camp that had to be dismantled in a night, the survey line laid over something that was assumed to be gravel. He was among those who made the assumptions.

When the children visit, he lets them handle his old instruments. Some still work. Others have scales for conditions the present crews no longer measure.

—

The children know the machinery mainly by its consequences.

They know which walls stay warm enough to lean against. They know that a strip turning amber is not an invitation to press the recessed button beneath it. They know the pale ring at a doorway must never be covered by a mat, even though it collects mud and looks like something a mat was invented to cover.

They play on the fixed ground around the buildings, where boundaries are indicated with low stakes. A ball that rolls beyond the stakes must be retrieved by an adult. This is an unpopular rule, especially when the ball remains plainly visible.

Their games involve crossings, deliveries, elaborate rescues. A visiting teacher once tried to stop them pretending that the floor had washed away beneath a table. By the end of the week she had helped them make better warning flags.

They also build houses that do not move. They spend a long time arranging roofs and windows, then defend these houses furiously against smaller children who want to introduce a flood.

At night they sleep with the river in their rooms. When they travel somewhere quiet, some of them cannot sleep at all.

—

Other inhabitants need far less fixed ground.

Beneath one of Olan’s sheds lives a vole with a ragged left ear. It has moved its nest three times this season. Its current entrance is behind a boot scraper, under a lip of warm gravel. Olan sees it when he empties crumbs outside. It pauses only briefly, whiskers trembling, before carrying away a piece almost too large for its mouth.

There are many voles. This one is recognizable, which gives it a disproportionate place in the household.

Their runs stitch together roots, sedge tussocks, fallen branches. After a flood, some runs end at vertical mud faces. Others become channels themselves. The animals retreat upward through the vegetation, occupying spaces that were previously only passages. A hollow stump may contain several nests for a few nights, then none.

The birds follow the exposed food. They work collapsing banks almost before the clay has stopped falling. Along warm drains, small wings flicker over insects too fine to see from a doorway. In the abandoned station’s underfloor cavity, their droppings whiten the roots.

Sen counts them when she visits. Not carefully enough for a survey. Carefully enough to notice a change.

Once she finds only two where there have usually been six or seven. She stays longer that day, listening through the foundation with the receiver pressed against her ear.

The fault is minor. She adjusts the station and leaves.

On her next visit there are five.

—

The deer belong more obviously to the margins. They come down among the houses when the upper scrub is iced over, stepping across cables and stacked timber with an awkward delicacy. Their coats are dark with rain; water hangs from their lower jaws.

One doe has learned that the delivery platform is firm underfoot. She stands there during storms, beneath no roof, apparently satisfied with the certainty of the surface.

People give her space. Someone leaves peelings, though everyone has agreed not to.

Ravens inspect almost everything people put down. They open poorly fastened bags, pull insulation from exposed packing, and wait above the fish-cleaning boards. A pair nests on the remains of a survey tower. During the worst winds they disappear into the spruce, where they become difficult to distinguish from the branches until one shifts its feet.

In summer, the valley is full of young animals. In autumn, it is full of evidence that they have dispersed, been eaten, or failed to find shelter. The residents see enough of this to resist calling the place generous merely because it is green.

Still, more life arrives.

Seeds lodge in the fleece of work clothes. Eggs come upstream in fish. Winged insects appear in a newly warm hollow before anyone has decided what to call it. Under stones that were bare two seasons ago, there are roots now, and pale animals that withdraw from the light.

—

At the estuary, the trap workers are sisters. They spend enough time together that their hand signals have become difficult for anyone else to follow.

They bring fish back in low sledges and record the catch before cleaning it. Their notebooks distinguish what was caught from where the traps were intended to be. These are often different facts.

The younger sister has been offered work on a larger vessel. She has not accepted or refused. The older one knows about the offer, although neither has mentioned it directly.

They argue instead about replacing a sledge runner.

On days when the causeway closes, they stay in the waiting hut. They remove their wet clothes, hang them in the drying cupboard, and eat from the stores. There are cards with softened corners, old magazines, a kettle that whistles too quietly to be useful.

Sometimes they sit for hours without attempting conversation. When the markers reappear, they check the tide and wait a little longer.

They both remember people who crossed too soon.

—

The buried systems do not make decisions. Sen is firm about this, especially with visitors who hear the ground hum and begin asking whether the installations know where they are.

They maintain assigned conditions within tolerances. They accept changes only through the proper controls. A crossing can remain perfectly supported after it has ceased to lead anywhere.

Nevertheless, people speak to them while working.

“Come on,” says the engine repairer, who claims never to do this.

Sen says, “That will do,” when a status strip returns to its steady color. Then she checks the receiver again.

What lives here includes this patient, repetitive attention: someone cleaning grit from a seal; someone replacing the food they took from a waiting hut; someone noticing that a neighbor’s lamp has stayed on later than usual. There is enough work that some gets postponed. There is always a list.

At dusk, Sen returns from the eastern flats with mud up to one knee. She stops outside Olan’s kitchen and wipes her hand before touching the window.

Inside, he turns from the sink.

She makes the sign for all well, then modifies it with a small sideways motion: well enough.

He lifts the latch.

Behind her, rain moves across the thawed oval around the house. A vole waits beneath the step. Farther out, birds settle among the station’s exposed roots, shifting close together as the light fades. The river is taking earth from somewhere upstream, and before morning someone will hear it fall.

For now, Sen gets her boots off. Olan moves a chair nearer the stove. There is bread left, and she is hungry.
