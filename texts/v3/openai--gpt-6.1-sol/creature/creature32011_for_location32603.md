The inhabitants are easiest to mistake for part of the work.

From a bridge, the people moving along a lower terrace seem to have the same destinations as the water. They follow the channels, stop at the same constrictions, disappear under the same arches. Their clothes are usually pale above the waist and dark below it. The dark is not always dye. Trousers acquire a permanent waterline; skirts are weighted at the hem so they will not fall into a gutter. Porters wear short coats with the pockets high on the chest.

They carry things in vessels that can be set down on a wet surface: metal baskets, shallow glazed tubs, bags held open by hoops. A parcel wrapped only in cloth is something one keeps under an arm.

Up close, they cease to resemble their surroundings. There are narrow faces and broad ones, dark skin and pale skin, people who stoop from years in the galleries and people who have spent their working lives on the rims, blinking in the basin light. Some walk quickly despite the general slowness of the machinery. Some dress extravagantly, with stiff collars, bright enamel buttons, polished shell ornaments that knock together when they turn their heads.

A woman in a coat the color of hot iron can cross an entire square without touching any of the damp patches.

This is admired. It is also considered a rather pointless accomplishment.

Most children learn the city first as a collection of temperatures.

They know the wall that is warm all winter, the stair landing where a bare foot can be scalded, the public channel in which it is safe to sit. Before they can read a street name, they can distinguish bathwater from culture water by smell. Adults teach them to look at the mouths of drains. Froth is not necessarily dangerous. Clear water is not necessarily clean.

There are lessons that sound like games.

Find the stone that has moved. Find the gutter that is flowing uphill. Stand still and say which pump has stopped.

A child who gives the wrong answer is corrected, then sent off to do something else. These are ordinary precautions, taught alongside crossing freight tracks and keeping fingers out of a capstan.

The children themselves prefer games involving the animals. They race small shelled grazers across a wet board, encouraging them with smears of bacterial paste. Usually none reaches the finish before the children lose interest. They make little enclosures of mineral flakes and keep drain scavengers in them until a parent discovers what is producing the smell.

There is a particular age at which almost every child wants a roof bird.

The birds tolerate handling less well than they appear to from a distance. They have strong necks, unpleasantly warm feet, and bills capable of opening a thumb. Nevertheless, some become attached to households. A gray bird might spend fifteen years entering through the same window, stealing food from the same bowl, and attacking anyone who tries to repair the shutter.

It will never accept a second window, even when the first has been built over.

People say such birds have a better memory for property than the courts.

***

The large pale animals are less individual from a distance and more individual to those who work with them.

Each has a name, including most of the ones that belong to nobody. Their names are short and easy to shout under a bridge. They are named after damaged equipment, dead relatives, parts of the body, minor inconveniences. There are several animals called Late.

They have wide mouths with hard ridges inside, and eyes set far enough apart that a handler must move around to be properly seen. Their hides look smooth but are scored with shallow lines. In those lines, mineral dust accumulates. An old animal may have pale stripes that no amount of washing removes.

They browse at the feeding beds by drawing sheets of growth into their mouths and compressing them slowly. At work, they lean into a harness with their whole weight before taking the first step. The waiting period can be considerable. A novice pulls at the lead, shouts, strikes the paving with a pole. An experienced handler checks the harness, clears the path, and waits.

Once moving, the animal is reluctant to stop.

This makes them useful at capstans and troublesome at intersections.

Their attachments are obscure but durable. One will refuse a particular bridge for years. Another will continue to wait outside a demolished loading house, although its handler and work have moved to a different district. They recognize some people by voice. Others they apparently identify by the way a pole is held.

Handlers argue about whether the animals are affectionate. The argument usually takes place while one of them is scratching an animal behind the jaw.

The ownerless ones are fed by workshops whose pipes they sleep above. Nobody quite admits responsibility. When an animal is injured, however, someone fetches a veterinarian, someone brings a trough, and someone objects to the bill.

***

Food is plentiful in the basin, but good food requires considerable effort.

Fresh growth from the galleries has a mild, fatty taste. Depending on the spring, it can carry an iron bitterness, a faint sweetness, or a sulfurous finish that outsiders never learn to enjoy. Most of it is pressed, washed, fermented, or fed to something else before people eat it.

There are dense loaves that must be sliced hot, thin sheets dried on heated tiles, soft curds sold in perforated bowls. One district is famous for a gray paste whose preparation involves running water through it for nine days. Another insists this removes everything worth eating.

Snail flesh is common. The largest animals are valuable for their shells as well as their bodies, and are killed carefully. Market workers use narrow tools passed beneath the shell’s lip. Apprentices spend months learning to do this without leaving fragments in the meat.

Small scavengers are roasted whole. Their legs catch between the teeth.

Fish from the open lakes taste different from those raised in enclosed tanks, though people disagree about which are better. Lake fish are cheaper unless a particular shore has been closed after a dredge uncovers something troublesome.

Meals are salty, warm, and often accompanied by something crisp. Crispness is highly prized in a city where almost everything left uncovered becomes damp.

Imported fruit arrives by rail.

On market days, buyers stop to look at it even when they do not intend to purchase any. The smell spreads through a covered hall with surprising force. A single cut fruit can make a row of stalls seem briefly unfamiliar.

Children are told not to waste the skins. Some obey by keeping them in boxes until they decay.

There are people who dislike fruit, particularly those who first encounter it as adults. They find it perfumed and insubstantial. Visitors sometimes mistake this for a local affectation. It is not. There are also inhabitants who spend unreasonable portions of their income on it, and vendors who recognize them before they come down the steps.

***

Inside the houses, dryness is maintained in small, deliberate territories.

Letters are kept in sealed drawers. Bedding is aired above the indoor channels, high enough to catch warmth without collecting spray. A shelf used for photographs may have its own little hood and a vent leading toward the chimney.

Wooden objects are repaired for generations. A chair can have a metal seat, shell inlays, and four replacement legs while still being called the wooden chair. Children are permitted to touch it but not to sit on it with wet clothes.

The richer households have dry rooms. Poorer ones have dry cupboards.

Even so, the city does not divide neatly into those who live uphill and those who live down. Some old families remain in lower houses with beautiful rooms, elaborate sluices, and permanent staff employed to keep them habitable. Some high apartments are cheaply built and leak from every direction. A respectable address can mean a good view, a reliable overflow, or simply that the retaining wall was inspected recently.

Guests notice the precautions before residents do.

A bowl is moved away from a seam in the wall. A sleeping mat is raised onto its feet. A conversation pauses while someone listens to three successive gulps from the courtyard shaft.

If the fourth comes normally, the conversation resumes.

People who have left the basin sometimes miss these sounds more than they miss the heat. They find dry houses peculiarly exposed. There is nothing moving behind the wall to tell them whether the night is proceeding as it should.

Others leave and sleep well for the first time in their lives.

***

Many occupations require knowledge that is difficult to write down.

A gallery keeper knows the smell of a bed that is failing before its color changes. A dredge worker recognizes masonry by the way it breaks in the bucket. Someone who maintains household channels can place a palm against plaster and say where the seepage began, though it may have traveled through three houses to arrive there.

Such workers are sought after, badly paid in some districts, and capable of making themselves unpleasantly indispensable.

They train apprentices by sending them back to look again.

There are written tables, gauges, survey marks, numbered valves. The basin’s industries could not function without them. But the instruments are supplemented by habits acquired over years: touching an iron fitting with the back of the fingers, tasting a trace of deposit, watching whether the animals choose to lie on one side of a conduit or the other.

A mechanic who notices a change may not yet know what it means. Often the first precaution is merely to tell the next shift.

In the older factories, messages survive for decades on slate boards beside the machinery.

*Knock before opening.*

*Second gate binds in cold weather.*

*Do not trust the north measure.*

The person who wrote a warning may be dead. The machine may have been rebuilt twice. Nobody erases it without asking around.

There are people whose families have worked the same installation for four generations. There are also people who arrive on the morning train, take one look into a hot feeding gallery, and decide they will find employment elsewhere.

The city absorbs both kinds.

***

Not everything living there is accustomed to people.

In the chambers behind the retaining walls, where warm water enters cooler darkness, there are animals rarely seen whole. Maintenance crews encounter a pale joint withdrawing into a hole, a soft disk contracting around a beam, a row of small reflective points beyond the reach of a lamp.

Some are harmless grazers. Some eat the grazers. A few will take a finger if it is put where a finger should not be.

They do not invade the houses so much as fail to recognize that a new wall has made a house out of part of their territory.

A family may discover a flat-bodied animal behind a cupboard when the plaster begins to lift. It has been feeding there for months, perhaps years, advancing by fractions through a seam of mineral growth. Removing it requires opening the wall. Leaving it requires accepting that the cupboard will no longer sit level.

People make both decisions.

The translucent cistern eels are more welcome, though not in every room. In a lit bowl they look unfinished: muscle, spine, and the dark interruption of the gut. In a deep tank they are almost invisible until they turn. They eat small larvae and fragments of waste. Healthy eels reassure the household.

A cistern without them is inspected.

An eel found in a washbasin is returned with a cup. An eel found in the soup is cause for an argument about whose job it was to check the strainer.

***

The dead are usually taken uphill.

The dry rim has burial chambers cut into sound stone, above the active seepage. The journey is long enough that mourners ride part of the way, sharing a carriage with flowers imported for the occasion or with ornaments made to resemble them.

Below, the city remains audible. The pumps reach the rim as a faint, irregular pressure in the air.

Some families return each year. Others rarely make the climb. A grave can be farther away in practice than a relative living two cities along the railway.

There are older burials beneath districts that have since become wet. Their remains are moved when found, if anyone can reach them safely. Sometimes only a name is recovered, or the corner of a carved stone. Work stops, records are consulted, and an attempt is made to locate descendants.

Then the wall is strengthened, or the new passage opened.

Among the living, memory is often attached to a level rather than an address. Someone points to a blank stretch of retaining wall and says that an aunt’s kitchen used to be there. Someone else remembers buying hot cakes from a window now well beneath the lake.

The accounts are checked against one another. People disagree about dates, about which steps were still dry, about whether the shop closed before or after the pier moved.

The old stairway into the pool is especially good for these arguments.

On warm evenings, people sit on its upper steps with their shoes beside them. Children lean forward to look for fish. Small shells fasten themselves just below the waterline, where dangling feet occasionally dislodge them.

There is usually someone old enough to remember another usable step.

There is usually someone younger who does not quite believe them.

When the light is right, both can see farther down. The submerged stairs appear briefly distinct, their worn centers filled with fine sediment. A fish crosses one, pauses above the next, and vanishes into the brightness on the water.

Then a barge passes in the lake beyond. Its wake reaches the stairway after a long delay, lifting gently against the people’s ankles.

They gather their shoes and move up one step.
