The animal beside the road appears at first to be a defect in the road.

One of the black seams has widened into an oval, and along one edge the polish has lifted from the stone. There is a little dust beneath it. A copper-backed scavenger approaches, touches the raised edge with a foreleg, and waits.

Something under the oval turns.

The scavenger lowers its belly. For a few moments its transparent joints cloud with fine white sparks. Then it continues uphill, leaving its dark pellet behind.

The oval closes over the pellet. It has no visible mouth. The material simply passes between surfaces that had seemed continuous.

This animal lives at the junction, but it is not confined to the oval. Several hundred feet away, a narrow strip of the same polished black raises itself from the seam. Farther uphill, another does the same. They settle in sequence, making a movement that travels against the direction of the traffic.

Most of its body is underground.

Its exposed parts are contact surfaces: places where other animals can stop, surrender something, receive something, or pass across without knowing they have touched it. Beneath the ceramic crust lies a branching body, thin in some places as a wire and broad in others as the floor of a room. It grows along the roads because the roads supply it with metals, shed scales, residual charge. It also keeps the roads usable. Where traffic wears a seam into a hollow, it raises the floor from beneath. Where a fracture interrupts the current, it reaches across.

There is no distinction between its feeding and this maintenance. A break in the road is an opening into food. Repairing it brings the food closer.

For long stretches it is almost entirely passive. A heavy animal passes above; a membrane flexes. A current changes direction; a ring of muscle tightens around a conducting core. What looks like a continuous polished seam may contain scores of such responses, too small to notice separately.

Once in several years, the junction animal moves.

It does not pull itself out of the ground. Its downhill extremities begin to lose their sensitivity. Traffic abrades them into the seam. Uphill, beneath some promising convergence of tracks, fresh tissue spreads between the layers of ceramic. The thick organs travel more slowly. A mineral chamber empties, collapses, and is rebuilt fifty feet away. A coil loosens one turn at a time and winds itself into a new cavity.

During the move, a traveler may spend a whole day walking over it.

Its offspring begin as fasteners.

A parent presses several bright nodules into the underside of a frequently used seam. Each nodule has two jaws, a small reserve of charge, and a pale interior that darkens when exposed. For months it does nothing but hold the road together. Then it begins taking a little of what passes through.

Most never develop farther. They are crushed, stripped out by scavengers, or buried under roads that cease to be used. The successful ones grow a second contact, then a third. Eventually two contacts touch the same passing animal at once.

After that, their behavior changes.

They begin to recognize travelers.

Recognition is electrical and exact. A worn bearing produces a distinctive interruption in the current. A damaged leg draws more than the others. The copper-backed scavengers carry their private arrangements of spinning parts from basin to basin, and the junction animals learn these arrangements as they learn the grain of the stone.

One scavenger may be permitted to feed at a contact until its joints clear. Another is disconnected almost immediately. A third receives a charge before it has lowered its belly.

The favored individuals bring useful pellets. They also remove growths from the exposed surfaces, picking carefully along the edges with their front limbs. They pause at junctions that offer them nothing on that visit. Sometimes they wait for hours.

There are relationships here that outlast the participants. A scavenger dies beneath a ledge; years later, one of its offspring approaches the junction with a similar arrangement of internal parts. The black surface opens before it is touched.

Along the quieter roads live animals whose backs resemble patches of old shadow.

They are thin, dull creatures, scarcely larger than a hand, with soft margins that admit dust. In full light they flatten so closely against the stone that their internal movements become visible as small ripples. In a persistent shadow they thicken. Tiny limbs unfold underneath, and a fringe of clear filaments explores the ground.

They feed on what remains at the boundary between a shadow and the illuminated stone. Their filaments bridge it. Minute contractions pass around their bodies, drawing them along the ragged edge.

They cannot survive under just any darkness. A ledge provides shelter but little food. The shadow of a living animal is better; the shadow left after the animal has gone is best.

Around an old resting place there may be hundreds. Over decades their feeding rounds the shadow’s outline. First the legs disappear, then the joints, then the distinction between front and rear. The oldest inhabited shadows are pale ovals with dark centers, quite unlike anything now walking on the plateau.

When a fresh shadow remains, the little animals find it quickly.

Their arrival is one of the few hurried events in the high country. They detach from the worn margins where they have been living and move across the open stone in wavering files. Many fail to reach it. Their surfaces lighten; their legs stiffen. By evening they look like flakes of glaze.

The survivors gather so densely that the new shadow seems to have acquired a velvet edge.

Above these small movements stand the ridge animals.

Seen against the sky, they have the simplicity of a frame erected to carry something that has not yet arrived: narrow legs, a crosspiece, several long projections held well apart. Closer, the crosspiece proves to be a heavy suspended body. It hangs inside its own skeleton, supported by tensioned fibers and slowly turning axles.

The long projections are sensitive to the air. Dust settles differently on each of them. Fine arcs pass between their tips, sometimes too faint to see except after sunset.

They lift a foot to test the ground before transferring their weight. The test can last days. In that interval the foot gathers a film of charged powder, and small scavengers climb the planted limbs to collect debris from the joints.

A ridge animal tolerates them until one approaches a bearing.

Then a shutter closes deep inside the limb. The scavenger stops, backs down, and takes another route.

Their young remain beneath the suspended body for years, arranged in warm sockets. At first they resemble compact bundles of transparent rods. The parent supplies metals through a feeding head that travels from socket to socket. Later, the rods separate into limbs. They grip the parent’s inner framework and practice lifting one foot while the others hold.

Detachment happens slowly. One connection dries. Another opens. For a season the young animal hangs by a single flexible conductor.

When that finally parts, the offspring must find its own electrical difference between ground and sky.

Some never stand high enough. Their limbs spread under them, and they remain on the ridge as low, crowded heaps. They may live for centuries in this condition. Other creatures shelter among their struts. Junction bodies grow beneath them. Their quiet workings become part of a basin’s pitch.

Predators visit these failed giants often.

Their arrival is first felt as a change in the traffic. Scavengers leave the broad seams and cross the rough ground. The shadow feeders draw their filaments inward. A ridge animal closes its bearing shutters one after another, producing a series of knocks that can be felt through the soles.

The predator itself may be far away.

Its small angular body makes the irregular tapping. Each tap is accompanied by a tightening somewhere else: beneath a grazer, inside a joint, around one of the buried animal’s thick chambers.

It appears to test its prey before taking it. Old damage attracts it. So do places where a body has recently grown across a gap.

Sometimes the intended victim adjusts. A grazer loosens its grip along a hundred feet of slope. A junction animal lets a distant contact die. A giant holds its raised foot a little higher, and the tapping ceases.

Sometimes no adjustment is enough.

The body folds out of sight, leaving its weight distributed through the stone. Around the loss, the roads sag. Dust begins moving toward a new center. The survivors alter their routes by inches.

In time the junction animals follow those routes. The shadow feeders gather along whatever darkness remains. A buried grinder finds the newly fractured mineral easier to draw beneath its rim.

Generations later, a young scavenger crosses the basin and pauses at a warm black oval. It lowers its belly. Its joints clear.

Underneath it, something large and patient opens a contact that has never been used before.
