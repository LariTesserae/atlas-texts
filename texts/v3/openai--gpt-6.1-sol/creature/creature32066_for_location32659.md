Under one of the oldest arches, a length of wall is warmer than the stone around it.

The difference is slight. Water passing over it carries away almost all the evidence. It has the same charcoal coating as the masonry, the same small shells lodged in its uneven surface. White bacterial threads grow along its lower edge. A crab has been sheltering beneath one end for several days.

Then the whole length loosens from the wall.

It does not fall. Along its underside, a broad muscular foot releases in sections, leaving the animal attached at either end while its middle sags into the passage. Water enters the space behind it. Two pale folds extend from what had seemed to be a damaged corner, touch the floor, and remain there.

The animal is longer than a person and scarcely thicker than a hand when pressed flat. Away from the wall, its body becomes fuller. There are overlapping plates along its back, each irregularly edged, each partly buried under growth. Between them the flesh is gray-brown and finely pleated. The plates slide over one another as it bends. They are thin enough to yield a little without breaking.

Its head is the narrow end, though this becomes apparent only when it starts to move.

Four feelers reach ahead. Two are short and stout, used against the stone. The others are finer, almost transparent, and trail downstream before the animal draws them forward. They sample the water at different distances from its mouth. A fringe of smaller processes runs along both sides of the body. In still water these lie folded into grooves. When the current strengthens, they lift.

There are no eyes. Beneath the skin near the head are two small organs that register a change in illumination, but the animal seldom encounters enough light to use them. It knows an opening by the way water moves across its fringe. It knows a living colony by the substances leaking from the feeding crowns. A ray passing above it produces a pressure change that reaches one side of its body before the other.

The city is full of such arrivals.

Most pass without requiring movement. A shrimp settles on its back. Fish turn beneath the adjoining roof. Something small works at the coating on one of its plates. The animal remains stretched between wall and floor, receiving all of this through its skin.

It has stayed under the arch through two feeding pulses. Before that, it spent a long interval in a chamber farther below, where warm water seeped through a crack behind a buried column. Its belly still carries fine mud from that room.

Now there is food in the avenue.

It advances by gathering its foot beneath itself and spreading it forward again. The motion is nearly continuous, but different parts of the body are doing different things: the head exploring, the middle gripping, the rear slowly releasing its hold. A little mucus seals the foot against rough stone. On polished surfaces it needs less.

Behind it, the crab retreats into a joint that the animal’s body had kept free of silt.

Its mouth is on the underside of the head. A broad, rasping ribbon passes over the surface of the avenue, lifting bacterial film, settled particles, and the soft remains of animals too small to distinguish. The mouth follows the water’s recent work. Where a pulse has laid a narrow drift of food against a threshold, the animal turns and feeds along it.

Living crowns close as its head approaches. Most reopen after it passes. A few tubes lose their occupants.

It feeds without clearing everything. Its mouth cannot reach deeply into cracks, and the irregular edges of its foot push some sediment aside while pressing other sediment into the floor. Behind it is a pale, interrupted track. Shrimp enter this track almost at once. Their legs find particles the mouth has missed.

The animal carries part of the settlement with it.

Small builders occupy its older plates. Their tubes rise from places where the shell no longer grows quickly. Near the rear, a colony has formed a low ridge across three plates, joining them so firmly that this part of the animal bends less readily than the rest. It can tolerate the restriction for now. Eventually the ridge will crack, or the animal will grind it away beneath a low roof.

Its living surface is not uniformly hospitable. Around the edges of the plates, where new shell is being laid down, the skin gives off something that discourages attachment. Elsewhere there are bacteria, tiny grazers, and a pair of worms that withdraw into holes whenever the animal brushes the ceiling.

Its back remains behind in the water it has just crossed. A disturbance there takes time to reach the head.

There are others in the basin.

They are widely spaced during the still intervals. Some occupy the undersides of terraces; some fit themselves into collapsed arches, where their backs complete the shape of the rubble. One has grown inside a circular room and can no longer leave by the passage through which it entered when young. Food reaches it from above. Its plates have thickened, and the room’s little filter-feeders live along them.

Their sizes vary greatly. The youngest are slender enough to pass between the tubes of the builders. They graze the films there and vanish when a crab begins to pick at the colony. Larger animals use the sheltered routes through the old foundations. A few have backs so heavily occupied that, moving across an open flat, they leave a wake of trembling feeding crowns.

They encounter one another most often after strong currents.

At such times they pause with their heads close together. The long feelers pass over each other’s skin. Each can detect where the other has fed, something of its condition, and whether it is ready to breed. Sometimes they remain together for hours. Their bodies make a pocket of slower water, and small animals gather in the shelter.

Eggs are laid beneath overhangs in folded ribbons. The ribbons are almost clear when fresh. Silt soon settles on them, outlining each little chamber.

An adult may remain beside a ribbon through several changes of current, scraping the adjacent stone and keeping an approach open with its foot. It leaves when food becomes scarce. The eggs continue developing in the warmth.

Most young are eaten. Some are drawn into the wells. Others find their way into chambers where the water is nourishing but insufficiently renewed, and disappear there. Those that survive begin by keeping one side of their bodies against a surface. Even when a passage offers abundant food, they seldom venture into its middle.

An older animal remembers routes through repeated contact. It slows before a familiar sharp turn. It lifts its head where a ceiling used to descend. If a wall collapses across its way, it may explore the obstruction for a long time, returning to the same edges before finding another passage.

The one beneath the arch has used this avenue often.

It approaches the quarter where the spring is changing course. The water touches its left side more firmly than before. Its left-hand fringe rises. The feelers sweep toward the wall that has begun to thicken, and the head follows them.

For a while it feeds at the colony’s base. The crowns withdraw above its mouth. Shrimp scatter a short distance, then return behind it.

Across the passage, the older wall offers less. The animal reaches toward it once, holding a feeler in the weaker current. Then it draws the feeler back.

By the next still interval it has settled beneath the growing wall. Its body fits into a shallow recess that did not exist on its previous visit. There is room for most of it, with the rear plates extending across the floor.

Over time, this recess will narrow. Fresh tubes will descend along its edges. The animal will continue to use it while it can, leaving to feed and returning by the warmth and the familiar pressure against its skin.

One day it will return, press its head into the opening, and find that its shoulders no longer follow.

For now, it spreads its foot against the stone. The fringe folds down. On its back, the small crowns open into the current.
