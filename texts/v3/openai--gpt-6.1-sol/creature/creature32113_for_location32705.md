The keeper is usually mistaken for someone kneeling.

There is a dark curve beneath a propagation bench, the broad back of a coat, perhaps, and one arm reaching into the plants. Visitors wait for it to straighten. When it does not, they look elsewhere for a person to ask.

It can straighten, though not completely. The roof has not limited its height; the benches have determined its posture. Its shoulders fit beneath them, and its spine, if that is the right word for the succession of firm joints under its back, holds a long forward bend. Standing in an aisle, it is scarcely taller than the handrail at the bottom of the stairs. It has to turn sideways where the routes narrow.

Its outer surface resembles several things that have been wet for a very long time. In places it is smooth and brown, like the skin of a soaked seed. Across the shoulders it is fibrous. Pale seams run down its sides. Dirt collects in these seams, and little roots enter them, though not all the roots belong to the keeper.

The workers do not agree about that distinction.

They are careful when cutting anything close to it.

It has two hands. This is the part of it everyone can describe with confidence. They have palms, thumbs, and long fingers with flat, tough ends. They can turn a tap, lift a pot, or work the spring catch on a pair of secateurs. When the keeper is occupied with cuttings, it sometimes holds six or seven stems between its fingers without bruising any of them. It makes the insertion holes first, then settles each cutting into the compost with two small presses.

Its face is less definite. There is a place at the front of its head where the surface becomes finer and gathers into shallow folds. Two dark recesses are usually taken for eyes. Whether it sees with them is uncertain. A worker once covered them with a strip of cloth while trying to clean away a fungal growth, and the keeper continued sorting pots by size.

It recognizes people, but shoes seem to matter more than faces. New boots cause a hesitation. A familiar pair set beside the stair will sometimes draw it out before their owner has come down.

No one has heard it speak.

This is not as inconvenient as it would be elsewhere. Conversation in the growing house is already an affair of repetition and gestures. The keeper points. It lays a hand over the valve that should not yet be opened. If a tray has been put beneath the wrong lamp, it moves the tray. When it needs assistance with something heavy, it waits in the aisle with both hands resting on the object until someone understands.

It is capable of waiting for most of a shift.

***

There is no record of its arrival.

The oldest surviving inventory describes a *resident stock attendant*, followed by a number and a note about keeping the western service passage unobstructed. That might refer to a person. A later sheet lists an allowance of coarse gravel, cotton binding, and two replacement sleeves under the same number.

The sleeves are puzzling. It does wear them, though never with any other recognizable clothing. They are made of stout blue cloth and tied above the elbows, protecting the forearms when it reaches among thorned stems or carries gritty pots. Fresh ones are stiff enough to impede its fingers. It leaves them beside the settling tank until they soften.

One hangs there now, empty, with a green stain spreading along the cuff.

There used to be an elderly man who claimed to have worked with the keeper when it was smaller. He said it had lived in a shallow box under the warming pipes and had been moved out of the way with a shovel.

He was not consistent about the date.

What is known is that the keeper has outlasted three complete changes of staff, the replacement of the main pump, and an unsuccessful attempt to convert half the court to storage. During that last period, boxed office furniture occupied two aisles. The keeper kept moving the boxes into the service passage. Workers moved them back. After several weeks, damp entered the cardboard, and the proposal was abandoned.

It did not seem pleased. It simply resumed using the aisles.

***

Most of its work happens low down.

It clears the mouths of drains, lifting away mats of fine roots and depositing them in the waste tub. It separates pots whose roots have joined through the drainage holes. It presses loosened moss back against the wall wherever a stream of water has begun exposing mortar.

It does not preserve every plant.

A sick specimen is removed early and without hesitation. The keeper carries it to the little tiled space beside the stairs, where incoming stock is examined. There it waits for a worker to take the plant away. If the worker merely shifts it to another bench, the keeper brings it back.

Its fondness, if it has one, is for the unremarkable plants that leave the building in large numbers: the ferns for shaded ticket halls, the glossy shrubs that endure heated corridors, the trailing varieties hung too high for anyone to water comfortably. It checks these repeatedly before dispatch. A pot may be lifted, turned, set down, and lifted again. Its fingers enter the compost at the rim.

Sometimes it removes a perfectly healthy plant from a consignment.

Those plants remain behind. They grow around the place where it rests.

***

It cannot spend long on the stairs.

Once, during a power failure, it followed the workers as far as the turn. Someone held the street door open, admitting a wedge of cold daylight. The keeper stayed with one hand on the rail.

Its skin began to change. The fine folds at the front of its head tightened. Along one wrist a seam opened slightly, showing a pale, moist layer underneath.

A worker fetched a watering can and poured warm water over its hands. It turned and descended.

There is now a battery lamp by the lower switchboard, and a written instruction that the keeper is to remain below during an evacuation unless flooding reaches the electrical housings. No one who drafted the instruction knew what to recommend after that.

The keeper occasionally climbs three or four steps to retrieve a fallen leaf. It does not approach the turn again.

***

It rests beside the central overflow, in a space too narrow for a person to lie down.

There, it folds its arms underneath itself and settles against the brick side of a bed. Several pale cords trail from its lower body. In motion they are tucked close, almost invisible. At rest they loosen and enter the wet growth between the bricks.

For a few hours it becomes difficult to distinguish from the bed.

Its surface darkens. Leaves that brushed its shoulders remain against them. Water runs along its back and drips from one elbow at regular intervals. It may be asleep, or feeding, or doing something for which the workers have no name.

They know it takes water. They know a pinch of fertilizer spilled over its arm left a mark that persisted for months. They have seen it hold a decaying leaf against the softer surface beneath its ribs until only the veins remained.

They do not leave meals for it. An early employee tried bread, milk, and pieces of apple in a saucer. The keeper tipped the saucer into the waste tub, washed it, and put it with the small pots.

***

The pump with the worn bearing troubles it.

As the note has risen over the years, the keeper has spent more time beside the partition, one hand laid against the pipe emerging from it. It can remain there through watering, deliveries, and the passage of an entire trolley of plants.

The bearing is on the replacement schedule. Other repairs have repeatedly taken precedence.

Last winter, the keeper began placing things on the maintenance desk: first a rusted washer, then the broken wheel of a trolley, then a smooth pebble from a growing bed. Each object was round, with a hole or a depression at its centre. The supervisor put them in a drawer.

The keeper took them out.

Now they lie beside the requisition form.

***

At closing time, the last worker checks the lamps and walks the service passage. The keeper usually follows a little way behind, making its slower inspection. Its feet leave broad wet marks that disappear beneath the next wash of water.

There is no farewell. The stair door closes, and it continues.

It tests the lip of the settling tank. It removes a fallen tag from a drain and lays it on the correct bench. Where the day’s movements have opened a gap in the foliage, it reaches in to feel the exposed soil.

Then, before resting, it visits the new cuttings.

Most have changed very little since morning. One has sagged. Another has lifted a leaf clear of its neighbour. The keeper adjusts a tray by less than the width of a finger, bringing its farthest row into the lamp’s narrow strip.

It stays there for a while, its hands on either side of the tray, waiting for the water to drain through.
