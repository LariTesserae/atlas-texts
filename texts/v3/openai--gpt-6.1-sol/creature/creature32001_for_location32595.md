The residents are easiest to mistake for residents’ belongings.

One sits on the upper landing with its knees drawn up, a folded length of insulating cloth over its shoulders. Another stands beside a cabinet, so still that the only evidence it is not part of the cabinet is a hand resting on the wrong side of the hinge. In the central court, three pale shapes lean against the tank supports. Then a relay clicks somewhere below them, and one turns its head.

They are not all made alike.

Most have a hard outer body assembled from small ceramic plates. These are dull cream when new, gray at the seams, with an occasional replacement panel that does not quite match. Beneath the plates is a flexible material the color of old copper. It shows at the throat, inside the elbows, and in a narrow strip down each side of the torso. Their hands have more of this exposed material. The fingers are jointed, broad at the tips, and capable of folding farther back than seems useful until you watch someone reach around a pipe to tighten a fitting.

Others have metal skins, thin enough to show a slight change of shape when they move. These residents are careful on the stairs. A burr can leave a crease that will have to be worked out from inside.

There are differences in height, in the number and placement of joints, in how a head is carried. One resident in the second row has no distinct neck. Another has a narrow, articulated stalk between its shoulders and face, allowing it to look above a tank without standing up. There are bodies with brackets still attached, though whatever the brackets once held is gone.

Their faces are less blank than they first appear. Several have dark glass set into shallow recesses; others perceive through arrays of tiny holes. A soft shutter protects each array from grit. Attention is visible in the shutters: a small widening, an uneven closure, one side remaining open after the other has turned away.

They do not breathe. They do pause.

A pause can mean that a calculation is unfinished, or that an answer has been heard and is unwelcome, or simply that someone would prefer to keep sitting where they are.

The heat suits them only within limits.

They take energy from the district’s warm surfaces, usually through contact pads in the soles and palms. You see someone standing with both hands against a wall, elbows slightly bent, apparently absorbed in the tiles. Someone else sits on the floor beside a cable box with the backs of their calves laid against its housing. The contacts darken with use. Older ones become polished at their centers.

But the same heat that sustains them can accumulate faster than they can shed it. Fine mechanisms lose precision. Seals soften. A hand begins to tremble while holding something small.

This is why the stools gather at the unfinished end of the excavation. The residents go there to cool, singly or in pairs, sitting among the chalk marks and capped connections. They leave gaps between themselves. A large body gives off enough heat to inconvenience a neighbor.

Conversation there is slow. Their voices come from vibrating membranes inside the chest or beneath the jaw. Some sound almost ordinary, though without the little movements of breath around the words. Others have two tones at once. A sentence can acquire a faint metallic accompaniment when its speaker is too warm.

No one raises their voice to be heard over the machinery. There is rarely any need. An argument may be conducted so quietly that, from the next stool, you hear only the ends of words.

The rooms are arranged for bodies that require rest but do not always need sleep. The folded beds have pressure contacts along their edges. A resident lies down, connects a wrist or the base of the spine, and becomes less immediately present. Their shutters close. Small adjustments continue under the skin.

A sink is used to rinse dust from joints, to cool a removable part, to wash a cup before offering it to a visitor who takes liquids. Not every resident does. Those who do sip carefully and hold what they have swallowed in an internal reservoir. A transparent strip in the abdomen may reveal the level.

They are private about some maintenance and practical about the rest.

A stiff shoulder is discussed in the court. A damaged hand is set on a table for everyone to inspect. But certain hatches remain covered even when the room is unbearably hot. There are knocks one makes before entering, and knocks one makes when the occupant has not answered for too long. The difference is understood without being posted.

Nobody here is very old, though several carry old components.

One resident has a chest plate worn smooth around a designation no one can read in full. Another remembers a stairwell that is not in this district, including the sound of someone descending it. They cannot remember the person. They sometimes ask newcomers whether they have heard a step with that particular uneven interval.

Such recollections are neither entirely trusted nor dismissed. A remembered instruction may still be useful. A remembered fear may belong to equipment removed years before the body was assembled.

The residents keep records of their own lives on strips of pale ceramic. These fit into shallow racks beside the beds. Some strips carry dense marks; others record only a date, a room number, the name of someone who visited. Moving rooms means carrying the rack yourself. People are reluctant to entrust it to the service lift.

They work on the district, but they are not simply its attendants.

There is a resident who tends the plants and does almost no other work well. They spill grit into hinges, forget where tools have been left, and can distinguish the vine’s older leaves by touch. They turn its tray a little after every substantial rain. No one else is certain this helps.

The pencil lines around the green film were made by a different resident, one with a careful interest in limits. They have also marked the comfortable position of a stool, the farthest a door could once open, and the height reached by a visitor from above. They dislike having these marks called measurements. Several record events that have not happened again.

Someone in a room behind the pump makes small objects from offcuts: a hinge that unfolds into six useless sections, a ceramic disk balanced on one edge, a shallow box with a lid too intricate for what it contains. These circulate through the district. They turn up beside gauges and on windowsills. Occasionally a practical component is removed to give one a better place.

They have begun to disagree about the next room.

A few want it kept as a common cooling space. One wants a door that closes and no duct passing through. Another has been preparing trays for the light beneath the work lamps. The bolts in the floor make some proposals easier than others, but have not settled the matter. Each evening the stools are dragged back, and the discussion resumes in a slightly different arrangement.

Above them, the city is audible chiefly through weight: a distant impact, a brief vibration in a tank frame, the occasional fine fall of dust. Some residents go up. They return with new parts, messages, or descriptions of weather.

One keeps bringing back descriptions of trees.

The others listen with varying patience. They know plants can become large, but the account of a trunk holding branches, each bearing smaller branches, all moving freely in air, remains difficult to picture. The teller demonstrates with both hands, then runs out of joints.

At night, the residents who are still awake move their stools closer to the warm walls. A few remain in the court beneath the louvers, looking through the openings even when there is nothing visible beyond them.

In the lowest passage, one person stops at the chilled pipe. They wait until a bead of water separates from the insulation, then catch it on a fingertip.

They have done this before. The drop spreads across the contact pad and cools it for a moment.

Afterward they continue home, taking care around the unfinished edge of the stair.
