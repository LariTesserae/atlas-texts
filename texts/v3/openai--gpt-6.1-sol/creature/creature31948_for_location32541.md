She is usually easiest to find by looking for the tools.

One will be missing from the rack, and farther into the garden its bronze shaft will catch the light. Follow it past the leaves and you will find a hand, then an elbow, then the rest of her, folded into a space that seemed too small to contain anyone.

She is neither especially small nor especially supple. She knows which leaves can be moved together, where a knee can rest without compressing a nutrient line, which rails will take her weight. Passing through the garden, she makes a succession of temporary openings. They close behind her without much disturbance.

Her name is Iven. It appears beside the pressure records, in an uneven hand. She writes with a grease pencil on ceramic cards, bracing the heel of her palm against the wall. Seven years of cards are stacked in a drawer beneath the gauge. Most record temperatures, flow rates, the slow advance of new terrain. Some contain remarks that would be difficult for another keeper to use.

*The west cups are holding less.*

*Leave the pale runner until after the pressure drop.*

*Noise returned. Third joint, probably.*

She was sent up to supervise the first filling. Before that, she inspected the tower’s expansion joints. She had worked at heights where a dropped instrument could disappear into cloud without making a sound anyone would ever hear.

For the filling, she brought two changes of clothes, a set of calipers, and a hard-sided case containing her personal things. The case is still in the service niche. Its hinges have acquired a green pile so thick that it opens slowly.

She has stayed longer than anyone expected, including herself.

At first she went down every night. Then the young roots needed watching through the dark cycle, and she began sleeping in the garden. Later, when the pumps were stable and the roots no longer needed her attention, she found that she slept better here.

There is a sling under the second terrace, sheltered from the main circulation. It supports her at the shoulders and hips. One foot usually slips free during sleep and rests among the roots below. She wakes when the pressure changes, sometimes a few seconds before the gauge registers it.

She breathes through a soft collar fitted close beneath her jaw.

Two narrow tubes run from it into the ceiling canopy. They are long enough to reach every part of the room and buoyant enough to stay above her, though they occasionally become entangled with the hanging ribbons. She disentangles them with the patient irritation of someone freeing a sleeve from a door handle.

The collar takes air from the reserve, warms it, and returns her exhalations to a different set of bladders. She changes its filters at the same time she changes the nutrient cartridges. Beneath it, her skin has a permanent pale band.

She can speak while wearing it, but speech costs more breath than she likes. Visitors sometimes mistake her pauses for solemnity. In fact, she is deciding whether an answer is worth the effort. Once she knows someone well, she becomes remarkably economical.

She will point at a valve, hold up two fingers, and expect to be understood.

Her hands are broad, with short nails and several old scars made almost colorless by the water. The skin on her fingertips is faintly ridged. She can identify most of the plants in darkness by drawing a margin gently between finger and thumb. With the fused screens, she uses the backs of her fingers instead. A fingernail could open three or four chambers before she felt any resistance.

She has learned to touch things with less force than she thinks they require.

This did not come naturally. During the first year she cut back a ceiling plant because its bladders were pressing against an inspection plate. The plant sealed its cut ends, diverted its growth, and obstructed two valves instead. For months afterward she had to service those valves lying on her back.

Now she places a warm pipe where she wants a root to go. She shades a leaf rather than shortening it. A bent frame can remain bent for a season if straightening it would tear what has grown around it.

She still prunes. The garden needs pruning. There are places where the water would stop moving if she merely waited.

The cuttings go into a fine mesh bag at her waist. She sorts them later in the service niche, keeping pieces that might take on the new terrace. Some are scarcely larger than an eyelash.

Her meals arrive through a small delivery lock below the stairs: sealed portions, mineral supplements, occasionally something chosen by a person rather than a schedule. She eats with her face inside a transparent hood connected to the breathing reserve. Crumbs float against the hood until she wipes them away.

She likes sharp flavors. She dislikes anything that flakes.

Once a month she leaves the garden for an examination in the tower’s clinic. The first few minutes out of the water are unpleasant. Her limbs hang heavily; the collar mark itches; every movement seems to involve a little collision with the air. She sits wrapped in heated fabric while the clinician checks her lungs, circulation, eyes, skin.

She is not becoming aquatic in any useful sense. She knows this. She keeps the emergency air supply charged and practices reaching the vestibule with her eyes closed.

Nevertheless, there are changes the examinations do not measure. She has stopped turning her head to locate a sound. She feels for its passage through a rail or rests her fingertips against the ceramic spine. She notices a visitor’s breathing long before she sees their face.

She can tell when someone is afraid by what they hold.

Visitors clutch living stems because they look substantial. Iven comes alongside, loosens their fingers one at a time, and places their hand on bronze.

She does not mind being grasped herself. People who panic often do. She has been kicked, struck with a mask, held around the throat. Once she is certain they can breathe, she waits for them to discover that nothing is pulling them down.

There are people she welcomes back. A delivery worker stays after unloading to examine the newest shelf. A technician brings recordings of music and complains that Iven never remembers which ones she has already heard. She remembers the music; she forgets the titles.

She enjoys company best when it has something to do.

By evening, she is often beside the dark patch in the service niche, checking the developing ledge with her calipers. It has advanced less than the width of a hair. She records the measurement anyway.

Then she puts the instrument away and remains there for a while, one foot braced on a step, her breathing tubes rising into the roots.

This is the place she first considers when something in the garden must move. A tray could be fixed beneath the ledge. The sling might fit above it, once the ports open. There will be a current along its lower edge; she has already left a gap for that.

She has made no mark showing where she intends to put herself.

For now, while the ledge grows, she rests her palm against its warmth.
