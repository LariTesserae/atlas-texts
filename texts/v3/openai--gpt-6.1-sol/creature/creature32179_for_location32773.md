The people who live here are easier to recognize by their movements than by their faces.

One carries a jar with both arms but never looks down at it. Another turns sideways before reaching the narrow place beside an oven. A woman crosses a courtyard with her head bent, although nothing hangs low enough to strike her; she is listening for a change in the water beneath the paving. A child running ahead of her stops at a white patch of ground, steps around it, then runs again.

They learn the town first through their feet. There are stones that ring, stones that answer dully, and stones one does not put one’s full weight upon. On the bare shelf, a safe route may be only a few handbreadths from a place where the crust will give way. Children are taught these differences before they are allowed to carry anything breakable.

Their elders sometimes test unfamiliar ground with the end of a stick. The stick is not necessarily wood. A long bone will do, or several short pieces fitted together. Good wood is too useful to be left leaning against a wall.

Most people go barefoot indoors and wear tied soles outside. These are patched with hide, cord, and whatever can withstand wet paving without becoming treacherous. A person’s soles may be grotesquely large by the time they are discarded. They leave recognizable tracks in the clay: the narrow waist of a repair, a doubled edge, the marks of knots.

In a dispute over who went into a pen after dark, someone will ask to see the footwear.

Their clothes hang close to the body. Loose hems catch on jars, drag in channels, and collect filth. Sleeves are tied back while working. The cloth is brought from elsewhere, much of it already worn when it arrives, and the people here are ingenious at making one garment supply the missing part of another. A shoulder can be dark wool, the back pale woven fiber, the fastening a polished fish bone.

There are households that take pride in neat seams and households that regard neat seams as a waste of good daylight. Their quarrels over this are as sharp as quarrels over drains.

Everyone is damp, but not everyone is dirty. Considerable care goes into keeping the difference.

A clean bowl is inverted. Drinking jars have covers tied to their handles so that no one can set a cover down and lose it. The hands that have lifted silt do not enter the grain jar. People who would think nothing of gutting fish beside a doorway may become furious at a child rinsing a muddy scoop in the wrong runnel.

They do not explain these distinctions every time. They seize the wrist.

The drinking channels have attendants, though no single household owns the water. An attendant may be an old man whose knees will no longer tolerate the pens, or a young woman paying off a debt, or someone entrusted with the work because they are known to be intolerably exact. These people lift lids, inspect the mouths of drains, remove dead insects, and notice when the flow is wrong.

They are not necessarily liked. They are listened to.

A visitor who mistakes a clean-water opening for a convenient place to wash is likely to remember the correction for the rest of their life.

Much of the town’s knowledge has this form: precise, local, and attached to a person. One woman knows how long a new footing must stand before a roof is laid across it. A potter can tell by touching a vessel whether the wall is thick enough to survive the heat of a particular oven. A boy knows which pen has a gap beneath its outer sill because he has put his arm into it, although his father insists that there is no gap.

The boy is right. His father will repair it without saying so.

Their voices are strong. From childhood they learn to send a call through noise without spending all their breath at once. Names are shortened to the parts that carry. Calls intended for a person across a basin rise differently from warnings meant for everyone.

Indoors, some still speak too loudly. Others become almost inaudible as soon as the door slab closes. They like the small luxury of not having to project.

There is a man who mends cord in one of the thick-walled rooms and tells stories so quietly that his listeners must lean toward him. Children are sent out if they fidget. His stories are not always good, but the manner of hearing them is rare enough to bring people back.

The roar also conceals things. A jar can break without a neighbor noticing. Two people can argue behind a wall while those beside them continue their meal. Lovers choose places where the water is loud; so do people discussing debts.

The town is intimate without being entirely informed.

Households extend into one another in awkward ways. A room may belong to the woman who sleeps in it, while the roof belongs to her brother’s household and the wall is repaired by the family on the opposite side. An oven can serve five kitchens and still be spoken of as one particular grandmother’s oven. When she dies, someone will continue to call it hers.

Marriage does not always mean moving far. Sometimes it means cutting a doorway where there was a shelf, shifting the grain jars, and persuading an uncle to sleep elsewhere. Sometimes it means leaving for the pale roofs across the lagoon, a distance short enough to see and long enough to resent.

There are people who have never gone beyond the cliff. There are others who return down the road with cracked lips and stories of places where water must be carried uphill. Both groups find the other insufficiently aware of ordinary good sense.

The carriers bring grain, cloth, timber, and news. They take away dried fish, shell lime, salt, and goods made by hands accustomed to fine work in dim rooms. They arrive with dust in their clothes. For a little while, that smell follows them through the wet passages.

Children crowd around the loads, hoping for a spilled handful or an unfamiliar fastening. Adults inspect the timber before asking about distant troubles.

A beam can change a household’s plans for years.

The animals have lives interwoven with these arrangements, but not all of them are treated as possessions.

Dogs belong to doorways, benches, and people who habitually drop food. A dog may accompany a particular fisherman for months and then transfer its loyalty to the woman cleaning his catch. They sleep heavily despite the noise. When something unusual happens, the first sign is often a dog lifting its head while everyone else continues working.

Cats receive less instruction. People leave them gaps in the storehouse walls and become angry when they fail to use them. A cat carrying a rat across the paving is watched with satisfaction; a cat carrying a small fish is pursued with whatever lies nearest.

The pigs know the sound of baskets being lifted. They know who carries scraps generously and who arrives with little. Some begin squealing before the carrier comes into view. Others stand on their hind legs against the partition and wait in a silence that people find almost reproachful.

Birds are tolerated, cursed, robbed, fed by accident, and occasionally mourned. Particular ones become recognizable: a gull with a crooked foot, a dark bird that steals only from one landing, an old pale bird that holds its stake against all challengers.

Children name them. Adults claim not to, then use the names when no children are present.

The creatures below the water are less distinct to the casual eye. Those who work the pens distinguish them by appetite, season, and the way they gather against a wall. A catch that seems abundant to an outsider may worry a keeper because the wrong fish have come in.

The keepers spend long stretches appearing idle. They watch foam, backs, ripples, the angle at which small bodies turn. They lower a hand into the water and leave it there.

Then, all at once, several people move.

There is beauty here, though it is not generally displayed in large things. A repaired wall may contain one pink shell placed where the person who sleeps beside it will see it on waking. A jar handle can bear the thumbprint of someone long dead. A woman polishes the bone fastening at her throat every evening, although the rest of her clothing is work clothes. Someone scratches a bird on the underside of a roof slab before it is set in place.

The bird will remain above the room for years, visible only to a person lying down.

The town has its idlers, its generous neighbors, its people who borrow bowls and return excuses. Some keep their thresholds immaculate while letting the shared gutter choke. Some do good work only when watched. Some are trusted with another household’s children but never with its tools.

There are sleepers who wake at the slightest scrape, despite living their whole lives above the springs. There are old people who complain that the roar was different when they were young. No one can prove them wrong.

And there are those who love the town with an impatience that makes them threaten to leave it every season.

They know which walls will need attention, which cousin will fail to help, which passage will smell unbearable when the warm weather deepens. They know how little room there is. Still, when they return from even a short absence, they pause before entering the dense lanes.

Someone calls from a roof. A door grinds open. There is a bowl waiting to be washed, a footing to inspect, a child who has grown confident enough to cross a channel alone.

They put down what they have brought and find out what happened while they were away.
