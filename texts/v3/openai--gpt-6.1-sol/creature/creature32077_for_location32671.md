The first part of it you notice is a little ridge moving against the direction of the wind.

It advances under the leaves, stops, and becomes indistinguishable from a fallen stem. A few moments later, something farther off moves in the same way. You might reasonably take these for two animals. Even after you have seen it clearly, you will not be sure how many there are.

Most of its body lies within the upper layers of old growth. It is thin and loosely jointed, pale brown where the light reaches it, almost translucent underneath. Its segments are neither quite alike nor arranged with any obvious regularity. Some are no wider than a sedge blade; others spread into soft, flattened pads. Between them run narrow lengths of tissue, flexible enough to follow the spaces around roots without shifting them.

It has no surface that stays clean. Fine particles lodge in its folds. Moss grows on the older pads, and creeping stems occasionally root through them. When one of these rooted parts moves, it moves very slowly, drawing the stem along until the next joint finds room. A patch of leaves can travel several inches in a summer without losing its appearance of belonging exactly where it is.

The smaller, freer portions move more quickly. These are what disturb the cover near your feet. They pass between the living plants and the dead ones, extending into a gap, thickening there, then pulling the rest of themselves forward. In a place where the litter is tightly packed, they may spend hours working past a single twig.

There are heads, although finding one does not establish where the body ends.

A head is a blunt, soft projection with several short feelers held close against it. Under a lens, the feelers are furred with finer branches. They examine water, exposed root tips, the undersides of leaves. There are two dark pits that may detect light. There is also a mouth, small and puckered, usually pressed against something rather than visibly open.

It feeds on the softened margins of dead plant material. A leaf skeleton left in its passage is almost intact. It takes the thin films from buried wood, the yielding tissue inside a broken stem, perhaps the minute animals living among these things. Its feeding is too slow to watch comfortably. You can keep your attention on the mouth for several minutes and see nothing happen, then look away and find that it has moved to the other side of the leaf.

It is fed upon in turn. Beetles bite the narrow connections. A shrew can pull a whole length free and carry it away, trailing moss. Such injuries do not produce much commotion. The exposed ends contract and seal; the surviving portions continue in different directions. Sometimes they meet again. Sometimes each develops another feeding head.

This makes its age difficult to say.

There are parts of it that have probably formed since the last rain. Others lie beneath the oldest moss cushions, broad and nearly motionless, with the worn, dark appearance of something that has spent many winters in contact with the same wood. These older portions remain alive. If uncovered, they draw moisture across their surface and slowly close the folds that the light has opened.

It does not gather itself for winter. It loosens. The active lengths withdraw below the leaves, while the larger pads flatten into sheltered spaces. Connections become finer, and some break. In spring, what remains begins reaching outward again. Two portions encountering one another may join along their moist edges. Whether they belonged together before seems to make no difference.

The machinery beyond the trees enters its life as vibration.

When the heavy pulse begins, the feelers settle against the heads. The narrow connecting lengths draw taut, and much of the body becomes still. After a while it resumes feeding. A sudden clatter brings another pause, shorter this time. It has lived through enough repetitions that the sound no longer causes a full withdrawal, though a dropped branch, transmitted directly through the mat, can empty several square feet of moving tissue in an instant.

On days when the machinery stops, it extends farther upward. You may see a head resting among the sedges in plain daylight, its feelers slowly separating. It stays there until the leaves dry. Then it lowers itself through an opening too small to be visible once it has passed.

It is sensitive to dryness in a way that makes the whole hollow a shifting arrangement of available rooms. A shaded branch, a fold beneath an overlapping leaf, the damp base of a moss cushion: each can hold a portion of it through the afternoon. After rain, these separated shelters become connected, and it moves more freely. For an hour or two, there are delicate tracks everywhere beneath the plants.

An alder seedling sometimes takes root through one of its pads. For a season they grow together. The seedling lifts its first leaves; the pad thickens around the little roots. Later, when the seedling dies, the roots soften and are consumed. The place remains occupied.

If you put your hand down, it usually withdraws before you feel it. Occasionally a cool strand touches the side of a finger. There is a faint muscular resistance, then a slow slipping away. It leaves no slime, only a few grains of dark litter.

You could spend a long time beside the hollow and never see more than that. A contact, a lifted leaf, a short length disappearing. Meanwhile, under the plain green surface, one portion is feeding, another is being eaten, another is joining something it has encountered in the dark. A head reaches the underside of your footprint and waits there while the compressed leaves rise.
