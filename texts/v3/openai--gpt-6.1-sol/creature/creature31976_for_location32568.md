At first, the inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from things they have set out to dry.

One stands beside the long work court with its back to the light. It is tall and narrow, brown along one side, greenish along the other, with several loose strips hanging from its shoulders. It might be a bundle of fiber propped against a post. Then a hand closes around the handle of a tray. The strips draw together. The figure turns, lifts the tray, and carries it under the roof.

Others are easier to recognize once this first movement has been seen. There is one sitting on the reservoir steps, its feet in the water. Two are crossing a court with a jar suspended between them. A smaller one stands almost motionless beneath a climbing plant, holding a stem while an adult winds a tie around it.

They are not made of wood, although parts of them harden into something like it. Their bodies consist of living cords gathered around a softer interior. Across the chest and along the limbs, these cords run close together; at the joints they separate slightly, leaving room for a bend. The outer surface is smooth on new growth and finely ridged on old. It holds moisture without shining.

Their proportions vary more than the proportions of a single animal species would. Some are broad through the middle, with short, stout legs. Others have long arms and a slight twist to the body. An old inhabitant may carry most of its weight on three supporting limbs, one of which began as a branch from the hip many years before. These variations are ordinary. Doorways, stools, handles, and steps have been made to accommodate them.

Most have two hands. A hand divides into four or five flexible tips, each blunt and slightly flattened where it meets an object. There are no nails. A tip pressed against wet clay leaves a faint pattern of parallel lines.

Their feet spread when they bear weight. On smooth paving the edges lie flat; on rough stone, short gripping fibers emerge from underneath. These are retracted before the foot lifts. Crossing a courtyard, they make a series of soft, definite contacts, with none of the scrape of a sole.

They wear little that could be called clothing. The loose shoulder strips are their own outer growth, thin sheets that become dry at the edges and are eventually shed. Woven bands hold these clear of the arms during work. Around the waist, a belt carries small tools, a folded cloth, perhaps a glazed cup. People working in the gardens wrap their lower limbs against gritty water. Those splitting stone wear thick woven sleeves.

A new band may be dyed yellow or a deep red-brown. There are inhabitants who keep theirs bright, and others whose bands are so old that the color survives only beneath a knot.

Their faces occupy the upper part of a thickened stem. Two shallow, dark surfaces receive light beneath a projecting fold. These are eyes, though there is no white around them and little movement within them. To look closely, an inhabitant turns its whole head. To follow something small, it often leans.

Below the eyes is a narrow opening, usually closed. It is used for taking in soft food and clean water, but not for speech. There are no teeth.

They eat cooked grain, crushed leaves, the soft flesh of garden fruits, and thick preparations made by leaving roots in covered jars until they break down. Food is ground thoroughly. At meals, a person holds a bowl against the opening and remains still for some time. Between portions, the bowl is set down and the others may continue talking.

Their green surfaces receive something from daylight, but not enough to sustain a working body. A day spent in the shade is harmless. Many days without light leave them pale and slow. This is one reason the town is spread out: each house needs more than a doorway onto a narrow street. It needs an open place where its occupants can sit without being shaded by their neighbors.

In the morning, several members of a household may stand along the pale wall of a court. They turn occasionally. One does so with a bowl still in its hand. Another has brought a broken gate to mend. A young one, bored with standing, moves into the garden and must be called back.

Their voices come from the upper chest.

A pair of thin, taut surfaces within the body can be drawn against one another, producing low, rounded sounds. These carry poorly through walls but very well across the still courts. Speech has few sharp consonants. Its distinctions lie in changes of pitch, length, and the small interruption of a tone by another.

Someone listening closely inclines the head and raises one shoulder. Someone calling across a square cups a hand against the chest.

There is laughter. It is a short, uneven sequence of notes, often accompanied by a loosening of the shoulder growth. In a quiet court, one person’s laughter can start another’s at a considerable distance.

They also knock on things. A wooden bar struck against the reservoir rim announces that a channel is about to be opened. Two taps on a doorpost mean that the visitor has brought a load and needs help. The work court has a flat stone with a hollow beneath it; striking different parts gives several plainly distinct notes.

The town is quiet because its sounds are made by those who live there, for purposes that can usually be understood.

Water matters to them differently according to where it has been.

The warm water from the rock is useful for soaking the feet and the lower limbs. It enters through the fine fibers there, restoring the firmness needed for walking and lifting. An inhabitant returning with a heavy load will often stop at a shallow basin before going home. It sets the load down, stands in the water, and waits.

But the mineral content gradually stiffens delicate tissues. For drinking, for tending new growth, and for washing the light-receiving surfaces of the face, they use rainwater from the cisterns.

The covered cisterns are therefore among a household’s most carefully maintained possessions. A cracked roof gutter is repaired promptly. A child is taught not to put an unwashed hand inside the cover. After a dry interval, the first rain is allowed to rinse the roof before the gutter is turned toward storage.

The channels, too, are maintained with an attention that can seem excessive until one has watched a person try to cross a court after a night of standing water. Too much soaking softens the outer cords. The joints begin to lose their definition. A foot put down badly may fold beneath its weight.

They fear this sort of damage. They fear drying as well, though in the warm, damp town it happens less often. They are careful with exposed edges, and few willingly approach the cliff alone. A fall would tear apart what takes years to grow.

Their young begin in shallow vessels.

A living knot is taken from the inner growth of an adult, sometimes with a second piece joined to it by another member of the household. It is laid in clean, damp fiber and kept under a loose cover. For a long time it does little except thicken.

Then it begins to make choices visible.

It turns toward a window. It withdraws from a touch. It keeps hold of one particular smooth stone while letting other objects go. The household moves it into a wider tray, then into a low basin where its first supporting limbs can spread.

No one calls it a child simply because it has grown large enough. The change comes when it begins to answer: repeating a tone, reaching deliberately for a familiar person, refusing to surrender something it wants.

Early names are short enough to be sounded without difficulty. Longer names are taken later. An adult may keep the small name at home.

Children do not remain attached to those from whom they grew. Each develops an enclosed interior and a surface of its own. Grafting remains possible, especially in medicine, but it is intimate and difficult. One person cannot casually share another’s injury, strength, or thought.

This separateness is important to them. A young inhabitant’s first private container—a cup, a box, a little covered jar—is given with considerable ceremony.

They live a long time, but they do not remain unchanged. Growth accumulates. Old supporting cords harden and must sometimes be cut away. New ones take their load gradually. A person may spend a season learning to use a hand that has grown differently from the one it replaced.

There are workers who once carried stone and now sort seed. Others, unable to walk far, keep account of the cisterns or teach children the channel turns. Their knowledge is not automatically preserved in anyone else. It must be explained, remembered, practiced.

Death is recognizable. The body ceases to hold its inner moisture apart from the surrounding wetness. Its surfaces darken. The cords relax and do not tighten again.

The dead are laid in deep soil beds beyond the inhabited courts. Much of the town’s carefully gathered soil has passed through these beds before being moved elsewhere. A few small trees grow there, each placed where a household wished to keep a particular name.

They are not solemn all the time.

People complain about the long detour around the central basin. They dispute the timing of water releases and whose roof shaded whose garden first. Someone takes more clean water than was agreed. Someone makes a beautiful jar that leaks and will not admit it. A young person wants to live on the farther shelf; an older one insists that the masonry there cannot possibly be sound.

At the work court, such arguments continue while hands keep grinding, tying, sorting. A quarrel may last through the preparation of an entire tray of fiber. When the tray is ready, the disputants carry it out together because it requires two.

Toward evening, the open courts fill more visibly. Inhabitants bring bowls, unfinished work, and low seats out of the houses. Children move between the garden beds. Someone sits with one foot in a runnel and the other on dry stone, absorbed in repairing a cup.

Across the valley, a small pale shape appears beside the distant line of masonry. For a while it remains there. Then another joins it.

In the nearer town, a person on a roof notices them and lifts a broad sheet of newly woven fiber into the light. It is held upright, lowered, and raised once more.

The answer is too distant to hear. After a little while, something pale lifts on the other shelf.

Below, the channels continue to carry water past the doorways.
