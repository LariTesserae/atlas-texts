The little furred animals behind the panels are not rodents, though the residents call them mice when there is no reason to be precise.

Their bodies are flat, their fur close and faintly luminous at the roots. Seen head-on, one has a blunt face, dark nostrils, and eyes set so far apart that it seems to be looking past whatever is in front of it. Its feet have several more joints than expected. These fold away when it runs. At rest, they unfold and lie along the surface beneath it, feeling for something.

They eat the soft skin that forms inside condensation pipes. They also eat moth eggs, crumbs, certain adhesives, and the blue chalk used by surveyors, which is why surveyors now carry their chalk in metal boxes. A colony can strip every blue mark from a newly installed stair overnight while leaving the white marks untouched.

They build nests out of protective film.

The film is pulled through openings much narrower than the sheets ought to permit and folded into chambers with dry floors and damp ceilings. Sometimes a worker loosens a panel and finds, behind it, an entire transparent settlement: little sleeping pockets, passages clouded with breath, a nursery suspended from the head of a screw. The young are almost hairless. While the adults are out feeding, the young hold one another’s hind feet.

Panels containing nurseries are marked with a crescent. Unless the machinery is failing, they are not opened again until the crescent has been rubbed away from the inside.

Nobody claims the animals do useful work. Nevertheless, a housing with a colony behind it seldom develops a serious leak. The animals do not repair the seals. They sleep beside the places where a seal is going to fail, and a good fitter knows how to read the arrangement of their bodies.

There are fitters who can do this and fitters who cannot. It has proved difficult to teach.

***

The human residents have ordinary faces, although the light makes them hard to remember at first.

They dress in several thin layers. At a distance their clothes seem gray or white; indoors, one sees plum-colored sleeves, yellow linings, trousers the green of bottle glass. Bright cloth is common in kitchens. Outside, it can make a person look farther away than they are.

Most wear soft-soled shoes with a narrow metal strip under the arch. The strip catches on ceramic when the gradient changes unexpectedly. Children receive their first pair after they learn not to follow their shadows around corners.

People here tend to stand beside one another rather than face to face. This leaves both of them a view of the passage. It also avoids the small embarrassment of speaking to someone who has become, for the duration of the conversation, several steps more distant.

They are not particularly solemn. A great deal of humor concerns badly fitted things, ambitious drains, the habits of foremen, and the enormous fish supposedly kept by somebody’s aunt in a washing bowl. Some of these stories are true. The difficulty lies in establishing which aunt.

Their homes are crowded but seldom cramped.

A cupboard may hold only six cups when opened by a visitor and the whole household’s crockery when opened by its owner. This is inconvenient rather than miraculous. Residents learn to lay out what guests will need before they arrive. Borrowed objects are handed over in doorways, not left to be collected from shelves.

The most prized rooms have two windows that agree about the weather.

In the mornings, people wipe moisture from the inside of the glass and set breakfast on tables bolted to the walls. There is bread with a tough pale crust, preserved fruit from districts below, fish paste, hot drinks served in cups with deep inward rims. Children complain about the same food being served too often. Adults glance at the clock and judge whether they have time to complain as well.

Laundry is a persistent concern. A shirt hung too near an unfamiliar wall can dry with one sleeve longer than the other. Usually it returns to its proper shape after being worn for an hour. Sometimes it does not, and somebody acquires a rag.

***

The long-bodied grazers are called lintels.

When standing, their backs lie nearly level with the lower edges of the reservoir housings. Their legs are narrow, and the joints are hidden so completely by short hair that their kneeling appears less like bending than settling. Each has a heavy lower lip with which it tests the grass before biting.

They feed in groups, but not on the same terrace. A herd may occupy five or six levels of the valley, its members visible through different gaps in the machinery. They keep in touch by laying their jaws against warm pipes.

Sometimes a resident resting a hand on a rail feels a slow vibration and waits until it passes.

Lintels give birth standing in empty tree sockets. The newborn lies curled in the circular depression while the mother grazes nearby. Birds gather above it. They pick loose hair from its shoulders, and their feet leave tiny dents in the soft, dark pads along its spine.

For the first few weeks, a young lintel has difficulty with distances. It will push its head into a passage and emerge from another opening with its hindquarters still visible to its mother. She waits. If it becomes distressed, she presses her muzzle against its flank until it comes back all at once.

Workers dislike finding a calf in a service shaft. It is not dangerous, but returning it can occupy a whole shift.

Old lintels sleep beside the same walls as the rest of the herd, though they no longer rise every morning. Their grass is brought to them by residents whose routes pass nearby. When one dies, it becomes unexpectedly heavy. Platforms that carried it easily while alive cannot always lift it.

The valley has several warm alcoves where these animals are allowed to remain. Their hair loosens. Moths arrive. Eventually there is room for another.

***

The children know more animals than the adults do.

This is partly because they spend longer looking into drains, and partly because they have not yet acquired a reliable sense of which openings are too small.

They keep translucent lizards in ventilation cages, though the lizards can leave whenever they wish. A child indicates ownership by putting a bead or a twist of colored wire in the cage. The lizard returns to sleep beside it. If it stops returning, the bead is moved to another cage after a suitable interval.

Nobody ties anything around a lizard’s neck.

There is a game played with the black parapet birds. A child places three bright scraps on a rail and turns away. The bird moves one. The child must say which without looking directly at the scraps. Reflections are permitted; shadows are not. The birds understand the game well enough to cheat.

A winning child is sometimes followed home.

The pale birds beneath the bridges are less companionable. They nest in crowded, immaculate rows and tolerate hands only when a nestling has fallen. Their eggs are long and soft when laid, hardening as they cool. A fresh egg held in the palm makes the fingers ache.

People return fallen nestlings by leaving them on the nearest overhead pipe. The parents fetch them after everyone has gone. No one has seen this happen clearly. A nestling too young to walk will disappear from the pipe and be found back in its nest, carrying a trace of dust from some intervening place.

Children occasionally try to follow them. This is one of the few things that makes adults shout.

***

The fish are not tame, despite appearances.

They lie in the basins with their backs near the surface, pale scales crossed by the moving light of the towers. Some have whiskers; some have broad, almost square heads. Their mouths open slowly. There are fish that have spent years beneath a particular tile, visible only as a dark edge at noon.

A person standing in the basin may feel one pass against an ankle. The contact is cool and solid. There is nothing beneath the foot except tile.

The basin cleaners work in pairs. One scrubs while the other watches the fish. If every fish turns in the same direction, both cleaners get out.

There is no agreed explanation for this rule. It has been retained through several revisions of the manual.

In winter, when the feeding lines to the trees run warmer, some fish come up through the sockets and rest among the exposed roots. Their gills work in the wet bark. Residents put low screens around them to keep lintel calves from licking their eyes.

By morning they are usually gone.

***

Among the people, the most familiar figures are those whose work takes them everywhere.

A woman with one stiff knee reads the condensation cups. She carries no instruments beyond a short ruler and a cloth. She remembers which cups were slow to fill last year, which have begun spilling at night, which receive water warm enough to support a film of life. People leave questions for her tucked under the cup rims.

A narrow man delivers replacement latches. His trolley has followed him for so long that it takes corners before he reaches them. He dislikes this and keeps meaning to replace the steering assembly.

Two sisters tend the trees. One checks the feeding lines; the other trims branches that have grown into places they cannot safely be allowed to occupy. They disagree continually about how much to cut. In the empty sockets, they sometimes leave a little water, though neither will admit to the other that she does it.

There are night workers with chalk dust in the creases of their palms, cooks who open their shutters before the first shift, people who have never gone below the third terrace, and people who return from lower districts with damp cuffs and no account of their afternoon.

Not everyone understands the machinery. Most understand their own part of it. A resident may be able to judge a flywheel by the pressure in his ears and still need help replacing a kitchen hinge.

They make repairs, keep appointments, raise children. They quarrel about noise in a place that outsiders find silent.

***

And there are occupants for whom no panel has been marked.

An empty socket stays warm after its feeding line is disconnected. Something brushes the underside of a freight platform while it crosses a dry passage. In an uncommissioned building, dust is pressed flat in several small places near a window.

Workers notice these things. Usually they leave them alone.

A newly opened room may remain empty for months, then acquire a moth, a thread of moss, a furred animal sleeping behind the door. Someone installs a light. Someone else, passing regularly, begins to use the room to eat lunch. By the time it appears on the district plan, it may contain two chairs and a plant brought from home.

At the viewing rail, people sometimes pause beside the bolt with the hanging drop.

Inside it the roofs are inverted, the bridges crowded. A lintel moves along a white wall. Laundry stirs behind a screen. On a ledge too fine to distinguish directly, several birds are settling down.

The person at the rail shifts closer.

The drop trembles. Nothing in it falls.
