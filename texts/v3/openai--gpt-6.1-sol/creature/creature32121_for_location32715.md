The people are easier to find by their possessions than by their bodies.

A coat hung in a recessed doorway. A drinking cup with its handle repaired twice. Three pairs of boots on a shelf, soles turned toward the room so that the grit will not fall into them. In a freight office, a chair has been moved out of the square of sunlight, but not very far. Someone will be back before the square reaches it again.

They do not ordinarily put their names on their doors. The doors have numbers, and anyone likely to visit knows which number is theirs. Names are written inside things: under a cup, on the lining of a coat, on the removable plate of a listening rod. A name on the outside wears away.

At first they seem uniformly dressed. Their outer clothes are pale, close-woven, and loose at the joints. The cuffs can be opened and shaken without taking the garment off. Fastenings are covered by narrow flaps, not because the fastenings are fragile but because cleaning thirty exposed buttons is work a person need not create. A worker’s coat may have a darker patch between the shoulders, where a load rests, and another at one hip, where a tool hangs.

Indoors, the differences appear. Someone wears an old red shirt that has survived so long it has become almost pink. Someone has sewn a row of dark shell fragments around a collar. The inside of a shutter is painted blue, invisible from the street and brilliant when closed.

They are people who have learned to keep certain things on the protected side.

Their faces do not all have the same expression. This takes longer to notice.

In the open air most narrow their eyes and keep their mouths closed. Conversation happens in doorways, under platforms, in the lee of a storage tower. Two friends crossing a broad district may walk together for half an hour without speaking, then stop beside a wall and finish an argument begun at breakfast.

They have jokes about this. A man famous for talking is said to have swallowed a sheltered courtyard. A tedious speaker is asked whether the listener should bring a chair or whether the subject will conclude before the next train.

Their laughter is often sudden and quite loud.

The stone does not make them solemn. It makes them choose where to stand.

***

Children spend much of their early lives on the floor.

The inhabited rooms are warm, and the floor is the largest safe surface available. Infants sleep in shallow padded trays that can be set down while an adult works. Older children push objects along the joints between slabs. They invent games with washers, scraps of packing, and the discarded mouth plates of grazing animals. A plate laid flat will skate; turned over, it catches. This difference supports an elaborate family of games whose rules vary between buildings.

They learn to distinguish a moving animal from a useful object before they learn to read.

A parent may let a child pick up a resting grazer, provided the child supports all six legs and does not cover the breathing slots beneath the shell. The animals dislike being lifted. They grip with surprising force and then release all at once. Small children laugh at the abruptness; older ones learn to hold them close to the ground.

There are rules about the soft scavengers. Do not take one into bed. Do not feed it from your cup. Do not keep it in a box without perforations, however often it escapes through the perforations.

Every generation discovers these rules independently enough to resent them.

Children are taught the railway before the cliff. They learn the shapes of signals, the places where it is safe to cross, the difference between a wagon that is standing and a wagon whose couplings have begun to tighten. A train may move more slowly than they can walk and still be something they must never approach without permission.

The edges come later.

The first visit is usually to a fenced outlook with a thick sill. The adult holds the child by the back of the coat, even if the child has promised not to climb. They point out a ledge, a maintenance door, a dark fracture on the opposite wall. They do not ask the child to take in the whole depth.

Some children are disappointed. The clear air makes the far wall look close enough to throw a stone at, and the bottom cannot be seen, so the height has no useful shape.

Others go silent and remain uneasy for days.

Adults remember which sort they were.

***

Water has a place in their manners that an outsider would repeatedly get wrong.

Offering someone a drink is ordinary hospitality. Asking how much water a household receives is intrusive. Commenting on the weight of another person’s recovery cylinder is worse.

A guest is not expected to finish a cup. The remainder is poured into the household return, without reproach and usually without discussion. But a cup left where it can be knocked over suggests a kind of carelessness difficult to explain away. People will forgive anger sooner.

Drinking rooms are social places. They have benches, noticeboards, charging sockets, sometimes tables for games. The padded floor beneath a dispenser is repaired with patches of whatever material is available, so that years of dropped cups leave a soft, irregular map.

Older people go at the same hour each day. They may have perfectly good dispensers at home. They go to learn who has arrived, whose shift has changed, whether the woman from the far switching station has finally had her knee treated.

Adolescents discover that the room stays warm after the attendant dims the lights.

Water itself is not always the subject of the room. That would become exhausting.

Their songs, when they sing, are mostly about people: someone who departed without settling a debt, someone who came back with a different accent, a child who would not sleep, an incompetent supervisor. There are songs about rain, but these are old or imported, and the words often have to be explained.

One child imagines rain as a fault in the ceiling of the world. Another imagines all the dispensers opening together.

A person who has seen it may be asked to tell the story many times. Eventually the account becomes practical: what clothes felt like afterward, how long shoes took to dry, whether the water could actually be left where it fell.

The listeners find that last part hardest.

***

Their work does not require them to be interchangeable, although visitors sometimes suppose it does.

In a processing hall, one operator can judge a mineral bed by the sound of the scraper against it. Another watches the edge of the powder as it falls. A third insists on taking samples and is considered irritating until something goes wrong.

The rollers move slowly. Decisions need not.

A variation that seems small at the beginning of a shift can become a great quantity of spoiled material by its end. Workers learn to notice what has changed and what merely appears different because a window shutter has been opened. They carry notebooks with observations too modest to enter in a works report: a smell near the fourth bed, a faint vibration in a handrail, the scavengers refusing a patch of floor they usually occupy.

These notebooks are often lent, rarely discarded.

The most respected workers are not necessarily the strongest or longest employed. They are the ones who can explain a difficulty without making the next person feel foolish for failing to see it.

There are also careless workers, ambitious workers, people who have stayed in the same position because it is near their home, and people who secretly dislike the whole business. One spends every break drawing narrow, impossible towers. Another repairs small machines with a tenderness absent from all his conversations.

A quiet factory can contain a good deal of quarrelling.

Their disputes travel through offices and along walkways rather than being shouted across the hall. Everyone knows which supervisor moves the benches to prevent people sitting together. Everyone knows which crew restores them after he leaves.

***

They live among the animals with neither constant affection nor constant suspicion.

A grazer working along a doorstep is usually left alone. If it reaches the door track, it is turned around with the side of a boot. Some people perform this operation gently; some do not.

Households recognize individuals by damaged shells or unusual markings. A broad animal with a pale crescent above one leg may have fed outside the same kitchen for twelve years. Nobody owns it. Nevertheless, when it disappears, the occupants look for it.

They ask the sweeper. They check beneath the neighboring steps. They are embarrassed if anyone makes much of their concern.

The scavenger colonies require a different attention. Works crews keep records of their numbers, feed them in designated recesses, and move the boxes when machinery is to be restarted. A colony that is useful beneath one platform can be ruinous beneath another. People argue over responsibility when it spreads.

Some maintenance workers develop a fondness for the scavengers that others find difficult to understand. The animals gather at the perforations when a familiar pair of boots comes near. Perhaps they recognize the footfalls. Perhaps they have learned that those vibrations precede grease.

The distinction does not matter much to the worker carrying the box.

Cliff animals are watched more distantly. Their journeys can interrupt a conversation for several minutes. Someone points; everyone turns; the dark membranes open.

There is no satisfactory way to say when one has arrived. The watcher loses it before it reaches the far wall.

Children are told not to claim they have seen the landing unless they truly have. Adults sometimes claim it anyway.

***

Their houses are not spare in the same way.

One room contains little beyond a bed, a table, and carefully maintained tools. Another is crowded with things brought by rail: colored glass, woven straps, a clock that has never kept time, six shallow boxes of shells sorted by a system understood only by their owner.

There are households where every surface is swept daily, and households where a line drawn through the dust on the settling shelf remains until someone comes to visit.

People keep souvenirs of places that no longer exist.

A piece of numbered doorplate. A small tile. A photograph taken before a fracture became wide enough to fence. Objects from abandoned districts are not necessarily valuable, but they can be impossible to replace. A woman may own the handle of her grandmother’s kitchen shutter, though neither the shutter nor the kitchen survived the move.

She uses it on a cupboard.

When people are asked to leave a building, they do not always agree about the danger. Surveyors bring measurements. Residents bring memories of earlier warnings. There are meetings, appeals, angry exchanges in corridors.

A family may comply and still return for months to look through the fence.

The houses on detached pillars are empty, but they are not anonymous. From certain viewpoints someone can still identify which dark window was a bedroom, which wall held shelves, where a neighbor used to sit in the afternoon.

Then the train rounds a curve and the houses pass out of sight.

***

They are capable of leaving.

The slowness of a departure does not make it less final. A person can stand beside a passenger window through several changes in the light, discussing luggage, relatives, the address to which a message should be sent. By the time the carriage begins to move, everything important may already have been said badly.

Some walk alongside longer than they intended.

Others go home before the couplings tighten. They prefer not to watch.

Passengers carry food, a drinking vessel, something to read, and a cloth for cleaning the window. On long journeys the cloth becomes important. A dust film can turn the view into a pale reflection of the carriage, and many people travel precisely because they want to look out.

Not all of them return. Letters do. Replacement parts arrive bearing unfamiliar maker’s marks. New words appear in the drinking rooms. Someone brings a different way of preparing the cultivated powder, and for a season everyone has an opinion about it.

The country is high, but it is not sealed.

***

At night their rooms disclose them more plainly.

A person comes home stiff from a walkway and lies with one shoulder against the warm floor. Someone else sits at a table, taking apart a latch whose small fault has bothered her all day. A child, meant to be sleeping, presses a listening rod to the bed frame and hears very little.

In a nearby room an old man uses a better rod. He holds its upper end against his jaw, changing the pressure until the scattered clicks become distinct.

He knows some of the patterns. Others remain uncertain. Feeding, perhaps. A grazer turning beneath a shelf. Several animals meeting at a seam.

The sounds reach him through the foundations of the room where he has lived for most of his life. He listens without needing each one to mean something.

Outside, a neighbor drags a chair onto a doorstep. After a while he puts the rod down and joins her.

They discuss a delayed shipment, then her sister, then a loose cover on a service duct. She thinks it should be reported before the next dust comes. He thinks someone has already reported it.

Across the street, a child opens a shutter and is told to close it again. Farther away, the train lamps have moved a little.

The two remain on the step. Beneath their feet, the grazers work along the warm stone, passing in and out of the small reach of light from the door.
