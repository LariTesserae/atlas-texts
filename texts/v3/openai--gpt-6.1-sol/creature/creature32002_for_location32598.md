The people who live here know one another partly by sound.

There is a woman whose tread can be picked out on the upper galleries because an old injury makes her set one foot down flat and the other on its edge. There is a grain seller who clears his throat before giving a price, even when the buyer is his sister. Children recognize the knock of a particular knife against a chopping board and know whether it is worth visiting that kitchen. With so many rooms hidden behind leaves, shutters, and other rooms, a person is often heard well before being seen.

They have voices suited to several distances. A low voice is enough across an alley. A call pitched upward will reach a sleeping platform without carrying much farther. During heavy rain, they shout directly into one another’s faces, then continue shouting for a little while after they have gone indoors.

Some are quiet by temperament. Some are astonishingly loud. The town accommodates both, though not without complaint.

Their clothes have been altered by the place and by repeated use. Trouser hems are rolled, stitched shorter, or darkened permanently by wet paths. Shoulder cloths hang near the cooking fires, warming without quite drying. A woman carrying greens wears a patch on one hip where the basket rubs; a man who works on roofs has reinforced his knees with fabric from an old grain sack. Children wear garments that have already belonged to several children, with the cuffs let down and the old fold still visible.

There are people who dislike being damp and mention it every day of their lives.

There are people who seem not to notice until they sit down.

Most keep one dry thing put away: a shirt for a visit down-valley, a blanket for a sick person, a small bundle of papers wrapped several times and stored above the smoke. The keeping of it dry requires attention. Someone must remember where the roof began leaking last season, whether the storage basket has been moved, whether a child has borrowed the covering cloth.

A good housekeeper here is not necessarily someone whose house looks orderly. It may be someone who can reach the lamp, the fever herbs, and the spare lashings in the dark.

The builders have broad thumbs, fine scars, and strong opinions. They disagree about the age at which a bamboo culm should be cut, about the right spacing of floor supports, about a bridge repaired before any of the younger people were born. One old carpenter likes to say that nobody listens to him. He says this while three people hold a beam exactly as he has instructed.

His daughter does much of the climbing now. She carries tools in a narrow basket that lies close against her back, and tests a questionable floor strip first with the end of her knife, then with her heel. She does not inherit all his preferences. Sometimes she replaces a joint he would have saved. Sometimes she leaves one he would have replaced. Their arguments can continue across two roofs and a public stair.

When the work becomes dangerous, they stop arguing.

Not everyone can build, though almost everyone can make a temporary repair. There are excellent cooks who tie poor knots, sure-footed porters who cannot keep a gutter clear, and careful basket makers who fear the lower bridge. The town’s smallness makes these differences widely known. It is hard to pretend to a skill when the result can be inspected from six neighboring windows.

It is also hard to hide a kindness.

A bowl sent to a sick household passes along a gallery where someone else adds a handful of greens. A child sent to fetch an aunt arrives carrying a message for a third household. A borrowed basket may make several stops before it returns, and its owner has to ask sharply where it has gone. Generosity and irritation occupy the same narrow passages.

The woman at one cookshop is famous for her broth and for refusing credit. She has refused credit to the same man for years and still serves him when he has no money. She does not consider this a contradiction worth discussing. At the next cookshop, a young couple keeps careful accounts scratched onto thin bamboo slips. They lose customers because they count too closely and gain them because their portions are reliable.

People compare the three kitchens every day. They have favorites, grievances, and loyalties that survive changes in the cooking.

The children know a somewhat different town from the adults.

They know where a hand can fit behind a retaining stone, where a shutter can be lifted without making its latch click, and which roof gives access to another roof that is supposed to be inaccessible. They know the hollow note of a loose plank. They know the little pools that keep tadpoles after the drains have emptied.

Their competence is real, but uneven. A child capable of carrying a full bowl up a wet stair may leave a younger brother standing in the path of a goat. Another who can identify several birds by their calls may be frightened of the moths beating against a lamp.

Adults watch them without always appearing to. A conversation at a stall pauses when a small body moves too near the landing. A hand catches the back of a shirt. Someone calls a child by a sibling’s name, then corrects it, then gives the warning again.

The older inhabitants retain a map of things that are no longer there. They point to a wall and describe the door it replaced. They remember when a particular bamboo clump was thin enough to see through, when the fig roots had not reached the square, when a household lived above the washing platform instead of beside it.

Their memories do not agree precisely. The same flood occurred in the year of a marriage, before a birth, or after the death of a goat, depending on who tells it. What they agree on is often practical: where the earth opened, how high the water reached, who had rope ready.

Some younger people want to leave. They have heard of streets broad enough for carts, houses with dry storage rooms, work that does not involve lifting wet things uphill. A few go down to the valleys for trade or employment and return with different sandals, unfamiliar habits, and stories listened to several times.

Those who return may find the place smaller than they remembered. They may also find that their feet still know every stair.

The animals recognize distinctions just as definite as the people’s.

The goat at the market knows the length of its tether and works at the edge of that allowance. Chickens learn which doors conceal food and which conceal a broom. Ducks settle in drains where the current brings small edible things, shifting grudgingly when someone clears the channel.

The macaques know the poles used to drive them off. They watch the hands that reach for those poles and retreat before a blow is necessary. A roof repair changes their route; a fruiting tree changes their schedule. Young ones make mistakes the adults have already learned to avoid. They lower themselves toward baskets too openly, snatch food that is too hot, and misjudge which human is paying attention.

The swallows live under the market roof with less negotiation. Their nests remain through arguments, shortages, celebrations, and the replacement of stalls below them. People look up when a nest breaks. A fallen chick can briefly interrupt the whole business of buying vegetables.

Other inhabitants are seldom named unless they become troublesome: ants in a food basket, borers in a beam, soft pale growth spreading beneath a mat. Snails travel the retaining walls at night. Tiny frogs occupy folds of leaves close to windows. Moss takes hold where hands and feet have stopped reaching it.

Each household keeps a small boundary against this abundance. Someone scrapes the threshold. Someone lifts the sleeping mats. Someone cuts roots away from a shutter so it will close.

By lamplight, the people are easier to see through the openings between houses. A man bends over a split basket, holding the new strip between his teeth while he frees both hands. Two sisters comb a child’s hair and argue about whether it should be cut. A boy lies awake beside his grandmother, listening to her breathe. In another room, several people have crowded around a visitor who has news from below.

Their rooms contain goods saved from years of replacement: a smooth-handled knife, a chipped bowl favored over sound ones, a length of cloth too fine for everyday wear. They contain things brought home that morning and not yet dealt with. Wet sandals wait by doors. A chicken mutters under a bench.

Beyond the lamps, the frogs begin calling. The monkeys settle higher in the trees, and the swallows press into their nests. Water continues beneath the floors.

A person gets up once more to move a bowl under a drip. From the next house comes the sound of someone laughing, then the scrape of a shutter being drawn closed.
