The inhabitants wear their clothes short at the ankle and loose behind the knee. Even those who own shoes often carry them. There are stretches of paving where the surface must be felt before weight is put on it, and a sole conceals the small tightening that precedes a channel opening.

Their feet are broad, though not uniformly so. A newcomer’s feet broaden too, after some years. The people disagree about whether this is a change in the flesh or merely the result of learning to stand properly. They have the same disagreement about their hearing. Children raised here can distinguish water passing beneath a room from water passing beneath the room beside it. Adults who arrive from elsewhere sometimes acquire this ability, sometimes only acquire the habit of claiming it.

They look ordinary at a distance: a woman carrying folded cloth, a stooped person with a basket, two children dragging something they have been told not to drag. Up close, there are small difficulties. The skin at an old person’s wrists has the fine, parallel creases of a leaf that has repeatedly folded shut. Some people have a pale seam running from the heel to the middle of the calf. It is not a scar, they say, although it can become sore in dry weather. Their hair is usually dark when wet and surprisingly light when it dries.

No one appears to be in much of a hurry until suddenly everyone is.

A woman may spend half an hour waiting for a walkway to finish rising, seated on her parcel with her chin in her hand. Then a certain knock sounds from a house behind her, and she gets up at once. She lifts the parcel, calls through a window, takes a child by the shoulder, and moves them both to another court. Two minutes later, the place where she was sitting folds downward and receives a sheet of water.

The child complains about being interrupted. The woman tells him that next time he may listen for himself.

Most of what the inhabitants know is taught this way, through interruptions whose necessity becomes apparent shortly afterward. They do have schools. These occupy whichever dry rooms are available near the market, and the lessons include writing, quantities, the preparation of food, and the histories of places beyond the basin. But a teacher will stop in the middle of a sentence to have everyone feel the floor. On some days the children are sent outside to find three surfaces that will still be surfaces by evening.

They return with arguments.

A roof counts, says one child. A sleeping animal does not, says another. A third has selected the gray-green clearing by the rim, and is accused of choosing something too easy.

The teachers allow considerable disagreement about the living ground. They are less indulgent about carelessness with seeds.

The dark seeds in the market are handled by a small number of people whose occupation is difficult to name. They are gardeners, certainly, but also surveyors and, on bad days, arbiters. They know which growths will bear a kitchen without drawing all the warmth from the adjacent sleeping room. They can persuade a passage to remain open for a season. They can make a basin hold fresh water beside another basin that tastes of minerals and old leaves.

Their tools are modest: a shallow spoon, a polished wedge, several lengths of cord, a hollow needle kept wrapped against the damp. One carries a flat piece of black material that nothing in the town will adhere to. This is valuable enough that people stop speaking when she sets it down.

They do not command the town. A seed placed correctly may still do nothing. A wall expected to withdraw may thicken instead. The practitioners return, examine what happened, and sometimes sit beside it for a long time. Their disagreements are conducted in low voices, with much pointing at things an outsider cannot see.

Among them is a man whose hands tremble so badly that he can no longer place the smallest seeds. He still chooses them. His daughter does the planting, impatient with his instructions until the moment she begins, when she becomes very still.

He knows several ways of making a threshold refuse growth. No one else in the basin knows more than one.

The inhabitants eat many parts of the place, but not indiscriminately. The soft green skin on a sunny wall is bitter and gives some people a fever. The pale tissue underneath may be edible if the wall has not carried standing water during the previous week. There are swollen nodes taken from beneath roofs, a sour membrane lifted from certain pools, small fish, crabs, eggs, and fruits that develop inside closed chambers and must be found by smell.

Harvesting a house is a domestic task. A person opens a cupboard, cuts away a portion of its back, washes it, and puts it in a pot. The cupboard is left open afterward. If it closes too early, the new growth is stringy.

Visitors are sometimes distressed by this. The inhabitants understand the objection but find its limits confusing. They will ask whether the visitor objects to all cupboards, or only this one.

Families are not attached to a house in quite the way they are attached to its contents. A cooking bowl, a dry blanket, a carved figure, a packet of letters: these are followed faithfully through the basin. Rooms are less reliable. A child may have slept in five different places by the age of six while considering all of them the same bedroom, because the same hanging cloth was moved each time.

There are nevertheless people who resent the changes.

One elderly woman has marked her doorway with a pair of ivory-colored pegs. Whenever it shifts, she removes them and reinstalls them at the new entrance, angrily. She has been doing this longer than most residents can remember. The town has never granted her request to stay still. She continues to make it.

People bring her news because she gives them tea, remembers their relatives, and is often right about trouble. She knows which pools used to be kitchens. When a channel begins to smell wrong, she can sometimes name what lies beneath it.

The oldest inhabitants carry a map no one else can use. They remember a public room under a sleeping house, an old orchard under the market, a narrow stair descending where there is now a long uninterrupted wall. Asked whether these things still exist, they answer according to mood. Sometimes they say no. Sometimes they say, “Not where you could get to them.”

There are objects retrieved from depths: a handle worn through at one end; a cup too heavy for its size; a square of cloth compressed to something like horn. These are dried slowly and passed around. Occasionally someone recognizes one.

That is a difficult afternoon.

The animals have different accommodations with the town.

The bronze frogs are tolerated in every room, including rooms where food is stored. A frog that leaves a niche is useful; a frog that returns to it is more useful still. People make room for both movements. Children name particular frogs and are repeatedly disappointed to discover that the adults cannot distinguish them.

The crabs are less welcome. They enter seams, widen them, and settle where a bowl or bench is trying to heal. Residents lift them out with two fingers and carry them to the drains. Some crabs return so consistently that they are taken much farther away, in covered baskets. This is usually done by children, who are supposed to come straight back.

There are thin, soft animals inside the walls that almost no one sees alive. They leave minute openings around their mouths, just large enough to produce a thread of clear fluid. A healthy house contains many of them. When the threads stop appearing, someone sleeps elsewhere and the wall is opened.

Birds belong to particular roofs for a few years at a time. Their nests may migrate from one household to another without the birds changing partners or losing an egg. The people who receive a nest are expected to leave access to it. This expectation causes quarrels. There is a man near the market who has been unable to close the top of his wardrobe for three seasons.

The larger creatures in the pools are neither livestock nor entirely wild. The inhabitants recognize individuals by their grazing tracks and by the vibrations of their calls. One is fed bruised fruit near the hall. Another must not be fed, because it begins pressing against doorways when encouraged.

On quiet evenings a broad muzzle sometimes rests at the edge of a court. Its nostrils open one after the other. It waits while people pass, with its small eyes just clear of the water. A resident may stop to scratch the filaments behind its jaw with the end of a stick.

No one stands between it and the pool.

The young leave the basin, though not all of them and not always permanently. There are routes across the low surrounding country, marked by growths kept short enough to see over. Travel requires provisions, patience, and knowledge of the settlements whose darker outlines appear through the mist. News arrives with travelers: a family has moved, a basin has become too warm, a place three days away has exposed a stone.

The stone attracts much more interest than the family.

Returning travelers are asked what it felt like. Was it cold throughout? Did it sweat? Could a fingernail mark it? Someone asks whether it was really stone, and this offends the traveler, who has anticipated the question all the way home.

The inhabitants have names for the deeper noises. Not all of these names are agreed upon. Some refer to a remembered occasion: the sound heard when a court dropped, the sound heard before the long dry season. Others are ordinary words applied with great specificity—turning, loosening, teeth.

When the loud cracking comes, they pause because there is information in what follows it. A single crack with no settling vibration may require only a visit to the sluices. A faint repeated clicking afterward brings people out of their houses carrying blankets and covered food.

Children are frightened by the silence of adults listening. Adults remember being frightened by it.

They have celebrations, and music, and visitors who overstay. They fall in love with people from inconvenient parts of the basin. They borrow tools and deny having borrowed them. During heavy rain, when rooms expand, a feast may acquire unexpected guests as an interior passage opens into another household’s supper. Some people welcome this. Others keep a screen ready.

Death requires practical decisions before anyone has had time to become accustomed to it. The body is washed, wrapped, and laid on a raised surface that will remain dry. People come carrying food. Someone stays awake beside it.

Later it is taken to one of the places where the older layers can be opened without disturbing the water. A trained person makes the opening. The family lowers the body. The growth closes slowly, over several days, and a relative returns until it has finished.

They do not say that the dead become the town. Asked directly, most find the question intrusive.

They do know that certain floors must not be cut.

At the quiet place near the rim, the low seat is generally occupied by someone who has done enough for the day, or someone who has not been able to begin. People sit there after quarrels. They bring infants who will not sleep. The seed-workers come when their judgment has failed.

There is often a small creature beside the pool, scarcely longer than a finger. It has four legs, a blunt head, and a translucent fold running down its back. No one has seen it eat. It is not always the same size. Children look for it beneath the seat when it is absent, though the adults tell them to leave it alone.

It moves only when water spills over the rim.

Then it steps into the shallow overflow and stands facing the town. For a moment the fold along its back opens, delicate and veined. The people on the seat may glance down at it. More often they are watching a doorway form across the court, trying to see whether someone they know has come out safely.

After the water has passed, the little animal folds itself again.

By then there is usually something to carry, somewhere to go, a person calling from the rain.
