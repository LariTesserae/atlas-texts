The people who live here carry traces of the town on parts of the body they cannot easily wash.

Clay gathers in the folds of their ears and in the pale crescents beneath their nails. It dries at the ankles, above the places where sandals rub. A basket carrier may have two clean stripes down the back of a shirt, where the straps have polished the cloth, and a crust of dust everywhere else. Children go gray at the knees before breakfast. Even those who work with grain or cloth have earth on them: a little falls from a lintel, a shoulder brushes a passage wall, a sleeping mat is shaken out too close to the morning’s mixing.

Their hands differ. The diggers’ palms are hard, but their fingertips remain surprisingly sensitive. They feel along a cut for grit, a buried root, a change in the clay. Those who plaster have smoother hands, often cracked beside the nails. The water carriers develop a way of setting a jar down without looking at it, bending the knees at the last instant so that the base meets the floor gently. An old woman who has spent her life doing this can lower a full jar more quietly than her grandson can put down an empty cup.

Most people have several occupations, although there are things everyone agrees should be left to someone who knows them.

There is a man who places the first stone in a channel. He does not oversee the rest of the work, and sometimes leaves before it is finished, but the stone he sets determines where the water will turn. He squats for a long time before touching anything. Younger workers find this irritating. They have seen him sit through an entire conversation, apparently idle, then move his stone the width of a thumb.

There is a woman who can tell whether a room will remain damp by pressing her cheek to its wall. She is called in before sleeping platforms are built. She has a crooked front tooth and laughs readily, except when someone tries to persuade her that a wet wall is dry enough. In her own house, a corner stays dark throughout the year. She keeps nothing there and dislikes being asked about it.

The inhabitants do not all possess the patience their work seems to require. Some strike too hard with the picks. Some plaster before the straw has softened. One young man repeatedly makes doorways taller than necessary, to the annoyance of the people carrying earth away. He is very tall himself, but that is not his only reason. He likes standing in a doorway and seeing sky.

People know one another by these habits as much as by their names.

They know who returns a borrowed basket with a repaired handle, who leaves the repair to its owner, who brings it back full of something. They know whose roof is safe to cross without calling down first. A household may be generous with its fire and jealous of its steps. Another will let anyone pass through the courtyard but becomes angry if a jar is moved even a hand’s breadth from its appointed place.

Courtesy here is precise. When entering a crowded room, you turn your hips away from the water jars. When passing someone on a stair, you put the load toward the wall. If a woman is feeding a child behind a hanging blanket, you announce yourself before your shadow reaches the cloth.

These courtesies are not always observed. There are arguments about shortcuts, about dripping laundry, about children who climb onto roofs and loosen the stones. Two households have stopped speaking because one raised a threshold and sent rainwater into the other’s kitchen. They still pass baskets to each other when the work requires it. The baskets travel across the silence with unnecessary care.

Children learn the town first as a succession of permitted and forbidden heights.

They may sit on this roof but not that one. They may jump from the last stair into the soft mixing clay, never into the settling pit. The uncut rib is a road, not a place to wrestle. The rim belongs to errands until someone old enough agrees to take them beyond it.

Small children are fascinated by the fields. From below, a person arriving at the bank appears feet first, then legs, then a face against the light. A child who has never been up often expects the whole country to slope away from that face.

The older children carry messages and small loads. They know passages that adults cannot use comfortably. They emerge behind kitchens, squeeze between a wall and a stair, and come home smelling of somebody else’s supper. They imitate the specialists: a serious pause over a spoonful of water, two fingers laid against a mud wall, an authoritative shake of the head. Their little houses are built in corners from rejected bricks. Usually they begin by making the door.

Births take place with people working nearby. A pick is put aside when the cry comes, but farther down the lane someone continues sifting straw. The first bath of a child is small and carefully warmed. Afterwards, the water is carried out to a pot or a bed of onions.

Infants spend much of their time watching hands. Hands twist reed, wipe a bowl, mend a strap, lift them away from a cold patch of floor. By the time they can stand, they reach instinctively for the wall. Their earliest routes are sideways, between a sleeping platform and a parent’s knee.

There are people who remember the fields before the hollow was broad.

They disagree about where things stood. One remembers a boundary stone where there is now a storehouse. Another insists it was farther west, beneath a flight of steps. A bent woman points to an exposed seam and says her father once sowed barley above it. No one doubts the barley; several doubt the father’s ownership.

The oldest inhabitants do not necessarily prefer the oldest rooms. Some like being near the new work, where there is always someone to call to and something to examine. Others resent every fresh passage cut beside them. A man who once dug all day now lies awake at the sound of digging. He says the workers have forgotten how to use their tools. His daughter says he cannot bear anyone beginning before he does.

Illness is difficult here. A cough can persist in a cool room long after the sick person feels otherwise recovered. Families move bedding toward a doorway, lend one another the dry corner, wait for sun to reach a wall. During a fever, someone may climb to the rim with a blanket and sit in the open wind. From the lanes below, only the blanket’s edge is visible.

For all their skill with earth, some people are frightened of it.

A boy will not sleep with his face toward the wall. A woman dislikes the smell of a newly opened chamber so much that she stays with her sister until it has been cooked in. One digger dreams repeatedly of a room with no entrance, though he has never been trapped. He keeps these dreams to himself and is meticulous about leaving two ways out of a working cut.

Others sleep better with earth close around them. Visitors complain of the stillness, but an inhabitant lying in a house above the rim may wake because the wind keeps touching the roof. There is too much distance outside the door. Nothing holds the sound in place.

The lean dog knows where weakness is.

It waits beside the old people, the distracted cooks, the children given more bread than they can finish. It avoids the woman who tends the onion patch, though she has never struck it. In wet weather it sleeps halfway up a stair, occupying the driest step and making everyone go around. Nobody claims it. Several people believe they are chiefly responsible for keeping it alive.

The swallows are less accommodating. They bring the fields down into the town in brief, sharp movements, mud in their beaks, insects caught over places the people cannot see. When the young begin to fly, work beneath the grain-house eaves slows for a few days. Someone always stops to watch.

At evening, the inhabitants cease to look alike.

The shared dust is washed from faces. A bright hem comes out from under a work garment. Someone oils a comb. Someone takes a small wrapped object from a niche and unfolds it on the sleeping mat: a bead, a letter, a piece of glazed pottery brought from elsewhere. There is singing occasionally, usually inside, and arguments continue after the lamps have been lowered.

In the newest room, a family tries to arrange familiar belongings against unfamiliar walls. The mat is shifted twice. A jar rocks on its base, and a child is sent outside for a flat chip of stone. The curtain has come from their previous doorway and is too narrow for this one.

When their neighbor strikes the earth, a little dust falls onto the father’s sleeve. He brushes it off. Later, hearing the work continue, he calls through the wall to ask whether they have eaten.
