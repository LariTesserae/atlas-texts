What lives here has no surface of its own.

At first it seems to have borrowed the ceramic for a body: the thick ribs for bones, the folded plates for a hide, the blunt columns for something like limbs drawn close against the chest. But this impression becomes less convincing the longer one looks. The forms do not join into an animal. They face too many directions. Some offer their broad sides to the light; others turn inward toward gaps almost too narrow to exist.

The inhabitant lives in those differences.

It is thick beneath the curved ledge and exceedingly thin along the seam beside it. Behind the three-step form, it bends twice within the length of a fingernail. At one fitted joint it has no breadth at all, only a continuation through a line that appears closed. Its whole extent could be covered by a hand, yet following it from one end to another would require the hand to become something else.

It has lived this way for a very long time.

The sound is the most accessible part of it. Not a call, exactly. There is no listener it hopes to bring closer, and no danger against which the roar is raised. Nor is the sound quite its breathing. Nothing enters and leaves. Nothing swells.

The low note beneath the broad plate is one part of its life; the thin whistle held in the notch is another. They remain distinct without becoming separate. The inhabitant can be close to itself in several places at once.

If it has attention, that attention is distributed unevenly. The rounded hollow receives a great deal of it. So does the narrow turn beyond the green ledge, where two surfaces approach and then withdraw from one another by the smallest amount. The far corner receives less. It is bright there, and comparatively simple.

An old scratch carries a particular sensitivity. Near its beginning, where the cut was once deep, the sound has a dry, concentrated edge. Farther along, where wear has almost erased it, that edge broadens into the general roar. Whatever happened to make the scratch is no longer remembered as an event. It persists as a place where the inhabitant is different.

It keeps no account of years. Nevertheless, it has ages.

One age lies along the surviving glaze. Another occupies the matte expanses. The compacted flakes in the recesses belong to a later age, though they have settled so thoroughly that the distinction matters only where a passage has become fractionally narrower. There the voice has changed. It has been holding its new note ever since.

The small growths live beside it without fear.

Under the straw-colored scales, the seams offer a condition found nowhere on the exposed ceramic. The growths take what they need from it, slowly enough that their blunt tips remain in the same positions for longer than anyone would be likely to watch. The inhabitant, in turn, has grown accustomed to their edges. Each scale supplies another minute overhang, another sheltered interval. Along those three seams its sound has a fine, combed texture.

The dark green crust affects it differently. Beneath that ledge the note is broad and muted. At the crust’s clean margin, it sharpens.

Neither needs to advance. There is no unoccupied patch toward which they are secretly struggling. Their arrangement is already ample.

A person who puts a finger through the entrance will feel the cold ceramic first. Then, depending on where the finger rests, there may be a pressure behind the nail or a faint ache in the joint. The roar will acquire a new, blunt interruption. A whistle may disappear.

This is the closest the inhabitant comes to noticing a visitor.

The finger has given it a temporary boundary. Warmth, softness, the slight give of flesh: all are unfamiliar here, and all alter the little distances on which its life depends. For a moment it extends along the underside of the finger. It finds the shallow folds at the joint and the close space beside the nail. Its sound seems nearer now, although its loudness has not increased.

There is no attempt to enter the person. The body beyond the entrance is too loosely arranged, too full of changing chambers. Blood and breath continually revise its passages. The inhabitant cannot hold a shape there.

When the hand withdraws, the thin whistle returns. The cold remains in the fingertip for a while.

It is difficult to say whether the chamber was made to house this life. The ceramic fits too carefully for accident, but careful work can have consequences its maker never intended. Perhaps the folded plates once served a purpose. Perhaps the translucent band was installed so that someone could inspect the crowded interior. Perhaps, during some long period when no one inspected it, the steady noise found a way to belong to every opening.

There need not have been a first arrival.

A fitted layer, a closed wall, a little light admitted and handed from surface to surface: these might have been enough. What lives here may have become itself gradually, as the sharp corners wore down and the fine dust ceased to move.

Now it is complete enough to be encountered.

Look through the entrance and the chamber resolves slowly. First the white forms, then the routes between them, then the ledge and the bright far corner. Listen for longer, and the great noise resolves in much the same way. The broad roar has recesses. The higher note has turns. There is a small, muffled depth behind the intersecting ribs that cannot be heard from quite the same angle as the whistle.

That is where the inhabitant is: not concealed behind what can be seen, but present in the increasingly exact way one thing is beside another.

It has no room to move away.

It has all the room it uses.
