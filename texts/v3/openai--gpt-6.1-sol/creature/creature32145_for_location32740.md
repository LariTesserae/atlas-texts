What looks like a passage between two trunks is occupied by an animal holding itself open.

Its body follows the narrow space down through the understory. At the top, where the light reaches, it is hardly thicker than a leaf. Lower down it widens into several translucent folds, each pressed against a different stem. One fold encloses a branch. Another passes beneath a cushion of plants without disturbing their roots. Near the ground, where you might put your foot, there is a dark recess.

The recess is not its mouth. It is the space between its forelimbs.

It has been standing there for eleven days.

During that time, smaller animals have traveled over it, through it, and along the edges of its armor. Water has collected on its upper surfaces and run inward. A tree has turned three leaves toward the sun. The animal has adjusted with the leaves, maintaining contact, so slowly that the movement could be mistaken for their own.

Then a patch of shade crosses its highest plate.

The plate closes. Somewhere below, a joint releases. The dark recess narrows until there is no longer anywhere to put a foot.

The animal begins to move.

***

Seen free of the vegetation, it would be a long, low creature with six pairs of limbs and an asymmetrical body. But it is almost never free of vegetation, and its asymmetry changes.

Its central organs occupy a narrow black column, insulated beneath transparent shell. Around this column are chambers that can flatten, swell, or fold back against one another. Pressure moves between them through muscular valves. A chamber emptied on one side makes room for a stem; another fills on the opposite side and becomes a brace. The limbs have more joints than are apparent, because several can lock together and function as a single rigid length.

It does not push its way through the forest. It establishes a new arrangement of contacts, transfers its weight into them, and withdraws from the old arrangement.

There is always a pause in which it belongs equally to two places.

On a difficult crossing, that pause may last most of a day.

Its feet are soft at the center and hard around the rim. The soft part conforms to bark, shell, or fresh stone. The rim divides into little hooked sections, each independently tightened. A foot can grip a smooth leaf without piercing it, or hold in a mineral crack too fine to admit a root.

It tests every purchase before trusting it. A faint tremor passes down the limb. If the surface answers with the vibration of living tissue, the grip relaxes slightly. If it answers as stone, the hooks close.

On an old ridge grazer, where vegetation and armor have grown into one another, this testing can continue for hours.

Adults are longer than a person is tall, though their actual bodies contain surprisingly little mass. Most of what you see is space enclosed by membrane: pressure chambers, insulating chambers, passages through which air is moved and moved again. Their bulk diminishes in deep cold. An animal that filled the interval between three trees at noon may, by midnight, have drawn itself into a thin bundle against one trunk.

The limbs remain spread.

They are waiting for the morning’s warmth before they take their weight again.

***

They feed wherever the forest has made a seal.

A leaf collar closes around a reservoir. Bark grows over a damaged conductive root. A grazer’s plate overlaps the next plate, trapping moisture and warmth beneath it. In each place there is a protected margin, and along that margin there are organisms living on what escapes.

The animal places its head against these margins.

Its head is small and usually hidden under the foremost chamber. Two broad mouthparts come together to form a ring. Within the ring, finer parts move independently: a row of scraping teeth, a stiff insulated tongue, and several pale feelers that enter gaps without enlarging them.

First it listens.

The feelers register pressure, heat, and the minute movements of bodies behind the seal. The animal may remain with its mouth in place for several days, waiting for a reservoir to refill or a colony to move toward the warmth of the surface.

When it feeds, the mouth-ring grips. The teeth open a slit. Warm fluid enters one passage; small animals enter another. Its tongue touches any exposed conductive fiber only briefly, then folds into a sheath.

It takes much less than the opening would permit.

Before withdrawing, it presses a clear secretion into the cut. The secretion hardens in the glare. Beneath it, living tissue closes over the damage.

The pale oval patches on old trunks are often its repairs.

Certain trees tolerate repeated feeding. Their collars thicken, and the animal returns to the same place season after season. Other trees respond by turning their dark leaf faces away, cooling the reservoir until the visit is no longer profitable.

An adult carries the history of these responses in the habits of its route. It may pass dozens of apparently suitable collars to reach one on which its mother fed.

***

There are creatures that live only on these animals.

The upper shell supports a powdery growth that turns violet during brief wet weather. Tiny grazers work through it in close rows. Under the limb joints, pale mites collect salt from dried secretions. A narrow, sightless hunter occupies the groove behind the head and emerges whenever the mouth opens, catching things dislodged but not swallowed.

The host occasionally draws a rear foot along this groove.

It does so carefully. There are small hooked ridges on the foot that remove hardened debris while leaving the hunter’s sheltered pocket untouched.

Along the lower chambers live organisms with dark, branching bodies, scarcely distinguishable from stains. They spread when the chamber is warm and contract when it cools. Heat passes through them toward the outer shell. Their descendants are carried from parent to young at birth.

Without them, a juvenile can stand in full sun and still remain too cold to feed.

***

They are not silent, but most of their sounds never enter the air.

A foot sends a pulse into the tree it grips. Another animal receives it through a branch farther along. The pulse says very little: weight here; movement beginning; this contact will be released.

Replies arrive with different delays.

Sometimes the delay is long because the other animal is cold. Sometimes it is long because the vibration has traveled through roots into an adjoining stand. A creature preparing to cross a fissure may remain motionless while answers accumulate from places it cannot see.

When it finally moves, others move with it.

A shell-dweller raises its edge. A small grazer retracts its head. Two trunks, held together by a web of living roots, bend apart by a fraction. None yields very much. Together they make enough room.

Young animals are poor at this.

Their signals are too strong, or badly timed. They disturb resting feeders. They put weight onto branches whose occupants have not yet moved. A juvenile often spends its first season wedged in a position from which an adult could withdraw easily.

There it feeds on whatever passes close to its mouth and grows into the inconvenience it has made.

Eventually its chambers become capable of folding tightly enough to release it.

The first successful crossing may take several weeks.

***

Birth takes place beside warm water, but not in it.

The parent braces across a crack and lowers the underside of its body toward the vapor. For days, nothing seems to happen. Moisture gathers on the limbs. The lower chambers become faintly clouded.

Inside one of them are the young.

At first they lie folded together, their shell plates soft and their feet clasped around one another. The parent circulates warmth through the chamber and admits a little water vapor with each pressure cycle. The dark branching organisms spread from its lining onto their bodies.

When the chamber opens, the young do not fall out.

They attach themselves to its edge.

There may be seven or eight, each scarcely longer than a finger. They remain there while their armor stiffens, drinking condensation and extending one limb at a time into the surrounding vegetation. Their parent cannot leave until the last has found another hold.

Some never do.

Their bodies are taken back into the chamber, and the opening closes.

Those that leave remain near the pool through their first cold interval. Among the congested banks, their narrow bodies occupy spaces too small for the adults. They learn the vibrations of drinkers overhead, the changing pressure of roots, the difference between a warm rock and a warm animal.

Long before they can travel far, they can tell when a drinking place is about to become crowded.

***

On rare bright mornings an adult rises above the understory.

It straightens several locked joints and lifts its central body into the sun. The transparent chambers expand. Light passes through their partitions, showing dark vessels, cloudy reservoirs, a slow succession of valves opening and closing.

For a little while its whole shape can be seen.

Then the warming shell begins to receive visitors. Small winged animals settle along its edges. Grazers emerge from the plates below. A neighboring leaf, turning over several days, comes to rest against its side.

By afternoon, the outline is difficult to follow again.

Only the feet remain plainly visible: one on stone, two on a trunk, another gripping the back of an animal that has not moved since winter.

And beneath the body, a narrow space is opening.

It looks passable.
