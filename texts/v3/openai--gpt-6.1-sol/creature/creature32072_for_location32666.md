The inhabitant is easiest to see when it comes out to clear the drain.

It does this in the morning, before sunlight reaches the concrete apron. First a hand appears between the lower leaves: broad, pale, with four fingers held close together. It rests on the concrete for some time. Then the rest of the creature follows, slowly enough that the leaves slide over its back rather than spring away from it.

It is about the length of a person lying down, but carries itself much lower. Its forelimbs are long and capable of bearing weight. Its hind limbs remain folded beneath the body, giving it, when motionless, the appearance of someone kneeling in a heavy coat. The back is rounded. Water runs from it in narrow paths that remain in the same places even when it moves.

At first these look like wrinkles. Closer inspection shows that they are shallow channels in the skin.

The head is small in relation to the shoulders and held forward, almost level with the hands. There are eyes, though you can stand quite near without finding them. Each is set beneath a soft overhanging fold, where a bead of water gathers and periodically falls. The mouth is a short horizontal opening. Below it, the throat moves with a slow, irregular swallowing that continues whether the creature is eating or not.

Its coloring varies with how recently it has been out. Under the grove it is gray-green, with a faint translucence at the wrists. On the apron, as its surface begins to dry, it becomes chalky. Old mineral deposits whiten the shoulders and the outer faces of its arms. Some of these deposits have cracked into little plates. Fern spores lodge between them; occasionally something germinates there.

It removes the seedlings it can reach. There is one small fern behind its left shoulder that it has never managed to pull out.

The drain is a narrow slot beside the building, usually choked with torn leaves. The creature works through it with two fingers, lifts the debris, and places it in a heap beyond the concrete. It does not hurry. It separates living roots from dead stems with considerable care. A root that has entered the slot is eased back into the soil, even if this means returning the next morning to do the same work.

There is a bent steel rod inside the pump house that would make the task easier. Sometimes it fetches the rod. More often it uses its hand.

Afterward it rests with its palms flat on the apron.

This is not fatigue. Beneath the concrete, water passes through an elbow in the pipe. The creature stays until some condition in that flow has satisfied it. Then it goes back under the leaves.

***

Its usual resting place is within the mound, in a hollow accessible through several of the grove’s low rooms. No single entrance is large enough to admit its whole body comfortably. It enters shoulder first, compressing itself between roots, and draws the hind limbs after it. The skin folds readily at the flanks. Its mineral crust does not; small fragments break away and remain in the litter.

The hollow contains no bedding. Warm water seeps through its floor and leaves by a crack at the far end. Much of the time the inhabitant lies submerged except for the ridge of its back. Fine roots trail over it. Where they touch the soft skin beneath its arms, they tend to branch.

It feeds there, although eating is an inadequate description of what happens. The mouth takes in mouthfuls of the sediment, holds them, and releases them again. The discharged material is slightly darker and settles more quickly. It also eats fallen flowers, especially those that have begun to ferment, and scrapes a certain slick growth from the submerged roots. It leaves the snails alone. A snail can cross its hand without causing the fingers to close.

Along its sides, hidden when it walks, are rows of soft slits. These open in warm water. A very slight current moves through them, carrying loose grains away from the body. In cold water they close tightly enough to become almost invisible.

Its eyes are useful for finding an opening among stems or a tool against a wall. Most of its attention seems to reside elsewhere. It can locate a leaking joint beneath several feet of mineral ground. Before a blockage becomes audible in the pump house, it will have begun visiting the relevant pipe, lying with one side of its head pressed against it.

The machinery makes speech difficult for a human visitor. For the inhabitant, the roar contains distinctions. A worn bearing, a pocket of gas, grit passing a valve: each draws a different response. Once, after a repair, it spent three days approaching and retreating from the building. The new motor ran smoothly, but at a speed just different enough to disturb it.

Eventually it resumed clearing the drain.

***

It can open the pump-house door.

This is worth knowing before assuming the swollen bottom of the door provides any sort of exclusion. The creature lifts the handle, pulls once, and then shifts its weight to tug at the lower corner. It knows where the wood catches.

Inside, it stands more upright. Its hands leave wet prints on the bench. It has learned to use a wrench on two particular fittings and is careful to return it to its outline afterward, although it sometimes hangs it backward. It does not touch the switches.

The person who visits brings grease, replacement washers, food in a closed tin, and occasionally a piece of cloth. The food is eaten by the visitor. The cloth is left on the bench, where the inhabitant will find it.

A dry cloth interests it intensely. It turns it over, presses it against its wrists, and puts it in the hollow beneath the mound. Within a day the cloth is soaked. Within a month it is usually unrecognizable. Nevertheless, another dry one receives the same attention.

There is no evidence that it wants clothing. It seems to value, for a short time, an object that takes water without immediately giving it back.

The visitor speaks to it at close range, usually while working.

“Move your hand.”

“That one’s staying.”

“Let it settle.”

The inhabitant responds to some of these requests, but not reliably enough to establish whether it understands the words. It is attentive to demonstrations. If shown where a new pipe runs, it will trace the route afterward with one palm on the ground.

It permits contact on the upper back. The visitor sometimes breaks away a thick mineral plate before it can catch on a root. Beneath the plate, the skin is tender and faintly pink. The creature holds still through this, then turns its head far enough to inspect the removed piece.

It always wants the piece returned. It carries such fragments to a particular dead pool and deposits them along the rim. There are several years’ worth there, perhaps more.

***

For most of the day it does very little that can be seen.

It lies beneath the grove. It shifts when a cold draft reaches the hollow. It lifts its head at certain changes in pressure, long before the heavy knock comes from the building. Sometimes, just before that knock, its fingers spread against the ground.

No one has seen it sleep with all its senses withdrawn. Nor has anyone watched long enough to say that it does not.

Toward evening it may go to the iron steps. It descends until the water reaches its shoulders and stays facing the pale stones at the bottom. Bubbles collect beneath its chin. The folded skin above its eyes loosens in the warmth.

Here it looks less like an animal crouching and more like a body at ease.

Its hind feet, seldom visible on land, open against the submerged treads. The toes are long, joined by a thin membrane, and curved at the ends. They grip the iron without apparent effort. Beneath each foot there is a clean patch where the white deposit has not been allowed to form.

It sometimes brings something with it: the bent rod, a stone, a length of root broken in a fall. It holds the object underwater for a while, turns it, and carries it back. There is no clear practical result. A few of the stones are handled repeatedly, until their sharp edges have worn smooth.

***

It avoids the outer rim.

Even when a blocked overflow sends water spreading toward the cliff, it approaches from the side and keeps one hand hooked around a trunk. The trees’ inward lean gives it good purchase. If that is insufficient, it leaves the obstruction for the visitor.

The distant summit does not seem to interest it. Lightning beneath the clouds sometimes does. The brief light reaches into the grove, and the creature raises its face, waiting through the interval in which thunder ought to arrive.

Then it lowers its head again.

There are no smaller creatures of its kind here, no visible eggs, no second hollow. This does not necessarily mean it is the last of anything. The mountain contains warm passages that cannot be entered, and the three rear pipes return water from depths known only imperfectly. Once, during a shutdown, the inhabitant remained beside the rear wall for six days. It kept its throat against a fissure and made a sound too low to hear clearly over the wind.

Nothing answered within the visitor’s hearing.

When circulation resumed, it stayed there until warmth returned to the stone. Then it went to the drain, where six days of leaves had accumulated, and began lifting them out.

At present its right hand has a cut across two fingers. A shard of fresh rock caused it. The wound stays pale in the wet, opening whenever the fingers spread. It has been pressing the hand into the soft mineral film beside a warm channel, patiently, several times a day.

The visitor has left a bandage on the bench. The inhabitant has taken it into the grove.

Whether it will use it is uncertain. But in the morning it comes onto the apron with the injured hand held against its chest, clears the drain with the other, and waits over the buried elbow until the water is passing freely.
