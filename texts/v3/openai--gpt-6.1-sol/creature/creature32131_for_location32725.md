The pale animals beside the columns have faces, though it takes some attention to find them.

What first looks like a head is a thickened fold of fin. The true face lies farther back, beneath a clear plate of skin: two small eyes, a row of sensory pores, and a mouth whose opening is almost lost among the red vessels. They have no obvious front. Their bodies are built around a shallow cup, with fins extending from its rim in unequal pairs. The largest are broad enough to shelter a house beneath them. Most are smaller, their outlines faint until a luminous seam shows through their flesh.

They occupy particular distances from the bark.

Close to the conducting veins, their fins are short and densely ribbed. Farther out, the same sort of body opens into long, nearly transparent vanes. There is a point beyond which none are found. It is not marked by any visible boundary; the animals simply furnish the bright seam with a loose envelope of flesh.

Their mouths are open, but they are not taking mouthfuls.

Inside each mouth lies a comb of soft, dark plates. Behind the comb, vessels spread into the fins, where narrow bands of tissue match the electrical light in the column. The correspondence is exact enough that a vein in an animal can seem to continue a vein in the bark across several yards of water. Nothing crosses that interval that can be watched crossing it. Nevertheless, the animal is nourished.

Their bodies carry the warmth that the water lacks. It is slight: the warmth of an enclosed hand, held deep inside translucent flesh. Along the fin edges, the vessels become so fine that their redness disappears.

The eyes are dark and plainly formed for sight. Each contains a small, clear image of the column, the open basin, and other animals at their several distances. A creature whose face points away from a seam has eyes set on the underside of its body. One whose fins obscure the view bears additional eyes along their ribs.

They do not need to turn to possess the world around them.

***

Under the surface live the seed-shaped creatures.

From below they appear black, each pressed against the brightness with a narrow halo around it. They are not attached to the upper leaves. A little water separates them from the surface, and in that interval lies a second, much finer anatomy.

Their backs are hard. Their bellies are almost all sensory tissue.

Viewed from the side, one resembles a dark husk containing an intricate white flower. The flower is an arrangement of folded organs: light-sensitive cups, minute gas chambers, fringes whose threads are finer than the root hairs on the floor. There are tiny feet as well, folded close to the shell. None touches anything.

Some shells hold one such body. Others hold a dozen, arranged around a shared hollow. These are not mothers surrounded by young. The smaller forms have their own complete proportions, their own shells, their own white organs. They belong together as the separate chambers of a fruit belong together.

Through their translucent undersides, the upper sky is visible in fragments. A cloud below the sea’s rim occupies one little cup. A strip of blue occupies another. Their anatomy divides the enormous view into pieces without obscuring it.

Among them are occasional empty shells. These have the same precise distance from the surface, the same halo. The opening where a body might be expected is smooth and finished. Nothing indicates whether it has been vacated or was always empty.

***

The long animals among the roots are easier to mistake for plant tissue.

Their skins are brown-green, crossed by pale diagonal lines. A body passes through an opening in the woven floor, lies along a channel, and appears again farther on, with no visible thickening to mark a head. Only the arrangement of its sides distinguishes it from a root. Roots branch. These animals remain single.

Their fins are narrow strips tucked into the grain around them. Along one side runs a line of mouths, each no larger than a fingernail, each fitted to a pore in the living wood.

They are neither burrowing nor feeding their way onward. The whole length of an animal belongs to the passage it occupies. Where the passage widens, its body widens. Where the wood makes a sharp angle, there is a corresponding joint.

Yet the fit is not perfect. A narrow margin of water remains around the skin. In that margin are eyes: dozens of clear beads, sheltered under small hoods. They look out wherever the rootwork permits a view.

One animal can have an eye overlooking a shallow plain and another overlooking the opposite side of a ridge, miles away. Between them its body lies enclosed in wood, furnished with mouths and pale internal cords.

There is no central chamber where all those views must meet. The cords connect one region to the next, but their knots are evenly distributed. Each visible portion contains enough anatomy to be alive without becoming a separate creature.

***

On the plains are the grazers, flat and modestly colored.

Their edges fit the channels worn into the floor. Some are scarcely wider than a palm; others cover whole humps of polished root. Their backs carry low ridges that resemble the ridges beneath them, except that the spacing is closer and the material is faintly blue.

Beneath each grazer is a broad mouth, divided into hundreds of small soft contacts. The contacts rest against the living membrane. Neither surface is damaged.

The exposed fringe bears sensory bristles, blunt limbs, and a few tiny eyes. These features establish that an animal is present even where its back matches the floor almost exactly. At the edge of a large specimen, a leg is held over a groove, its hooked end clear of the wood. There is no reason to expect it to find a foothold. The posture is complete as it stands.

Some grazers overlap. Their edges accommodate one another in shallow folds, leaving every mouth its share of the substrate. In places, a dozen backs make what appears to be a single low mound. Close inspection finds the separate fringes, the separate eyes.

Each retains a small difference in color.

***

The largest inhabitants are out in the basins.

Distance conceals their anatomy. A pale line may be a fin several miles wide, its body farther away in the green. A dark interruption in the floor may be a shadow cast by an animal too transparent to see directly.

They have long backs and broad, folded fins. Their heads are small in proportion to their bodies. The eyes face downward.

Some correspond to the animal-shaped hollows in the substrate. Above the impression of a fin lies a fin, separated from it by hundreds of feet of clear water. A curved back has its answering curve in the wood below. Others have no visible counterpart, though the floor beneath them may lie beyond the light.

There is substantial anatomy inside these bodies: immense pale organs held in nets of vessels, chambers divided by tough membranes, dark cords gathered near the spine. But their boundaries are difficult to follow. A fin thins until water and flesh have nearly the same appearance. The outermost vessels look like red threads laid directly in the sea.

Small animals occupy the clear spaces between those vessels. They have eyes and fins of their own. Their bodies are intact; there is no wound around them, no enclosing stomach. They inhabit the larger creature’s breadth as other animals inhabit a reach beside a column.

The giants’ mouths are lined with blunt white plates. Nothing is caught between them. Nothing in the surrounding water has the posture of flight.

***

Near the columns, many animals bear an additional organ: a stiff membrane held inside a ring of cartilage.

It may lie under a fin, across the throat, or deep within the body, visible only through the skin. Its dimensions vary, but its orientation is always exact. The membrane faces the same portion of the column that faces it.

The great noise occupies these organs as it occupies the water. There is no visible trembling. Around each membrane, nerves spread into the flesh, ending in the eyes, the mouths, the fine muscles of the fins.

Farther from the columns, the rings are larger. On the great basin animals, they form chambers big enough to contain the smaller pale creatures. Among the root-dwellers, they recur along the entire body.

This seems to be how the inhabitants know where they belong. Light gives them the open distances; contact gives them the floor. The immense sound gives each body its relation to the living structure holding the sea.

No animal looks vacant. Their eyes are clear, their tissues supplied, their fins held with all the delicate particulars of muscle. The grazers have their mouths against the floor. The surface creatures hold the sky in their small cups. Deep in the rootwork, a sheltered eye has an unobstructed view through a slit in the wood.

They live in those relations without exhausting them.

Across the broadest basin, where the nearest column is scarcely visible, a giant’s transparent flank contains several widely separated red vessels. Between two of them stands a small pale animal with its fins fully open. Its face is turned toward the distant electrical seam.

The giant’s own eye lies far below it, looking at the woven floor.

Both views are clear.
