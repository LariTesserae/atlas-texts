The people have ordinary feet. This is the first thing a stranger notices after expecting otherwise.

They have not grown webbing or broad soles. Their toes are often crooked from gripping the inside of wet footwear, and the skin at their heels splits despite the grease worked into it each night. Children learn to cross the flats by placing their feet carefully, not by being especially well made for the country. An adult carrying a basin of young grazers may take several minutes to cover a distance that looks trivial from a shelter door.

They step on the rough places. They stop before transferring their weight. If the water is cloudy, they test ahead with a pole.

A person who moves quickly is not necessarily skilled. Often they simply know that particular stretch of rock.

Their clothes are close-fitting at the wrist and loose at the shoulder, so an arm can be drawn inside to warm against the body. The outer hides are scraped thin, rubbed with animal fat, and patched until almost none of the first garment remains. Everyone has something dry put away in a high recess: a sleeve, a sleeping cloth, a strip for wrapping a baby. These things are brought out sparingly. A dry cloth used at the wrong moment can become one more wet thing that must be carried.

There are no large fires. In some households there is no fire at all for days. Fuel arrives as fat, as dried tissue, as dark fibrous scraps traded from a coast beyond the flats. A lamp is covered whenever possible. People sit with their hands around it before they put food over it.

They know the difference between being cold and beginning to lose the ability to act. Someone who stops fastening their own cuffs is made to come indoors, however firmly they sign that they are well.

Out on the rock, their hands are rarely still.

The signs used between shelters are large enough to read through rain: a forearm laid across the chest, two hands drawn apart, a fist opened toward the ground. At close quarters the language becomes much smaller. A thumb moving along one finger can distinguish water that is rising from water that is merely arriving from another pool. The position of the wrist identifies a grazer’s age. A brief downward movement means *leave it*. Repeated, it means *leave it, I have already tried*.

Children learn this last sign early.

There are household signs, too. A bent little finger may mean a particular aunt, because years ago a grazer closed on her hand and broke the joint. A sweep over the head may stand for a shelter whose roof was replaced with a shell so large that four households helped drag it into place. People from farther away do not always understand these names. Introductions can involve a little pantomime, followed by laughter that is visible before it is audible.

They do speak. Indoors, with mouths near ears, their voices have the intimacy of people sharing a bed even when they are discussing a broken scraper. Children sometimes shout inside simply because they can hear the result. An adult will touch the lintel, reminding them how far sound travels through the slabs.

The country is noisy enough to conceal a hunter’s approach, but a person can still be overheard through a wall.

***

The hollow-backed grazers spend much of their lives feeding on something too thin for a human hand to collect.

Lift a young one from a pool and its underside contracts into pale folds. Put it down and those folds spread slowly, finding the stone. The animal does not scrape every surface equally. It passes quickly over some films, lingers on others, and leaves narrow, clean tracks through the violet growth around a seep.

The people watch those tracks. A pool can look richly colored and still be poor feeding.

Good keepers are less concerned with how much a grazer eats today than with what remains beneath it tomorrow. They move the animals before a surface is exhausted. They open a channel with the end of a scraper, allow bitter water to mingle with rainwater, then wait to see which film returns. Some films feed the animals well but poison them when allowed to grow thick. Others are harmless until the water deepens and a different skin develops over them.

A household may keep three or four kinds of grazer, distinguished by the shape of the rear opening, the texture of the shell margin, and the way the feeding folds withdraw when touched. The largest are not always the best to eat. Some grow broad shells around very little flesh. Others are kept because their young settle readily, or because they continue grazing in water that would kill a more delicate kind.

One small, ugly variety is prized for cleaning nursery stones. Its call is a short, harsh burst that visitors mistake for a tool being dragged across rock.

The keepers select animals, though not always deliberately. Those that wander are brought back or lost. Those that stay in a shallow basin leave more young there. After many generations, a household’s grazers may be recognizable by their low shells and reluctant movements. This makes them easier to tend and easier for hunters to catch.

No advantage is allowed to remain simple for long.

The translucent swimmers slip under a grazer’s rim to take fragments loosened by its feeding surface. Some are tolerated. Some are collected in fine traps and eaten whole. Others bite into the soft folds and must be picked out with a bone point. Seen in a dish, they look almost identical. Held above a dark slate, one has a faint line along its gut and another does not.

A child may spend an afternoon learning to see that line.

The long hunters have no reason to fear the nurseries. Rain brings them food and opens passages through the stone barriers. They can narrow their bodies, brace against opposite sides of a crack, and remain there while the water falls. When a person lifts a nursery stone after a storm, they do it from the side.

Old people often have scars on their forearms. The scars are not all from hunters. A broad grazer can close its shell opening hard enough to crush a finger. A scavenger caught in a net may drive a jointed limb into the palm. The films themselves can inflame a cut until the whole hand becomes swollen and useless.

There is someone in almost every cluster of shelters who knows how to clean such wounds. Their tools are kept apart from the feeding scrapers, however scarce good edges may be.

***

A household is not simply the people under one roof.

It includes whoever tends its pools when someone is ill, whoever holds its young animals through a flood, and whoever remembers which stone regulates the cold seep beneath the western basin. These relationships can outlast affection. Two people who have ceased speaking may still exchange nursery stock because each keeps an animal the other needs.

Ownership is easiest to see in dry weather. A row of stones, a scraper left in a socket, a familiar pattern of shallow basins: these mark work that should not be disturbed. Under floodwater the marks become less certain.

Disputes are often about what happened while no boundary could be seen. An animal may have crossed into another yard. A barrier may have been moved to save one pool at the expense of another. Someone may have opened a channel during the night.

People argue over these things with considerable precision.

The remembered height of the water matters. So does the direction in which a grazer was facing when it was found. A witness who felt the evening calls through a sleeping ledge may be able to say when two pools joined, although they never went outside.

Large shells are marked on the inner lip where grazing will not wear the mark away. Smaller animals cannot be identified so easily. After a flood, a household may regain exactly the number it lost and still feel cheated: the returned animals are older, thinner, or of the wrong kind.

The scattered shelters do not make the people indifferent to one another. Much of the day is spent looking outward. Someone notices that a neighbor has not come to reset their yard. Someone sees a figure sitting too long on a low ridge. Across two pools, a hand asks a question.

The answer may be private. The question rarely is.

Children travel among shelters more freely than adults burdened with work. They carry small tools and messages, learn routes, and discover which households offer a warm mouthful without asking them to perform some task first. Their games use things that float, sink slowly, or can be persuaded to move. A young grazer turned gently on its back becomes an object of fascinated pity. Usually an older child rights it before an adult has to intervene.

They also make small shelters in the abandoned ones. Scraps of shell become roofs; pebbles stand for people. Then the tide enters and washes the arrangements loose.

They build them again higher up.

***

The largest grazers are heard much more often than they are seen.

Their shells acquire stains and patches of growth until they resemble the slopes on which they rest. Around an old animal there may be a clean feeding margin, a ring of scavengers, and several smaller grazers taking advantage of the same seep. From a distance, all of this looks like an ordinary pool.

Then the shell lifts.

Water pours from beneath it. The animal turns with slow muscular effort, revealing stone that has been covered for years. Small creatures hurry across the exposed place. A child seeing this for the first time may run for an adult, convinced that part of the ground has come alive.

These large animals are difficult to bring down and costly to lose. Some are killed for food and roofing shell. Others are left because their calls can be recognized across a great distance.

A keeper knows the low, uneven note of an old grazer beyond the eastern ridge. If it fails to sound, they cannot immediately know why. The animal may have moved. The seep may have altered. A hunter may have entered the pool.

Someone will eventually go to look.

This is how much of the country is watched: not continuously, and not by any one person, but through familiar disturbances. A pool sounds different. A cluster of scavengers gathers where it usually does not. The violet film pales beside a crack. A grazer feeds along the upper edge of a basin rather than the lower.

These observations accumulate around the evening lamps. They are scratched onto slate when they need to survive a conversation. Important ones are cut into the stone.

Even so, a seep sometimes dwindles before anyone accepts what is happening. The family deepens a basin, clears a channel, brings in healthier animals. For a season there is improvement. Then the films thin again.

Leaving such a place takes time. Tools go first to the new shelter. Nursery animals travel in covered basins. The heavy things follow when enough neighbors can help. The last inhabitants continue to sleep in the old building while they tend what remains.

On the final night, there may still be callers outside.

Years later, someone passing the empty shelter will recognize a mark on the lintel or find a broken scoop beneath a ledge. They may stop to check the seep. Water chemistry changes; old feeding grounds occasionally become useful again.

Most do not.

The people sleep above these slow losses, above animals feeding and being fed upon, above channels opening in darkness. A sleeper feels a deeper note arrive through the stone and draws a hanging sleeve onto the ledge. Another wakes, listens with a palm against the wall, and decides there is no need to wake the children yet.

Outside, a grazer reaches the lip of a newly flooded basin. Its feeding folds loosen. It tests the stone, settles, and begins to eat.
