The people are easiest to recognize by what they carry.

Most have a lamp at the hip, a folded insulating cloth, and a narrow case of tools. Even someone going to visit a sister will carry a scraper and a little sealed tube of repair oil. The scraper is for freeing things that have begun to be taken into the city: a shoe left against a curb, the foot of a chair, the lower edge of a cupboard. There is no disgrace in needing it. There is some disgrace in needing to borrow one.

Their clothes are loose at the joints and close at the neck. Fastenings are usually grown horn or ceramic, because a metal clasp lost in a wall can cause trouble much later, in a part of the building no one associates with the loss. Children’s clothes have very large fastenings. Children lose things anyway.

At a distance, a person looks narrow and dark against the ivory streets. Up close, there is considerable variety. Some are broad through the shoulders; some have backs that have bent early; some move with the careful stiffness of people who have spent too many working periods in cold districts. Their skin ranges from gray-brown to a deep reddish color, with a slight sheen over the knuckles and cheekbones. It is thicker on the palms than one expects. An old worker’s hands may look almost polished.

They have hair, though not much of it. It grows densely along the back of the head and down behind the ears, where it protects a set of fine respiratory folds. These are kept covered in public. To uncover them is not indecent, but it suggests illness, intimacy, or extreme fatigue.

Their mouths are for eating and speaking. Breathing happens elsewhere.

This makes their voices unusually even. A person can finish a long account without pausing for breath; the pauses come where thought requires them. Someone telling a lie may speak too fluently. Someone grieving may go silent in the middle of an ordinary word.

Their bodies contain very little free water. The fluids that keep their tissues supple are heavy, pale, and slow to evaporate. A cut on a finger first beads, then skins over. The skin must be left in place until the tissue underneath has joined. Children are forever picking at it.

They are warm, but not as warm as their lamps.

A hand will encourage a door, given time. A lamp will open it promptly. Consequently, arriving without a lamp can be taken as a request to linger outside and talk. In the older residential terraces, where neighbors know one another’s habits, this has acquired several meanings. A lamp held low says that one does not intend to stay. A covered lamp says that the visit should not wake the household. People disagree about whether an uncovered lamp carried in both hands is respectful or theatrical.

The city’s strangers learn these distinctions badly.

There are not many strangers, but there are enough: workers transferred from chambers farther down, relatives from settlements beneath other salt fields, apprentices following a specialist. They stand too close to doors. They put warm parcels on unfinished shelves. They admire an open square without noticing that the floor is under tension.

Residents are patient about this until something expensive happens.

***

In a maintenance building off a boulevard called the Third Return lives a woman named Eren. She is responsible for several blocks of residential walls and one side of a freight bridge.

Her rooms are above the tool store. She chose them because the stairs had finished thickening, and because she could hear freight approaching without hearing the motors once it had passed. The building offered her an additional room nine years ago. It is still unsuitable for use. For a long time the floor sloped toward a warm corner; now the floor is level, but the ventilation pores are all at ankle height.

She stores empty crates there.

Eren has a son who lives in another district and a younger daughter who comes home at irregular hours. The daughter works on ceiling growths. On some visits she arrives with green-white dust along her cuffs, and Eren makes her take off her outer clothes before sitting down. Neither considers this an important conflict, but they have had it many times.

Eren’s working lamp has three settings. The lowest lets her read a wall without disturbing it. The middle warms mineral paste. The highest is locked behind a turning collar and used to draw the long-bodied cleaning animals out of places where they have gathered too thickly.

She does not dislike the animals. She dislikes being asked whether she dislikes them.

They are useful, troublesome, and capable of learning where she keeps the paste. A particular one has occupied the bridge abutment for nearly six years. It has a split ridge behind its head, making it identifiable when it emerges. Eren has tried moving it twice. Both times it returned through supply sheaths.

Now she leaves it alone unless the bridge begins to shiver.

The animal recognizes the tapping pattern of her listening disc. Sometimes, before she has warmed the bait, a narrow blunt head pushes through a service opening. She says, “Not yet,” and it remains there.

Whether it understands the words is not a question she finds interesting.

***

Food is served warm more often than hot.

There are dense loaves made from milled storage tissue, thin shavings of salt-tolerant tuber, and pastes whose mineral content is adjusted for age and work. Food for ceiling crews is richer than food for clerks. Some pastes are sharply bitter; others leave a pleasant oily film in the mouth. People cultivate preferences beyond any nutritional need. A household may go to considerable trouble to obtain meal from a particular factory whose products are otherwise unremarkable.

The small browsers are also eaten, though not by everyone. Their cheek pockets must be emptied carefully. There are jokes about cooks who sell the contents back as seasoning, and occasional prosecutions.

Meals are usually taken on insulated trays. Setting a warm bowl directly on a table will not ruin either bowl or table, but it can leave a raised patch that persists for years. In Eren’s kitchen there is such a patch beneath the place where her son used to sit. She levels it every few seasons. It returns.

She says this is because the grain of the table was badly trained.

When her son visits, she puts his bowl there.

***

They sleep in rooms designed to give back as little warmth as possible.

The walls are lined with mature bark or removable sheets of dark, inert fiber. Beds stand on ceramic feet. A bed allowed to root into the floor becomes difficult to move and may begin transmitting the small contractions of the street. Some people claim to find this soothing. Most of those people are young.

Before sleeping, they close covers over their respiratory folds and lower their lamps into hooded niches. The lamp remains faintly warm. Without it, a room may begin its unoccupied adjustments during a long sleep: tightening a doorway, testing a partition, advancing a shelf into space formerly kept clear.

Buildings do not know the difference between absence and stillness very well.

For this reason, tending the lamps of the sick is an ordinary duty. Neighbors take turns. Someone sits, repairs clothing, reads, occasionally walks around with the lamp so the room continues to receive evidence of use.

A seriously ill person may wake to find three acquaintances discussing freight tariffs beside the bed. This is comforting even when the discussion is irritating.

***

Their children grow slowly enough to remember several versions of the places where they were small.

A child may first know a square as a shallow bowl, later as level pavement, and finally as the base of a public hall. Children learn early to look for their own old marks high on walls. A careless cut made at knee height can rise beyond reach as the tissue beneath it lengthens.

There are regulations against cutting names into young structures. They are widely ignored.

Schools teach tension-reading alongside arithmetic. Pupils walk a practice floor barefoot and identify the concealed line of a forming root. They are taught which noises are harmless, which require an adult, and which mean they should leave without collecting their belongings.

They also learn things that have no immediate use. They memorize accounts of vanished workshops. They study music. They argue over the proper shape of letters. Some become very interested in distant settlements and spend their youth wanting to leave.

Others become surveyors.

It is difficult to explain the attraction of surveying to someone who has not watched a competent surveyor work. At the chamber’s edge, there is very little that resembles a finished place. Yet one person will look at a crease in the floor, check a measure, move a lamp by the width of a hand, and say that a passage can go there.

The passage may serve people not yet born.

This does not make the surveyor solemn. The work includes tedious disputes about gradients and access. A mistake can leave generations of freight workers maneuvering around an inconvenient pillar. People remember who approved such things.

***

There are old inhabitants who have never visited the newest district.

They do not feel deprived. The chamber is large, and traveling from one side to the other takes time. They have their shops, their companions, the particular bridge from which they watch one district brighten before another. Their lives fit inside a few kilometers of a city that continues beyond their experience.

Others travel compulsively through the empty quarters.

They like unfinished doors and streets without names. They take measurements no one requested. They return with news that a certain hall has finally opened, or that an isolated growth has begun making balconies. A few quietly occupy rooms before they have been assigned.

The city accommodates them imperfectly. A person sleeping in an unauthorized room may find that it offers a fine window and no dependable exit.

There are arguments about how much empty space should be maintained. Some residents see the vacant districts as provision for descendants. Others see them as a burden consuming heat, labor, and mineral feed while occupied buildings wait for repairs.

Eren belongs to the second group, except when she visits the station site.

There she becomes interested despite herself.

She knows one of the workers who adjusts the heat. He used to maintain sockets in her neighborhood and was considered too meticulous for that job. At the station he can spend an entire working period persuading a shoot to advance a finger’s breadth.

He shows her where the platforms will be.

They stand among the low white growths, each holding a lamp. Far away, a freight vehicle crosses the commons with two pale browsers traveling underneath it. The wheels make almost no sound.

The worker explains that the station will have four platforms at first, with room for nine.

Eren asks who is going to use nine platforms.

He gives her the current estimates. She finds fault with them. They discuss a connection that has not been funded and a residential quarter that has not been occupied.

While they talk, one of the blind branches reaches into the warmth between them. Its tip opens into several small, searching lobes.

The worker notices and steps back.

Eren remains for a moment longer, looking at the place where the branch has nearly touched her sleeve. Then she moves her lamp away too.

By morning it will have settled elsewhere. Someone will come to measure it, and the work will continue.
