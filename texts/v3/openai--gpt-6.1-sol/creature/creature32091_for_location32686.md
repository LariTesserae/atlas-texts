The smallest of them can be mistaken for a doorway.

It has a thickened rim, a shallow recess, and a lip of pale tissue tucked into the darkness above. For most of the day it does nothing distinguishable from the wall around it. Heat passes through it. Sap gathers along its lower edge. When the street begins to close, however, the recess deepens faster than the walls advance. The lip draws back. Somewhere beyond the visible surface, a chamber makes room.

By the time the two sides of the street touch, the doorway has become a passage through them.

There are dozens of these along some lanes, and none along others. They persist through the destruction of the surfaces that bear them. One may survive a collapsed tower, appearing first in the tower’s exposed core, then in the pavement beneath it, then at shoulder height in a wall that has risen from the wreckage. Its rim changes color. Its opening changes size. But it retains a small fold at the upper left corner, or a line of stiff hairs across its threshold, and through that opening the air has the same warm, mineral smell.

It is difficult to say whether something has moved, or whether several successive growths have made room for the same thing.

A larger inhabitant becomes recognizable by the surfaces it keeps shaded.

Its visible body may consist of an arcade, two leaning towers, and the underside of a plaza. None of those structures need endure. The arcade can close, the towers can fall, the plaza can lift and split. Yet within all that alteration, a connected region remains cool. New leaves turn their backs on the sun. Thick skins grow translucent on their inner faces. The chambers communicate through narrow throats lined with dark red tissue.

At noon, when everything around it swells, it contracts.

This contraction is slow enough to resemble the ordinary settling of a building. Then one notices that it is drawing fluid through the roots below. Nearby paving pales. A row of young stems loses its shine. The inhabitant’s chambers fill, their walls rounding until the little throats between them are almost shut.

It holds what it has taken until evening.

When the woody tissues begin to harden, its lower surfaces relax. Moisture spreads through the district. Clear beads appear on roots that had seemed dry and dead. The long fractures of sunset are quieter around it.

Several such inhabitants can occupy the same square. Their territories interleave vertically: a hot, narrow-bodied growth above; the keeper of cool chambers beneath; something thinner still lying between them, detectable only by the way a fracture refuses to cross one particular layer.

They do not have separate skins everywhere. At their borders, their fibers pass into one another. A root can belong to one pattern of growth near the surface and another deeper down. There are places where the same tissue thickens for one inhabitant while being consumed by its neighbor.

Their encounters are most visible in the avenues.

For hours, two facing walls will hold an exact distance apart. Fresh leaves emerge into the gap and are pressed flat. Roots rise at the edges of the paving but stop short of crossing. The avenue stays open while streets on either side disappear.

Then a small change occurs—a seam wets, a buttress shifts—and the whole arrangement gives way.

One wall folds inward. The other sends a ridge across the floor. High above, branches graft with a succession of heavy clicks. What had been a boundary becomes an interior. For a while, both growth patterns remain visible: different veins, different shades of green, opposing directions of grain. By morning there may be only one.

Elsewhere they separate with equal violence. A continuous facade splits down its middle, exposing a pale corridor full of membranes. Each side withdraws its roots from the other. Fibers snap. Fluid runs briefly in the new street.

The inhabitants on either side spend the next day sealing themselves.

They also produce young, though the youngest are seldom the smallest.

A district may raise six identical towers in a morning. All six unfold the same balconies, turn the same waxed leaves toward the light, and send roots beneath the same distant plaza. They look like separate creatures. For their first several hours they respond as one: injury to a lower sheath on the nearest tower causes the highest frond on the farthest to curl.

Later, the likeness begins to break.

One tower keeps its chambers warm after sunset. Another opens narrow cups to a passing shower. A third bends toward a pool and remains bent, its unfinished crown suspended above the water.

The others continue growing. Their shared roots thicken, flatten, and finally part. The separated ends seal over without rotting.

By the next morning, the six towers have little in common except the angle at which their first branches emerged.

The ones near water are hard to observe for long.

They grow rims around the pools, adding layer after layer of dark, smooth tissue. Their exposed leaves scarcely move. Beneath the water, their roots are white and delicate, branching into finer divisions than any root elsewhere in the basin.

Occasionally a reflection moves before its source.

A frond opens in the black water while the frond above it remains folded. A fissure travels across a reflected wall. Several breaths later, the real wall splits along that line.

The inhabitant at the rim then draws some of the water into itself. Its skin bulges. Deep inside, something cracks very slowly.

Around these pools, old and new growth can remain joined in ways they cannot elsewhere. A tender shoot enters the side of a hardened trunk and emerges from the trunk’s broken tip already broad and weathered. A dead-looking root holds a fresh leaf beneath the water for days. When the leaf is finally lifted out, it bears scars from fractures that have not yet occurred in the surrounding streets.

Other inhabitants approach these margins cautiously. Their advancing roots flatten and divide. Some stop altogether. Others make a sudden, narrow crossing, sacrificing the connecting fiber as soon as new tissue has established itself beyond the pool.

There are injuries from which they do not recover.

A tower may continue raising fronds after the pattern that held its parts together has failed. Its chambers open at incompatible hours. Water collected at its crown spills into sealed tissue and ferments. Roots pull against one another until the base tears.

For a time, the remains are extravagantly active. Shoots rise from every broken surface. Doorways swell in the fallen trunk. A canopy spreads over a chamber whose floor is already collapsing.

Gradually, other inhabitants take these growths into themselves.

The dead tower’s upper half may become part of a shaded district. Its roots may support a row of sun-seeking spires. Its last intact chamber may remain sealed under a street, warm for years.

Some of its habits survive there. At a particular hour, leaves along the street turn briefly toward a tower that no longer exists. New paving grows around an empty space the size of its former buttress. These shapes persist until another growth needs the room.

Beneath all of them are the slower inhabitants.

Their surfaces appear only in fissures: wine-colored membranes, beds of short hairs, ivory cables too thick to bend within the opening that reveals them. They accommodate districts the way the upper growths accommodate leaves. A plaza gathers over one of their joints. Streets spread along a region of tension. When a deep body shifts, miles of towers alter their stance.

There is no clear evidence that these lower creatures perceive the structures above individually.

A whole neighborhood can be drawn downward with the gradual opening of a chamber below it. Roofs descend intact. Fronds brush against one another and fold. The paving rises around the edges, closing over the last upper leaves.

Elsewhere, the pressure produces an avenue that no surface inhabitant has begun to claim: fresh, pale, astonishingly straight, with damp air moving along its length.

At sunset, the deep bodies make themselves briefly apparent.

Their movement passes upward through every graft and shared fiber. The keepers of shade release their stored water. The divided towers lean together. The rims of the pools tighten until the water rises above them without spilling.

Even the little passages preserve themselves, drawing their openings inward as the walls around them harden.

The groan continues for a long time.

Then the pressure eases. In different quarters of the basin, growth resumes at different rates. One district settles into the night with all its chambers closed. Another begins to split along a line that has been thickening since dawn.

On a newly raised wall, a shallow recess develops a rim. Pale tissue gathers above it, folds once, and withdraws into the green.
