What lives here is easier to find by kneeling than by looking.

Choose a place where a stream disappears beneath the sedge. Put one hand into the water and the other on the rooted bank. Wait through the first discomfort of the cold. The stream will tug steadily at your fingers. Under your other palm, something may tighten.

It is a small movement, quite distinct from the give of wet peat. A tension passes through the bank, pauses, then releases farther along. Several stems lean together although no wind has touched them.

For a while, that is all you get.

The animal lies between the turf and the stones, in the shallow space that water keeps open. Most of it is hidden by the living surface above it. You might uncover a section where a bank has collapsed: a pale, folded flank, slick as the underside of a mushroom, pressed against gravel. It withdraws without thrashing. First the folds deepen; then the exposed part becomes narrower; then it is gone, leaving the water slightly clouded.

A full-grown one can extend beneath several yards of bank. It is not equally substantial along that length. There are broad portions, flattened almost to the thickness of a hand, joined by lengths narrow enough to pass between stones. Its outline changes as it moves. In a wide hollow it spreads; at a constriction it gathers itself into overlapping pleats.

Along its underside are paired limbs, short and numerous. Each ends in a blunt, divided pad. The pads grip stones, roots, concrete, anything that gives them a firm edge. One pair takes hold while another releases, so that the animal can ease itself uphill through a stream without appearing to struggle against the current.

Out of the water, it is much less capable. Its weight pulls at the broad sections of its body. Its skin dulls. It has to lift and set each pad separately, feeling for somewhere that will bear it.

This is why a wet seam crossing a hillside matters to it. A seam only a few inches wide may connect two otherwise separate parts of its world.

Its head is usually downstream.

The head is small in proportion to the body, blunt, with a shallow groove across its upper surface. Water runs through this groove and over two dark pits. These are not eyes. Fine hairs inside them register changes in the flow: the irregular beating of an insect, the soft obstruction of a fallen leaf, the pressure of another animal moving under the same mat of roots.

The eyes lie lower, almost beneath the head, where the reflected brightness is weaker. They are small and clear, with pupils shaped like short horizontal cuts. When the animal crosses an exposed pool, a whitish membrane slides over them. It continues by touch.

It feeds slowly. Its mouth can widen around the base of a sedge tussock without damaging the stems. It draws water and loose material inward, closes, and works the mouthful against ridged plates inside its jaws. Small larvae, freshwater worms, softened leaves, and the minute animals living among them are separated from grit. Sand comes out through two openings behind the head in brief, pale streams.

A feeding place has a clean bottom. The stones look recently washed. The peat around them is left in low, neat heaps.

There are very few of these animals in the basin, but each uses a considerable stretch of ground. One may occupy a chain of hollows from a birch knoll to the edge of the intake pool. Another may live entirely along a stream that has not yet appeared on the surface.

Their routes are maintained by use. A body passing repeatedly beneath the turf keeps a channel open, presses loose gravel aside, and makes room for more water. Roots descend into the channel. The animal parts them on its next passage. Over time, the roof becomes strongly woven and the floor grows smoother.

When a channel finally opens to the sky, the animal usually abandons that section. It needs the cover, and the exposed water grows too bright. It begins again a little to one side, following whatever dampness remains beneath the bank.

The older ones carry small stones.

You can see them through the skin at the edges of the broad body sections: angular pieces of quartz, dark chips, a grain of brick near the installation. Each sits in a separate pocket. The pockets can be tightened or relaxed. When the animal needs to hold position in a strong flow, the stones hang beneath it, adding weight while its pads find purchase.

At other times it deposits them. A narrow passage may contain a little sill of selected gravel, each piece too large to have arrived in the gentle current flowing there. The sill slows the water and deepens the chamber above it. That is where the animal rests.

Near the spillway, the chambers are particularly deep.

One lives beneath the permanent strip of spray. It is larger than the others, though scarcely anyone has seen more than a part of it. Its feeding mouth appears in the clear pools early in the morning. Through the shaking surface you can watch the lips open against a submerged stem, then close with extraordinary delicacy.

The roar does not distress it. The pressure of falling water reaches it through the bank, a steady vibration against which smaller disturbances stand out. A boot on the concrete apron is distinct. So is a wrench dropped by a maintenance worker. The turning fan reaches it faintly through the shed’s foundations.

It withdraws from footsteps, but not always from the fan. On cold nights it sometimes climbs the drain beneath the shed, following the slight warmth of the outflow. There it folds its head under the first broad section of its body and remains still until morning. The unusually tall grass roots into water that has passed over its skin.

Once, the worker clearing the doorway found a pale length of it between two cut tussocks. He took it for a strip of insulation pulled loose from a pipe. When he touched it with the toe of his boot, a row of pads released from the ground in order, nearest first and then farther away, and the strip slipped beneath the uncut grass.

He has not tried to clear that patch again.

In late summer, the young appear in the shallow pools beneath overhanging turf. They are no longer than a birch leaf and almost transparent. Their few grains of swallowed sand show plainly inside them. They remain together at first, gripping the same submerged root, their heads pointing into the weak current.

Rain disperses them.

As the pools rise and join, the young let go. Some find hollows that will hold water through the following season. Others are carried into streams too open, too steep, or too swift for them. At the intake, they gather among the weed strands on the grilles. Most can pass through. A few cling until the water slackens and then crawl back into the moss around the concrete.

By autumn, a survivor has become opaque. It has acquired more limbs, its first stone pockets, and enough strength to push beneath a rooted mat rather than wait for an opening.

Winter confines the animals to deeper channels. Ice seals the surface, but water still moves among the rubble below. They shorten their bodies into thick folds and hold to the stones. Their feeding nearly stops. Occasionally, beneath the snow, one changes chambers. A little water rises into the place it has left and freezes there.

In spring, these routes open before the surrounding ground has thawed. Meltwater follows them. The animals become active while the sedges are still pressed flat and brown.

By the time the basin shines green again, they are spread beneath it.

You can walk across a whole slope and see nothing. Yet at the edge of a clear pool, if you remain still long enough, a stem may begin to lean against the current. A pale mouth closes around its base. For a moment the water shows you the lower curve of a head, a dark eye beneath it, and two pads holding patiently to a stone.

Then the mouth releases the stem.

The animal goes on under the bank, and the stream runs into the space behind it.
