The person holding the jug is an older man with a narrow chest and a thickened right wrist. His sleeve has slipped back. There is a pale strip of skin where a cuff usually protects him from the sun, and above it a scatter of small scars, some round, some no larger than a grain of barley. He has worked near heat, though the table is not his workshop.

He holds the jug with both hands. It is heavier than it looks.

The cup belongs to a young woman sitting across the corner from him. She has turned slightly toward someone farther down the table, but her fingers remain around the cup, keeping it steady under the stream. This is a familiar arrangement: he pours, she steadies, neither needs to look closely. Her mouth is open a little. Whatever she is saying has reached a point at which her listener knows whether it will be a complaint or a joke.

There are places at the table for people who have not yet arrived.

One bowl is smaller than the others. Beside another lies a spoon with a repaired handle, wrapped in fine cord that has darkened through use. The folded cloths do not match. Someone has contributed the jug; someone else has carried up the bowls. The meal belongs to several households, and there has already been discussion about where everyone should sit.

The man’s eyes are on the rising water.

He lives here.

So does the woman whose hand is inside the loom. She has a broad face, a short upper lip, and a little indentation beside one nostril from an old injury. Her hair is secured with a wooden pin cut shorter after its tip broke. On the floor beside her stool is a bowl of damp fiber covered by a cloth. She is making a strip of fabric narrow enough to be worn as a belt or used to bind a load.

One thread has pulled tighter than its neighbors. Her hand has gone in to ease it.

Her house is two rooms deep. The room behind her contains a sleeping platform, three storage baskets, a small shelf of things too useful or too valued to leave under the eaves. Among them is a smooth dark stone from somewhere below the pale mountains. It would be easy to call it a keepsake. It may instead be the best weight she has found for holding a pattern flat.

She has duties outside this room. The basin above her district needs clearing at intervals, and her household owes labor on the retaining wall. She knows which neighbor can be trusted to return a knife and which must be reminded. There are people she likes who tire her. There is a person whose footsteps she recognizes before they reach the door.

None of these relations has been simplified by her stillness.

The porter on the stair is younger than the strain in his face suggests. The frame on his back carries two sacks and a rectangular parcel protected by wicker. He has placed one foot on the next step but has not transferred his weight. His whole body is engaged in that incomplete decision: knee bent, heel lifted, neck pressed forward against the load.

A little farther uphill, a bench waits in the shadow of a wall.

He knows it is there. He also knows that if he stops, getting the frame onto his shoulders again will be unpleasant. He is capable of passing the bench out of pride, or stopping because he has learned what pride does to the back. Nothing in his posture reveals which choice he was about to make.

The city has been built by people acquainted with this question. They have made room to put things down.

That does not mean burdens are fairly divided. In a storeroom below the main contour road, an elderly woman stands beside a row of sealed jars, holding a tally stick between her thumb and forefinger. A man opposite her has turned one empty palm upward. His other hand grips his belt. They are not smiling. There is a discrepancy between what has been brought and what has been recorded, and each believes the other ought to understand it already.

Elsewhere, a boy has taken three fruits when he was permitted two. The third is inside his shirt. A woman pruning the tree has her back to him, but her head is angled enough to suggest that his success is uncertain.

The deer among the orchard trunks do not belong to the gardeners in the same way the trees do. One has a ragged notch in its ear. Another is scarcely taller than the low branch beneath which it stands. Their mouths are full of leaves. Where bark needs protecting, woven guards surround the trunks; where fruit is being trained, the ties have been put beyond an easy reach.

The goats on the roof are less welcome. One has set a forehoof on a stone plate that does not lie quite flat. Below the eaves, a door is open, and a person inside has turned toward the sound it would have made.

Even now, the goat occupies the roof with the confidence of an animal for whom a roof is simply another agreeable part of the mountain.

The fox beside the granary is thin under its full coat. It has not come to admire the grain. Its nose points toward a gap beneath the threshold, where the scent of a smaller life has drawn it close. Inside, behind a sack, a mouse has gathered scraps of husk into a cavity in the wall. There are young there, pressed together, each with its own small body and undeveloped claws.

The fox, the mouse, and the person responsible for the granary all inhabit different versions of the same passage.

So do the beetles in the warm wall joints. So does the lizard with one forefoot spread against the edge of a stair. Its throat is slightly expanded. Sunlight rests on its eyelid, which has not quite closed.

The fish in the cisterns are narrow-bodied and pale underneath. Some face the place where fresh water enters. One hangs under the lowest step, almost invisible until its eye catches the light. A child has scratched a mark into the stone above it, perhaps to say how large a fish was once caught there. The fish now present is smaller than the mark.

The children on the bridge are not posed at equal distances from its ends. One has gone out farther. The other is leaning back, chin raised, unwilling to yield a point. A loose lace extends from the nearer child’s shoe. Between them lies enough space for a dare, an accusation, or an offer to begin again.

Below, the bird has almost reached its stone.

Its toes are extended. The pale feathers of its throat are separated slightly by the air. It is alive in a way that admits no confusion with a carved bird: there is tension in the legs, adjustment in the half-folded wings, an exact readiness for contact. The body has reached that readiness through a life of feeding, cold nights, flights along the water. Nothing about it appears finished.

The same is true of the people.

A woman asleep in an uphill room has one hand tucked beneath her cheek. A man in a workshop holds a blade against a whetstone. Under a covered walkway, someone has bent to retrieve a dropped peg. In a house whose windows face the valley, two people sit far apart after an argument; one has begun to turn toward the other.

Their stillness gives away no verdict.

It has not made the tired porter tireless. It has not repaired the spoon, emptied the mouse’s nest, or settled the account in the storehouse. The cloth remains difficult at the point where the weaver is working. The child still has the fruit hidden against his belly.

And at the table, the old man continues to hold the weight of the jug without lowering it or lifting it away.

The woman’s cup is not yet full. Her hand is there to steady it. Behind her, one of the empty places has been set close enough to the edge that whoever comes can sit without asking anyone to move.
