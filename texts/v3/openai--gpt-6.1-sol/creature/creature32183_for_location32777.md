The people who live here are easiest to recognize by the way they stop.

A visitor stops in the middle of a bridge to look at the mountain. A resident reaches the next pier first. There, the walkway is supported, the rail is cooler, and anyone approaching with a loaded trolley can pass. A visitor sets a bag down wherever there is room. A resident tests the surface with a heel, then leaves the bag on its strap, hanging from a hook.

These habits persist elsewhere. People who have left the city still look for the wet underside of a table before sitting at it. They distrust a floor that shines.

They are not all engineers. There are cooks, clerks, midwives, quarrelsome landlords, people who repair musical instruments, people who spend whole afternoons doing nothing useful. But nearly everyone has some competence with water. A dressmaker knows which channel will stain cloth and which will stiffen it. A schoolteacher can tell, from a change in the steam beyond her window, that the children should be sent home by the upper walk. The customs officers inspect the undersides of imported crates for eggs, and the eggs are not always those of animals.

Their clothes are pale but seldom clean white. Repeated washing leaves them faintly yellow, pink, or gray, according to the district. Loose sleeves are fastened above the wrist when working. Hats have broad, uneven brims, longer on the side that faces the reflected glare. Almost everyone owns a pair of dark glasses; almost everyone has broken several pairs.

Older people have small scars on their hands. Burns, mostly, and cuts from deposits that looked soft. Their fingernails are thickened by work or protected under little caps of horn. In conversation they turn their palms upward rather than putting a hand on someone’s shoulder. For much of the day, touching another person adds heat neither of them wants.

There are exceptions. Babies are held constantly. The city’s cradles stand well above the floor, but parents distrust them anyway.

***

The grazers under the houses are not as stone-colored as they first appear.

Lift one into shade and its armor shows narrow bands of green, rose, and smoky blue. Each band records a period of feeding in a different channel. The oldest part of the back is at the center, a small smooth oval no larger than a thumbnail. New material grows outward at the edges. On a large animal, those edges turn down over the legs like the eaves of a roof.

They have six legs, but rarely move all six at once. Four hold while two search. The searching feet tap and slide, testing for a surface the mouthparts can reach.

Under the armor, the animal is almost transparent. Its gut makes a dark loop around a pale central sac. When frightened, it draws its legs inward and seals the rim of its shell against the ground. A person may step on one without knowing it. Often the animal survives; the mineral crust beneath it does not.

Children keep them in shallow dishes. Adults discourage this, partly because the animals die in household water and partly because, given the right water, they grow until they cannot be removed from the dish.

There is a grazer beneath the western school steps whose back is wider than a serving tray. Generations of pupils have scratched initials into its outer layers. Most of the marks have disappeared under fresh growth. It emerges when the steps are washed, advances several inches, feeds, and withdraws.

The caretaker claims it was already there when he was a boy. Nobody can prove him wrong.

***

A sluice keeper named Sera has charge of eleven gates, two of which have not been turned in her lifetime.

She checks those two every afternoon.

One has a spindle fused into a sleeve of cream-colored mineral. The other can still move, though no one wants it to. Its gate separates waters that would precipitate a heavy, suffocating powder if mixed too quickly. Sera’s predecessor painted a black ring around the wheel. The paint is renewed each year, even though every worker in the district knows what it means.

Sera was a refinery assistant before she became a keeper. She liked the refinery: its careful measurements, its shelves of labeled vessels, the small satisfaction of producing something that met a specification. She left because she developed a tremor in her left hand. It is slight, but fine wire does not forgive it.

The sluice work has proved more accommodating. She braces a sampling glass against the shaft of her tool. She turns wheels with both hands. When she needs to taste a trace, she rests the rod on her lower lip before opening her mouth.

Her neighbors think she knows everything about their water. This irritates her. She knows where it has been and what it usually does. There are afternoons when she waits beside a gate for twenty minutes because she cannot decide.

During those waits, animals gather around her shoes. Her felt soles hold cooler water brought from the shaded walks. Small grazers climb onto them. Transparent swimmers approach the faint seepage, then retreat.

She used to shake them off. Now she stands still until she is ready to leave.

At home she shares two rooms with her sister, who makes dentures, and her sister’s son, who wants to work on freight ships below the clouds. The boy has never seen a forest. He is tired of hearing how astonished he will be.

***

The long-legged scavengers do not look quite so weightless when they lie down.

They fold in stages. First the hind legs bend, lowering the narrow pelvis. Then the forelegs spread, and the chest descends between them. Last, the neck is laid along one folded limb. What stood taller than a person becomes a complicated low bundle, with the head still able to turn.

Their skin is tough and finely wrinkled. Beneath the jaw hangs a pouch that changes color as blood moves through it: pale when the animal rests, dark plum when it is overheated. They seek shade before people do, and warehouse workers sometimes follow them.

They eat dead swimmers, bird eggs, the soft bodies of grazers whose shells have cracked, and almost any kitchen refuse they can swallow. They also dig at the dry salt with their many toes. What they find there is difficult to see: dormant eggs, shed skins, little packets of flesh sealed inside mineral grains.

They are patient with people until food is involved.

One will wait all morning beside a loading platform without seeming to notice the workers. At the sound of a lunch tin opening, its head rises. It does not beg. It comes closer and watches with an attention many people find unbearable.

Feeding them is prohibited around the customs office. The prohibition has made them particularly troublesome there.

Their young are born dark, with short legs and no heat pouch. For the first few weeks they travel beneath the mother’s belly, catching the folds of her skin with small hooked feet. From a distance, a nursing female appears to have too many knees.

Workers leave such animals undisturbed. Not out of sentiment alone. A startled mother can rise fast enough to throw a child clear of the platform and into a channel.

***

Much of the city’s food arrives from below.

Grain comes in sealed sacks. Fruit travels wrapped individually against heat and bruising. Dried fish is common. Fresh leaves are expensive and eaten soon after delivery, before they soften in the warm rooms.

There are local foods, though outsiders often need persuading. A little swimmer that gathers at cool inflows can be dried whole on mesh trays. Its body scarcely shows when alive, but turns opaque during drying, revealing two eyes and a silver thread of backbone. People eat handfuls with sour salt.

The larger basin animals are harvested less often. Some are good to eat only during a particular phase of the water. Some retain metals in organs that must be removed without breaking them. A good fishmonger is also a chemist, but need not be an agreeable person.

Cooking takes place over insulated tanks, hot plates, and narrow steam vents fitted with removable baskets. Kitchens are frequently outside the main rooms. In older houses, the cook stands on a little bridged platform where an earlier generation once had a front door.

People eat late. At dusk, bowls and stools appear on the walkways. There is more conversation then, but still little shouting. Across an open pool, a dropped spoon may be heard more clearly than a whole family talking.

Visitors mistake this for solemnity. They have not understood the jokes, many of which consist of repeating an ordinary remark at exactly the wrong moment.

***

The birds know the freight schedule.

They gather over the western platforms before wagons arrive, turning in wide circles until the handlers begin unloading. Then they descend to the rails and inspect torn wrapping, spilled grain, and the machinery itself.

Wire is taken with deliberation. A bird will pull a length, release it, move along, and pull again until it finds a loose end. If challenged, it flies away carrying the wire so that it trails below like an extra leg.

Their nests are untidy on the outside and surprisingly exact within. Fine loops hold the eggs clear of the roof. Air moves underneath. Heavier lengths anchor the bowl against wind.

During storms beyond the mountain, when pressure changes reach the plateau before the new water does, nesting birds rearrange these anchors. People once treated this as a reliable forecast. They no longer do. The birds are sometimes wrong, and occasionally they are merely stealing from one another.

The harsh warning call belongs to several different alarms. Residents distinguish them without much thought. One means an unfamiliar large animal overhead. One means a nest has been approached. Another, shorter and repeated, is given when a bird finds a dead bird.

That last call empties nearby walkways. A bird lying beside a channel may be the first visible evidence that its water has changed.

***

Not every living thing in the city can be easily counted.

In the seams below the refinery, the films grow in overlapping communities. A gray layer uses what an amber layer releases. Beneath both, almost hidden against the stone, a violet layer tolerates conditions that kill the others. Changes in flow expose one and bury another.

The workers know these patches individually. They speak of a seam as vigorous, exhausted, or coming back. A productive film can keep the same appearance for decades while every part of it is replaced.

There are disputes about whether it is alive in the same sense as a grazer or a person. The disputes become practical when a factory wants to divert a channel.

A seam destroyed to widen a tank may return the following year. Or it may not. The minerals can be right, the temperature right, and still nothing grows. Someone then brings a wet stone from another district, installs it in the current, and waits.

The animals arrive before the workers are sure it has succeeded.

First come the smallest swimmers. Then a grazer establishes itself downstream. Later, at night, something larger moves out from beneath the tank supports and opens its mouth toward the flow.

***

There are people who never become comfortable here.

Some were born in the city. They dislike its heat, its exposed distances, the vigilance demanded by an ordinary walk to the shops. They leave, or remain and complain. Some refuse work involving water. Some insist on growing plants in imported soil, beneath awnings, with an extravagance of cool fresh water that their neighbors discuss disapprovingly.

A woman near the northern sheds has kept a small tree alive for nineteen years. Its leaves are dusty and its trunk leans toward her bedroom window. She carries its fallen leaves outside one at a time and shows them to children.

Several children think the tree is a kind of animal.

At night, when the steam rises between the houses, the city’s inhabitants become less easily separated. Scavengers unfold from the platforms. Birds settle with wire ends ticking softly beneath them. Swimmers enter newly flooded courts. People bring bedding onto roofs that have finally begun to cool.

Below Sera’s rooms, a grazer works around the foot of a pier. Each pass leaves a narrow clean line. Upstairs, her nephew lies awake imagining the freight ship that will take him away. Her sister washes a set of false teeth in a covered bowl. Sera checks the water mark on the wall before lying down.

It is higher than it was last night, but not by much.

Under the bridge, something large shifts in the dark. Water enters the space it has vacated. For a moment the reflected window breaks into several pieces, then joins again.
