The smallest inhabitants are almost clear. Held against the light, one would show little more than a mouth, a forked intestine, and several pairs of feet moving around a particle it has swallowed. The feet are not transparent. They are dark and sharply articulated, like fragments of wire embedded in jelly.

They live in the spaces the larger animals leave inside their own bodies: beneath loose folds of skin, between the plates of a shell, in the moist pocket behind a joint. An animal that appears solitary may have thirty of them moving over it. They take away what dries there. Sometimes they carry something in.

They are not young versions of the larger creatures. Their young are smaller still, and for a considerable part of their lives they have no feet.

There are many kinds.

The animals under the doorsteps have a hundred legs only when several of them lie together. Each has sixteen, folded in four groups against a body shaped like a flattened seed. They fit their heads beneath the tails of their neighbors. Their backs are darker where a little light reaches them; farther under the threshold, they become the color of soaked paper.

A row of these animals can withdraw without appearing to move. The outermost folds itself more tightly. That gives its neighbor room to turn, and the turning travels inward until the whole row has slipped another fraction of an inch into shelter. What remains visible is a clean, dark line.

They do this before a door opens.

There are no footsteps on the other side, no hand approaching the latch. The warning comes through the threshold. Somewhere in the building a belt has changed tension, or a valve has admitted pressure into a different pipe. By the time the loading door swings outward, its lower edge passes over bare stone.

Afterward, the legs emerge again.

The broad-backed animals in the workshops have less opportunity to withdraw. Some have grown into their stations. A collar that once fitted around a neck is now enclosed by flesh; a pin entering the flank has become the center of a hard, smooth swelling. It is difficult to say which parts of a station are fixtures and which are bones.

They remain capable of small preferences.

The animal beneath the press turns its head between strokes. Not far: the socket allows only a few degrees. It uses those degrees to place its lower jaw against one side rather than the other. That side is polished. When the cup is changed, it follows the cup briefly with an eye so low on its face that the eye looks at first like another opening for fluid.

The wheel animals do not all walk alike. One puts its third foot down heavily. Another drags a foot over each bar before gripping it. Their wheels turn at the same speed, but the individual sequences can be heard through the shared knocking of the machinery. An animal moving nearby will sometimes pause until a particular footfall comes around.

At rest they are much less substantial. Their broad backs settle. The ridge beneath the press lowers almost flush with the surrounding skin. The wheel animal hangs from two feet and lets the other four trail. Small attendants enter the slack places, carrying grains that shine briefly before disappearing into the flesh.

Nothing here is idle for long, but there are intervals in which an animal is occupied chiefly with being kept alive.

The creatures beneath the eaves have mouths at the center of their undersides. Each mouth is surrounded by a pale adhesive field which must stay damp. Their grinding plates grow continuously and wear one another down. The powder falls onto the wall, where smaller animals collect it.

Their sound changes when the powder is scarce.

It is a minute change, lost to a listener in the general noise: a longer scrape on one side, a short silence on the other. Down at the gutters, the heavy creatures respond by shifting the stones in their stomachs. A series of clicks passes along the roof. In the workshop below, a wheel animal alters the pressure of its feet without altering its pace.

The calls travel faster than the substances they concern. A shortage can be answered before it arrives.

Much of the district’s noise consists of such small adjustments. Other sounds continue when there is nothing to correct. Animals newly hatched against a pipe make a faint rasp with their jaws, and the nearest adults answer them. The young repeat the sound, poorly at first. Over several days they acquire the local interval.

An adult moved to another wall would keep its old interval for a while. The surrounding calls would overlap it. Eventually it would fit.

The canal animals have almost no occasion to meet anything face to face. Their heads point upstream. Their bodies lie in the current, and what reaches one has already passed another.

They taste changes in the water with bands of soft tissue inside their mouths. A disturbed animal presses its body flatter, allowing the disturbance to pass over it. Those downstream feel the altered flow and flatten in turn. Beneath a bridge, this can make a patch of granular water suddenly smooth.

Their grip is released only a few times in a lifetime.

The first release occurs when they are scarcely longer than a grain of rice. They drift until a suitable joint in the masonry catches them. The second comes when their bodies have grown too long for that joint. They loosen at a particular vibration from the pumping house and travel a short distance downstream, arriving among animals of their own size.

The final release is less orderly. Sometimes an old mouth holds after the body has gone. The head remains on the stone, still surrounded by the current, until smaller inhabitants dismantle it.

There are dead animals here. They are simply difficult to recognize as dead before they become several other activities.

An ivory creature at the foot of the bench may cease tightening its jaws. For a while its neighbors continue to press against it. Then a transparent animal enters through the mouth. Another comes out carrying a white speck. A joint loosens; a leg is taken beneath the paving. The shell remains longer, holding the space the animal occupied.

Eventually something small lives inside it. Eventually that animal, too, becomes too large, or the shell is carried away.

The district does not maintain a constant number of bodies. In narrow places there are seasons of eggs, though no weather marks them. Some weeks the underside of a handrail is rough with capsules. At other times the capsules have opened and the passage seems unusually empty, because the young have entered gaps too small to see.

The large workshop animals reproduce rarely. One swells beneath its mounting until a row of seams appears along the belly. The seams part, releasing soft, folded creatures that do not resemble their parent much. They are collected by long-legged animals usually found behind the racks.

For a time they are carried from station to station. Most are set down and picked up again. One may be left beside a warm bearing, another beneath a pipe whose bracket has begun to move in the brick. They unfold differently in those places.

A few acquire broad backs.

You would not see this happen during an afternoon on the bench. You would see a small creature crossing a strip of exposed paving with something pale beneath its body. It would move quickly, stop under the next overhang, and remain there until the tremor in the stone changed.

The open square is crossed, then, but seldom casually. Its width matters to the inhabitants. So do the soft shadows of the bollards, the thin shelter beneath a sill, and the little enlargement of darkness made by your shoes.

While you sit, animals gather against the bench’s feet. Some were there already. Others have followed the pressure of your weight through the paving and found that the wood now bears differently on the iron frame.

Beneath the seat, a creature with a flat, unfinished-looking back raises itself against the new load. It presses, relaxes, and presses again. Nearby mouths change their hold. The fine vibration reaches you through your thighs.

If you stand, it will take them some time to settle back.
