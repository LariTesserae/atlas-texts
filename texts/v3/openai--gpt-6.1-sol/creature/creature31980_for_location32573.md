The first one you notice is sitting in a shelter you have already looked into.

It has not come through either entrance. What you took for the back of the sleeping shelf has leaned forward, and a pair of hands now rests on its knees.

It is about the height of a seated person. The proportions are less certain. Its shoulders are narrow, but behind them two folded structures run down almost to the floor, giving it the outline of someone wearing a long coat. Its legs are tucked beneath it. There is a head, tilted toward the water outside, and a face whose features become clearer as it turns.

Nothing about it is transparent. Its skin is the color of a peeled stem, with a faint green cast at the joints. Along its forearms, very small veins fork and disappear. The hands have four long fingers and a thumb set farther back than yours. The fingertips are broad and smooth.

Its eyes are dark enough to be startling here.

They are not large. They sit well apart beneath a slight brow, and each has a clear covering that catches the yellow light. It blinks by passing a membrane sideways across them. There are no lashes. Below the eyes, two narrow openings lie in the shallow groove where a nose might be. The mouth is a soft horizontal seam.

It looks at you without opening that seam.

Then it puts one hand against the wall.

You can see, now, that the hand was never entirely separate from it. Three fine strands run from the wrist into the leaf behind the sleeping shelf. They stretch as the creature moves, becoming almost invisible. One releases. Another releases. The third holds a moment longer, making a little dimple in the skin, and slips free.

There is no broken end on either side.

The creature gets up.

Its folded back opens slightly to accommodate the movement. What looked like a coat is part of its body: overlapping, ribbed panels, pale on the outside and a warmer green within. They settle together again when it stands. Its feet are long, divided at the front, with a broad pad under each heel. It places them carefully on the floor, not because the footing is difficult, but as though there is something to be learned from each place it touches.

It is shorter than you expected.

At the entrance it pauses, presses two fingers to a thick upright stem, and waits. A tremor passes through the small leaf beside its hand. Only then does it step out.

Others live here, but you begin to understand why you have not seen them.

They spend much of their time joined to the country.

In a resting body the distinction between person and shelter is slight. The back panels spread against a wall; the feet settle into shallow recesses; threads emerge at the wrists, spine, and ankles and meet the surrounding tissue. The head inclines. A leaf lifts beside the face. After a while, what remains visible has the same quiet irregularity as any other fold in the growth.

They do not turn into plants. An attentive watcher can still find the closed eyes, the mouth, the fingers nested against one another. But there are miles of pale surfaces to look at, and little reason to favor one seam over the next.

A shelter with an apparently empty sleeping shelf may have someone resting along its inner wall. Another may really be empty. Its owner is out beside a well, standing so still that a bend in the root conceals them without casting a shadow.

When they walk, they usually keep a hand near the growth. Not always touching it. Often the fingers follow an inch above a root, moving over each swelling as if following words on a page.

Their knowledge of the place is intimate but not complete. A closed corridor surprises them. They stop before it, feel several stems, and choose another route. They can persuade a narrow passage to widen, but it takes time, and they do not casually disturb a ridge to shorten a journey.

You may see one kneeling for hours beside the same bundle of roots. Its hands move only occasionally. By the next visit, a stiff blade has curved aside and another has laid itself along the floor. The opening is no wider than a shoulder.

Making a shelter is slower work. Several inhabitants take turns resting at the chosen place. They leave the impression of a palm, the reach of an arm, the space needed by a bent knee. The growth accommodates these pressures without acquiring the marks of force. A chamber is gradually furnished with the dimensions of its occupants.

Some are built for two. In these, the sleeping leaf has a shallow rise down the middle, and the hand recesses occur at different heights.

The inhabitants drink little. Beside a well, one lowers its fingertips to the surface and holds them there until the skin darkens. Sometimes it bends and touches its mouth directly to the water. Each swallow makes a small movement under the jaw.

They also receive something while joined to the walls. You can tell by the fullness that returns to their limbs after a long rest, and by the way an exhausted traveler attaches at several places instead of only one. They bring nothing into the chambers to eat. They have no hearths, bowls, or stores.

This does not make their lives effortless.

The spaces beside water are attended with great care. A fine root straying over the lip of a well is lifted and laid back with the others. A leaf that threatens to cover the surface is held away until it accepts a different curve. If a bead gathers at the roof but fails to fall, someone may spend a long while directly beneath it, touching the stem that bears its vein.

They cannot command the water to come.

When it does, they often watch it in company.

Two or three stand around a pocket, their feet placed beyond its waxy rim. A drop falls. Their eyes follow the little disturbance outward. One puts a hand on another’s shoulder, and leaves it there after the surface is still.

Their speech, when you hear it, is very quiet. The mouth opens barely enough to release a breath. There are low notes, small clicks, intervals in which both speakers touch the same stem. A conversation can continue after one has walked away. The one remaining keeps its fingers against the bark and occasionally answers.

They are not all alike. Some have broad, nearly flat back panels; others carry them in narrow folds. One walks with a shortened left leg, placing a hand on the floor at every third step. Another has a dark seam down the cheek that never smooths away. Resting against the country sustains them, but does not erase what has happened to them.

Their young are easier to find.

A young one has trouble settling. Its feet detach too soon; its fingers keep moving after the wrist strands have joined. It turns its head to watch an adult cross the chamber, and the leaf at its shoulder pulls crookedly. Eventually someone comes back and supports it, one hand under the jaw, another between the folded panels.

Small faces sometimes show in the wall recesses. Not ornaments or buds shaped by chance: faces with their own restless attention, following a pale six-legged animal as it moves beneath the shelf.

The adults are patient with those animals. They neither catch them nor drive them away. If one approaches an occupied resting place, a hand may lift to let it pass.

You once see an inhabitant remove an empty casing from a leaf. It holds the translucent thing on one fingertip and turns it toward the light. The casing preserves the bent legs, the tucked head, the delicate shape of a body that is no longer inside it.

For a long time, the inhabitant studies the opening through which the animal left.

Then it places the casing in a dry recess above its sleeping shelf.

On a later visit, it is still there.

The inhabitant shows it to you before settling for rest. It lifts the casing with extraordinary care, offers it close enough for you to see, and looks from the torn seam to your face.

You do not know what it wants to ask.

When you leave, it remains upright in the chamber entrance, holding the little empty form against the light.
