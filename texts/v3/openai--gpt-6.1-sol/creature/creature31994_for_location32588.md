The animal beneath the moss has a broad head and a body that narrows gradually into a swimming tail. Seen from above, it is easy to mistake for a length of submerged wood. Its back is dark brown, almost black along the spine, with irregular pale marks where the skin has thickened over old injuries. Silt settles into these marks. Fine roots sometimes catch there and remain for weeks.

An adult is about as long as a person is tall, though most of that length is tail. The forelegs are short and strong. The hindlegs fold close against the body, their long toes lying one over another until the animal needs them. In open water it swims with a slow sideways movement. Beneath a raft, it pulls itself through the hanging roots, using its hands to spread them without tearing them away.

Its head is flatter than it first appears. The eyes sit high, under clear lids, but the mouth opens on the underside. An animal resting at the surface can watch the bank while feeding below it. Around the jaw hang short, fleshy filaments. These keep moving when the rest of the body is still.

There is usually only a little of it to see: two eyes in a gap between leaves, a hand holding a submerged branch, the blunt end of the tail disappearing through pale roots. When it comes onto bare mud, its size becomes obvious. Its belly leaves a wide, smooth furrow between the footprints. The prints have five toes in front and six behind.

It seldom comes ashore for long. Its skin loses water slowly, but exposure seems uncomfortable even in the basin’s wet air. On a ridge it keeps turning, presenting one side and then the other to the mist. Before it returns to the water, it presses the underside of its jaw against the stone.

It does this most often beside the dripping cups.

***

The filaments around its mouth are sensitive to ordinary things: the movement of prey, dissolved substances, the pressure of a current against a root. They are also sensitive to the basin’s dispersed material. Beneath the skin of each filament lie minute, orderly grains, the same structure visible in the stone cups. They occur in patches along the animal’s belly and in a narrow band behind each eye.

The grains are present in the young, although hatchlings have fewer of them. More accumulate as the animals feed and grow. Some pass out of the body with shed skin. Others remain in place for years.

This gives the animal several ways to attend to water.

It can distinguish a cold pool from a warm one without entering either. At close range, it can feel where an inward current will develop before the leaves begin to turn. It also responds to the sounds that precede disturbances. A resting animal may flatten its filaments against its jaw, release its grip on a root, and move aside. Several seconds later a branch passes through the space it occupied.

The response is not always accurate. There are old animals with broken fingers, crooked tails, and deep scars along their flanks. A connection may close while something is moving through it. A quiet pool may receive a volume of water too large to pass without violence. During the sudden reversals after heavy rain, even the oldest animals stay close to the undersides of their rafts.

There they hold on with all four feet.

***

They eat the small crustaceans that crowd the white roots, along with soft larvae, water snails, and the pale growths on submerged timber. Their mouths contain two ridged plates rather than obvious teeth. The plates close with enough force to crush a shell. For grazing, the animal draws them lightly along wood or stone, leaving narrow clean tracks through the coating.

A raft occupied for several years has a recognizable underside. Some roots hang long and tangled. Others have been shortened to leave a passage beneath the thickest part of the moss. A branch is often wedged across that passage, giving the animal something firm to hold while the raft shifts.

It maintains this space with its mouth and forefeet. Loose roots are gathered, tested, and either pushed into the moss or bitten through. Rotten wood is carried out. Sound wood is retained. If a raft splits, its occupant may spend a day pressing the two edges together, braced beneath them while the current carries both downstream.

Sometimes the pieces join again. Sometimes they drift apart as soon as the animal lets go.

A displaced animal is more likely to follow the larger piece than to seek another raft. It knows the weight above it, the points where it can surface, and the particular sounds made by the roots when wind moves the bushes overhead. These remain useful even after the raft has crossed a lake or passed through a connection into a different reach.

An unfamiliar raft may offer more food and better shelter. The animal can spend several days circling it before moving underneath.

***

Their calls come from two sacs low in the throat. The sacs inflate unevenly, producing the rough double note that is usually heard as a single sound. Close by, it has a wet, granular quality. You can feel it through a branch if both you and the animal are touching the wood.

They call most often when the water level steadies at dusk.

An individual will lift its mouth into an opening in the moss, leave its eyes submerged, and give one short call. Then it waits. The pause may last several minutes. Sometimes it lowers its head without calling again.

A reply need not come from nearby. The basin carries the vibration along routes that change with the water. On one evening, two animals beneath neighboring rafts cannot hear each other. On another, a call from a distant lake reaches both clearly.

They seem to recognize particular voices. A familiar reply makes the throat relax; the filaments open and hang loosely. An unfamiliar one may bring the animal to the edge of its raft, where it listens with its belly pressed against the clay.

Pairs sometimes meet. One leaves its shelter and moves through the channels, stopping repeatedly to call. The other answers from beneath a raft or among the buttresses of a tree. Finding each other can take much of the night.

They also call without approaching. The same two voices may answer one another for a season, each animal continuing to feed, mend its shelter, and avoid the dangerous pools within its own reach.

When one voice stops, the other often keeps calling at the accustomed time.

***

The eggs are laid against the underside of living roots. Each is small, clear, and slightly flattened, with a dark comma of tissue suspended inside. A clutch looks like a patch of thick dew.

The adult stays beneath it. It removes snails with the tips of its fingers and blows water upward through the eggs whenever silt begins to collect. If a root dies, the eggs on it usually die too. The parent tests the clutch continually, passing its jaw filaments over one row and then another.

The hatchlings emerge with fringed external gills and almost transparent bodies. For their first weeks they cling to the roots by a small adhesive pad beneath the chest. Their tails hang into the water. Food catches in a ring of fine hairs around the mouth.

At this age, many are carried away.

A parent can shelter the clutch from a sweeping current, but it cannot keep every hatchling when the raft’s underside begins exchanging water with another pool. The young disappear singly or in little groups. Some surface beneath roots elsewhere in the basin. A few reach water where there is no suitable shelter.

The surviving young grow slowly. They lose their adhesive pads, develop legs, and begin scraping food from wood. Their gills shrink only after the pale grains have spread into the skin around the throat.

For a while they follow larger animals. They shelter under a fold of the adult’s tail or cling to its back while it moves between feeding places. An adult may carry several young of very different sizes. It removes them when grazing in tight spaces, setting them down among the roots and collecting them afterward.

Eventually each begins spending time beneath a separate patch of moss. The patch can be scarcely larger than the animal itself. A young one will bring it root fragments, pieces of soft wood, and fresh moss from the banks. Most of these small shelters break apart. A few thicken, catch against bushes, and become rafts.

***

The red leaves that arrive in the western marsh are useful to them. The animals eat the soft tissue between the veins and press the remaining leaf skeletons into damaged parts of their shelters. In the season of red leaves, a raft’s underside may be threaded with fine branching patterns.

No one sees the animals go to the trees those leaves came from.

Sometimes an adult disappears from a familiar reach and returns months later with pale mineral crusts along its belly. Sometimes a young animal appears under a raft where no eggs were laid. It may spend its first day testing the roots and scraping at the wood, then settle beside the resident as though it had arrived only a little late.

They remain few. Much of the water holds no sign of them, and a person crossing the basin can go for days without seeing more than a lifting edge of moss.

To see one well, stay beside an occupied raft through the quiet hour after dusk. Keep your weight on the same patch of ground. Eventually a hand may emerge and close around a root near your foot.

The fingers are blunt, webbed at the base, their dark nails worn smooth. The hand holds still while the filaments rise through the water beneath it. Then the eyes appear.

For a long time the animal examines the movement of water around your ankles. It may touch the impression your heel has left in the clay. When a distant concussion comes through the ground, its grip tightens.

The root draws taut. Water gathers in your footprint.

Only then does the animal lower itself beneath the moss, leaving its hand in place until the last of its body has passed.
