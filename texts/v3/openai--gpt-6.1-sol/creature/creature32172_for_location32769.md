The lizard is smaller than the movements that first disclose it. Its tail makes the dry stems tremble; its feet dislodge a little dust from a pot; then it comes into view, a narrow animal the color of the mortar, with darker flecks along its sides. It can lie across the rim of a bowl without either end touching earth.

It does not keep to one hiding place. There is a gap beneath the largest trough where it spends the hottest part of the afternoon, a broken handle through which it can pass, and a crack in the rock that admits its head and shoulders but not the sparrows. Its safety depends on knowing these distinctions. When it runs, it seems to disappear into solid things.

For now it stands high on its legs, keeping its belly off the hot paving. Its throat moves. One eye watches the ants, though most are not worth pursuing. A larger insect would bring it down from that rigid stance in an instant.

The ants have a different map of the courtyard. The walls are not boundaries to them, and the shelves are not separate levels. A joint leads up behind a jar, continues beneath a root, and emerges beside a flower stalk. Along it, ants pass in both directions, touching briefly before continuing. Some carry pale fragments too small to identify. One drags part of an insect wing, stopping whenever its edge catches in the grit.

Their nest is somewhere beyond sight, inside the masonry or behind it. What is visible is the work of keeping it supplied. They take the flower as it falls apart, investigate the sparrows’ discarded food, and find the soft places in things that appear wholly dry.

Under the leaves, smaller lives avoid even this much exposure. Mites gather in the crease of a curled leaf. A spider occupies the dark interval between a pot and the wall, with only two legs showing at the edge of its web. The web holds dust, a seed husk, and a tiny fly. It would be easy to mistake it for something abandoned until the spider advances and begins to free the fly from the accumulated litter.

The sparrows are the most conspicuous residents and the least capable of secrecy. They arrive quietly enough, but the young betray them. At each landing, the jar fills with demands.

One parent pauses outside the crack with something held in its beak. Its feathers are worn at the edges, its feet pinkish against the pale pottery. It looks toward the entrance, then toward the shrub, then goes in. For a moment the calls become louder; afterward they subside to an intermittent rasp.

Inside, the nest has fitted itself to the jar’s remaining shape. Dry grass and fibers fill the low part, with feathers nearer the center. The clay shelters the brood from the open air, but the parents still have to come and go through the same exposed slit. Their confidence is a sequence of short decisions, made again at every visit.

The plants stay, and endure being lived upon.

Their lower leaves bear the marks of former seasons: puckered surfaces, healed breaks, edges browned and hardened. New growth is modest. A pair of leaves opens at a stem tip; a rosette adds another close-set ring. Much of what looks like fullness is old growth retained beneath the new.

They have made cool, enclosed places at their feet. Dead leaves lodge between stems instead of blowing away. Dust accumulates there, along with droppings and insect remains. Beneath one sprawling plant, a root has found the joint between two stones and thickened until it can no longer be pulled out whole. The container above it has become only part of its dwelling.

Someone still tends this place. The pale cord is too clean to have been there long. A branch has been lifted just enough for a person to duck beneath it, and a loose shelf has a fresh wedge under one corner.

Whoever does this knows how little room there is. They must enter sideways, set down anything they carry before reaching upward, and move a pot with both hands so its rooted neighbor is not dragged along. They may come at an hour when the paving has cooled. They may stand as you stand now, waiting for a bird to return before deciding whether a repair can wait.

Their care has left room for uses they did not arrange: a jar for sparrows, mortar for wasps, shade for a lizard. Even the tied branch gives something a place to perch.

Beside your shoe, the ants continue around the fallen grain. Farther up the wall, another has found a newly opened flower. The lizard lowers its body and turns its head. Somewhere inside the jar, a young bird calls once, then listens.
