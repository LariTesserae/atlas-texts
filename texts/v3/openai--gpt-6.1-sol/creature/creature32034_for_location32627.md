At first, the figure beside the channel looks like something the current has gathered.

There is a forked length of wood, upright but not rooted, with several stones lodged against it. Strips of pale growth hang from the fork. One of the stones is the size and shape of a shoulder. Another rests where a knee might be, although nothing joins them except water and a wavering strand of weed.

It remains there while loose material passes. A leaf catches against the wood, stays for a little while, then comes free.

Eventually the shoulder-stone turns.

The movement is slow enough to be mistaken for settling. But the stone rises slightly before it turns, and the pieces below it rearrange themselves to receive its weight. The fork inclines. What seemed to be an accumulation has placed one part of itself farther upstream.

These are the inhabitants of the channels.

They are easiest to see where the water narrows. In broad pools, their outlines loosen until little distinguishes them from suspended debris. They can cross a basin that way, a few dark fragments traveling together at different depths. At a ledge or a fall, they gather. Water draws taut between the fragments. The pieces assume positions that they have occupied before, and a body becomes visible.

No two bodies are assembled alike. One carries a long slab of stone down its back. Another has a ribwork of branches, some still alive, bearing small leaves along what would be its sides. The smallest may consist of three pebbles and a loop of dark fiber. The larger ones take on the proportions of someone standing with a burden held against the chest.

There is usually a hollow through the middle.

The current passes through this hollow more quickly than it passes around the body. Fine sediment draws its shape for a moment: a narrow passage, widening below, then narrowing again. Within it, a few grains move upward against the descending water. They rise, hesitate, and return. Even when the figure is otherwise still, this circulation continues.

They do not warm the water. Close beside one, the cold is the cold of the basin. They do, however, gather warmth where it exists. In a grove, an inhabitant may spend a long time with one end of a wooden member laid against a spring crack. The pale growth along its frame opens. Mineral clouding settles on its stones.

When it leaves, it carries that small difference with it.

For some distance downstream, the leaves touching its body remain unfolded.

Their substance is mostly local. A branch lost in one ravine may become part of a body in the next. Stones are exchanged at the foot of falls. Sometimes an inhabitant stops, lowers itself, and releases a piece that has served it for years. It will wait beside the released piece until the current has placed it securely.

The worn objects in the shelters are harder to account for.

Among the river stones and weathered sticks, one sometimes sees a shaped wooden peg, a small grinding stone, or a length of cord with knots too regular to have formed in the water. These are held more closely than the other materials. They remain near the central hollow as the outer frame changes.

An inhabitant whose entire visible body has dispersed may still be found by one such object, moving slowly along the old road.

The cords are particularly important. Most are scarcely cords now. Growth has thickened them; individual fibers have separated and been bound together again by fine roots. A knot can become a swelling the size of a fist. Yet the inhabitants handle them without confusion. They pass a strand through a gap, draw it back, turn the swollen knot once, and wait.

Afterward, a loose door may rest more firmly against its wall.

The huts were made for bodies more definite than theirs.

A seat is too broad for the stones that now settle upon it. The lintels admit a height that most inhabitants cannot sustain except in a strong current. Their rods fit into sockets beside the channels, but the rods are rounded at one end in a way that suggests they were once held.

Some inhabitants still use them.

One braces itself above a waterfall, places a rod against the lip, and shifts a stone a little to the left. Its body thins with the effort. For several moments there is almost nothing holding its upper pieces together. Then the rod slips free, the current passes cleanly over the lip, and the pieces come back into alignment.

There was no blockage. The water would have continued without the adjustment.

Nevertheless, the inhabitant waits until the pale ribbon falls in the particular way the stone permits. Only then does it leave.

Much of their activity has this character. They clear channels that would eventually clear themselves. They lift leaves from roofs before the leaves have gathered enough weight to matter. In the garden shelters, they separate crowded roots with the end of a stick, passing it slowly through the pale beds. Plants pulled free are carried to a crack where warmer water emerges, or laid under a stone until they take hold.

They do not eat the plants.

There is no mouth into which anything disappears, no resting place strewn with the remains of food. What the work supplies them is less apparent. After tending a garden, a body often holds together farther into still water than it did before. Its stones remain high. Its loose fibers trail less.

Neglected inhabitants become difficult to distinguish from the things they carry.

There is one beneath a fallen slab in a hanging basin, where the stream now passes above the rock. Two wooden members project from the silt. Between them lies a small stone with a hole drilled through it.

When the ice sounds, the wood trembles. A thread of sediment rises through the drilled hole.

Nothing else has moved there for many seasons.

Others continue to visit. They settle along the edge of the slab and remain until the faint rising thread stops. Sometimes they bring a different pebble and leave it nearby. None attempts to prise the slab away. The stream crossing its upper face is an old one, and they are reluctant to disturb an old course.

They travel chiefly by these courses, but not always downstream.

To climb a fall, an inhabitant dismantles itself from the bottom upward. A pebble rises along one edge of the descending ribbon and lodges on the upper ledge. A second follows. Fiber streams between them. The heavier wood takes longest, swaying below the lip until enough of the body has collected above to draw it through.

At the top, the pieces rest apart.

Then, with no abrupt movement, there is once again someone beside the water.

They can leave a stream to follow the road. This is more laborious. They keep close to the paving, using the low vegetation to maintain their outline. A stone advances from one patch of growth to another. Wood rolls, stops, and rises on one end. The central passage becomes shallow and broad.

Long crossings cost them pieces. The dark expanses between groves contain isolated pebbles that are smoother than the surrounding rock, and lengths of worked wood lying far from any building. An inhabitant passing such a piece may pause beside it. Sometimes it takes the piece up. Sometimes it continues without it.

At the house facing the black basin, one has remained for longer than the rooted branch has occupied the roof.

It is rarely visible in the doorway. Its frame rests inside, against the rear wall, spread around the niche. The clear strip across the floor passes through it.

In the niche lies a shallow bowl.

Unlike the bowls beneath the trickles elsewhere, this one is not fixed in place. It has been lifted often enough that the silt beneath it remains thin. Its rim is worn on one side. A short cord passes through a hole in the wood.

When the current strengthens and raises the curtain of leaves, the inhabitant gathers around the bowl. A dark stone comes away from the wall. Two pale sticks rise from the floor. The cord tightens.

It carries the bowl to the threshold and holds it there, level, facing the basin.

Water flows across the rim and through the doorway. Nothing enters the bowl that does not also enter the house. Still, the inhabitant holds it until the leaves begin to descend.

Then it returns the bowl to the niche.

During the brief thaw, when seams of light open in the ice, the inhabitants become easier to follow. Green shows along their wooden parts. Their stones disclose differences that darkness conceals: white quartz, red-stained shale, a black piece polished almost smooth. Some contain fragments of the road itself.

A few climb toward the close roof.

They stop below the hanging needles, where there is scarcely room to assemble upright. Flattened against the mountain, they let the new light pass through their hollows. The small grains circulating inside become visible, each making its slow journey upward and back.

When the seams close, they descend before the ice can fasten upon them.

The one in the house does not make this journey. It continues to raise the bowl when the roof leaves lift.

Sometimes another inhabitant arrives by the climbing branch of the road. It waits outside until the curtain rises, then comes into the clear strip. The two remain close together, their loose fibers moving in the same narrow current. A piece may pass between them: a splinter, a stone, once a length of cord.

The visitor leaves by the descending road.

After its departure, the figure at the niche takes longer to put itself away. One stone remains near the doorway. A wooden member leans across the threshold until the falling leaves brush it.

At last the member turns inward.

The stone follows, scraping a short pale mark through the silt.
