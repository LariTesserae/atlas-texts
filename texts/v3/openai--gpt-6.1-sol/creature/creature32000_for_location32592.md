The narrow animal wakes when the wall around it becomes slightly too small.

Not much too small. One of its ribs can no longer move freely against the lining of its pocket. During sleep it has grown, or the plants have drawn together as the water in them cooled. It waits through several breaths to learn which. Then it presses the side of its face against a stem.

The fingers covering its face unfold.

There are more than twenty, arranged in unequal rings around a mouth so small it is seldom visible. The outer fingers are furred almost to their ends. The inner ones are naked, translucent, and divided by little joints that bend in either direction. Each ends in a dark, moist pad. They touch different parts of the stem, holding still while fluid moves beneath the skin.

Its eyes are far back on its head, under the fur. They can distinguish a bright channel from a dark one, but not much else. Most of what it knows arrives through its face: pressure, salt, the minute displacement of a vessel as a distant leaf closes. A swimmer brushing a submerged root reaches it as a soft interruption. Somewhere above, an ice splint has begun to lean.

It uncurls without haste. Its body is longer than its resting pocket first suggested, narrow enough to pass through spaces that seem closed. Six short limbs run along its underside. The front pair have blunt digging nails; the others end in divided pads that adhere to wet tissue. Its fur lies in fine, backward-pointing grooves. Water passes through these grooves and leaves the skin beneath almost dry.

It does not shake itself. Sudden movement would cost warmth, and there is nowhere for the water to go except onto something else.

For a while it hangs with its face against the stem. Then three fingers close around a loose scale of leaf and pull.

The leaf yields along an old line. Under it is a blister of thick, nearly clear fluid. The animal opens its mouth and drinks a quantity smaller than a tear.

The blister is not a wound. Similar blisters occur along several of the older trunks, always behind scales that are easily lifted. Most remain untouched. Sometimes the animal passes one, tests it, and continues to another farther away. What distinguishes them is not visible.

After drinking, it pushes the scale back into place.

Its sleeping pocket will have widened by the time it returns.

***

The broad white animals occupy the lowest hollows.

Seen from above, each resembles a disk of frost caught among the roots. The stiff hairs around its body hold droplets at their tips. Beneath the fringe are four limbs, a folded tail, and a face pressed almost flat against the floor. Two small eyes open sideways, where they are shielded from falling crystals.

The hair is hollow. Near the skin, each shaft branches into a cluster of finer tubes, all filled with fluid that thickens as the animal cools. An awake animal draws its fringe close. A sleeping one lets it spread, placing hundreds of hair tips against the vegetation.

This is when it calls.

The sound begins in a chamber under the breastbone. It is too low to seem loud at first. Loose leaves lift along their margins. Water inside a nearby stem contracts into a faster thread. Farther away, another white animal answers without opening its eyes.

No two calls are quite alike. The oldest animals have interruptions in theirs: a soft catch, a double pulse, a thin trembling at the end. Their sleeping places bear matching irregularities. A rib of vegetation curves around a hip that once healed crookedly. A shallow groove accommodates a fringe worn short on one side.

They eat the floor from underneath.

A white animal pushes its head into the felt and opens a broad mouth lined with flat, closely spaced teeth. It removes the oldest shoots, the ones whose channels have narrowed and whose leaves no longer lift. Fresh growth above them remains connected through fine strands. As the animal feeds, those strands settle into the space left behind.

Its droppings are soft pellets of pale fibers, almost without color. Clear grazers gather over them. Within a few weeks there is no pellet, only a patch of floor with unusually many small leaves.

The white animals are heavy. A young one can sink into tissue that would hold the narrow animal easily. They seldom cross the youngest cushion, though they sleep along its edge. When a folded leaf there opens, it sometimes opens toward the warmth beneath a spread fringe.

***

The ceiling animals are individually very small.

Their six feet enter pores in the leaves. Each foot ends in two hooks, one rigid and one soft; the soft hook swells after insertion. An animal cannot simply fall. Even dead, it must be loosened by the growth around it.

They feed with their backs toward the floor and their mouths against the leaf undersides, rasping away films too thin to see. There are so many that their feeding becomes the continuous hiss of the hollow. Where a patch falls silent, the leaves soon acquire a dull silver coating. Moisture spreads across that coating instead of gathering into beads.

The ceiling animals move toward damaged tissue, but slowly. Their young are faster.

A newly hatched one is little more than a dark dot with pale limbs. It walks over the backs of adults until it reaches a vacant pore. Sometimes it enters a leaf that has not yet opened. It remains there as the leaf expands, carried farther from its siblings with every increment of growth.

There are old leaves whose original occupants are still attached. Their shells have become almost transparent. They scrape only briefly between long periods of rest, yet the patch beneath each mouth remains clear.

***

In the pool, a swimmer can be mistaken for a leaf turning over.

Its body has no fixed upper surface. Either side can become the back. The fins fold inward, the violet gills withdraw, and the mouth shifts along a crease at the body’s edge. When it passes through a narrow space, one half of it goes first and the other follows like cloth drawn through a ring.

The smallest swimmers shelter directly beneath the white hairs that keep the surface open. Older ones descend into channels where water presses past them in slow sheets. Their bodies become narrower there. Their fins lengthen into ribbons.

Some return to the pool after years, carrying grains of unfamiliar color in the folds beside their gills.

Others remain below. A light passing beneath the floor may reveal one stretched through several adjoining chambers, its head resting in one and its tail in another. It feeds on drifting matter, turning its body across the current until a little accumulation forms along its mouth.

When such an animal dies, the water has to find another route.

The living channels respond gradually. A wall thins. A root divides. A passage that has been dim for a long time begins to glow, and the clear grazers enter it.

***

The inhabitants of the mineral sacs have hard mouths and soft bodies.

Their skins are pale enough to show the dark material in their guts. They enter a sac through its closing lip and test the forming stone with short blows of the jaw. A sound travels through the sac, into the supporting root, and away.

Some nodules are left untouched. Others are eaten from within until only a thin shell remains.

The powder passes through the burrowers into small storage pouches along their sides. They may carry it for months. At the margin of the hollow, where new sacs swell under the root curtains, they press those pouches against receptive pores and empty them a grain at a time.

A burrower’s skin bears faint ridges from the last sac it inhabited. On old animals, several sets overlap. Their bodies preserve shapes the stone never kept.

***

On the youngest cushion, the clear grazers are almost invisible except when full.

A clipped shoot travels slowly through one, turning as it goes. Behind it come smaller fragments, then a green cloud. The animal has two jaws that close sideways and many feet so fine they leave no lasting impression.

It feeds along the crease between two folded leaves. At first only its mouth fits. Then its head enters, and eventually its whole body.

The leaves do not close behind it. Their inner surfaces begin to change. Pores open where there were none. Fine hairs reach into the wet gap. Somewhere beneath them, a channel produces its first dim light.

Other animals follow. A ceiling hatchling finds purchase on the new surface. A swimmer pushes a fin into the water gathering below. The narrow animal touches the opening with three fingers, then withdraws.

Much later, a white animal settles nearby.

Nothing living in the hollow knows the whole of it. The swimmers encounter water that the grazers never reach. The burrowers spend long intervals sealed inside darkness. The ceiling animals know the undersides of leaves through their mouths and feet.

Even the joined vegetation changes unevenly. One stem learns to shed frost sooner than its neighbor. A new leaf admits moisture through a structure absent from the older leaves around it. For years, a useful change may remain confined to one small part of the hollow.

The inhabitants keep passing through those parts. They carry powder, warmth, clipped tissue, water caught in fur. They leave pressure in the floor and scrape clean places on the ceiling. Some return to the same pocket throughout their lives. Some enter a channel and are never seen again.

Behind the pool, the gap between two leaves has become wide enough for a grazer to turn around.

It turns, feeds along the opposite edge, and rests there with the newly swallowed green visible inside it.
