The people who live here usually have one hand free.

Even the basket carriers arrange their loads so that they can put a palm against a wall. They do it at corners, on stairs, when entering a room they have not visited lately. Sometimes they merely steady themselves. Sometimes their fingers linger, spread wide, while someone behind them waits without asking what is wrong.

Their hands are their most visibly worn parts. The nails are short and thickened; the skin at the fingertips splits, heals, and splits again. Fine clay stays in the creases after washing. Old workers develop a polish along the heel of the palm from smoothing blocks, while rope handlers have a dark, hard band across the base of each finger. You can often tell what a person does by the way they take a cup.

They wear close sleeves, tied above the wrist. Loose cloth catches on wedges and drinks water from walls. Most garments have a second layer sewn across the knees, and patches of stiff cloth over the shoulder on which their owner carries loads. These patches outlast the garments. They are cut free and sewn onto replacements, so that a young woman’s shirt may bear a shoulder piece worn smooth by someone who died before she was born.

There are bright threads here, but you see them mostly indoors. A narrow red seam inside a cuff. Blue knots in a child’s cap. Someone has stitched a yellow bird onto a sleeping blanket, with a long beak and legs much too thin to support it. The owner says that is how birds are made. She has never seen one herself, but she trusts the person who told her.

Their eyes are ordinary eyes. They do not see miraculously in darkness. They squint, misjudge distances, lose things, and burn precious lamp fuel searching for them. What they have learned is how little sight a task actually requires. A person can mend a basket while talking across a room, locating each broken strip with a thumbnail. Two neighbors can pass in a narrow passage without a light because one taps the wall and the other knows which side of that particular passage has room.

Strangers are less fortunate. People hear them coming: hesitant steps, a shoulder striking a jar, silence where there should have been a greeting.

A greeting is often practical.

“Low lintel.”

“Wet third step.”

“Stay left. They’ve opened the floor.”

Among friends, these warnings may be abbreviated to a click of the tongue or a touch on the elbow. Affection has other forms as well, but it is difficult to separate from looking after someone’s passage through the place. A man who quarrels bitterly with his sister will still stop on his way to work to shave the bottom of her sticking door. She will complain that he has taken too much off.

They do not all build.

There are people who cook, count baskets, mend lamps, carry messages, tend the sick, or sort material brought up from the shafts. There are sellers who keep their goods in shallow trays, letting customers feel what they cannot afford to illuminate. There are people who make very little and borrow a great deal. A woman in one of the upper terraces has become notorious for claiming every cracked jar was cracked before she borrowed it.

But almost everyone can pack a seam, brace a sagging shelf, or pass blocks along a line. When something begins to fail, occupation briefly ceases to matter. A man still wearing his flour-covered apron may spend half the night holding a lever while a lamp maker crawls beneath a floor.

Some enjoy this more than their usual work. Others resent it with great fluency.

Their households seldom fit entirely inside their houses. One family sleeps in three chambers on different levels because those were the spaces available when the children grew. The youngest eats breakfast with his aunt, crosses two workshops to fetch his shoes, and goes to sleep beneath a platform occupied by people who are not relatives but have agreed to wake him if the drip reaches his bedding.

There are disputes over these arrangements. There are disputes over nearly everything that can be moved, occupied, obstructed, or made damp. People drag stools into passages to reserve places. They move one another’s stools. They remember who supplied a brace during the last bad settling and bring this up during arguments about entirely different matters.

Old age does not necessarily make them gentle. An old block layer may refuse to let anyone alter a course he laid thirty years ago, even while its face visibly bulges. Another, unable to lift much anymore, spends her days finding faults in other people’s walls. She is tiresome, often correct, and seldom invited to celebrations until someone remembers that a great many people would not be alive without her.

Children are given small useful jobs long before they can judge when a job is no longer small.

They collect fallen wedges, watch bowls fill, and carry messages whose wording they embellish. They learn that some knocks mean a basket is ready to rise, while others belong only to the workers making them. They also discover how easily adults can be deceived by a confident imitation. At least once in most childhoods, someone produces a very convincing settling noise in a room full of sleepers.

The punishment is severe. The story remains funny for years.

Children play at building houses, but their houses have extravagant flaws: six doors into one cupboard, stairs ending in the air, a basin large enough for all the water in the valley. They press their hands into fresh clay where they have been told not to. Occasionally a print escapes smoothing and becomes part of a wall. Its maker grows up, moves away to another terrace, and returns to find that the little hollow still holds a spoonful of water.

The pale drain creatures live closer to these people than anything else does.

They are usually no longer than a forearm, though that is difficult to judge because they fold themselves oddly when resting. Their backs carry a single hard ridge broken into rounded sections. The rest of the body looks unfinished: soft flanks, a broad mouth, feet with too many small pads to count while they are moving.

They eat the thin growth that forms inside wet vessels. They also eat scraps, lamp residue, and occasionally the soft edge of an unfired block. A creature left alone beside a new wall may make a surprisingly deep notch by morning.

Drain workers tolerate them because a drain with these animals in it tends to remain open. Potters dislike them for the same reason. Children keep them in bowls until they escape. They are poor pets. They show no gratitude, dislike being carried, and can remain immobile long enough to be mistaken for dead.

When one dies, its back ridge dries almost white. People sometimes use the pieces as scrapers. Others will not touch them.

There are smaller inhabitants too: pale insects under matting, mites in grain, tiny things that thrive in the greasy warmth above lamps. Their presence is usually discovered by damage. A stored rope loses strength; a sleeping mat acquires a fine powder beneath it. In a place made almost entirely of things that can be handled, something is always trying to eat what holds another thing up.

The larger creatures are less well understood.

No one agrees on how many there are. People on opposite slopes may describe the same dragging sound on the same night, or separate sounds on different nights and insist they belong to one animal. Some call them wall animals. Others use a word that also means an obstruction too heavy to remove.

A few people have seen parts of them.

A shaft worker once watched a blunt limb withdraw through a space between braces. It bent in two places and ended in a cluster of hard, pale tips. A woman opening an old storeroom found what she took for a folded mat until the folds tightened and warm air struck her face. She closed the door. The storeroom has not been used since, although people still argue about whether she was entitled to abandon the jars inside it.

There are reports of a wet eye, a ridged belly, a broad moving surface with pieces of pottery embedded in it. None establishes the shape of the whole.

They seem to occupy cavities that should be too small for them. This may only mean that the cavities are larger than anyone knows. Sound travels strangely through packed vessels and hollow braces. A dragging at your ear may be several rooms away.

Their passage sometimes leaves a wall firmer than before. Sometimes it loosens everything.

This is why the rules are obeyed even by people who ridicule the stories. The danger is not dependent on anyone’s interpretation.

After a large movement, workers inspect the supports and listen at the new cracks. They may find clay pushed tightly into an empty joint. They may find a brace crushed to powder. Once, beneath a kitchen, they found six intact bowls nested inside one another, none belonging to the household. The bowls were washed and put to use.

People do not universally believe that the creatures arrange such things. They also do not universally believe that they do not.

The deepest crews have their own habits. They speak softly while opening a tiled face. They leave unfamiliar handles where they find them until the surrounding material has been cleared. Some refuse to work beside anyone who whistles.

They come home carrying fragments in their pockets: an unusually smooth bead, a tiny hinge, a piece of patterned floor. These are given to children, used as counters, or laid on a shelf and forgotten. A few households have little stairways recovered from below. The steps are narrow even for a child’s fingers. Dust collects on them like it does on everything else.

The people continue to enlarge the valley because they need rooms, storage, workshops, better routes for water. Some are proud of it. Some think the next terrace is badly planned. Some intend to move there as soon as it is finished; others say it will never dry.

At the unfinished edge, the workers are mostly young, but not all. An older man kneels among them because he is good at establishing the first course. He has a cough made worse by the cold. Beside him, a girl keeps the cord clear while her brother brings blocks. They argue about the pace. Behind them, someone has already marked a place for a cooking hearth.

When a faint knock comes from beneath the new floor, all three pause.

The older man puts his palm down. The girl holds the cord motionless. Her brother shifts the weight of the block against his chest.

They wait long enough to hear whether anything follows.

Then the man asks for more clay, and the girl reaches back for it without letting go of the line.
