The people who live there are pale, but not uniformly so. Where a collar rubs, or a hand is habitually braced against a wall, the skin has a faint yellow cast. Their knuckles are darker than their palms. Old bruises show for a long time. In the gray light, it is often easier to recognize someone by these small marks than by their face.

They are no taller than the distance between the first two joints of a human finger. Most can stand upright inside their houses, although they seldom do. Indoors they sit with their knees raised or lean in the corners, putting some of their weight on each wall. A visitor, if there could be one, might mistake this for fatigue. It is simply how they occupy a room. Standing unsupported in the middle of a floor feels to them like standing on a roof.

Their shoulders are narrow enough to pass through openings that will not admit their heads facing forward. They turn sideways before entering, without interrupting what their hands are doing. The youngest children must be reminded. A child’s forehead may bear a line of small abrasions from learning the proportions of home.

Their feet are broad at the toes. Each toe moves separately, and the outermost can grip the edge of a step. They go barefoot on public paving. Shoes, where they are used at all, are soft coverings worn inside to prevent the feet from catching on bedding. A mason’s soles are often whitened with stone dust; a water measurer’s are so clean that the creases show.

Their eyes are large, though not enough to see in the unlit districts. There they navigate by contact: two fingers against a wall, the edge of a foot searching for a familiar groove. A person carrying something in both hands will ask another to go ahead and guide them by a cord. The cord is never pulled sharply. A tug might be an instruction, or it might mean that the guide has fallen.

They hear very well. This is not entirely an advantage.

A child newly brought to the square may cry at the sound of the falling drop. Its mother holds the child against her neck, making a little shelter with her chin and shoulder. Later the child learns to endure the crack, then to distinguish the lesser sounds around it: a vessel set down on a bench, a heel striking the far bridge, the dry rattle of a measuring stone being shifted.

In old age, some lose the finer sounds. They can still feel the sluice through the floor. They can still tell when someone has arrived at the threshold. Their families move conversations onto their hands.

The language spoken against an ear is brief and full of sounds made with the lips. Its words change from quarter to quarter, since very little speech carries far enough to correct a neighbor’s usage. The language touched onto the wrist travels more easily. A question begins near the base of the thumb. A refusal is made with two fingertips held still. Consent involves a small movement toward the elbow.

Children learning it sometimes answer too soon, mistaking the beginning of a long statement for a short one. There is a gesture that means, approximately, *leave your hand where it is; I have not finished*.

Among friends, touch becomes less exact. Someone may rest a thumb on the inside of another’s wrist without communicating anything that could be carved into a wall. Two people sitting together can remain in this position through several changes of the light.

They are not a solemn people, although their public business gives that impression. Their jokes are difficult to convey without a hand. One common trick is to announce a perfectly ordinary obstruction—a basin in a passage, a loose chip on a stair—then continue adding details until the listener realizes that the obstruction is the person telling the story. Children imitate the measured, patient way a council member feels along a wall before ruling on its dampness. Council members are known to enjoy this, provided the imitation does not take place during an inspection.

They own few things that are wholly private.

A basin belongs to the household that carved it, but anyone may need to borrow it. A ladder belongs to a person until it has been left long enough in a useful position; after that, moving it requires consultation. Bedding is private. Beads are private. A good flint may pass through several hands during a day and return at night to the same narrow shelf.

Their clothes are pieced together rather than cut from single sheets. Long strips of membrane are valuable and are reserved for hinges, carrying slings, and the flexible joints of water scoops. A sleeve may contain six generations of patches. In the damp, the seams become stiff, so people soften them by working them between finger and thumb while they sit.

No one remembers obtaining fresh membrane. When a garment becomes too fragile to wear, its sound pieces are removed. The rest is scraped, soaked, and pressed for other uses. Children wear scraps too small to serve an adult, joined with stitches large enough to be unpicked. At a certain age, a child begins receiving pieces of clothing from people outside the household. This is how they learn whom they resemble, and who has been watching them grow.

Their food is difficult to distinguish from their work.

In each house there is a small settling vessel, sheltered from splashes but fed by a thread of incoming water. What collects in it looks at first like nothing more than a discoloration. It is lifted with a fine blade, folded, and left on a porous chip to drain. Prepared food is translucent, slightly elastic, and eaten in pieces no larger than the point of a flint.

They have strong opinions about its texture. Water from the back crack produces a firmer sheet. Water drawn farther along the channel is softer and must be handled gently. Some households mix their portions; others consider this wasteful. The creature behind the council house feeds in the same current, though no one has succeeded in preparing food exactly where it grazes.

Meals are small and frequent. A person may be seen pausing halfway through a repair to take a folded morsel from the back of a fingernail. Eating together requires more space than eating alone, so a household meal often takes place with one person on the floor, one on a shelf, and another sitting in the doorway. They pass the food down.

They are capable of wanting more room.

A young couple will inspect every unusable recess in their quarter. They test ceilings with their knuckles and measure the widths of cavities with a bent arm. Occasionally they discover a place large enough for a bed, provided the sleeper is prepared to share it with a drain. The discovery can occupy family discussions for months.

Those who already have good rooms are reluctant to surrender them. This causes bitterness, particularly when an old inhabitant lives alone in a house built for several people. The law does not compel a move. Neighbors may nevertheless become remarkably attentive, offering help with washing, repairs, and errands, until the old person understands that each kindness is also an inspection.

There have been quarrels over this. There have been doors sealed from within.

The people of the city are patient with stone and water. They are less consistently patient with one another.

When someone dies, the body is wrapped and laid in a dry recess while the household decides what to do with the room. There are cavities set aside for the dead, narrow spaces reached through openings too small for anyone to enter. The wrapped body is passed inward with a forked tool. The opening is then closed with a fitted chip, not mortared shut.

For a while, those who knew the dead person touch the chip as they pass. Eventually there is no one who remembers which name belongs to it. The stone remains fitted to the opening. It may be removed generations later when a wall needs repair, and fitted back afterward.

Children are born into rooms where there has been no spare place to put them. A shelf is cleared. Tools are moved to a neighbor’s house. Someone sleeps across a threshold. For several years the arrangements are inconvenient, and then the child becomes useful: small enough to retrieve a dropped bead, light enough to cross a doubtful slab, able to enter a passage through which an adult can only reach.

Adults try not to ask too much of this usefulness. They do not always succeed.

The translucent animal is treated with a gentleness not extended to everyone. When it slips, people steady it with boards rather than with their hands. Its skin is easily damaged. It sometimes remains motionless in a lane for hours, blocking the shortest route to several houses. No one attempts to hurry it. Those same people may spend the delay complaining about a neighbor’s basket left on a stair.

The shell beneath the bridge receives less attention. A few inhabitants visit it regularly. They lower their faces close to the pool and watch the feeler. One woman maintains that it bends against the current on certain days. Others say she is seeing the movement of her own breath on the water. She goes on visiting.

Most people have work that brings them repeatedly past one particular place. They know it more closely than they know the city as a whole: a shallow landing with a warm-colored stain, a lintel that touches the back when one stoops, a bridge whose left edge is sharper than its right. Away from such places they are cautious. At home they move with astonishing speed.

Now and then, a footstep on the bath stair above them sends a heavy vibration through the ceiling. Dust drops from joints that otherwise yield nothing. Conversations stop beneath the hands. People carrying basins set them down.

They wait.

When the movement has passed, they examine their roofs. Usually there is no damage. A child may ask what made the sound, and receive different answers from different adults.

There is no agreed name for whoever walks above. Most inhabitants have never needed one. They have names for the places where the ceiling sheds grit, and for the rooms in which the great vibration is felt most strongly. They know which shelves to clear afterward.

Then the mason retrieves the pebble hammer. Someone picks up the basin. A hand finds again the wrist it had been touching, and finishes what it was saying.
