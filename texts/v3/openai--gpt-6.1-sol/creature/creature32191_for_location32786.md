At first you mistake one for part of a resting platform.

There is a pale strip beneath the woven seat, folded back upon itself. It might be a spare length of binding. Then one end lifts, separates into four narrow lengths and a shorter fifth, and rests against the platform’s edge.

The hand remains there for some time.

It is not gripping anything. Its fingers are laid along the wood with the careful spacing of someone testing whether a surface is dry. There are small dark knots at the joints. The thumb has been repaired with a different fiber, almost white.

Eventually the rest of the inhabitant draws itself out.

It does not unfold all at once. An arm comes first, then a shoulder; a broad, shallow chest rises between them. What seemed to be the underside of the platform becomes a back. The body has been resting flat enough that you could have looked directly at it without seeing a creature.

Standing, it is shorter than you are. Its legs are long, its torso disproportionately wide. The feet spread as they take its weight, opening into flexible soles whose edges turn up slightly on the hot clay. There is no sound when it walks.

Its surface is neither skin nor clothing. Fine strips overlap across the ribs, changing direction at the shoulder, tightening into dense whorls at the elbows. Through the looser work at its throat you can see something red moving very slowly.

The face is a shallow oval of pale clay.

It has been worked by hand. One cheek is fuller than the other. Below the eyes, a thumb has smoothed the material upward and left a faint ridge. The mouth is a short horizontal opening with soft edges, not quite straight. Two small holes beside it appear to be places where a fastening was tried and abandoned.

Its eyes are wet and dark. They do not look unfinished.

It watches the cup in your hand, then your face. When you put the cup down, it approaches, lifts it, and runs one finger around the rim.

You cannot tell whether it is inspecting the cup or learning something from where your mouth has touched it.

***

There are others.

Once you have seen the first, their resting places become distinguishable from the things around them. A bundle against a roof support has heels. What appears to be a thickened portion of a tree trunk has a cheek turned toward the wood. Beneath a bed of creeping plants, two eyes are open in the interval between leaves.

They do not all resemble one another.

One has a long torso divided into overlapping clay plates, with each plate free to rise a little when it breathes. Another is dark, soft-bodied, and almost entirely woven, except for its teeth. An inhabitant beside the lake carries its face on a short stalk above its shoulders, leaving room below for an arrangement of pale folded membranes. When a breeze reaches it, the membranes open. For a moment you see all the way through.

Some have hands suited to broad work: fingers thick enough to press a channel into clay, palms that could leave the marks you saw in the ground. Others have more fingers than you can readily count. These sit near the roots of plants and manipulate threads so fine that their hands appear to be moving without holding anything.

None is identical to any other. Similar bindings occur in different places, and the pale clay of the first creature’s face appears again as the shoulder plates of another. Looking at a group beside a pool, you begin to recognize recurring decisions: a generous width across the chest, strong soles, loose work where the body must bend.

Repairs are visible everywhere.

A knee has been strengthened with red cord. A broken edge along a jaw has been wrapped rather than replaced. One creature wears a small polished shell over its left eye, tied behind the head with a loop that seems too loose to stay in place.

It stays.

***

They are not hiding from you. But they seldom occupy the center of an open court, and when they cross a path they do so close to its wet edge.

They spend a great deal of time in contact with things.

An inhabitant rests its wrist against a tree’s hollow trunk. Another lies with one foot in a watercourse and its head on a dry panel. Two sit facing away from each other, their backs joined by a loose length of green cord.

Their bodies are warm. You discover this when the first inhabitant touches your forearm.

Its hand is warmer than the clay. The dampness at its finger joints is cool.

It presses gently, releases, and presses again in a slightly different place. Then it lays your hand against its own chest.

Behind the basketry there is no single heartbeat. Something contracts low on one side; something else answers nearer the throat. The interval is long enough that you almost remove your hand before the second movement comes.

The creature holds you there until you have felt both.

***

Their voices are easy to overlook.

One draws a strand through the edge of its palm. Another turns its head sharply enough to tighten a binding beneath the ear. A third runs two fingertips over the ridges of its breastplate.

You hear a dry scrape, a click, a low sound like water passing beneath a panel.

The others respond.

Sometimes nothing visibly moves except the leaves beside them. Sometimes a creature at the far side of a court raises its head before the nearer one has finished adjusting its cord.

You cannot separate conversation from the small sounds the basin has been making all along.

At the lake, two inhabitants sit with their feet below the crosshatched surface. One pulls a strand of water up between its toes, holds it stretched, then lets it go. The other opens its mouth. You hear a brief, soft rattle.

Both lean forward. Their shoulder bindings slacken. For several moments they keep making the same sound.

You think they may be laughing.

***

They work, though not quite in the manner the workbench led you to expect.

The inhabitant with the repaired thumb lifts a panel near the grove. It sets the panel against its knee and reaches into the layer below. Its arm goes in up to the shoulder.

When it withdraws, it is holding a dark strand scarcely longer than a hair. It examines the strand, wets it in its mouth, and threads it back through an opening you cannot see.

Then it replaces the panel.

The surface is exactly as it was.

Elsewhere, one adjusts the angle of a fruit by less than the width of a fingernail. One eases a binding around a trunk. One carries a fish in both hands from a pool to a channel. The fish bends quietly against its palms. Before releasing it, the creature presses along its side, checking the seam from mouth to tail.

There is no hurry in any of this, but there is attention. A task may occupy an inhabitant for as long as you remain nearby. Others pause to watch, or bring a bowl, or take over holding the thing being worked upon.

At the bench, the unfinished groove attracts no worker.

An inhabitant comes inside, places both hands on the frame, and looks down at it. After a while it sits on the floor. The roll of leaves expands beside its shoulder and settles again.

Neither touches the blade.

***

The smallest ones are not necessarily young.

Some fit inside the folded leaves at a pool’s margin. They have tiny, complete faces and hands capable of untying the knots in an insect’s leg. One rides beneath the reed-covered shell of the broad-backed animal, its fingers hooked loosely through the weave.

Others are large enough that a grove conceals them.

You notice one only when a whole arrangement of leaves rises and falls together. Under the green covering is a curved shoulder, and below that an arm laid along the ground. Roots pass through its elbow. Water enters the open weave of its side and emerges farther downhill.

A smaller inhabitant climbs onto it and sits near its head.

For a long time nothing happens. Then a hand broad enough to cover the resting platform lifts from beneath the foliage and settles against the smaller creature’s back.

***

They have possessions.

A narrow belt holds three lengths of cord, all the same color. A leaf cup has been shaped to fit a particular hand. Beside one sleeping place lies a small clay bird with no openings for eyes.

The inhabitant that sleeps there picks up the bird whenever it rises. It carries it for a short distance, sets it down, then returns for it before going farther.

You see no purpose in the object. Its wings are uneven. There is a crack along its underside that has not been bound shut.

When another inhabitant reaches toward it, the owner places a hand over the little bird and keeps it there.

The other withdraws.

***

You never see them eat the fruit. They drink, but only a little, moistening the interiors of their mouths. Sometimes an inhabitant takes a loose fiber between its lips and holds it there until it darkens.

They rest often.

A body lowers itself against a wall and gradually conforms to it. A face remains visible after the shoulders have flattened into the screen behind them. Hands tuck beneath thighs. Feet fold lengthwise.

Their eyes usually close last.

You begin to understand why the basin first seemed unoccupied. Many of its inhabitants spend long intervals fitted into the places made for them. Those who remain awake move among the resting bodies without disturbing them, checking a binding or touching the wet edge of a mouth.

One sleeps beside the half-open leaf. Its fingers lie beneath the exposed stalk, supporting it without drawing it farther out.

***

Before you leave, the first inhabitant brings you the sandals from the grove entrance.

They are not its size. They are close to yours.

It sets them on the dry center of the path, then waits beside them. You sit to try them on. The straps yield, warm and slightly damp, and tighten when you put your weight on your feet.

The inhabitant kneels. With its repaired thumb it shifts a binding away from your ankle.

It keeps one hand there while you take a step, then lets go.

You walk a little way along the court. When you look back, it is still kneeling. Its eyes follow your feet.

Near the bench, another creature has lifted the polished blade. It turns the blade over and lays it down in a different place. The groove remains unfinished.

The first inhabitant rises and goes to the platform where you found it. It lowers itself beneath the seat, one limb at a time. Soon only its face and the pale repair at its thumb remain distinct from the weave.

Its hand rests on the edge.

As you turn toward the ridge, the fingers lift once, making a faint sound against the wood. Farther along the path, from beneath a roof you had thought empty, another small click answers.
