It lives where the frozen layer ends.

That boundary is not level. It rises beneath a sheltered tuft, drops beside a trench, follows the underside of a board for a short distance, then descends into the peat again. The creature follows it too. Most of its body is spread through the basin in a shape determined less by its own anatomy than by the places where water can still move.

At the surface, you would take it for another part of the moss. There is a brown fold beneath a pipe, a pale fringe along the underside of an exposed root. Neither is quite vegetation, but both are small enough to overlook. Only where a trench has cut across it can you see anything resembling flesh: a gray, finely ridged material tucked between two dark layers, wet despite the cold.

It contracts if touched. Slowly, but not as slowly as a root would.

The exposed portion draws back into the wall, leaving a thin opening that closes as the surrounding fibers settle. A drop of water gathers there. By morning, the drop has frozen and there is nothing to distinguish the place from the rest of the cut.

It has no single outer surface. Some of it is enclosed in old stems; some lies naked against the peat. Its thicker parts are flattened sacs, no broader than a hand, joined by lengths of translucent tissue that thread through the ground. These lengths branch and reunite. They can be torn without killing it. They can also be separated for years, until a wetter season gives them a way to meet again.

Whether those separated pieces remain one creature is a question that does not arise for it.

Its oldest parts lie lower than the excavator has reached. There, the tissue is dark and tough, almost indistinguishable from the material around it. It has taken on the impressions of sedge stems, the small cavities left by roots, the compressed grain of vanished wood. The living portions nearer the surface are softer. Held to the light, one would be the color of weak tea, with minute darker bodies moving through it.

But no one holds it to the light. When pieces come up in the excavator bucket, they look like peat. They lie among the angular fragments, bristling with torn fibers, and freeze.

The portions left below withdraw from the new cut. Over the following weeks they establish a route around it.

It eats little. What it needs arrives dissolved in water, or becomes available where a root dies and softens. There are places in the basin where it has remained against the same buried stem for longer than the pumping shed has stood. Along the fresh trenches, its growth is quicker. The excavator exposes material, breaks things open, admits air. There is briefly more to take in, provided the cut does not dry too far.

The creature extends into this abundance and then abandons much of what it has extended.

Its life contains a great deal of abandonment. A fringe beneath the moss survives one winter and not the next. A strand crossing a bank becomes brittle when drainage lowers the water beyond its reach. An entire compartment may cease to be inhabited, leaving tissue that darkens, compresses, and eventually joins the surrounding peat.

Elsewhere, in a seam no wider than a finger, it continues.

The pumps are present to it everywhere.

Their vibration enters through the wet ground and passes along its body. Near the shed, its thicker sacs brace against roots and buried timber. Farther away, narrow strands tighten and loosen with the engines’ uneven beat. The movement is slight. It would take magnification, and patience, to see it.

It does not hear the machinery as a roar. It feels a continual series of pressures, overlapping and returning, with small differences that tell it which parts of its body are wet, which are drying, and which have become detached. A strand in open water responds differently from one packed tightly into frozen peat. A buried board carries the vibration along one edge. Loose fragments scarcely carry it at all.

Since the engines began running, the creature has grown more densely around things that conduct their movement: the supports beneath the discharge pipes, the abandoned platform, the lower posts of the conveyor frame.

This has brought it nearer to people, though neither has noticed the other.

At the service door, a soft gray pad lies beneath the bottom step. It occupies the sheltered space between metal and ground, where meltwater drips during inspections. When someone stands on the step, the pad compresses. A little fluid passes from it into a strand beside the foundation.

After the person leaves, it slowly fills again.

The inspection visits reach it as a succession of localized pressures: boots crossing gravel, a handrail shifting under a person’s weight, the shed door striking its frame. The light means almost nothing to it. The brief warmth matters more. On very cold days, several of its surface fringes unfold after the door has closed, taking advantage of warmth that will be gone within minutes.

These are its most active moments in winter. Even then, you could watch one without being certain that it had moved.

The hare crosses over it. The bird pulls food from trench walls a few centimeters from its exposed tissue. Moss grows through its upper branches. It neither pursues nor avoids them. Occasionally an animal’s weight breaks a strand. Occasionally a dropping, thawing in a sheltered hollow, sustains a small new patch for a season.

If the pumps stop, the first change is in its whole body at once.

The repeated pressures cease. Tissue held taut around the pipe supports relaxes. Minute openings close along the trench walls. For a while, nothing replaces the engine rhythm except scattered disturbances: wind loading the banks, a boot on the ramp, water releasing from beneath ice.

Then the water begins to rise.

The creature responds unevenly. Some of its stranded parts soften and become active again. Others, accustomed to the aerated margins, are submerged and die. Through the lower seams, new connections begin to form. They are pale, soft, easily damaged. They require days without frost to cross even a small gap.

When the engines restart, the old pressures return through a body that is no longer arranged quite as it was.

Tonight they are running steadily. Beneath the service step, the gray pad holds its little reserve of water. A fresh fringe has reached the underside of a moss tuft beside the outlet. Farther north, beyond the last recognizable machine tracks, a narrow strand is advancing through a wet layer between two beds of sedge.

It has reached the bend in a buried root. It rests against it, thickening there, while water passes slowly along the seam toward the pumps.
