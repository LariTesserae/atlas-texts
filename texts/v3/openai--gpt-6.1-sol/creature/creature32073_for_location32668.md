The people who live there are not all quiet people. Some were loud before they came down, and some become loud again as soon as they reach the street. A woman who speaks almost without moving her lips in the neighborhood sells work gloves at a surface market, where her voice carries half a block. Her grandson once went up to find her and stood behind her stall for several minutes, unsure whether to interrupt.

Belowground, their voices have acquired distances. There is a voice for someone beside you, one for someone across the table, and one for calling up a stairwell. Children learn the difference imperfectly. For a few years they whisper secrets loud enough for three households to hear.

Most of the first residents came because the rooms were cheap. Some had worked on the excavation; others repaired the machinery underneath it. A few moved down intending to stay only until a debt was paid. Their children are now adults, and the temporary shelves they installed have mineral lines running through them.

The older residents remember when one person could stand in the court and see another on the uppermost landing. They disagree about what happened in the intervening years. One says the neighborhood became crowded. Another says it became sheltered. Both have raised families in the same two rooms.

There are people who have never liked it here. They keep their shoes near the door and their belongings in containers that can be carried. They complain about the heat while performing small, exact acts that preserve the place: clearing a funnel with a needle, wiping fungus from a seed drawer, turning a lamp so that one neglected cutting receives its share. If asked why they have not left, they give practical answers. Rent. Work. A mother who cannot manage the stairs.

Sometimes those are the whole answers.

***

The old woman beside the basin is called Sera. She was once responsible for the accounts at a laundry aboveground. She still writes numbers with unusually clear separation between the digits.

She does not consider trimming the roots a ceremonial duty. She took it over because the previous person stopped doing it reliably. Her stool has one shortened leg to accommodate the slope of the tiles. She keeps two pairs of scissors: one for living roots and one for the tough, discolored lengths that have already died. Nobody else can tell which pair is which without opening them and looking at the blades.

The roots she cuts are not all alike. Some part easily and show a moist, pale center. Some have a gritty strand that damages the edge. Occasionally she encounters one that resists for several afternoons, although it is no thicker than the others. She ties a scrap of thread around it and trims what grows nearby.

A boy once asked whether those were special roots.

“They are difficult roots,” she said.

She saves the cuttings in a shallow tray. A few can be replanted. The rest go into the soil-making cupboards, except for the gritty ones, which she leaves to dry in a chipped saucer. A repairer has found that they can be rubbed down and used to shim loose tiles. Sera is pleased by this, although she dislikes the way he comes to ask whether she has “anything ready,” as though she were manufacturing them.

Her daughter wants her to move upstairs. Sera has agreed to consider it when the stairs become a problem. The daughter points out that the stairs are already a problem.

“Yes,” Sera says, “but so is moving.”

***

Children grow up with an unusually precise knowledge of what can bear their weight.

They know which pipes are supported and which only appear to be; which root can be leaned against; which balcony belongs to a household that permits climbing. They can cross certain parts of the hollow without putting a foot on the lane. This is forbidden, and therefore done with considerable care.

They have games involving the shafts of sunlight. On the rare days when a rectangle reaches a clear stretch of floor, the youngest stand inside it while the others count. The light moves too slowly to make this exciting to an outsider. To the children, who know the marks between the tiles, it is enough.

Their drawings of houses often include the rooms next door. Asked to draw a tree, they draw a box, a stem, and several lines leading away beyond the edge of the paper.

There are quarrels over space. One child’s sleeping niche may also be the place where another household keeps a ladder. A girl who wanted privacy put a curtain across hers and found that the ladder had been left outside it, blocking the lane. She moved the ladder. Someone moved it back. Eventually a shallow recess was chipped into the pillar opposite, and everyone had to put up with three mornings of work above the permitted noise level.

The girl stopped using the curtain after a month. The recess remains.

***

There is a man on the fourth terrace who pollinates the fruiting vines before going to his shift in a municipal kitchen. He uses a brush kept in a spectacles case. His spectacles are stored elsewhere.

He knows the flowers by how far they have opened, by the slight tackiness of their centers, and by changes in color that are difficult to see beneath the lamps. When he is ill, three neighbors divide the work. They do it conscientiously, but fewer fruits set.

He does not keep this knowledge secret. He has tried to teach it. The difficulty is that his explanations concern things his hands notice before he does.

One year a fruit grew in the narrow space behind a hot-water riser. By the time it was discovered, it could not be removed whole. Several people proposed loosening the pipe. The fruit was cut into pieces instead.

For weeks afterward, the man looked at the empty place behind the riser. He had been proud of that fruit. Its flavor was disappointing, but this did not seem relevant.

The plants here are descended from things brought down in buckets, wrapped in newspaper, or tucked into pockets as stolen cuttings. Their names have become unreliable. A plant may be called celery because its great-grandparent was celery, although its leaves are leathery and its swollen underground stem is the part people eat. Another is called Aunt’s Bean in households that no longer remember whose aunt it was.

Cuttings are given to newly occupied rooms. Usually the gift comes with instructions about what the plant must not touch.

***

Other living things persist in the margins.

The translucent snails emerge after the basin has been cleaned, as though cleaning had produced them. Their shells are so thin that an amber lamp makes each one look empty. They graze where the tile glaze has crazed, leaving trails visible only when viewed from the side.

People move them out of reach of feet. No one has established whether this is necessary. Some residents suspect there are always only two or three snails, repeatedly encountered. Sera says she has seen five at once. She has written the date down.

There are molds inside walls, yeasts in dough, fine white growths in the bins where soil is made. Residents distinguish these by smell more confidently than by appearance. A cupboard that smells sweet is opened. One that smells sour is left to work. One that develops a particular dry, dusty odor is emptied immediately, regardless of how useful its contents might have become.

That rule began with an illness nobody likes discussing. The household involved still lives there.

***

What else lives in the hollow has not acquired a settled name.

It is tempting to speak of a single thing passing through the stone and the roots. The residents themselves sometimes do. But their observations are awkward for that explanation.

One mineral stripe advances through plaster while another remains unchanged for nine years. Two cuttings taken from the same vine develop differently on opposite sides of a landing. A root warms enough to make a child withdraw her hand, then stays cool for the rest of the month. The shoots behind the swollen door continue growing without water, but a plant set beside them in a dish withers.

Whatever is happening does not make all the plants healthier. It ruins some. Leaves stiffen before they can unfold. A tuber develops a hard white center that cannot be cooked soft. There are roots that stop taking up water once the pale bands reach them.

The affected matter is useful sometimes, troublesome at others. It has no consistent regard for the arrangements people have made.

Residents keep records because records are more helpful than arguments. Measurements share wall space with birthdays and reminders to buy oil. A pencil line showing the progress of a seam may end at a child’s height mark. Years later, the child is gone and both lines remain.

Some believe the stone is producing something alive. Others believe the plants are learning to use something in the stone. A retired boiler fitter says there is nothing to explain until somebody obtains proper samples. He has obtained several samples. They sit in labeled jars on his windowsill, apparently doing nothing.

He continues to watch them.

***

The person who opens the unfinished room changes from month to month. Two people go, usually, although only one enters. The other holds the door and writes down the measurements.

Nobody is supposed to go alone. There is no lock, however, and the rule depends on being observed.

They count the green shoots twice. They measure from the point where each emerges from the ceiling, not from the ends of the mineral lines leading into it. This distinction was agreed upon after a long disagreement. The shoots bend a little under their own weight. Their tips are soft. Farther up, near the stone, they are hard enough that touching them feels like touching the glazed rim of a cup.

Once, a tip was found broken on the floor.

It was brought out, placed in soil, placed in water, and finally left in an empty jar. Nothing came of it. After some weeks it crumbled when handled, leaving pale grit and a green stain on the thumb.

They still have the jar.

Outside that room, households continue with matters that cannot wait upon an explanation. Someone must replace a washer. Someone has been served too small a portion. Someone is old enough to leave and wants help carrying a chest upstairs.

When a room becomes vacant, its neighbors look after the plants until a new tenant arrives. They remove what will damage the plaster, loosen what has grown around the bed frame, and leave the useful growth intact. Sometimes they find, behind a cupboard or beneath the floorboards, a root none of them knew was there.

They inspect it. They test its warmth with the backs of their fingers. Then they decide whether it must be cut, supported, or simply allowed room.

A new resident usually asks what it belongs to.

If anyone knows, they tell them.
