At first it seems to be the part of the floor on which the least dust has collected.

This is not a clean patch. There is powder over it, and two small flakes of limestone, and a dry curl of leaf that must have come in years ago. But the powder lies differently there. It has settled into very fine, nearly parallel lines, too close together to follow individually in the weak light. A hand passed across them would feel something like the grain of old wood.

The patch extends beside the broad slab, then under it. Farther back, it widens around the base of the pillar. There is no point at which its outline becomes certain. What appears to be its edge in one place is only a fold; elsewhere, a portion continues beneath the mineral earth.

It is larger than a person lying down, but much lower. Most of its thickness occupies a shallow bed in the floor. Its exposed surface rises no higher than the rounded stones around it.

That surface is neither skin nor shell in any familiar sense. It has the dull finish of limestone rubbed for a long time by a thumb. Here and there it is darker, almost brown, and faintly pliant. In the palest places it has hardened enough to hold small cracks. Dust has entered those cracks, just as it has entered the ones in the walls.

If touched, it does not immediately respond.

Its warmth is the warmth of everything here. Only after keeping a palm against it for some time might one notice that the heat is a little more evenly distributed, less dependent on the depth of the stone. Beneath the surface there is a yielding so gradual that it could be mistaken for fatigue in the hand.

It has no exposed eyes. Along one side, where its body lies against a raised band in the limestone, are several shallow, closed depressions. They resemble the impressions left by smooth pebbles pressed into soft earth. These are sensitive to pressure, though not all in the same way. One registers a footfall. Another receives the expansion of the wall as the plain above grows hot. Another is pressed against a root.

The root is alive.

It reaches farther into the hollow than the plant can grow, threading through a narrow pocket beneath the floor. The inhabitant has grown around part of it without enclosing it completely. There remains enough space for the root to thicken, and a little dark earth held between the two surfaces.

Sometimes, after rain on the plain, something passes through this contact. The root becomes firmer. The body beside it acquires a slight tension. Nothing wets the floor.

It is difficult to say where its mouth is.

There are folds underneath it that can admit loose matter: a grain loosened from the wall, a fragment of leaf, the thin outer tissue shed by the root. These folds do not open into a single chamber. They lead into narrow spaces distributed through the body, where what has been taken in remains for a long time. A leaf may retain its curled shape through several seasons before its edges soften and it becomes part of the darker material beneath the pale surface.

It needs very little. Much of what it takes in seems to be used to maintain the surfaces touching the ground.

Its movement is mostly a change in how it rests.

One portion slowly bears more weight; another relaxes. A fold beneath the slab lengthens by less than the width of a grain. Over time, earth enters a space it has relinquished, while its underside occupies another. Nothing is dragged across the powder. The flakes on its back remain where they are.

Perhaps it once fitted entirely beneath the slab. Perhaps the pillar has always limited its growth. There is no scar or abandoned casing by which to judge its age.

It does notice visitors.

The first step inside reaches it through the floor. As someone approaches, certain parts become slightly firmer. The closed depressions along the wall tighten, and the small pliant places in its surface lose some of their softness.

It does not turn toward the person. There is no portion of it that could turn without disturbing the rest.

If a visitor sits on the slab, the inhabitant receives their weight through several points of contact. For as long as they remain, it makes minute adjustments beneath them. Someone sitting very still may eventually feel more securely seated, though the slab itself has not shifted.

When they leave, that adjustment persists.

For a while there is a firmer place beneath the stone, fitted to a weight no longer present. Then, gradually, the body resumes its ordinary distribution. The warmth stays even. Dust settles into the fine lines of its surface.

Near the entrance, the wiry plants continue to hold their leaves close. Farther in, beside the slab, a small pale area has become fractionally broader. It is too little a change to see on returning the next day.

The inhabitant has made room for itself.
