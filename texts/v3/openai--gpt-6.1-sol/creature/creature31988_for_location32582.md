One of the slabs beside the eastern shelters is not always in the same place.

Its movements are small enough to be mistaken for movements of the ground. A corner comes to rest against the doorway. Later there is a narrow interval between the corner and the wall. Dust accumulates along one edge and not the other. On a plain where whole ridges change their relation to one another, such differences would scarcely deserve notice.

But this slab moves against the tilt.

It does so when the horizon is darkest, drawing itself uphill a little at a time. Its upper surface remains level. Something underneath takes the weight, releases it, and takes it again. Once it reaches the shelter, it pauses with its leading edge inside, as though testing the darkness.

There are others. Most are not near the buildings.

They rest under overhangs, in the lee of upright fins, or on the narrow ledges between a warm seam and a sheer drop. From above they look like pieces of the country: long, low forms with broken outlines and surfaces ranging from dull black to the yellow-grey of old deposits. A few carry a bright fracture across their backs. Others are so thoroughly powdered that only their movement distinguishes them from stone.

Even then, it is easy to miss the distinction. They move with much the same patience as the land.

An adult is somewhat longer than a person lying down, though it can become considerably narrower. What appears to be a solid upper slab consists of overlapping plates. The overlaps are deep and irregular. Dust settles into them, mineral vapor leaves pale rims, and small chips lodge where the plates meet. None of this is groomed away. The accumulated material gives each individual a surface almost indistinguishable from the place where it has recently lived.

Below the plates, the body is softer.

It can sometimes be seen when one crosses a cleft. Several folded supports extend beneath it, pale at the joints and dark at their ends. There is no single instant when it leaps. The leading supports reach across and adhere to the farther lip. The rear ones remain fixed while the middle of the body lengthens. For a while it is suspended over the opening, its plates drawn apart enough to show a thin purple lining.

Then the hind portion follows.

The lining closes before the body settles. Very little of the interior is ever exposed to the air.

They have no obvious head. Either end can lead, although an individual generally favors one. Along the lower margins are small recesses sheltered by the plates. From these emerge flexible, blunt-tipped organs, rarely longer than a finger. They touch the ground ahead, withdraw, and emerge elsewhere.

They test more than temperature. On an apparently uniform surface, one will place a tip against a warm patch, lift it, and choose another that feels no different to a human hand. It may refuse a newly skinned fissure without approaching its visible edge. It may cross hotter ground a few yards away.

The supports themselves carry information upward. While resting, the animal keeps several in contact with the stone, widely separated beneath its body. A disturbance too deep to make a sound will pass through these contacts. The plates tighten. One end rises a little.

Often this happens well before the nearby stones click.

They do not always escape. Beneath the northern rim, one lies across a break that has pulled its supports from their sockets. Its back is intact. The body below has dried into folds as thin as the membranes between crystal faces. Dust enters the gaps and stays there.

The living sometimes pass close to it. They do not linger.

Their feeding is difficult to recognize because they do not bend over food or bring anything to a mouth. They settle beside a fracture and spread their underside against the rock. The lower margins become closely fitted to the surface, closing around irregularities that would admit air beneath a rigid slab.

For hours, nothing happens.

Then a faint change of color appears at the edge: a narrow violet band, darker than the body above it. It advances into the warmest part of the seam. When the animal lifts, a little of this material remains behind, laid flat against the stone.

Some of the scattered purple patches may have begun this way. Others occur on ledges no adult seems able to reach. Whether the animals carry the patches, cultivate them, or are themselves a later condition of the same life is not readily settled.

What is clear is that an adult returning to a fruitful seam usually rests beside the patch rather than on it. Its underside touches the patch’s edge. Over several hours, the boundary between the two becomes difficult to see.

At a failing vent, the animal stays longer. Its plates gradually lose their close fit. Their ends stand out from the body, and the supports make repeated, incomplete efforts to rise. Eventually it withdraws, leaving the patch exposed.

There is no pursuit of food through the air, no browsing over the plain. Whatever sustains them comes from the warm stone in quantities too small to be visible.

Their moisture is kept inside. The thin air receives almost none of it. A fresh wound beads with a dark, adhesive fluid that soon forms a dull skin; even that small loss makes an animal slow. The soft lining beneath the plates is glossy, but only while uncovered. Exposure rapidly turns it grey.

Heat presents a different danger. They need it and can take too much of it without knowing soon enough. An animal caught above a new source of rising warmth spreads its supports and lifts clear of the ground. If the heat continues, the plates begin to separate. A bitter smell comes from the openings.

This is one reason for the shelters.

The slabs forming their roofs are not necessarily the heaviest available. They are pieces that have cooled all the way through. Some were moved from one ledge to another over many intervals of rest. An animal fits its body under an edge, braces, and advances until the stone has shifted by a fraction of its width. Several may work on the same piece, without any visible attempt to move in unison. Their efforts overlap enough to accomplish it.

They wedge the uprights with broken stone held between their lower folds. There is no reserve of tools because the holding parts belong to their bodies.

A shelter need not last very long. It needs to remain dark and tolerably cool through one particular change.

Inside, an animal can loosen its outer plates.

The process starts along the sides. A seam that usually admits only a sensing tip opens wide enough to reveal the purple lining. The supports retract. The body lowers into the floor depression and becomes broader, occupying more of the available darkness.

For some time it is almost defenseless. The old plates are still present, but no longer fastened securely. They shift with the slightest movement. New surfaces form beneath them, dark and flexible, taking impressions from whatever they touch.

An animal that tried to do this on exposed hot ground would harden badly. Its joints might close. Its underside might attach to the surface and be unable to lift.

Within the shelter, it can turn a little, settle again, and allow the new plates to acquire their shape slowly.

The discarded covering is not a recognizable empty animal. It separates into broad fragments. Some become wedges. Some are pushed out and left among the natural chips. Others remain under the roof, where later occupants press them into the floor. They soon look like the stones from which the shelter was made.

Occasionally a second animal waits at the entrance.

It stands partly over the one within, not quite touching it. Its supports extend past the doorway and remain in contact with the ground outside. When a deep disturbance passes through the ledge, the waiting animal contracts. Its plates strike the roof with a single dry knock.

The soft animal stirs beneath it.

If there is time, both leave.

No fixed pair remains together throughout the basin. One that waits at a shelter may depart before the other emerges. They meet again at a vent without any obvious recognition, settle on opposite sides, and share its warmth. Yet they are careful around an exposed body. A fully armored animal crossing a crowded interior places its supports between the other’s folds, never on them.

The young are much harder to find.

They lie flat, with only a few thin plates, no larger than a spread hand. At first they cannot lift their whole bodies from the stone. They advance by fastening one margin and drawing the rest after it. The distance gained may be less than the width of a grain.

A cooling seam strands them. Some pale and curl. Some detach.

Most of the scales carried over the precipices never visibly move again. But a few that lodge in warm cracks flatten out after several days. Their edges become dark. A tiny support forms underneath, finds purchase, and pulls.

There may be no clean division between dispersal and injury here. A body reduced to a remnant can sometimes resume its growth. What it retains of its former shape is uncertain.

The oldest adults are recognizable less by size than by how they move. They test farther ahead. They rest with their supports on separate blocks rather than on a single apparently sound surface. At a shelter, they seldom place their whole weight beneath the roof until they have touched the back wall.

Even these precautions do not make the country safe.

The new northern shelf is empty when the copper light first reaches it. Later, something at its lower edge begins to rise.

An adult comes over the lip, dragging a long pale scratch of dust across its back. It stops where the surface is still too young to have gathered any deposit. Several sensing tips emerge. One touches the rock and immediately withdraws.

The animal waits.

Underneath it, the shelf gives a slow, almost inaudible creak. The leading supports release their hold. The body turns, awkwardly on the steep incline, and begins the long crossing toward a darker section near the wall.

By the next return of the horizon light, it has settled there. Its plates fit closely together. Beneath one edge, a small purple line lies against the warm stone.
