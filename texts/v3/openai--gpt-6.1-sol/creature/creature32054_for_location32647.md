The hollows in the central chamber are not always empty.

Sometimes there is a ridge at the back of one that was not there the day before. It might be a root raised by pressure beneath the floor, or a bundle of wet leaves pushed together by drainage. For several hours it does nothing. Then one end unfolds, exposing a narrow strip of pale tissue, and closes again before the cold can enter.

The occupants are easiest to see during a thaw. They come out while the ice is loosening but before the channels have filled. At first there is only a difference in the arrangement of things: a low shape crossing a threshold, a dark patch against a wall that moves against the direction of the water. They do not shake droplets from themselves. Their surfaces retain them.

An adult, drawn up to its full height, would reach a person’s chest. They seldom stand that way. In the lanes they go bent almost double, with their heads below the eaves and their long forelimbs close to the floor. Their hind limbs are shorter and very strong. At rest, these fold into depressions along the sides of the body, leaving an outline sufficiently compact to fit the dry hollows.

Their backs carry overlapping plates. The plates are flexible when wet, stiff in frost, and darkest along the spine, where grit accumulates in the seams. Small plants grow there. On an old individual, moss may cover most of the upper surface, interrupted by a few blue-black leaves that belong to the body itself. It is difficult to distinguish these from the leaves of the dwelling until the occupant moves.

Underneath, they are much paler. The joints show cream-colored membranes, finely folded. A hand opening against a wall looks briefly like new growth spreading under an eave.

The hands have several narrow divisions, each capable of curling independently. There are no nails. Their tips soften when held against living bark, flatten, and adhere. An occupant can remain attached to a wall by one hand while using the other to draw a trailing shoot away from a channel. When it releases its grip, a wet impression remains on the bark. By morning, this has usually disappeared.

They have faces, although the faces do not offer much that a person would know how to read. Two eyes sit deep beneath a continuous brow, their surfaces the dull gray of water under ice. Below them is a seam that opens only in shelter. The seam is sensitive to moving air. When cloud comes down through the upper lanes, the occupants turn their heads toward it with their mouths closed.

They hear well through contact. One resting in a hollow will raise its head when a foot is set down several rooms away, even if the foot makes no audible sound.

Much of their conversation passes through the floor.

Two may sit on opposite sides of the central chamber, each with a hand resting among the short leaves. Nothing appears to happen between them. Then one lifts its head; the other draws in a hind limb; after a pause, both turn toward the same entrance. The fine roots beneath their palms have carried a sequence of pressures too slight to hear.

They also make sounds. Close beside one, it is possible to distinguish a low, moist clicking, produced somewhere behind the closed mouth. A longer call has the quality of a twig bending without breaking. These sounds do not travel far. In the crowded rooms, they do not need to.

There are fewer occupants than there are roofs, but more than a count of the moving bodies would suggest.

Some remain almost permanently in their hollows. Their backs have joined the walls, and thick cords run from their flanks into the floor. They are not helpless. They can still turn their faces toward an arrival, close a hand around an offered wrist, or shift enough to open a little space beside themselves. The lanes nearest them are usually the best maintained. Water divides neatly around their chambers. The roofs above them seldom sag.

The mobile ones return to these residents at intervals. They crouch close and lay their hands against exposed patches at the shoulders or beneath the ribs. Sometimes the contact lasts only a moment. Sometimes it continues through an afternoon, while water gathers at the eaves and the green light changes.

During these visits, the stationary body may become fuller. Or the visitor may leave with its pale joints swollen, moving more slowly than before.

They take nourishment from the settlement through such contacts, and through resting. In each sleeping hollow there is a place where the bark remains thin and damp. An occupant presses its underside against this surface and becomes still. Fine filaments emerge along the contact and enter the wall. By the time it rises, they have withdrawn again.

It also eats things that drift down the channels. A drowned bird, brought into one of the settling basins, will draw several of them. They lift it carefully, holding it between their bodies to keep it from the current, and carry it into a lower room. For days afterward, the plants around that room put out unusually pale growth.

The larvae are usually left alone. An occupant tending a basin will move a submerged root with great care if larvae are clinging beneath it. They feed on what settles there, and the water leaving their basins runs clear.

Nothing is cut because a wound here is expensive.

A torn stem loses fluid into cold air. Exposed tissue freezes. The injury can pass down through a bundle of roots and leave an entire chamber unable to hold its leaves upright. The occupants work instead by pressure, patience, and repeated contact.

They ease a young stem into a new direction a little at a time. They warm it in the fold beneath an arm. They hold two branches together until the surfaces become soft and join. A doorway may require years of attention: a thumb-width gained during each thaw, then held through the long weeks when all growth stops.

Their work sometimes looks like idleness. One sits beside a threshold all morning, its hand curved over a root. Another leans its weight into a wall for so long that moss begins to rise between its toes. Yet afterward the threshold stands slightly higher, or a bulge in the wall has retreated enough to let a broad-backed resident pass.

They know the dimensions of one another’s bodies. A gap sufficient for the young may still need widening. A bridge that carries two light occupants may be approached cautiously by an elder whose back has accumulated stone.

Stone is not always removed. A fragment caught among the plates can become secure, and eventually useful. The oldest mobile resident carries a flat piece of gray rock above one shoulder. In rain, water runs off it into a groove along the arm. When this resident braces beneath a failing roof, the stone spreads the load.

There are young ones, though not often.

For a long time a young one resembles an extra fold in the wall of an occupied hollow. Its limbs are tucked close, its back soft and pale. It is attached along one side to a larger body. The attachment broadens during cold weather and narrows during a thaw.

Others come to touch it. Each rests a hand briefly on its underside, and each leaves a little fluid there. The young one’s plates darken gradually. Its eyes open before it can move.

Its first journeys are not toward the entrances. It crosses from its parent to the neighboring occupant, gripping one body until it has found purchase on the next. For several weeks, the adults arrange themselves across the central chamber in a loose, low chain. The young travel over them while the water runs underneath.

Only later do they learn the floor.

A pale track follows an unsteady crossing. An adult waits at the far end, touching the leaves but not reaching out unless the little body begins to turn toward a channel.

They are watchful near the broken lower edge.

Before the shelf fell, several occupied rooms lay beyond the present margin. The remaining passages still bend toward them. One doorway opens directly into mist. Its bark lips continue to thicken around an entrance that leads nowhere.

A resident goes there most mornings.

It is small, with an uneven back and one forelimb that cannot quite extend. It presses its good hand to the wall beside the opening and waits. Water beads on its brow. After a while, another comes and rests against its side. Neither looks down.

The bodies lost with the shelf have not all ceased to answer. Somewhere in the fallen tangle below, roots remain connected. During heavy rain, a faint pressure sometimes reaches the upper rooms. The occupants stop what they are doing and lower their hands to the floor.

They have tried to extend roots toward it. The new growth is slender, and the distance is great.

No one guards the settlement against strangers. There is too little food for pursuit, and too little room for a defensive line. An unfamiliar weight brings several faces to the nearest openings. The occupants watch where it places its feet. If it crushes a channel, they attend to the channel. If it slips at the lower edge, hands emerge from the wall and close around whatever they can hold.

Their grip is cold, but not rigid.

Afterward they may remain near the place where the stranger stood, touching the disturbed floor. Something of its warmth has entered the roots. For a few hours, young leaves there will stand a little higher than the others.

At night, most withdraw into the hollows. Those still working gather beneath the lowest roofs, where the cloud does not quite reach the floor. They rest against one another with their hands open. A head settles into the space between a shoulder and the wall. Water passes a finger’s breadth below a folded foot.

Near the broken margin, one stays awake.

It has threaded both arms through a loose mass of new roots. When the wind pulls them outward, it draws them back, preventing their soft tips from striking the stone. When the air becomes still, it lets them extend.

By morning there is a little more weight beyond the last secure threshold. Several roots have found one another in the dark. The occupant tests their join with a slow movement of its hands, then settles closer to the edge and holds them while they grow.
