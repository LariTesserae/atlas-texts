The people are easiest to notice when they move their hands.

Most of their clothing is dark, and a person standing among the leaf beds can resemble a bundle of roofing material put aside for repair. Their coats are made in overlapping pieces, with the joins sheltered under folds. A little moisture can stiffen an exposed seam until it cuts the skin beneath it. Their cuffs close tightly. Their sleeves are long enough to cover the fingers when there is nothing to carry.

At work, however, the hands emerge: reddened knuckles, pale palms, nails worn short against stone. People keep their tools inside their clothing until they need them. Before passing a chisel to someone else, they warm its handle between both hands.

They are human. They get headaches, lose teeth, dislike particular neighbors, and sometimes stand too quickly after a long task and have to hold the wall. A newcomer might take their deliberate movements for solemnity. After a winter, the newcomer begins moving much the same way.

Children are less cautious. They run on paving that their elders have tested and quarrel over the best places to slide a flat stone. They play a game in which one child closes their eyes while the others change the apparent slope of a little model street. A spoonful of water decides who has guessed correctly.

Their first lessons concern surfaces.

They learn which green cushions will bear a knee and which conceal cold water. They learn to lift a wet vessel with cloth wrapped around the fingers, to test a stair after frost, and to distinguish a loose stone from one that is moving with the ground beneath it. They are taught the route from their bed to the kitchen in darkness.

Adults repeat these lessons with an insistence children find tiresome. Occasionally an old person will interrupt a scolding to admit that they once made exactly the same mistake.

There are no animals in the houses. Nothing noses among the fuel stores or rustles in the roof. A dropped crust remains where it fell until someone picks it up or a film begins to spread over it.

Visitors from below sometimes find this harder to bear than the cold. They wake because a room is too quiet. They leave food outside and watch it. Plateau children gather around the visitor who can imitate a bird, asking for the sound again and again, then arguing about whether anything could really call so loudly while flying.

In pictures, animals tend to appear beside a person for scale. A horse is understood partly as an extraordinary way of carrying things. A moth is harder to explain.

The people themselves carry almost everything. Loads are suspended low, close to the hips, in broad frames that can be lowered without taking off the shoulder straps. Along the older avenues there are resting stones polished by centuries of baskets. Some stand beside nothing inhabited now, but travelers still clear them of growth.

A person leaving home takes water, food, repair material, a cup, and loose pebbles. The pebbles are kept in an outer pouch. It would be unpleasant to search through a whole pack after discovering that distance was failing.

Travelers also carry a strip of cloth bearing household marks. These say where someone may reasonably be expected, who should be told if they arrive late, and whether there are dependents waiting along the route. The strips are renewed whenever the household changes. Old ones are kept in a dry box.

A journey of several days can have consequences beyond fatigue. Someone may return to find a child walking who had been carried when they left. More rarely, they find the child old enough to meet them at the shelter.

For this reason, households are large and loosely bounded. Several people know how much water a garden needs, where a sick person’s cups are kept, and which stories a child asks for before sleep. Affection can be private; responsibility is usually witnessed.

When someone fails to return, their work is taken up almost immediately. Their place at the table is handled with more care. In some districts it stays empty through a full water calendar. Elsewhere, anyone may sit there, provided they rise without protest if its owner comes home.

There are people who have returned after their houses were dismantled. They live with descendants or with strangers who inherited an obligation to receive them. Some speak readily about the crossing. Others remember only an ordinary walk, a drink from the covered jar, and the discovery that the marks on the shelter board had become unfamiliar.

The inhabitants have disagreements about all of this. One city gives a returned traveler the property they left, if it can still be identified. Another holds that absence does not entitle anyone to undo years of other people’s labor. Along one avenue, these disputes have been conducted in the same meeting court for generations.

Food is plentiful in certain forms and scarce in others.

The gardens produce fleshy folds that can be sliced and dried, knotted underground stems, and glossy leaves whose bitterness lessens after repeated washing. Some translucent stalks are eaten fresh, snapping softly between the teeth. Others must be heated until their inner threads loosen. The best cooks can make a thick broth from three plants that, eaten separately, leave the mouth numb.

Seeds are gathered on shallow trays set just above the ground. During the brief release of flowering powder, gatherers lie beside the beds with cloth over their mouths, drawing the trays slowly toward them. A good harvest may be over before someone in the next courtyard knows it has begun.

Meal from these seeds is pressed into thin cakes. Fresh ones bend. Old ones are broken into soup.

Households keep cultures in ceramic bowls: films for clothing, films for binding cuts, films that will eat the bitterness from certain roots. The bowls must be watched. A useful skin left too long can become opaque and fasten itself to the vessel. People who tend them know the small differences in smell and surface sheen that precede this change.

A skilled film-grower can make a glove with almost no stitching. The fingers begin as five shallow channels in a shaped basin. Once peeled, the glove is turned, washed, and softened with warmth. Such gloves are expensive. Most people wear mitts assembled from flatter pieces and mend them until scarcely any of the original material remains.

The plants enter every part of life, but the people are careful about claiming to understand them.

They know that one bed can depend on another across miles of bare stone. They have seen a healthy garden fail after excavation in a distant court. In disputes over building, root maps are brought out alongside water records. The maps are provisional, with many lines ending in open circles.

Gardeners spend long periods lying on their sides.

From that height they can see a leaf lifting before the rest of the bed moves, a bead of moisture gathering where no rain has fallen, or the exposed tip of a runner turning toward a seam. They place little slivers of fired clay near these points and return later to compare positions.

Some gardeners speak to the beds. Others dislike the practice. Both may produce good crops.

There are households that will not harvest the first growth along a newly opened seam. They leave it until it has spread onto older ground. This is called patience in one district and waste in the next.

The large rooted groves shelter a different sort of worker. Woodcutters go in pairs, carrying wedges, bindings, and lamps. Dead timber can still be held under strain by living arches. When it is released, something elsewhere in the grove may settle.

Before taking a section, the cutters examine the ground on both sides. Afterwards they examine it again.

Occasionally there is a narrow passage where there was solid earth that morning. The air coming out may be dry and warm. No cutter is expected to investigate it as part of the day’s work. A piece of cloth is tied nearby, the load is carried out, and the matter is reported.

Nevertheless, some people go back.

The plateau has its curious, its impatient, its people who are tired of being told that a thing has always been dangerous. They disappear into openings, attempt the smooth country with elaborate measuring devices, or spend years planning a road toward the pale forms. Their neighbors may think them foolish and still help prepare their equipment.

A shelter will sometimes contain the marks of one of these expeditions. Beneath the count of those entering is a small sketch of what they hoped to reach. Later travelers add their own observations around it. Eventually the board is full and must be replaced. The old one goes into a storehouse, whether or not anyone has come back.

Record-keeping is an ordinary occupation here. So is forgetting.

The oldest courts contain more rods than anyone can readily interpret. Some measure movement against a wall that has itself moved. Others preserve the height of a vanished roof or the direction in which smoke once traveled. Apprentices spend their first season sorting useful comparisons from inherited ones.

They are paid for this work, and complain about it.

There is music in the cities, mostly indoors. Instruments are small enough to warm before playing: reed pipes, clay chambers, strings stretched across shallow wooden boxes. People sing while making fuel bricks or cleaning roots. In a room where a wall carries whispers too well, they hang thick mats and test them with an ordinary conversation before beginning anything private.

Celebrations fill houses until condensation runs down the shutters. Guests arrive with food, dry clothing, and news from streets that may be days apart. Someone always wants the door opened. Someone else insists that opening it will spoil the warmth.

Outside, the gardens continue their slower exchanges.

A runner crosses beneath a foundation. A clothing culture thickens in its bowl. Far from the occupied streets, a silver fragment opens to the pale day, closes, and advances a little over stone. Most of this life has never been named. The named plants are simply the ones people have had reason to distinguish.

Near evening, a road worker kneels beside a damaged joint and sorts replacement stones by touch. A younger companion holds the lamp low enough to show the gap without shining in the worker’s eyes.

They try one wedge, then another. The second fits. It must still be driven carefully, since the slab beside it has developed a slight hollow underneath.

When the work is finished, they pour a little water over the repair and watch where it goes.

Then they gather their tools. Someone has kept supper warm for them, and the road home is one they checked that morning.
