The people waiting beside the ropes are not all waiting for the same thing.

One is watching the far edge of the opening leaf. One has a bare heel pressed to its central rib. A third keeps putting two fingers into the joint of a carrying frame, checking a repair that has already held through three moves. The youngest is watching the animals fall. She has never seen one of the heavy climbers lose its grip before.

Their faces are mostly hidden by dust cloths. Above the cloths, their eyes sit deep under broad, bony brows. Each eye has a clear outer lid that closes sideways; behind it, a darker lid blinks more slowly. Pollen collects along the outer seams. From time to time someone brushes it away with the back of a bent finger.

They are narrow through the chest and wide at the hips, with long arms and feet that spread under weight. Standing still, they look slightly crouched. Their soles are divided into soft gripping pads, except for a hard strip along the outside edge that can be set against a fiber and used as a brake. On a steep leaf, they turn that edge downhill.

The children do this before anyone teaches them.

Their skin is dry, fine-grained, and folded at the joints. It darkens where it is rubbed by ropes or carrying straps. When cut, it closes first with a bead of cloudy amber, then with a thin crust. A wound must be kept warm enough for that crust to remain flexible. Someone with a badly injured hand will carry it tucked inside an armpit while working with the other.

They do not sweat. Under a heavy load, narrow slits open along their ribs, revealing combs of pale tissue. Air passes through these and carries off heat. A person hauling a brace may stop in a draft, lift both arms, and stand with the slits spread. The gesture is ordinary enough that others step around without looking.

There are no drinking vessels in the houses. There are little covered dishes for salt, and flat stones on which food is warmed. The inhabitants’ bodies use the same heavy, heat-softened substances that run through the plants. Cold makes them clumsy long before it makes them uncomfortable.

Even here, there are cold places: pockets beneath dead growth where the air has ceased to circulate. An animal found motionless in one may be alive. Children are warned not to pick such animals up. Some wake quickly in the warmth of a palm.

The woman with her heel against the rib is called Esset.

She is old enough that two of her gripping pads no longer open fully. She wears narrow braces beneath them, made from seed-case splints and loops of fiber. When she walks, one foot makes a faint click. She has been asked several times to pad it, but she finds the sound useful. People know when she is coming behind them.

Her work is to distinguish what the ground is saying.

She cannot name every vibration. Nobody can. She can tell a grazer column from a crowd of shell animals, a leaf opening from a stalk splitting under weight, and a burrower passing underneath from one turning toward the surface. She feels some of these through her feet and others by pressing her jaw to a wooden listening peg.

A good peg is a personal tool. Its broad end is held between the back teeth; its point rests against living tissue. Through it, distant movement comes into the bones of the face. Esset’s has been worn almost flat on one side. She carries it inside her clothing, where it stays warm.

Just now she hears the broad leaf stretching. She also hears something moving inside the rib beneath the work shelter.

She does not signal yet. It is a small movement. There are many small movements.

***

People here speak readily at close quarters.

Inside a house, with the wall screens lowered and their foreheads nearly touching, they have voices that are low, rough, and surprisingly quick. Much of a conversation happens on the inward breath. Their throats are fitted with ridged membranes that catch dust before it enters the breathing combs, and these give speech a faint scraping undertone.

Outside, they use their hands.

A touch on the back of the wrist asks for attention. Two fingers beneath the elbow mean *hold this weight*. A flat palm between the shoulders means *move without turning*. Children learn the signals while being carried. Before they can walk safely, they can object to being put down.

They also learn that an offered hand need not be taken. Someone feeling for a vibration may be doing important work, even if they appear to be idle.

Each settlement has its own additions. In these six houses, rubbing a thumb across another person’s knuckles means *I have checked the lashings*. Visitors sometimes find this too intimate. The residents find visitors’ habit of holding up both hands difficult to see through pollen.

Their knot messages are more formal than their touch.

A route bundle names a sequence of features: a split joint, a downward crown, a warm opening with bitter dust. It does not record distances. Those would become wrong too soon. The knots include what to do if a feature has changed, and which changes mean the route must be abandoned.

An old bundle may have been untied and remade so often that its fiber is polished. Esset keeps several such lengths. She no longer trusts their routes, but she likes the cord.

Her granddaughter has asked for one to make a trap.

Esset has refused twice and will probably give it to her.

***

Most of what they own is unremarkable to look at.

A household’s wall panels are patched with seed husk. Its floor consists of narrow trays that hook together, each tray holding a layer of springy shredded bark. Sleeping wraps are broad, stiff things, doubled beneath the body and folded over it. There are no cushions that cannot be emptied, no cupboard too heavy for one person to lift.

Yet possessions matter.

The finest brace in the work shelter has six mismatched corners because none of its owners has been willing to discard the whole frame. A thorn needle may pass through several generations of hands. Some people keep the jaw plates of a familiar grazer, or a little box of differently colored mineral chips collected from seams they will never see again.

The man checking the carrying frame has packed a strip of wall from his previous house. It bears shallow scratches showing the heights of his children.

He knows it is extra weight. His partner knows he has packed it. Neither has mentioned it.

They measure children against walls even though the walls will soon be elsewhere.

***

The settlement’s animals are less clearly divided into useful and troublesome kinds.

Under the work shelter lives a small grazer with one shortened row of feet. It cannot keep pace with a column and has remained here for months. It feeds on the fuzz beneath the floor, leaving a clean oval that moves with the shelter whenever the shelter is moved.

Someone carries it.

Nobody admits to being its owner. Several people have opinions about its diet.

When disturbed, it folds its many feet beneath its body and becomes a ridged, warm object about the length of a forearm. The children use it to hold down loose fiber while they braid. Occasionally it eats the fiber. They have learned to braid faster.

There are shell animals in every house. Most are brushed out daily. One kind, no bigger than a thumbnail, is allowed to remain near the food dishes because it eats the smaller creatures that bore into softened root slices. It announces danger by locking its plates together with a sharp snap.

A house full of those snaps is evacuated without discussion.

The moths are not kept, but their movements are watched. Several species descend before a new leaf tears open, drawn by gases leaking through the seam. Others abandon a chamber shortly before a canopy collapse. A dense flight may mean only warmth. The inhabitants disagree constantly over which flights matter.

They are less inclined to disagree about the pale hunters in curled fronds.

Those have a fringe of sensory hairs around the mouth, spread flat against the inner leaf. A footstep flexes the leaf and brings the hairs into contact with the prey. The hunter closes its jaws sideways, then pulls the meal into a dry pouch beneath its body. It may feed there for hours.

Near a settlement, curled fronds are prodded with long poles.

Some remain curled for so many days that people begin setting things on them. This is discouraged. It still happens.

***

The children spend much of their time sorting.

They sort edible seed cores from fragments of explosive casing. They sort straight fibers from twisted ones. They sort shed scales by size and test which will make a good scraping edge.

They also build settlements of their own in the leaf fur: tiny platforms, bent-shoot ladders, awnings made from translucent wing scraps. A model house may last only an hour before the surface lifts or a grazer eats through it.

An adult passing will sometimes adjust a miniature brace. Sometimes the child objects. The adult is expected to put it back.

There is no school building. Skills belong to particular people and particular tasks. A child might spend several days beside a seed collector, then follow a trapper, then sit with an injured relative stripping bark. There are things everyone must learn—how to release a loaded rope, how to find a breathable pocket under fallen material—and things only a few care to learn well.

The youngest watcher at the basin knows how to open the small seed bodies safely. She does not yet know when a tall seed case is about to discharge.

She has been warned away from one beside the path three times today.

Now she has forgotten it because the climber is falling.

It strikes a lower leaf with a sound like a basket of tools being emptied. Its hooked forelimbs catch. Its body swings once, scales rattling, and settles against the stem.

The child touches her father’s wrist to make sure he saw.

He did.

***

Esset lifts her heel.

The movement beneath the shelter has become distinct: slow pressure, a pause, then pressure farther along. A burrower is testing the inside of the rib.

She goes to the nearest lashing and loosens it one turn.

The others notice. Hands leave the carrying frames. One person takes the hot food stone out of its cradle; another gathers the unfinished braiding from the floor. A boy crawls beneath the shelter and comes back with the short-footed grazer clasped against his chest.

The ground rises slightly.

There is no alarm call. Esset touches the shelter keeper’s elbow, then lays two fingers flat along the rib to show the direction. He begins undoing the floor trays from that end.

The awning is already down. It has caught a great deal of pollen, and when they fold it a yellow cloud comes out. For a moment everyone closes their outer eyelids.

Beneath the leaf, the burrower continues. Its passing lifts one side of the shelter enough to spill a dish of salt. Someone catches the dish. Nobody catches the salt.

Beyond them, the new gap in the basin is widening. A strip of pale, unwalked surface has opened along its lower edge, sheltered by the older leaf above. Two residents are studying it. They cannot yet tell whether it will hold the houses.

Esset presses her peg against the nearest rib and bites down.

Behind her, the child waits with the grazer. Its feet have begun to unfold between his fingers.
