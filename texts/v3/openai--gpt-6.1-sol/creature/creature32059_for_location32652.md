The people have pale shins and dark hands.

This is not entirely their coloring. The shins are whitened by the crust they walk through, while the hands are stained by food, hide dressing, and the oily secretions of the animals they keep. In the creases of an old person’s fingers, the dark remains even after washing. Children try to acquire it by rubbing their palms against the undersides of drying racks.

Most go barefoot on familiar ground. Their soles are broad and thick, but they can still feel a tremor through them. On uncertain crust they wear flat shoes made from overlapping belly plates, with straps that can be pulled loose in one motion. A trapped foot is more dangerous than a cut one.

They walk with their knees slightly bent. Even while standing still, they shift their weight, testing the floor.

A visitor might mistake this for restlessness. It is closer to listening.

***

Among the broad-backed grazers, the youngest are the quickest to move.

They are born in pairs, small enough to fit beneath an adult’s belly, with all six feet already blunt and hard. For several days their scraping mouths cannot manage crust. They feed instead on a dark paste secreted between the plates of the mother’s underside. This is why so many little creatures live around the adults’ legs. Some steal the paste. Others eat the thieves. A nursing grazer travels inside a crowd of attendants, parasites, and opportunists.

By the time its mouth hardens, the young animal has learned to recognize the sound of feeding.

It follows the rasp, rather than the herd’s calls. When frightened, it tries to push its head beneath the nearest scraping jaw. Adults tolerate this until food becomes scarce. Then they lift their young aside with the front pair of legs and set them down on already worked stone.

Their mouths are not single blades. They are ranks of small teeth mounted in tough pads, each rank loosening as the one behind it matures. Shed teeth lie everywhere, indistinguishable at first from flakes of mineral. People gather them for grating tools.

A feeding grazer braces four feet, advances the other two, and draws its mouth sideways across the surface. The noise is intimate and unpleasant at close range: a moist filing, followed by a swallow. Beneath its wide back, the gut occupies most of the body. You can sometimes see slow contractions traveling from one flank to the other.

The oldest animals acquire ridges of stone faster than they can shed them. Their movements grow deliberate. Small grazers climb onto their backs and clean the seams; nesting creatures lodge in hollows behind the shoulders. An old female may carry an entire patch of life across the valley.

Eventually her weight exceeds what some shelves can bear.

People recognize these animals individually. They clear a route when one approaches a settlement, and send someone ahead to test the crossings. There are places where the remains of a fallen elder still form the safest bridge over a channel.

***

The pale hunters have narrow heads, long bodies, and tails that seem too delicate for them.

A hunter at rest coils its tail around its feet. When it rises, the tail unrolls and extends uphill or into the breeze. The last handspan is soft, densely folded, and always wet. It tastes what the moving air has picked up from the ground: fresh mineral water, disturbed nests, the sourness of crowded animals.

Its eyes are small and useful mainly for distance. Close work is done with its chin.

There are little pits beneath the jaw. When the hunter lowers its head, these pits register the warmth of bodies against the cooler crust. A sleeping shelled animal may be almost invisible, but its joints give it away.

The hunter does not crack the shell. It pins it down and waits for a limb to emerge.

People fear the young hunters more than the large ones. Adults are patient and reluctant to risk damage to their mouths. A juvenile will try anything that moves, including a dragging rope or a hand reaching into a trap. Children are taught to freeze if one begins following them, then stamp with both feet and call for someone carrying a broad board.

The boards are held low. A hunter sees a warm body above a cold barrier and cannot easily decide where to strike.

There are people skilled at driving them away without injury. They know how close to stand, how slowly to advance, when to let the animal smell an escape route. These people have scars on their calves.

***

The smallest inhabitants are the hardest to separate from the stone.

Some possess a shell no thicker than an eyelid. They seal themselves against the undersides of flakes, leaving only a fringe of limbs exposed. When water arrives, the limbs unfold into combs. A square of crust lifted at the right moment drips with hundreds of tiny, feeding hands.

Others live inside the pores. Their bodies are long translucent threads with dark beads spaced along them. The beads are stomachs. If a thread is broken, several portions may continue feeding independently, until each has grown the parts it lacks.

There are bladders that creep.

They move too slowly to notice unless you mark their position. At the bottom they have a puckered foot; above it, a soft chamber holds water and a colony of smaller organisms. Their amber coloring comes from these tenants. A bladder emptied of them is nearly clear.

People eat the tenants and keep the bladders. A cleaned bladder, carefully fed and never allowed to dry, makes a vessel that repairs small punctures. It must be anchored in a shallow dish at night. Otherwise it may crawl toward warmth, carrying whatever was put inside it.

The finest vessels belong to households that have tended them for generations. They grow broader rather than taller. An old one has a thick, folded rim and a foot that grips its stand strongly enough to resist lifting.

Moving such a vessel requires patience, warm water, and two people who agree about when to pull.

***

In the village beneath the bell lives a woman who tests drinking pools.

She is not old, though her hair has gone nearly white at the temples. One front tooth is dark. Around her waist she wears little sampling cups, tied close so they do not knock together.

She never tastes first.

At each pool she lays her palm on the surrounding crust. She watches the bubbles, if there are bubbles. She rubs a drop between finger and thumb, smells it, and puts another drop onto a folded scrap of living film kept moist inside a shell.

The film changes when certain salts are present. Sometimes it contracts. Sometimes its edge turns cloudy. Once, at a pool everyone had used for years, it came apart into fine strands.

That morning she emptied the village’s water vessels herself. People argued with her until the fishlike animals in the pool began trying to climb out.

She has two apprentices. One is her son. The other came from a settlement whose drinking spring sealed shut during a flood. She makes them record nothing until they have described aloud what they actually observed.

“Bitter” is not enough.

Bitter before warmth is different from bitter after warmth. A pool that smells clean at its center may carry a poisonous seep along one bank. The youngest apprentice is very good at noticing where the drinking animals place their feet.

The woman expects that apprentice to surpass her. She is less sure of her son, who can remember every pool’s history but sometimes sees what he expected to see.

***

Households keep a few small grazers in enclosures with smooth walls.

These animals are soft-backed and no larger than sleeping children. They eat fresh sheets brought in from safe ledges. Their dung is dark, cohesive, and unusually dry; it fuels cooking fires when mixed with shed shell.

Kept grazers learn the sounds of particular people. They crowd against the feeding side of the enclosure when someone approaches with a scraping basket. If disappointed, they make a low, sustained complaint, each joining the others until the wall vibrates.

Their eggs are laid in clusters inside a leathery envelope. Only some are allowed to hatch. The rest are cut open and cooked on warm slabs, where the white thickens around a yellow center streaked with blue.

There are no permanent owners of the wild herds, but there are claims to their passages. A household that maintains a crossing may collect shed teeth there. One that clears a lane through its houses may trap the little followers after the herd has gone.

Disputes often concern whether a passage was truly maintained.

Everyone can see a newly built wall. It is harder to prove who spent half a night clearing rubble from a channel that has since filled again.

***

The winged creatures roost above the reach of ordinary steam.

At rest, they look like bundles of broken fans. Their wings fold at several joints, and the thin plates overlap so closely that an animal can tuck itself into a narrow crack. Before launching, it opens each wing separately and shakes loose accumulated dust.

Some eat the creatures washed from newly opened vents. Others snatch parasites from grazers’ backs. They land badly, with feet and wings striking in quick succession, and spend a moment looking offended before settling.

Their young hatch with soft wings. For weeks they climb through the roosts, gripping with hooked toes, begging from any adult that lands nearby. Many fall.

People spread woven hide beneath certain nesting ledges. The rescued young are raised for meat, wing plates, and the long stiff spines used in tool handles. A few become household animals. They perch on roof edges and announce approaching herds long before anyone below can see them.

They also steal food, bite fingers, and rattle furiously when a roof panel is moved without their permission.

***

When the ground begins to lift, the bell keeper does not ring immediately.

He steps off his platform and stands on the open crust. He walks a short circle. Sometimes he kneels and lays the side of his face against the floor.

A herd approaching through haze can produce a convincing tremor. So can a shelf settling after water has drained beneath it. The bell means something more particular: take down what can be taken down; move the children and the stored water; leave the heavy things.

When it rings, nobody gathers beneath it.

They separate into tasks. Someone loosens roof lashings. Someone opens the animal pens. Someone checks the houses where people cannot move quickly. The pool tester takes her vessels uphill, with the apprentices carrying the oldest bladder between them.

Often the swelling stops. The village returns to find tilted steps, spilled food, a new crack running through a sleeping platform.

Sometimes there is no village to return to.

Then the people wait at the edge of the fresh deposit. Steam wets their faces. Their released grazers are already feeding, and pale hunters move among the gathering bodies with their tails extended.

As soon as the surface will bear weight, someone goes out with a cutting blade to test the new stone.

The first slab is usually poor: too soft underneath, too brittle at the edges. They turn it over, inspect the crowded life exposed beneath it, and set it aside.

There will be better material farther in.

Behind them, the children begin sorting the salvaged roof plates into piles.
