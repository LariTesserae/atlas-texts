At first you take one of them for something caught in the roots.

It is on the far side of a pool, below a tree fern whose trunk has bent almost horizontal. There is a pale patch under the trunk, a darker mass below that, and what looks like a dead vine trailing into the water. When the fern stirs, the mass does not. When the fern becomes still, the pale patch turns toward you.

The face is broad and rather flat. Two dark eyes sit far apart beneath a brow that overhangs them like the rim of a shelf. There are no visible ears. Along the sides of the neck, several deep folds open and close, exposing wet, pinkish skin.

The dead vine is an arm.

Its owner has been lying with that arm in the pool, fingers spread across a submerged stone. Now the fingers close. Something silver flickers between them. The creature brings its hand to its mouth, eats, and puts the arm back exactly where it was.

It watches you throughout.

The rest of its body becomes easier to distinguish once you know it is there. It has a heavy chest, a low belly, and four short supporting limbs, folded underneath it in pairs. The two arms are much longer. A sitting adult can reach the ground on either side without leaning. Its skin is grey-brown where exposed, pale beneath the folds, and dark green over the shoulders.

The green is partly its own colour and partly a close growth of little plants.

These do not cover it indiscriminately. Moss occupies a shallow groove down the back. Tiny leathery leaves grow behind the head, where water collects. The forearms are clean. So are the palms and the pads of the feet. Around one wrist is a cord carrying three drilled pieces of black stone.

After a while, it lifts its empty hand and holds it out toward you.

You cannot tell whether this means come closer, stay where you are, or give me what you are holding.

You discover later that it means all three, depending on how the fingers are arranged.

***

They live across the plateau in small, loosely connected households. There may be nine in a basin and none in the next three. Some sleep in the old buildings. Others use platforms woven between leaning trunks, or occupy dry shelves under overhangs where seepage keeps the air wet without reaching the floor.

“Dry” here means that water is not standing on the sleeping place.

Their bedding is made from the split inner stems of reeds, laid in thick parallel bundles. It drains readily and has to be replaced often. Old bedding goes into the planted trenches around the low, curved roofs. New growth comes up through it before it has entirely rotted.

Most adults are about as high as your chest when standing on all four supporting limbs. They can raise the front of the body and balance briefly on the rear pair, but seem to find this uncomfortable. They do it to reach fruit, inspect an unfamiliar visitor, or express a particular degree of annoyance.

Their arms are extraordinarily useful. Each hand has four thick fingers and two opposed gripping digits, one above the other. A hand can close around a branch while still manipulating something small against the palm. They carry fish hooks, seedlings, sharp flakes of stone, and half-eaten fruit this way, apparently without confusing them.

The feet are broad and divided into soft lobes. These spread on mud and draw together on stone. On the heavier ground an adult walks with a careful, rolling transfer of weight. In the light bands the same animal becomes almost buoyant. Its body rises higher between its limbs; its long arms swing clear of the earth.

They prefer those bands for travel, but do not remain in them all the time. An adult carrying a full basket will sometimes step out of the light ground to settle the load, pressing its feet firmly against the rock before going on.

Young ones are less cautious.

They run along the boundaries, crossing and recrossing them to lengthen a jump. They throw wet seed pods upward where the falling rain slows, then try to catch the pods before they reach ordinary ground again. This produces much excitement and occasional injuries. Near one roof there is an old adult with a crooked arm who can stop the game simply by looking toward it.

The children wait until that adult turns away.

***

Their skin must remain damp, though they are not helpless away from water. The neck folds hold moisture against a thin, richly supplied surface. In the afternoon these folds spread wider. A resting individual may lower its chin into a pool and remain there for half an hour, breathing through small openings high on the face.

They suffer in a dry wind. They suffer more quickly than the browsing animals do.

On exposed ridges they wear loose capes of felted plant fibre, wet on the inside. The capes have weighted hems and are pinned across the chest with pale thorns. From a distance, a traveller under one looks like a moving piece of fallen canopy.

They are not especially bothered by the heat until they have to hurry. Hurry changes them. Their skin flushes, the folds at the neck work rapidly, and they become abrupt with one another. After a fast climb, two companions may sit several yards apart until their breathing settles.

Their ordinary pace is slow enough to see things you pass.

They notice which pools have begun to cloud, which reeds have had their lower stems bitten, where a crab has moved silt against a root. On a walk that would take you an hour, one of them might stop twenty times. It will remove a blocking leaf from a channel, gather a fruit, smell a damaged shoot, stand with its hand on a warm wall, and wait for a bird to finish drinking.

Some of these pauses are work. Some are appetite. Some seem to be nothing more than interest.

***

They eat a great deal of fruit, but not only fruit. Their teeth include narrow front edges for stripping stems and broad, ridged surfaces farther back. Fish are swallowed small and chewed when larger. Crabs are opened with a tool like a bent spoon. Certain fungi are cultivated on fallen trunks, under covers that keep the rain from striking them directly.

They keep food in shallow hanging trays rather than closed containers. The trays are polished smooth and suspended over water, with little collars on the cords to discourage climbing insects.

The insects are discouraged, not prevented.

Cooking is done against the stone. In a few places, narrow wells descend into much hotter depths. A household lowers food wrapped in leaves into one of these wells, using a forked rod to retrieve it. There are arguments about how long things should stay down. You see an adult unwrap a bundle, examine its contents, and hold it silently beneath another’s nose.

Whatever is said afterward, it sounds familiar.

Their voices are low, made partly in the throat and partly in chambers along the neck. Some notes carry a long distance. Others are so soft that you notice them only by the movement of the folds.

They also speak with their hands.

At a distance, a lifted arm and a few changes of the fingers can conduct a lengthy exchange between opposite sides of a marsh. Up close, hands accompany nearly everything. One may remain occupied with cutting a root while the other comments on the story being told.

Laughter makes the neck folds tremble. It can be almost inaudible.

***

The buildings are familiar to them, but familiarity does not tell you who made them.

They have names for individual rooms, for a particular outlet, for places on a wall where it is safe to press. They know which surfaces can bear a hanging basket and which will release it without warning. They can enter chambers you never find a door into.

The change in the pale panels is useful to their wide-set eyes. They rarely face a panel directly. An adult approaching the tower turns its head slightly, looks past the wall, and keeps walking. From your position, you see its body disappear behind an opaque surface.

When you try the same thing, you bump your forehead.

This is one of the occasions on which you hear them laugh.

Inside, where they allow you to follow, the chambers are sparsely furnished. Reeds lie on raised shelves. A few tools hang from cords. Water covers much of the floor. There are dark columns with shallow hollows shaped by hands, and places where the wall gives under pressure.

Their own additions are easy to recognize. Woven screens divide sleeping spaces. A fruit-drying rack leans against a column. Someone has scratched a row of small marks beside a low opening, recording the growth of a child.

They maintain some parts of the structures and leave others alone.

At the tower, an adult clears a fibrous plug from one of the five descending streams. It works slowly with a hooked implement, catching each loosened piece so that none enters the marsh. When the flow is restored, it washes the tool and hangs it beneath the open hatch.

No one touches the outlet from which water rises.

They gather beneath it sometimes. Children hold objects near the ascending beads, testing which will be taken upward. Adults put out trays that collect the insects falling free of the column.

Once, very early, you see an old individual standing beside the rising stream with both hands open. It stays there until the light changes. Then it closes its hands, checks a tray, and takes breakfast home.

You never find out what the first part meant.

***

The green growth on their bodies is tended.

Adults inspect one another’s backs, removing dead patches and unwanted seedlings. A household may share the same small creeping plant, passed from skin to skin as cuttings. Strangers sometimes exchange a sprig after a long conversation.

This planting is not permanent. People change it. One adult has a bare back, recently scraped clean, with new pale growth appearing along the central groove. Another carries several kinds of moss in distinct patches. A very old individual has a little fern rooted above one shoulder, its fronds kept short by careful pinching.

The plants retain moisture and shelter small creatures. Some of those creatures are tolerated. Others provoke immediate, forceful scratching.

A white bird lands on a resting adult and begins to probe behind its neck. The adult permits this for a time, then reaches back and lifts the bird off by its body. The bird protests. It is placed on a branch, where it waits until the hand withdraws and immediately returns.

They know the other inhabitants individually, at least near their homes.

A particular browser is driven away from the planted trenches. Another is allowed close because it eats the coarse reeds nobody wants. Children carry crabs from one pool to another and are told to put them back. A long-tailed, moss-covered animal sleeps above a household platform for several weeks; beneath it, someone sets a broad leaf to catch its droppings.

No part of this appears to be a ceremony.

***

Their gatherings happen where paths meet water.

There are low benches at these places, sometimes no more than a smoothly worn trunk supported on stones. Food is shared. News arrives in pieces. A visitor may spend an entire afternoon describing a blocked channel several ridges away, who has already gone to look at it, and why the first proposed remedy failed.

They disagree. They interrupt. Some leave before the conversation is finished.

One individual is known for departing with the communal cutting tool still tucked against its palm. Another makes remarkably good woven containers but ties knots that nobody else can undo. A young adult spends several evenings watching the far eastern peaks instead of helping replace a shelter roof.

The others seem to have opinions about this.

They are not waiting for you, or for anything you can identify. Their lives have tasks that recur, pleasures that take time, and troubles small enough to be discussed at length.

Toward evening they return by the established paths, carrying whatever the day has yielded. Their damp capes darken. Water drips from their wrists. Calls pass between households as the vapour covers the basins.

In the gathering mist, the plants on their shoulders make their outlines difficult to follow. An adult stops beside a fern, and for a moment there seem to be only two ferns. Then a hand rises to move a trailing vine out of the way.

At the low-roofed dwelling, someone shifts a basket to make room for you.

The offered seat is a bundle of reeds on a warm shelf. Beside it is a shallow bowl holding a fruit you have seen split open on the path. This one has been peeled. Its large seeds lie neatly in a separate dish.

Your host lowers itself into the water at the edge of the room. The folds of its neck loosen. For a while it pays no attention to you, working a thorn out of the soft pad of one foot.

Outside, the waterfall continues. A child comes in carrying an empty cord and is sent out again, reluctantly, to find whatever was tied to it.

Your host holds the extracted thorn up to the light, drops it into the seed dish, and reaches for its own share of the fruit.
