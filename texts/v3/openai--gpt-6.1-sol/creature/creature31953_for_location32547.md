The first sign of them is a pool that does not accept the rain.

Drops strike its surface and remain there, each in its own little hollow, trembling without spreading. From a few paces away this looks like ordinary water. Closer, you see that the reflections have seams. The gray sky has been fitted together from several broad, translucent surfaces, their edges overlapping beneath a fringe of moss.

Something is lying face upward under the pool.

It does not rise when you approach. One of the seams opens, admitting a little water, and closes again. Far below it, where a reflection ought to be, a pale joint bends.

Most of these creatures live against the underside of the country. They occupy the wet interval between the felt and the water beneath it, holding themselves in place with broad, divided hands. Their backs hang downward. Their faces press into the roots.

There is food there, but feeding is only part of what they do.

A buried stem passes through the side of a mouth and emerges farther along, cleaned of its black coating. A root grows into a fold beneath the jaw and remains for several days. Small crawling things enter the body through openings that close gently around them. Some come out again with transparent eggs attached to their legs. Others can still be seen inside, moving through chambers close to the skin.

The creatures are not transparent throughout. There are regions of dense, pale tissue, and dark organs that shift when the body changes its arrangement. Water carries the weight of these organs. Lift one clear of it and the dark parts sag, pulling the skin into unfamiliar shapes.

They seldom expose themselves so completely.

When a sheet of turf begins to travel, they travel with it. You might see a hand briefly on the upper surface, its digits spread among the mosses, then drawn down again. Or a length of pale flank appears in the opening between two sheets. The opening closes. The flank is gone.

They can remain beneath one patch for years, even while that patch moves across the plateau.

Where they have been living, the roots form shallow chambers. These are not excavated. The roots simply grow around the bodies, leaving enough space for a shoulder to turn, for a folded limb to straighten. The chambers persist after their occupants leave. Blind white animals move into them. Water collects in the impressions of hands.

A person who fell through the felt might encounter several of these creatures before finding open water. They would be close together, arranged at different angles, with root bundles passing between them. One might move aside. Another might bring a palm against the falling person’s chest.

The palm would feel cold and heavily cushioned. Fine hairs on it would take hold of cloth, skin, a buckle, anything available. For a moment the fall would stop.

Then other hands might arrive.

They do not pull a person upward in the way one person rescues another. They spread the weight. They pass it from one grip to the next until some portion of the surface can bear it. A traveler may emerge a considerable distance from the hole, on a patch of turf that seems too thin to support a body.

Behind them, underneath, the creatures disengage slowly. The last hairs let go one at a time.

They do this with grazing animals too. Sometimes they do it with a loose bank of peat or a branch of one of the great root disks. It is difficult to know whether the distinctions matter to them.

Above ground, they are less easily mistaken for parts of the landscape, though it takes time to find the whole of one.

A creature comes up where the turf has folded. First there are two hands, then a smooth portion of the back. It draws itself through without tearing the opening wider. The body is longer than expected. Several folds that appeared to be empty skin fill as water moves into them, and a narrow chest lifts clear of the moss.

Standing, it is about the height of a person kneeling.

It has four limbs, although each can divide near its end. The divisions are not permanent fingers or toes. A hand used for gripping roots may have twenty slender tips. On firmer ground those tips draw together into two thick pads. One limb can remain spread and buried while another narrows to probe a pool.

The head is carried low, almost against the chest. There are no eyes on its forward surface. Instead, a curved band of small clear blisters runs from one side of the neck to the other. Each blister holds a dark speck suspended in fluid.

When a column begins its deep rotating thump, the specks move.

Some rise toward the skin. Others fall inward. Their arrangement changes with the pulse coming through the ground, and the creature turns before the sound reaches you through the air.

It can see something, certainly. It follows the movement of a raised hand. It avoids the feeding tendrils of the herds. But it pays little attention to distant objects. A belt of vegetation on the horizon may mean nothing to it until the water below carries a disturbance from that direction.

Near the columns, they gather in numbers.

They settle around the black root disks, sometimes so closely that their limbs overlap. The white leaves on the columns lift and flatten above them. Wet pollen falls across their backs, clings briefly, and washes away.

They do not harvest it. They do not tend the machinery.

During the long contractions before a stopped column begins again, however, the creatures become very still. Their neck blisters fill with dark specks. The skin between their shoulders draws tight.

Just before the first shudder, they release their grip.

A ring of pale bodies slips into the surrounding water. The column starts. Its root disk rises a little, then settles with enough force to squeeze water from several acres of felt.

Those that left early return first.

The others wait farther out, holding onto stems while the pressure subsides.

Sometimes one remains attached throughout the restart. Afterward it may come to the surface with one limb shortened, or with a dark patch where its internal tissues have been compressed. The others touch the damaged place. They remain there until roots begin to grow across it.

They communicate by contact, but not only by contact with one another.

A creature presses the side of its head against the turf. Elsewhere, another lifts a hand. Between them there may be a hundred yards of saturated growth. No visible motion passes through it. If you stand barefoot between them, you feel a brief tightening beneath one heel, followed by a faint movement under the other.

Their exchanges can last all afternoon.

They pause for the columns, for the underground rushes, for a herd crossing overhead. Sometimes the conversation continues after one participant has gone below. Sometimes a third creature arrives hours later and presses its head into precisely the place the first one used.

There are places where they gather without doing anything you can identify.

A shallow hollow may hold twelve or fifteen, each resting against a different portion of its edge. Their bodies slowly alter the outline of the pool. Water rises against their chests. A few of the leaf-winged birds root themselves along their backs.

One creature lets a small object out through a slit beneath its ribs.

It is a flattened piece of pale tissue, no larger than a leaf, with a dark branching mark inside. The creature passes it across the water. Another receives it, holds it against the clear blisters of its neck, then gives it to the next.

By evening the piece has become thicker. Fine rootlets trail from its edge.

Eventually someone puts it into the felt. The gathering disperses.

Such pieces are found deep in the older layers of the plateau. Some contain branching stains. Some contain the shapes of hands. If brought to the surface and kept wet, a few begin to flex.

The young are easier to recognize.

They appear in the little mounds left by heavy animals. After the narrow leaves have opened, a mound may continue rising. The leaves spread apart around a smooth, wet surface. Two minute hands grip their bases.

For a while the young creature remains rooted to the depression from which it grew. It feeds on the stems around it and turns its small band of blisters toward every tremor. Its limbs are too short to reach the water underneath.

Adults sometimes stop beside these mounds. They place a hand around the young without pulling it free. Water seeps upward between their digits.

When the young finally leaves, it goes down rather than up. The mound empties overnight. In the morning there is a small opening lined with pale roots, and beneath it the water is faintly cloudy.

Not every mound produces one. Most become grass. Some produce things the adults avoid.

During the great disturbances, when a tract of country drops beyond sight, the creatures come to the surface with the herds. They crowd the stationary patches, clinging to one another and to whatever still holds.

This is when their voices can be heard.

Each has a narrow opening beneath the chest folds. Water drains from it in spurts. Between the spurts comes a low, uneven note, scarcely louder than breathing, but many together can be heard through the concussion of the land.

The notes do not keep pace with the columns. They falter, overlap, stop abruptly when a creature loses its footing.

One may stretch a divided hand across a widening channel. Another reaches from the opposite side. Their tips join, and for a few moments smaller bodies cross along the joined limbs.

Then the channel moves. Both creatures disappear.

Afterward, some return in altered shapes. A limb has more joints than before. The clear band circles farther around the neck. A familiar dark organ is now on the opposite side of the body.

Others do not return at all.

Their chambers remain under the felt, slowly narrowing. A hand may still be found there, spread among the roots, its tissue dark but supple. New growth passes through it. Tiny animals use the spaces between its digits.

Sometimes an old chamber contains a creature that is neither moving nor entirely dead. Its back has become part of the turf’s lower surface. Its limbs extend into neighboring chambers. Water passes through the open folds of its body.

A younger one rests against it, neck pressed close to the place where the chest used to lift.

Above them, the grazing continues. Rain gathers on the stems. Somewhere, a column stops and begins its series of clicks.

The younger creature shifts its head.

Deep in the old body, beneath layers that have not moved for years, something closes slowly around a root.
