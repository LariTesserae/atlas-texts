What lives there has no need to pass between the four stands.

The bare intervals are not paths. Nothing has been cleared from them, and nothing waits at their edges for permission to cross. They are portions of the hollow that cannot yet support the same kind of presence as the planted ground. A grain can lie there. Heat can traverse them. Roots approaching from either side turn downward, following seams too fine to show at the surface.

Only once the ground has thickened do they begin to grow toward one another.

In the copper thicket, life is easily recognized. There are crowded stems, leaves competing for the light, bulbs pressing against bulbs. A leaf shaded by its neighbors lengthens until its tip reaches an exposed patch of mineral. There it stops, sometimes for years. The curl along its margin gradually loosens.

During the mountain’s cold nights, the golden material rises through the bulbs. It advances in blunt columns, leaving clear spaces below it. The leaves stiffen as it enters them. By morning they have become soft again, and the gold has withdrawn to the base, except for a little retained in each spiral. Those small reserves keep the tips warm after the rest of the leaf has cooled.

Among the oldest stems is one bulb with almost nothing above it. Its last leaf fell a long time ago. The broken stalk has closed into a small, smooth dome.

The bulb still fills and empties with the others.

In the dark fans, an apparently unhealthy plant may be carrying the greatest charge. Its leaflets draw tightly together, showing neither their broad surfaces nor the filaments between them. For weeks it resembles a closed black fist. Then a pale line appears at its base and proceeds outward through each branch.

When it reaches the tips, the fans open.

No pollen is released. No spore becomes visible. Yet afterward, in an unfinished depression some distance away, a metal thread may be found lying beneath the translucent floor. It is finer than any root in the hollow. One end divides into five.

The red stand is harder to follow. Its leaves do not become invisible when viewed head-on; their surfaces are simply elsewhere. Their edges belong to the hollow, while most of their breadth extends into a direction the hollow has not yet made available.

Occasionally a leaf turns far enough to show a patch of its surface. The patch is dull red, densely pitted, and bears marks like the impressions of neighboring leaves. These cannot have been made by anything presently beside it. Some of the impressions are much larger than the plant itself.

The pale specimen has a different abundance. Its leaves emerge with all their small veins already completed, including veins in portions of the leaf that remain folded out of sight. Their unfolding is irregular. One lobe opens, another stays enclosed. A leaf may spend a season exposing a narrow strip of tissue no wider than a cut.

Beneath the canopy, heat gathers in layers. A fallen grain can pass upward through several of them, hesitate, and settle against the underside of a leaf. If it stays there long enough, the leaf grows a small cup around it.

These cups contain much of what can be learned about the hollow.

Some hold ordinary mineral fragments. Others contain a chip of blue-black foliage, a copper rib, or a red edge severed from a leaf whose broad surface was never visible. None of these pieces is digested. Each remains distinct, although fine veins slowly extend around it.

One old cup holds a white stone.

It was once a drop of water brought in on the point of a needle. The drop solidified near the entrance and lay untouched until the growth of a ridge tipped it into a shallow channel. Over many years it moved along that channel, then up the side of a plate, finally coming to rest beneath the pale canopy.

The leaf surrounding it is smaller than the others. Its veins branch unevenly. Where they approach the stone, they stop short and begin again farther away.

Whatever lives here can accommodate an unfamiliar thing without knowing what to do with it.

There are other signs of this uncertainty. A copper root sometimes enters newly formed ground and then withdraws, leaving a clear tube behind. A shaft below the dark fans may turn through half a revolution and remain there for decades. Leaves formed during such an interval differ slightly from those formed before it: an extra division, a thicker rib, a tip that never quite opens.

The changes persist after the shaft resumes turning.

So the machinery beneath the plants is not merely supplying them. Nor are the plants simply using what the mountain happens to provide. Their living structures reach into the same mineral bands that carry pressure and current. At sufficient depth, root and vein become difficult to distinguish. The soft amber around a root hardens in successive rings; the crystalline wall of a chamber sends translucent threads upward. Somewhere between them, one material becomes the other without a visible boundary.

An exposed shaft bears a small inclusion shaped like a seed. It moves with the shaft, but it also grows. After a long enough interval, its outer layer separates into two shells. The shells travel apart within the turning stone. Something clear remains between them.

This may be reproduction. It may be the making of another working part.

The inhabitants do not divide themselves according to the distinctions an observer would bring through the seam. The four stands have separate needs, separate habits of growth, and recognizable ways of failing. Yet damage to one sometimes appears first in another.

If a copper leaf is scorched by unusually harsh sunlight, the blue-black fans close before the injured tissue changes color. When a chamber beneath the pale plant loses pressure, several red edges turn toward the entrance. They hold that position until the chamber fills again.

A response can take so long to arrive that it looks like an unrelated event. It can also begin in structures whose connection to the disturbance has not yet come into existence.

For this reason, it is difficult to say how many things live in the hollow.

There may be four kinds of plant sustained by a remarkable geology. There may be many organisms below the floor, each with a brief visible expression among the leaves. Or the entire warm recess may contain one life whose parts have grown apart, and which is slowly acquiring the means to join them.

It does not appear to be lonely. Solitude would require some history of separation, and the clean spaces between the stands show no remains of an earlier connection. Whatever is happening here began with the distances as they are.

At the entrance, beneath the lowest copper leaf, the unfinished edge grows warmer.

The leaf lowers. Its tip comes close enough to cast a sharp shadow on a surface that could not have held a shadow the previous day. Far below, a contact closes. The dark fans retain their sparks a little longer. High in the pale plant’s inverted litter, a rib comes loose and starts its slow passage toward the floor.

Eventually the copper tip touches the new stone.

For a long time nothing further happens.

Then, beneath the point of contact, a clear filament begins to descend.
