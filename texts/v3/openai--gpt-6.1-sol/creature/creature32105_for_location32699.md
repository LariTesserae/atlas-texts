What lives here is easiest to recognize in the small court with six doorways.

Not because it is smaller than the others. Much of it extends behind the walls. But the court contains nearly all the features by which an inhabitant can eventually be distinguished from its surroundings, and they are gathered closely enough that you do not have to cross a valley to understand what you are seeing.

Begin with the basin.

Under the bulge of water, something pale rests against the stone. You took it for a root. It has the thick, unhurried shape of a root, and the same fine creases where it bends. But it divides into several blunt ends, each fitted to a shallow depression in the basin’s floor.

There are too many ends for a hand. There are too few for the root system of the tree.

Follow it backward. It passes through the side of the basin without a break in either substance. On the outside, it becomes a raised vein in the stone, then widens beneath the tree into the smooth, low rise on which you have been resting your foot.

You step away.

Nothing withdraws. Nothing needs to.

The being occupies the court in much the way a seated animal occupies its own folded limbs. Part of it is mineral; part has the dark, moist grain of living wood. The difference is not between its body and something supporting its body. Both are tissues. Its softer portions are protected wherever they cross a frequently used passage. Its harder portions have grown broad and shallow where a visitor might need to stand.

The tree is not its head, though the crown serves some of the purposes a head would serve. The basin is not its stomach. The six doorways are not six mouths.

These comparisons help briefly, then begin to get in the way.

There is a place where the being receives things. There are places where it holds them. There are surfaces through which it knows what touches it, and cavities in which that knowledge is kept. None of these is confined to one organ.

The closed buds beneath the ledge are sensitive. So are the polished tops of the roots. So, apparently, are the undersides of the worn stair treads. The inhabitant can feel a foot on the other side of a thickness of stone.

What looked like an impression left by a leaf is one of these sensitive surfaces. The leaf and its green likeness in the wall belong to the same tissue, folded together. Between them is a space thinner than a fingernail.

It is listening there.

Not for sound. The roar is already inside it.

***

There are many inhabitants.

Once you have found the first, you begin to see where one ends and another begins. The boundaries are subtle but quite definite. A line of reddish stone thins to nothing. A root crosses another root without joining it. Two neighboring walls touch along a dark seam, soft enough to accept their differences in shape.

Some occupy only a stair and the rooms beside it. Others extend through several terraces. Across the valley, the horizontal grove belongs to a single long-bodied inhabitant whose lower portions enter the mountain far beneath the upright trees.

Size does not seem to determine importance. A small one beside a drain has an elaborate arrangement of sheltered niches, worn perches, and little cups of water. Its nearest neighbor is immense, carrying an avenue along its back, but has almost no interior space.

They are not houses that have come alive. Their bodies have always included room for other bodies.

That is what makes them a city.

A covered passage may belong to one inhabitant along its left side and another along its right. The roof is made where the two have grown together. They do not fuse entirely. A narrow strip of exposed tissue runs down the middle, pale and faintly ridged, like the seam between two clasped palms.

At some crossings, several meet. Here the architecture becomes generous: wide landings, basins with low rims, deep shade. Each contributes a surface. None encloses the whole place.

You had thought these were communal conveniences built for a missing population. They are places where the population touches.

***

Their faces, when you learn to find them, are extraordinarily private.

A face may be inside a shallow alcove, no larger than a bowl. It may occupy the underside of a bridge. One looks into a room from the wall behind the room’s apparent entrance.

There is usually a dark, wet hollow bordered by small folds. Sometimes there are two hollows, sometimes a row of them. They are not eyes, although something about their arrangement makes looking away feel courteous.

The folds carry minute variations in color: green fading into cream, a warm brown at the deepest edges. The stone around them is finer-grained than elsewhere. No moss grows there.

In the court, the face is low on the tree, beneath a root you climbed over.

You passed within inches of it.

It has no expression you can read. Yet its placement is expressive. It faces neither the avenue nor the basin, but the patch of shade where someone entering the court would naturally pause.

One of its folds is slightly flattened. It looks as though a cheek has rested there very often.

Perhaps another inhabitant has such a cheek. Perhaps a softer part of a neighboring body once fitted into this hollow and still does, somewhere farther inside the wall.

You cannot tell whether the contact is happening now.

***

They eat, though nothing is bitten.

The enormous fruits are carried close against their bodies. Beneath the swollen skins, the flesh joins the branches through broad attachments rather than stems. Each fruit is a store of food still contained within the tissue that made it.

The pale leaves gather light. Water enters wherever water and body meet. Mineral veins spread through the inhabitants from the mountain.

In the deeper rooms, you find small clear areas in the stone. Through one of them, a branching structure is visible, delicate as the veins in an eyelid. Amber water occupies every branch. Its surface is still, but the noise there is so forceful that you feel it in the bones of your wrists.

You put your hand against the wall and have the unmistakable impression of a great deal of activity occurring on the other side.

There is no detectable movement.

The inhabitants’ lives are not quiet. Whatever the stillness means, it has not made them vacant.

***

The wear belongs to them.

Their stairs grow hollowed because a hollowed stair is the shape of a limb accustomed to bearing feet. Their corners are rounded where contact is expected. Their thresholds develop the smooth places where a body can hesitate comfortably before entering.

They grow accommodations as naturally as a tree grows forks strong enough to hold its fruit.

This does not mean the wear is false. Touch the center of a step and you can feel how thoroughly it has received a weight. The mountain contains buried stairs because these beings do not cease to have interiors when stone grows over them. Far inside are rooms, joined rooms, passages folded against passages, with polished surfaces no daylight reaches.

Some inhabitants are mostly inward.

Their presence is marked by a single doorway, a patch of warm wall, a basin whose gravity faces into the mountain. The rest of the body occupies depths you cannot estimate.

You begin to understand why looking downward did not feel like looking into an abyss.

There is someone beneath almost every place you stand.

***

In the small court, the inhabitant knows you are there.

You cannot point to a change that proves this. The buds remain closed. The tree remains still. The water holds its low, heavy curve above the basin.

But now that you recognize the body, you recognize its attention.

The patch of shade is large enough for you. The root at your back offers a comfortable angle. Beside your hand, the basin’s rim has a shallow notch through which you could reach the water without pressing your wrist against the stone.

There is nothing ceremonial about these arrangements. They have the practical kindness of a chair pulled out from a table.

You wonder how long it has been waiting.

Then you notice, beneath the root, a second place to sit. It faces yours across the basin. It is much smaller, suited to something narrow-bodied, with a long shallow groove continuing up the wall behind it.

You look again at the polished seam between this court and the next.

The inhabitant has not prepared its body only for you.

It has neighbors. It has preferences in the way they touch it. It has sheltered places for the small ones and strong places for the heavy ones, and parts of itself it keeps out of the public passages. Somewhere within the mountain, beyond the six doorways, it may have a room that no one else enters.

You sit down in the place offered.

The stone is warm. The roar presses steadily against your teeth.

Beneath your palm, the wet bark has a fine grain, and along that grain runs a shallow depression, exactly wide enough to rest your hand.
