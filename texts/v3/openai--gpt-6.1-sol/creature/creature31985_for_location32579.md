The workers notice it first as a difference in the damp.

Most of the wet surfaces in the quarter dry a little toward noon, even under an overcast sky. The handrails lose their beads. The upper bricks of the courtyard wall turn a shade paler. But beneath one of the propagation beds, the moisture remains dark and continuous. If someone rests a hand there, on what appears to be moss over the concrete lip, the surface gives slightly. A few seconds later it gives again.

There is no particular reason to pull it away. It is not obstructing anything.

For much of the year, that is how the inhabitant of the pumping quarter is encountered: as something left in place.

Its largest portion lies beneath the raised nursery beds, where irrigation water falls through the slats and the pump-house wall supplies a steady warmth. Other portions extend into the shallow channels beside the walkways. These are easily mistaken for sediment caught around roots. They have the same soft, brown-green surface, the same small leaves and pale fragments adhering to them. In places they narrow enough to pass beneath a brick; elsewhere they spread into low cushions that occupy the full width of a drain.

They are connected, though the connections are seldom visible. One runs through an obsolete conduit. Another passes under the fig tree, deep in the space lifted by its roots. A third enters the roofless storehouse through a broken joint at the foot of the wall.

It is not clear how much of the quarter the creature occupies at any one time. Parts withdraw. Parts thicken. A length that was present beside a tank last autumn may now be empty moss, while a new fold has appeared beneath the hose rack.

It does not move often enough for this to be inconvenient.

Under the plants and adhering silt, its skin is pale grey, almost translucent along a thin edge. Small vessels branch through it. The underside is firmer, divided into pads that grip brick, concrete, or rusted steel. Each pad can loosen independently. When the creature advances, a short section lifts, gathers itself into a wrinkle, and settles farther along. The rest remains still.

A person watching for ten minutes might see only a leaf change its angle. By the following morning, an entire length may have passed through a gap beneath a gate.

Its upper surface carries loose, folded lobes. In the deeper shade these are broad and thin; near exposed paving they become smaller, with thickened edges that hold water. Moss grows on them. So do minute ferns, whose roots enter the accumulated material between the folds rather than the flesh itself. There are patches of algae on the wetter parts, and occasional seedlings that persist until the creature shifts into a place where their roots can no longer reach enough light.

The plants obscure the distinction between its body and its surroundings. They also keep its skin wet. A bare portion exposed to moving air contracts rapidly, drawing the folds together until only a narrow, glossy seam remains.

Water passes over the lobes and disappears between them. Later, it emerges from small openings along the lower edge, clearer than when it entered. On a quiet day, with an ear close to the paving, it is possible to hear this: a slight internal trickling beneath the larger noise of the pumps.

There is a front end, but it is not always at the end that moves first.

It usually rests in the disused tank where the spare hose is kept. Here the body gathers into a thick, low shape against the curved wall. A blunt part rises a few inches above the rest, bearing several smooth depressions. Two of these close when touched by a falling drop. Below them is a slit with a soft, ridged margin.

The slit opens against submerged surfaces. It takes in loose algae, softened leaves, the film that develops on old hose. Sometimes it encloses a whole fallen fig and remains around it for days. Nothing is bitten cleanly through. Food gradually loses its outline.

A length of hose lying across the creature is generally returned cleaner than it was left, except where the skin has held it tightly enough to leave a dull band around the rubber.

The maintenance staff know this part of it best. They call it the bed.

“Put that on the bed,” someone says, carrying a muddy coupling.

“Not there. It’s moved.”

They do not share a settled explanation of what it is. One worker believes the visible folds are many animals living together. Another says that her predecessor knew it when it occupied only the old tank, before the nursery beds were built. The oldest photographs show the railing clearly, but the dark material below it could be waterweed.

There are practical rules. Do not scrape a drain all the way down if the scrapings begin to bleed clear fluid. Do not leave solvent tins where irrigation water can wash them. If a length has crossed a cabinet door, lift it with the broad wooden paddle kept beside the engine room, not with a shovel.

It weighs more than it appears to. The lifted section hangs over the paddle with the heaviness of soaked cloth. Farther away, folds tighten around their supports. The worker waits, and eventually the grip releases.

Occasionally it needs help.

During a pump shutdown, the warm seepage beneath the nursery beds cools. The creature draws in from the courtyard and crowds into the remaining sheltered spaces. If the shutdown lasts several days, its outer lobes become opaque and begin to split at their margins. The staff run a small bypass hose into the old tank. This is not part of the official shutdown procedure, but the hose has its own hook and is replaced when it wears out.

In return, the creature does very little that could be called useful. It clears some soft debris. It holds moisture around the propagation beds. It leaves certain channels remarkably free of the greasy film found elsewhere. These benefits are too slight and irregular to account for the trouble taken with it.

It has simply been there through many shifts.

In late summer, small separate bodies appear in the shallow water beneath the alder seedlings. Each is about the size of a thumb, with folded sides and a translucent pad underneath. They remain attached to bricks or plastic stakes for weeks. Most disappear as the weather cools. A few can be found later in warmer recesses, feeding against the submerged edges of roots.

The workers avoid washing them into the canal. Beyond the service gate, the water is broader, colder, and exposed to disturbances that never reach the sheltered channels. Whether any survive there is unknown.

By late afternoon, the larger creature has usually opened its folds beneath the condensation pipes. Drops strike the moss on its back. Some run away into the old courtyard channel; others gather along a pale margin before being drawn inside.

Nearby, someone reads a gauge and writes the figure on a clipboard. A valve closes. The change in vibration reaches the nursery bed, and a row of small depressions tightens along the creature’s flank.

Then it relaxes. The pumps continue, and water resumes its passage through the folds.
