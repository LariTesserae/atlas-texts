Under the detached landing, where its lowest edge almost touches the wall, there is a seam that looks darker than the darkness around it.

A lamp makes it less conspicuous. The beam catches the rounded stone, the chalky deposits, a strand of creeper passing over the break. The seam becomes an ordinary shadow. Only if the lamp is held steady for some time does something in that shadow withdraw.

It does not withdraw all at once. A little section contracts, then another farther along. Between them, the darkness remains where it was.

What lives there has no common name in the maintenance records. On inspection photographs it is usually marked as damp material, occasionally as a deteriorating seal. A worker once scraped a sample into a screw-top container. By the time it reached the surface, it had become a few translucent flakes in a teaspoon of cloudy water.

In the hollow, alive, it is neither flaky nor quite translucent.

Its body is a flattened tube, about the length of a small finger and much narrower. The upper surface is grey-brown, carrying enough mineral dust to resemble the surrounding mortar. Beneath that is a soft amber underside, almost never exposed. Along each flank runs a row of short, blunt feet. These can enter cracks too fine for an insect’s leg, spread inside them, and hold.

The animal usually keeps most of its body in a joint. What shows is a narrow strip of back and, at one end, two folded membranes.

The membranes open in still air.

They are thin enough that condensation makes them sag. Their edges are fringed, and each fringe divides again into something finer. Extended, they give the animal a width several times greater than its body. They might be mistaken for a shred of fungus, except that their position changes. One tips toward a warm wall. The other turns toward the slow current passing beneath the landing.

Fine hairs cover their inner surfaces. Dust, spores, droplets, fragments of insect skin: all settle there. Every so often the membranes fold against one another, slowly, starting at the outer edges. The animal draws the collected material into a groove beneath its head.

It can spend most of a day doing this once.

There are several beneath the landing. There are others behind the fountain’s broken pedestal, inside the freight-door track above the lizards’ resting places, and under a paving block raised by sedge roots. They are not evenly distributed. Some apparently suitable cracks remain empty for years. In others, bodies lie close enough that their opened membranes overlap.

They do not share a continuous body. Each can leave.

On a wet night, one sometimes comes out onto the paving. It travels with its underside pressed flat, advancing in a succession of movements too small to follow. The front extends. The feet find purchase. The rest draws up behind it. On smooth stone it leaves a narrow, interrupted trace, visible only until the next film of condensation forms.

A crossing of one paving block may take an hour.

Its route is seldom direct. It follows the edge of moisture, turning away from a dry patch even when the wet way leads farther from shelter. At a channel it lowers its head and waits. If water is moving through the groove, the animal may remain there until the channel empties. A bead no larger than its head is enough to turn it over.

The lizards eat them when they can.

Most attempts end with a small piece torn from the exposed back. The blunt feet hold more firmly than the body holds together. Afterward the injured animal retreats deeper into its joint. For several days it keeps its feeding membranes closed. The wound fills with pale tissue, which gradually darkens as dust adheres.

Old animals have uneven outlines. Parts of their backs are thick and ridged; others are thin enough to show the slow movement of fluid beneath. One under the landing has a notch that never closes. When it opens its membranes, the damaged side cannot extend as far as the other.

It has occupied the same handspan of seam through at least six annual inspections.

The pressure reports govern much of their activity.

Before a report becomes audible, the feet tighten. The body shortens slightly. The membranes fold into pockets beside the head, and a clear secretion closes the pocket edges. By the time the shutters begin to chatter, the animal is a firmly seated strip in the masonry.

It responds to movement through the stone, not to the echo. A bird can batter its way along the galleries without disturbing those beneath the landing. A tool set down hard on the broken stair makes every exposed membrane disappear.

After a load exchange, they wait.

The lizards have often raised their heads before the first fringes emerge. Then, along a sheltered seam, small dull surfaces begin to unfold. In the dim handrail light there is no visible colour to them. During the brief opening of a maintenance fitting, they look faintly pink.

Their young are easier to see, though less often found.

They develop in clear capsules fixed deep inside the cracks. Each capsule contains a tightly bent body and a little cloudy fluid. When warm seepage reaches the nest, the capsule softens. The young animal works free, already equipped with feet, but carrying no dust and scarcely any pigment.

For a few days it resembles a moving drop.

Many are lost. Water takes them into the basin; beetles find them beneath the roots. Those that settle in a suitable joint darken quickly. Their first membranes are simple little paddles. The fringes come later.

During a transfer cycle, the adults remain fastened inside their shelters. The danger is greatest just before the drain stops taking air. Currents reach into recesses that are ordinarily still. Any animal feeding at that moment must close its membranes before they catch.

Sometimes one does not.

After the exhalation, a body may be found on a salt shelf or caught among the sedge stems. If its underside reaches damp stone, it can begin the long journey into shelter. On a dry balcony it contracts around its own moisture and becomes a small, hard curl.

There are curls in several window recesses. Some are old husks. A few soften when the western balconies drip.

No one has kept watch long enough to learn how long they can wait.

The animal beneath the landing with the damaged back is usually the first there to open after the ventilation resumes. One membrane comes out, then pauses. The other follows only when the air has settled.

Its fringes spread above the rounded edge of stone. A droplet gathers on one of them, enlarges, and is brought slowly inward. Behind it, farther along the seam, another animal begins to unfold.
