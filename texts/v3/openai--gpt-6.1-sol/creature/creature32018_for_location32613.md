The first inhabitants you notice are too thin to cast a shadow.

They live in the dark film around the shrinking water. At a distance, the film looks like wet clay. Up close, its surface is finer than clay, slightly gathered, with a dull sheen that changes as you move your head. Where a reed has fallen across it, the film climbs the underside of the stem. Where the water has withdrawn, it remains behind in small, overlapping plates.

A dry plate will lift at one edge if you breathe on it. Beneath it is a green so dark it is almost black.

These are not leaves. Each plate is a colony, spread through a transparent skin that holds water longer than the exposed mud. Its upper surface collects dust. Its lower surface loosens things from that dust and from the earth beneath it. When the basin fills, the plates soften and join. When it dries, they divide along boundaries too fine to see until the edges curl.

Some have lived through only one shower. Others have dried and opened many times. Those older ones are thick at the center, with pale margins added during successive floods. Lift one, and the earth underneath is darker and faintly pitted.

They are already changing what was laid down.

Elsewhere, the living surface is almost invisible. It forms a varnish over the channel bottoms, a clear tackiness between grains of silt. Water passing over it slows at the edges. Fine grit settles there. Over several rains, the varnish acquires a covering, then grows through it, fastening the new material to the old.

A channel that looks entirely bare may have this living skin along its whole length. When dry, it breaks into narrow polygons. The cracks in the skin do not quite match the cracks in the clay beneath it.

There are two patterns of drying, one laid over the other.

The grasses take root where either pattern offers an opening. They are sparse above ground but densely occupied below it. A tuft scarcely wider than a hand can have roots extending far along a horizontal seam, flattened between courses that are too compact to penetrate. The roots branch where a little space remains around a reed fiber. They turn downward at joints. Some emerge into a buried hollow and hang there, pale and hairless, until water reaches them.

The plants do not need much earth. They need access.

Around their roots live small white knots, soft when wet, hard as grains when dry. They draw substances out of the clay that the roots cannot readily use. In return, they receive what the plant makes in the light. A few knots become enclosed as the roots thicken. Others remain pressed against them, each in its own shallow stain.

A plant pulled from the ground loses much of this company. The exposed roots look surprisingly clean. Most of what sustained them stays in the seam.

There is another inhabitant there.

You would find it by opening a block along one of the builders’ horizontal joins. At first it would seem that the dampness had simply spread unevenly: a brown fan, darker at its center, thinning into many delicate lines. But the lines branch and meet again. Several end against individual grains. Others pass into the narrow impressions left by reed stems.

The whole fan is no thicker than a layer of settled dust.

It has no face or central body. Its living parts lie along the advancing edges and around the places where moisture persists. Between them, older threads have become empty walls. These remain useful. Water enters them, moves a short distance, and is taken up elsewhere. A portion that has ceased to live may still supply a portion that is growing.

In wet weather the fan spreads. In dry weather most of it closes.

What survives is not always its center. It may persist in six or seven small places along a seam: beneath a root, beside a salt grain, in the cup of a finger impression. These patches can remain separate for weeks. When water returns, they extend toward one another through their old passages.

Sometimes they reconnect. Sometimes one patch reaches the emptied portion of another and occupies it alone.

They feed on the reeds mixed into the blocks.

A broken fiber that projects from a bank may look sound on its sunlit side while its buried length has become soft and translucent. The branching inhabitants follow it inward. Around it they release a thin fluid that loosens the clay. Their growth leaves a narrow passage, reinforced by the remaining fiber until that, too, is consumed.

There are still too few of these passages to weaken an embankment greatly. The plain is young. Most of its reeds retain their shape.

But water has begun to find routes the builders did not make.

In some places the branching fans produce small bodies at the surface. They appear after rain, usually on the shaded face of a bank: pale beads, blunt-ended tubes, little cups whose rims are the same orange as the wet earth. They last a morning or several days. Nothing comes to visit them.

As they dry, their walls pull tight. A cup turns inward; a tube splits lengthwise. Fine living dust is exposed to the wind.

Much of it lands on ground that will not become wet again in time. Some falls into basins already occupied by a different kind of fan. Some lodges in the unfinished crease where a block was pressed against another.

There it waits.

Not everything in the water is rooted or spread flat.

When a basin holds water long enough, clear bodies appear beneath the floating husks. They are small enough that several could rest on a thumbnail, but they grow differently from the films. Each is enclosed, with a slightly thickened rim and a loose, folded interior. They turn slowly in the shallow currents.

At dawn they gather near the surface. As the water warms, they sink.

They have no legs. Their movement comes from minute hairs along the rim, too fine to see without magnification. The hairs draw water across the folded interior. Particles adhere there: fragments of living film, softened plant matter, the smaller inhabitants suspended in every cloudy thread of runoff.

In clear water the bodies are almost impossible to distinguish. In red water you see them as brief absences, little moving lenses through which the bottom looks less blurred.

When the basin begins to fail, they change. The rim contracts. The interior folds more tightly. A coat develops, taking up clay until each body resembles an ordinary grain on the bed.

A handful of dry basin earth contains many of them.

Some will open with the next rain. Others require several wettings. Their coats differ, so a brief shower cannot wake them all at once. The first to open may feed and grow, or may perish before the water has crossed the basin. The others remain among the silt.

Along the oldest pools, these grains accumulate below the salt line. A flood lifts them in great numbers and carries them down the channels. Many pass the exposed handprints without touching them. A few settle in the depressions.

For a day, perhaps, each impression holds a small life.

The open plain supports less visible activity. Beneath low mats of vegetation, dry husks are softened from below. Around swollen shrub stems, fallen fragments begin to decay only during the brief dampness before sunrise. The work stops when the sun reaches them and resumes on another morning. A dead stem may stand for years while its buried base is slowly hollowed out.

There are no scavengers arriving from a distance. What consumes a fallen plant was usually beside it already.

A bank collapse carries these inhabitants into new arrangements. Surface films are buried. Pale seam-dwellers are exposed and die in the heat. Enclosed grains spill from a former pool bed into dust that has never held standing water. Roots torn loose lie across the fresh folds of clay.

The next flood sorts them again.

You could sit beside a newly formed basin through an entire afternoon and see nothing move except water and one floating leaf. Yet below that leaf, clear bodies would be opening. Along the submerged bank, closed threads would soften. A green-black plate, buried upside down by the collapse, would begin growing around its own edge toward the light.

At evening, the leaf catches on a projecting reed fiber.

By morning its underside is slippery. A fine brown line extends from the reed onto the stem. Beneath it, in the water held between leaf and clay, several clear bodies turn.

The leaf will not travel farther when the channel fills again. By then it will be fastened to the bank, and something will be growing through it.
