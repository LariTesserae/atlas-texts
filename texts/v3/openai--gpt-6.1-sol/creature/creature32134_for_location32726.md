The beetle beneath the lintel has a body the color of the inside of a shell. Along its wing cases, two rows of shallow punctures hold the light. Its head is darker than the rest of it, not quite black except at the working edges of the mouth. One antenna lies against the underside of a shell fragment. The other extends into the opening.

It is not entering. It is not keeping watch. Those are things its posture resembles from a distance.

Close up, the beetle seems occupied with the exact fit of its head beneath the lintel. There is a little space above it, sufficient for the curve of the shell and the curve of the skull to remain separate. Its front feet rest on opposite sides of the notch. One claw touches white earth; the other touches a grain with a transparent center. The difference matters to the animal. It has the appearance of knowing both surfaces completely.

Something like a voice comes from it, although its mouthparts are still. Within the larger rasp, the beetle contributes a hard, close ticking. Each tick has the same small flaw, a roughness at the end. You could recognize it again among hundreds.

The snails are less easy to distinguish until you have been beside them for a while.

Their shells are so thin that the bodies inside give them most of their color. One has a faint yellow organ folded beneath the spiral. Another carries a white speck in its lowest whorl, enclosed there as securely as anything in stone. A third has a shell slightly too broad for its body, leaving an empty crescent around the soft foot. Light occupies that crescent. It does not make the snail look incomplete.

Their feelers are extended to different lengths. None has eyes. The tips have the smooth, rounded finish of parts made for touching, and each tip rests against something: mineral, root, another shell. One touches the side of a mite. The mite has not drawn away. There is no sign that either creature arrived at this contact first.

The snails’ sound has a thickness the air does not have. It is almost possible to hear the pale folds inside them.

Among the smaller animals, individuality becomes more exact rather than less. There is a springtail whose abdomen bends a little to the left. There is a mite with one leg folded under another, the two joints making a narrow diamond. There is a larva lying on its side with a patch of ceiling reflected along its wet flank. The reflection stays clear, though the flank is not flat.

They possess the ordinary equipment of living things. Hooks, hairs, pores, soft seams where a body can bend. Their guts contain minute dark portions of food. Under some of the shells, eggs can be made out, each enclosed in its own faint boundary. These are not empty husks arranged to resemble life. There is too much particular flesh.

Nor do they seem to be enduring their stillness. The exposed belly of the centipede has no tension in it. Its many feet rest easily on their ledges. Where a ledge is narrow, the foot has turned sideways; where the stone is rough, the claws are spread. The animal has a slight twist through its middle, accommodating the bend in the wall without pressing any of the creatures beneath it.

It is the largest inhabitant, but its size has given it no solitude. Mites occupy the intervals between its legs. A tiny snail rests beside a breathing slit. Under the last few segments, a translucent creature with branching hairs is visible through the gaps. The centipede’s body makes a roof for it, though there is no weather here.

The built things reveal other distinctions.

Some grains have been chosen for their flatness. Some have been chosen despite lacking it. In the little enclosing wall, an awkward, bulbous piece is held between two narrow ones, its bulge facing inward. The round animal within rests against that bulge. Its body fits there better than it would against a straight wall.

The bent hair has a flattened end. At the other end it forks into two fibers too fine to cast a shadow, even if shadows were possible. The groove beneath it follows the bend with small inaccuracies. Whoever made the groove did not simply impress the hair into soft ground. The workmanship has its own shape.

Perhaps the inhabitants built these arrangements. Perhaps only some of them did. Nothing about the beetle’s forelegs makes them look especially suited to lifting shell fragments, and yet the underside of the lintel bears three pale scratches at the height of its claws.

There is no larger creature concealed among them. No soft central body from which the others issue, no face made accidentally by the crowd. Looking for one makes it harder to see what is there.

At the base of the living root, six animals occupy six differently shaped spaces. One rests lengthwise against the stem. One has put its blunt head beneath a leaf. Another, almost spherical, touches the leaf only with the curved hairs on its back. Each leaf remains firm in its clearance. The root is alive down to its finest visible branch, and the animals beside it are alive in their separate ways.

The moss is inhabited even between its smallest leaves. A white creature no thicker than a thread lies along one green blade, ending just before the point. It has a row of rudimentary legs, scarcely more than scallops in its underside. From that part of the chamber comes a fine, tearing sound, so delicate it would be lost anywhere else.

Here it can be heard without effort. So can the beetle’s flawed tick, the snails’ heavy hum, the innumerable dry sounds of the crowded seams. None displaces another.

The bare shelf belongs to them too. Its rim touches the back of an animal in the uppermost layer, but the animal does not overlap it. A feeler extends beside the shelf rather than across it. The clear wet stain reaches its edge and stops. Nothing has been excluded roughly. There are no scrape marks, no compressed bodies along the boundary.

You could put a finger there only by imagining a different place.

What lives here has already taken the shape of this one. The broad-backed creatures occupy the broad hollows; the thin ones lie in channels that remain thin; soft feet rest on stone still joined to the substance it came from. An unfinished facet meets a finished shell. Beside them, a drop retains its sharp lower point.

The inhabitants are not waiting for any of these things to change. Their bodies bear all the fine provisions for movement, feeding, growth. Their sounds fill the chamber. The beetle keeps the small roughness in its ticking, and its antenna remains beneath the shell, with room enough above it.
