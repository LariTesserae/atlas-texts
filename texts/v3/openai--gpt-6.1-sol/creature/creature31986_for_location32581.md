The people who live here can carry a full bowl up a stair without looking at the stair. They look instead at the landing, to see whether someone is coming down, whether the hens have escaped, whether a basket has been left where it ought not to be. Their feet know the scooped centers of the steps. On an unfamiliar staircase they sometimes stumble.

There are several households, though counting them is difficult. A woman sleeps with her children in one room and cooks with her sister in another. An old man has a door of his own, but most of what he owns is on a shelf outside it, and he eats wherever a place is made for him. Two young men share a bed at different hours because one tends the kiln at night and the other carries finished ware to the market before morning. They quarrel about the bedding without meeting for days.

Doors establish certain rights. So do hooks, portions of ledges, and marks on the sides of grain bins. A particular stretch of railing belongs to the woman who dries noodles there, except on washing days, when it belongs to three families. Everyone knows this. Visitors discover it by putting something down.

The inhabitants are not all potters. Clay nevertheless gets into nearly everything they do. The grain grinder has a pale rim around each fingernail. A boy who delivers messages aboveground leaves dusty handprints on the doors he knocks at. The woman who makes wicker cages wets her cane with water that has sometimes stood in a bowl used for settling clay; when it dries, a faint powder comes off on her skirt.

There are those who prepare clay, those who shape it, those who can judge whether a firing is going badly from the smell at the kiln door. Others sort grain, mend baskets, keep rabbits, cook for people whose hands are occupied, or take loads through the passages to the square. A few leave each morning for work elsewhere. They return carrying the outside weather on their clothes: a coldness in a sleeve, the smell of wet dust, sunlight still warm on the back of a neck.

The children sniff at these arrivals when there has been something unusual outside. Smoke from a distant fire. Crushed leaves after a procession. Once, the smell of river mud.

Most children here learn the hollow before they learn its names. They know the way to a particular adult by the obstacles that must be passed: under the cage landing, around the jar with the lizard, up the stair where the third tread rocks. Asked where someone lives, a small child may demonstrate a sideways movement of the shoulders rather than answer.

They learn which surfaces can bear them. A drying rack may look like a bridge and must never be used as one. A pipe will hold a hand but not a whole body. Some lids are strong enough to stand on; others conceal deep water. This knowledge is taught with sharp voices, fetched children, and the occasional slap to a reaching hand.

There are games that require almost no clear space. Seeds are flicked toward the dips in the court tiles. Broken handles become animals with two curved legs. A strip of cloth is passed through a crack in a wall and retrieved from another opening, for reasons that are obvious to the participants and obscure to everyone else. The smallest children are forever trying to recover things from places their hands can enter but not withdraw from while closed.

Older children are sent after dropped tools. They resent being useful in this way, especially when summoned during a game, but they know which gaps lead to an accessible shelf and which lead down into the packed sherds. A narrow wrist is valuable here.

One girl has begun keeping an inventory of the blue pieces embedded in the drains and walls. She scratches their positions on the back of a discarded tile. She has found eleven, though her brother insists that one is green and another is only a reflection. No adult can tell her whether the pieces came from the same vessel. An old woman remembers a large blue bowl, but not its breaking.

The oldest residents possess other kinds of incomplete knowledge. They can remember when a stair ended at a room that no longer exists, or when the washing stone was on the opposite side of the court. They disagree about the sequence of alterations. Sometimes a repair settles an argument: behind a patched wall appears the soot-marked outline of a former hearth, exactly where one person said it would be.

Age makes the hollow harder to cross. The woman who once climbed the chimney ladder to clear birds’ nests now stays mostly on the lower level. Work comes to her in bowls and small bundles. She sorts dried beans quickly, rejecting stones by touch. She can still tell who is passing behind her from the pause they make before stepping across the channel.

She dislikes being helped before she has asked. This causes delays at the narrow stair. Someone waits above with a basket while she gets both feet onto a tread, rests, and reaches for the next. Conversation continues around the obstruction. Nobody pretends not to be waiting, and she occasionally tells them to stop sighing.

The dogs have learned a similar economy of movement, with less consideration for others. They rise for loads that might strike them, but not for empty-handed people. One old dog has chosen the warm tile beside the grinding treadle and sleeps through its motion. When the grinder stops, he opens his eyes.

The cats distinguish between the kiln workers. One man lets them into the fuel room. Another drives them out because they disturb the neatly stacked charcoal. They approach the door differently depending on whose feet they see beneath it. In cold weather, several cats tolerate one another along the same warm seam, lying close without quite touching.

There are animals here that belong to someone, animals that are fed by several people, and animals whose ownership is discussed only after they do damage. The hens have owners. The pigeons are claimed when there are eggs to take. The little brown birds belong to nobody, though a child has named the one with a white fleck on its wing and insists that it returns every day.

The rats know the parts of the place that people cannot use. They travel through old drains and gaps between courses of rubble. Occasionally a repair closes one route, and for several nights their scratching comes from somewhere new. The inhabitants hear them with irritation rather than surprise. Food is moved. A trap is set. Someone complains that someone else has left husks beneath a cupboard.

The toads receive more indulgence. One is larger and sits with its chin on the edge of the bowl. The other is usually hidden beneath the washing stone. People pouring out dirty water check where they are first. A child once moved the smaller toad to a cup of her own and was made to return it, with a lengthy explanation that it had work to do.

The moss is tended by a woman who is impatient with most things requiring care. She forgets where she has left needles, burns food while arguing, and lets the bitter herbs flower when they ought to be cut back. But she notices when the cloth beneath the pipe is failing. She prepares a new strip, loosens the old one, and transfers the cushion with both hands while someone holds a lamp.

She does not encourage an audience. The snails must be lifted separately, and people asking questions make her lose track of them.

No one here is without some judgment about everyone else. A neighbor uses too much water. A young man carries baskets carelessly and strikes corners. Someone is keeping more rabbits than the cool compartments can accommodate. A household has moved its cooking shelf outward by half a hand’s breadth, narrowing a passage.

These complaints may continue for years. Then a tank lid cracks or a drain blocks during rain, and the same people work together, passing tools along the route without discussion. Afterwards they resume the argument about the shelf.

They live with considerable skill, but not without mistakes. Bowls are dropped. Fires smoke. Grain goes sour in a jar that seemed dry. Someone patches a leak so successfully that the moss begins to brown, and the repair must be loosened a little. The place requires attention at inconvenient times, when people are tired or angry or have just sat down to eat.

In the evening, voices from above grow thinner. Below, the last grain is tipped from a tray and the grinding stone slows. People wash clay from their wrists, leaving pale streaks in the channels. A child is sent to fetch a cup and returns with news instead. Someone calls for the cup again.

At one threshold a woman sits with a sleeping baby across her knees, still working a knot free from a length of cord. Across the court, the old man checks the lid of a grain jar, pressing it at three points. The cats begin to gather near the kiln room. Behind the washing stone, a toad shifts into the open.

The woman with the moss bends to examine the new cloth. A snail has reached its lower edge. She puts out one finger, waits for it to draw in, and moves it back onto the green.
