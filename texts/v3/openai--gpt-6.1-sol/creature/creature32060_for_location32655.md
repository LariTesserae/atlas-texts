The first one you notice is holding the ground together.

It lies across a seam in the rock, so flat that its body looks like a stain left by an old leaf. One edge is tucked beneath a root; the other has entered the fissure. Between them, its skin is stretched almost transparent. You can see dark filaments inside it, drawn taut across the widening gap.

When you approach, it loosens one filament at a time.

The seam opens. The animal draws itself into the root felt, taking its filaments with it, and a little shower of warm mineral grains falls where it has been.

There are hundreds of them on that shelf.

Most are no larger than your hand. Some have spread across several yards of stone. Their upper surfaces are dull green, densely stippled with pores; their undersides are black and adhere wherever they touch. They have mouths, but not in a permanent place. A slit opens beside whatever needs eating: a crust of mineral salts, the moist edge of a damaged root, another animal too slow to withdraw.

Their eyes are the clear beads along their margins. Each bead sees a narrow portion of the world. Together they watch the ground on every side.

They do not watch the sky unless the sky is about to become the ground.

Then the beads turn in their sockets, and the animal begins to gather itself.

***

Gathering takes time.

The broad animals cannot simply run. Their tissues have to fold in a particular order, pores closing before a surface is tucked away, feeding slits sealing, the internal filaments brought into alignment. A large one contracts into a pleated length that could be mistaken for a fallen branch. It moves by passing a thickening down its body. Where the thickening reaches the front, a new stretch of underside fastens to the terrain; behind it, the old attachment peels free.

During these journeys, smaller animals cling inside the folds.

They are not all its young. Some drink from its pores. Some clean the places it cannot close. One little black species carries grains of magnetic stone and presses them into soft pits along the traveler’s back. The grains are eventually absorbed. What the little animals receive in exchange is difficult to see, though they leave plump and return thin.

A moving adult has a faint smell of wet iron.

When several meet, they lie edge to edge. For a while their eyes disappear into the contact line. Dark ripples pass through their bodies. Then they separate, sometimes taking different routes from the ones they arrived on.

Occasionally one departs with a scar that previously belonged to another.

***

The animals of the mineral herds are taller, although height here is a temporary arrangement.

Each has six limbs, with broad joints that can bend in either direction. The feet end in soft, divided pads. They place these carefully on fresh stone, testing before committing their weight. Their heads hang low between the forelimbs, and their mouths are lined with replaceable plates of pale, flexible material. A crystal pressed between those plates disappears with a faint crunch.

The polished growths above their shoulders are hollow.

Inside each is a branching cavity, warm at the base and cool at the tips. Dust settles in these cavities. Mineral vapor condenses there. Over the animal’s lifetime, the walls become layered with metals taken from a succession of feeding grounds.

Young animals carry only two or three short growths. Old ones bear thickets of them, some bent back along the spine, some split into delicate forks. A growth broken in a fall may continue shining at its fracture for days.

The herds gather around their oldest members.

They stand close without touching. Their shoulder growths incline. The luminous threads appear first between neighboring animals, then span the small spaces between groups. Along a ridge, you may see separate herds become linked into one uneven, flickering line.

Very young animals sleep beneath it.

The adults seem hardly aware of them, but whenever a sleeper shifts out from under the line, one of the surrounding bodies moves to restore the shelter.

During these gatherings the animals cease digesting. Their bellies become still. Something passes through the herd that leaves the old animals lighter and the young ones unsteady on their feet.

Afterward, some elders go alone into the forest.

They find trees with exposed inward chambers and stand against the wet wood. The trees close slowly around their shoulder growths. For several days the animal remains visible below the enclosure, feeding from low leaves, turning its head when something passes.

Then there is only a set of broad feet at the base of the trunk.

Sometimes those feet are still warm.

Years later, flowers growing high in that tree open whenever a distant herd begins to shine.

***

The forests contain hunters, but a hunt rarely looks like pursuit.

One predator occupies the underside of a canopy. Its body is divided into overlapping lobes, each the size of a large frond, connected by narrow bands of muscle. The lobes can move independently. Taken together, they form a dark ceiling over several hundred feet of undergrowth.

It feeds by making a sheltered place.

The ground beneath it is cooler. Its skin condenses a little moisture and releases it in beads. Grazers come there during the hot intervals, flattening themselves among the roots. Small creatures lick droplets from the low-hanging lobes.

For days, nothing happens.

Then one portion of the ceiling lowers. A fold closes around a sleeping animal. The other inhabitants scarcely move. The predator raises that portion of itself again, and by the following evening the shelter is cool and damp as before.

It does not take animals that are carrying young. These give off a bitter secretion that leaves pale marks on its skin.

Nor does it take the jointed creatures that emerge from the mountains. They walk straight through its folds, tapping with their forelimbs as if looking for a particular surface. Where they tap too insistently, the predator lifts out of their way.

The jointed creatures are almost blind.

Their heads are small, smooth knobs. Their limbs are longer than their bodies and end in arrays of fine, hard fingers. Beneath each finger is a dark cavity that resonates when pressed to stone.

At the beginning of a migration they gather in hollows and touch the walls.

Different individuals test different heights. A tremor runs through the group. One starts away, then another, until the hollow is empty and a pale column is flowing through the forest.

They travel along routes that do not remain available to anything following at a distance.

The last creature in a column pauses repeatedly. It braces itself against both sides of a cleft, or presses its whole body to a level patch of rock, or extends a limb over an opening. Those behind it can still pass. Once it lifts away, the route may become a cliff, or a long slope, or merely farther away than it was.

The rear guard is always an older animal.

Some never arrive.

***

There are inhabitants that remember places which have not yet formed.

They live beside the dark pools.

At first they seem to be accumulations of floating plants: little rafts with pale, fibrous undersides. But their fronds withdraw when disturbed, revealing a soft oval body and four narrow limbs folded tightly against it.

They spend most of their lives face down on the water.

A groove runs the length of the underside, filled with closely set eyes. These remain immersed. Whatever appears in the pool is reflected directly into them.

When the reflections begin to change, the animals become restless. They slide across the surface, stopping at particular points, dipping one side of their bodies, turning back. Sometimes they crawl ashore and press themselves into bare rock.

They may remain there for weeks.

Eventually a crack opens beneath them. Or a shoot emerges. Or the slope tilts until the rock becomes the bottom of a new hollow, and water gathers around their bodies again.

Others wait in places where nothing happens.

These are eaten, or dry into fibrous shells. Their shells persist in the shelter of the stone. Small grazers gnaw them cautiously, and sometimes become agitated afterward, staring at a tree or walking repeatedly around an unremarkable patch of ground.

Not every future is nourishing.

***

Farther inland, where the country is youngest, the animals are smaller and less distinct.

Some resemble folded scraps of dark skin. Some look like mineral growths until they open a row of feet. Many have no visible eyes. They wait beside new fractures with spores packed into grooves along their bodies.

When the warmth reaches a particular intensity, they enter.

Their young develop inside the fissures. They feed on the earliest roots, taking only their outer layers, and excrete a glossy lining that prevents the hot rock from drying them. As the roots branch, the animals follow. A newly green mountain contains an immense, delicate population moving just ahead of its forest.

In the first seasons, few survive.

A fissure closes. A chamber overheats. A direction of weight changes before a body has acquired the strength to cling. The dead are incorporated into roots or drawn down into deeper seams.

The survivors grow slowly. They acquire firmer backs, more elaborate mouths, organs that respond to the vibration of the buried cylinders. Some begin to resemble the animals of the older country.

Others emerge differently.

A new ridge may produce a grazer whose shoulder growths lie inside its body. A forest may shelter broad animals with no eyes at all, their margins fringed instead with filaments that turn toward distant movement. A column of jointed migrants may include one creature with an extra pair of limbs, carrying its neighbors over a stretch none of them can cross alone.

The established inhabitants do not always accept these newcomers.

They taste them. They withdraw from them. Sometimes they make room.

***

There is no single season when the young appear.

A folded traveler can carry offspring through several rearrangements of the land before releasing them. A herd animal may produce one calf during a long dim interval, or three after feeding on a seam that has opened beneath a turning mass. The canopy hunters divide when their territories become too large to remain cool; the connecting muscles thin, the lobes pull apart, and two shelters begin attracting different animals.

What persists is the habit of waiting beside one another.

Even solitary creatures seek company before the ground changes. They come out of their hiding places and settle close enough to touch. A small animal may climb onto the back of a predator that would eat it at any other time. The predator allows this. Both turn their sensory edges toward the same warm crack.

For a while, their bodies are still.

Then one moves.

The movement passes through the gathering in a sequence too quick to follow: pads loosening, membranes unfolding, mouths closing over the last scraps of food. Creatures hidden throughout the grove rise from surfaces you had taken for bark or stone.

The broad animal beside your foot lifts its edge.

Underneath are six smaller bodies, moist and black, and a pale jointed creature holding a grain of mineral between its forelimbs. They have been waiting there together.

The animal folds around them. Its eyes turn toward a dark interval between two trees, where the air smells cooler and the vibration comes at a different angle.

It begins to move.

Around you the forest is still upright. The slope has not changed. Nothing visible explains the departure.

But the little sheltered company is already going, and ahead of it the inhabitants of the next grove are gathering themselves, leaving their feeding marks unfinished on the warm stone.
