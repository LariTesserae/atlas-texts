The animal is easiest to find when a basin has just begun to warm.

There will be a place where the frost has loosened from the plants but has not yet melted: a shallow blister of white, raised slightly above the surrounding leaves. Watch that place long enough and the frost separates into fine lines. The lines widen. Beneath them is dark fur.

It has been lying there throughout the cold interval.

Its back is broad and almost flat, with a shallow depression along the spine where fallen leaves collect. Some of those leaves have put down roots. The animal carries a small, uneven garden: cushion plants near the shoulders, a fringe of translucent stalks above the hips, one or two ribbed fans growing in the old fur. When it moves, their roots pull free of the ground beneath it. They remain attached to the animal.

Getting up takes much of a night.

First it withdraws the thin parts of itself from the vegetation. These are difficult to distinguish from roots: dark cords, some no thicker than fungal threads, which spread outward from beneath its belly. They come back slowly, passing between leaves without breaking them. Their pale ends gather against the body and disappear into folds of skin.

Then the feet begin to work.

There are six, arranged close beneath it. Each foot opens into a soft, many-lobed pad before taking weight. The pads settle around stems rather than on top of them. On bare stone they spread farther, finding irregularities too small to see. A standing animal may be no taller than a person’s knee, although its body occupies the ground that person would need to lie down.

The head emerges last.

It is narrow, tucked far under the front edge of the body, and covered in shorter fur. Two black eyes face downward and slightly outward. Between them hangs a loose fold of skin that touches whatever the animal approaches. The mouth is underneath. When feeding, it works through the ground cover with a patient, almost surgical motion, taking the pale undersides of leaves, soft fungal growth, and the tender ends of succulent stems. It leaves the upper layer largely intact.

Behind it, the vegetation rises again. A passage is evident for a little while as a difference in the frost.

These animals travel alone, but not independently.

Before choosing a direction, one lowers its body until its belly rests against the stone beneath the plants. Along the underside are several bare patches, thin-skinned and faintly silver. They pick up the uneven turning below. A distant colony’s vibration reaches them through beds of metallic rock; nearer vibrations arrive through the conductive roots.

The animal lies still while its feet make small adjustments.

A basin may look uniformly dark from above, yet it is full of differences the animal can feel. One patch is warming. Another has stopped. A ring of roots is carrying a weak current toward the far side of a ridge. Somewhere a turning surface has begun to drag against its housing.

It follows these differences, sometimes toward food, sometimes toward a place that will not become warm for several nights.

This accounts for the animals found resting on bare, frozen shelves while lush vegetation lies within easy reach. It accounts, too, for the worn hollows beneath certain stones. Generations have waited in the same places.

Their cords are not roots, although roots grow beside them.

Each cord ends in a small, flattened organ sensitive to pressure, temperature, and current. Beneath a resting animal they spread through the living carpet, touching damp mineral seams and the buried wood of creeping trees. The longest may extend several body lengths away. In a good resting place, an animal can feel a grazer passing before the grazer comes into sight.

It can also feel another of its kind.

When two settle near each other, their cords sometimes meet. The ends remain together for hours. There is little visible response: a tightening of the skin along one flank, a slow redistribution of weight. Afterward, one animal may leave by a route it had shown no interest in before.

They do not readily share warmth. A newcomer approaching an occupied colony is usually met by a broad pad laid flat across its path. The gesture is slow enough to ignore and large enough to understand. But during the first uncertain nights of a basin’s waking, several may lie close, their cords crossing and uncrossing under the leaves.

The young are very different.

They are pale, narrow things that can fit beneath a single fan. Their fur has not yet darkened, and they have no plants on their backs. They move quickly through the low tunnels, taking advantage of warmth while it is available. Blue specks sometimes travel with them: small animals feeding on the moist secretions of their new skin.

A young one has only four working feet. The rear pair develops later, during its first long dormancy.

Until then it follows the warmth of an adult’s body. It shelters beneath the overhanging flank, where the fur is soft and the air remains damp. Several young may crowd there, though only one or two are the adult’s own. If the adult rises, they emerge and scatter into the vegetation. As it moves away, some keep pace underneath it, using the shelter of its body to cross exposed stone.

An adult slows for them.

This is most noticeable at narrow breaks in the carpet. Its front feet test the rock, then wait. Beneath its chest the pale young collect in a cramped knot. Only when they have found a protected line does the body move forward.

With age, the animal becomes harder to distinguish from a resting place.

Its garden thickens. The fur beneath the plants loosens into a fibrous mat, while the skin below grows tougher. Old animals may carry several kinds of moss that occur nowhere else in the basin. Small grazers climb onto them to feed. White frost-scrapers hang beneath the fans on their backs. Predators wait at the edge of a shoulder as they would beside a stone.

The animal tolerates most of this. Occasionally it contracts one side of its body, lifting the garden just enough to send an unwelcome inhabitant down into the surrounding leaves.

Very old ones cease making long journeys.

They settle beside a turning colony and remain through several warmings and stillnesses. Their cords grow thicker. Plants root across their feet. The mouth continues to feed in a little clearing beneath the head, but the rest of the body becomes a low island of sheltered life.

Such an animal can still leave. Some do, after years in one place. Its departure exposes a pale bed of compressed leaves, six deep hollows, and fine channels radiating through the soil. For a season afterward, smaller creatures continue to approach the empty bed and pause at its edge.

At the black pools, all ages behave with particular care.

They stop beyond the margin and flatten themselves. The head extends while the feet remain gathered beneath the body. Often a pale shape is already visible in the water.

The animal waits.

Its loose facial fold moves once across the plants. The reflected shape holds still. Then the animal advances, bringing its mouth to the place its reflection has occupied.

The young sometimes hurry. An adult nearby will put a foot over the approaching youngster, gently pinning it into the moss until the water has settled. There is no splash, no alarm. After a while the foot lifts.

Whatever matters at the pools is learned early.

They have no call that carries through the open air. Up close, a feeding animal makes a faint wet rasp. A young one searching beneath an adult’s flank produces a series of minute clicks, felt more easily through a fingertip laid on the ground than heard.

Their longest utterances happen while they are resting.

Small muscles along the belly contract in sequence, pressing the silver patches against stone. The resulting tremor enters the basin’s other vibrations. A second animal may answer from beyond a ridge. Sometimes several answer, so far apart that no one standing above them would know there was anything to hear.

Then the exchange stops. One broad back begins to lift out of the plants. Elsewhere another settles lower, extending its cords into the cold.

By the time the colony’s leaves turn, they have already changed their paths.
