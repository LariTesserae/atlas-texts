The people who live here have broad, patient hands and the habit of looking down before they look ahead.

This is not timidity. They can cross a plank above a deep shaft while carrying a sleeping child, and they will put an arm into a crack that has swallowed a whole bucket. But they want to see where the water is. Even in conversation, their eyes stray toward a threshold or a darkening patch of mortar. A visitor may think they are inattentive. In fact, they have noticed that the visitor’s left boot is about to freeze to the floor.

Their boots have soles in several pieces. A single stiff sole can leave you attached to two stones that no longer agree about what a wet foot should do. The pieces are bound with cord and can be cut apart. Children learn to cut the cord before they learn to mend it.

People dress in layers that are easy to separate: short coats, wrapped leggings, sleeves tied on at the shoulder. Long hems are impractical. Loose pockets are worse. Anything that hangs can dip into a current, and a current may presently be going somewhere a person cannot follow.

Nevertheless, they like finery. They embroider the undersides of cuffs and carve the backs of their visors. A woman taking off her visor indoors may reveal six tiny fish cut into the wood, each with a different open mouth. The outer face will be plain, scratched, repeatedly repaired. The carving against her forehead will have survived nearly untouched for forty years.

There are beautiful things here that are rarely seen by anyone except their owners.

***

The white birds are not all one kind.

Some have feet like slender bundles of wire and make their nests in the wet channels. Some have a small folded pouch beneath the throat. Some are nearly wingless, though their useless wings still brush rapidly when they are excited. From a distance they make one pale population. Up close, they dislike one another intensely.

The narrow-footed birds arrive first when a ledge is about to become wet. They stamp along it, testing the stone. The pouch-throated birds leave a ledge shortly before it freezes. People distinguish these movements from ordinary squabbling, but not perfectly. A whole row of shopkeepers once lifted their shelves because two birds were fighting over a strip of intestine.

No one was much embarrassed. Lifting a shelf unnecessarily is cheap.

Birds enter houses as a matter of course. There is usually a perch above the place where boots are removed. A bird that chooses to sit there gets the insects shaken from the boot seams; in return, its departure is worth noticing.

Some households speak fondly of their threshold bird. Others regard it as a disagreeable fixture. It may be the same bird.

They live a long time. One has a notch in its beak made by a woman who is now old enough to have trouble remembering the accident. She says it stole her wedding cord. Her husband says she tried to shut a door on it. Their granddaughter has heard both accounts and leaves the bird alone.

***

The roof grazers are less agile than they appear.

Their hooves spread around the edges of stones, but they cannot turn easily on a narrow plank. When two meet, they stand with their heads lowered until someone comes to settle the matter. Backing one of them up requires patience, a shoulder pressed against its chest, and often a basket of food visible at the far end.

A grazer can recognize the footfall of the person who feeds it through a roof.

It can also recognize the person who intends to clip it.

Their coats come away in dense sheets, warm beneath and crusted with old snow above. These are washed without being fully soaked, stretched over frames, and teased apart. The finest fibre grows behind the ears. It goes into infant caps and the little pads worn beneath wooden visors.

The animals are named for their failings: Backs-Too-Late, Eats-the-Knot, Won’t-Step, Other-Won’t-Step. These names are spoken with great affection. A household that loses a grazer may keep its empty basket hanging for a season, partly from grief and partly because the other animals become confused when the familiar baskets disappear.

They do not like changes that happen indoors.

***

The small furred creatures in the drains have almost no visible ears. Their whiskers are thick and flattened, and they press them to stone as they travel. At a junction they pause with their faces against the wall, as though listening through their noses.

They eat bird eggs, beetles, scraps of fat, and the soft deposits that collect where certain waters meet. They are a nuisance in kitchens and indispensable in narrow channels. A blocked drain often clears after one has passed through it.

People make little openings for them in repaired masonry. This is not kindness alone. A drain without animals must be cleaned by a person.

The creatures sometimes move their young through occupied rooms. They come in single file, each adult carrying something pale and curled. The household stops what it is doing. Chairs are lifted. A lamp is turned toward the wall. Someone stands by the door to prevent a bird from coming in.

Once the procession has passed, everyone examines the bread.

***

The translucent creatures the child collects are called panes.

Each has a flat body with a faint thickening down the middle, several soft feet underneath, and two dark points that become visible only after it has eaten. They are difficult to see in water. In a wooden tray they look like small, imperfect pieces of glass.

They do not like to be carried in a deep vessel. They pile against one another, turn cloudy, and may not become clear again. Eleven fit comfortably in the child’s tray. Twelve would probably fit, but her grandmother has told her eleven, and she is at the age when a number given by her grandmother is more trustworthy than an appearance.

At home she puts them into the channel that crosses the kitchen.

They settle along its lip and begin feeding on a nearly invisible film. By evening, where they have passed, the stone is clean and the water holds together in a narrow rounded strand. Her grandmother can take away a supporting wedge and mend the channel beneath it without spilling water into the flour chest.

Later the panes must be returned to the basin. If kept too long, they fasten themselves down. People have broken good stones trying to remove them.

The child likes watching their meals darken the two points inside them. She has favourites, although her grandmother doubts she can tell them apart.

She can tell at least three apart.

***

There are people whose work is to remember arrangements.

They remember that a stream once passed through a particular wall in late summer, that the house above it was unoccupied then, that the birds moved first to the low parapet rather than the high one. They keep records on thin boards, marking channels with grooves and laying cords across them. No board describes the whole town. There would be too many crossings, and too much of the record would already be obsolete.

One of these people is a man with only two fingers on his right hand. He works mostly with his left and holds the boards steady against his knees. Children bring him observations. Most are useless. He listens to all of them.

A girl says the fish under the square have begun facing the same way.

A boy says a stone is warm.

The man asks which fish, which stone, what time, whether anyone else touched it. He does not praise them for being observant until they have answered.

He has a room full of boards, some left by his predecessor, some much older. On one, a channel runs through the site of his own room. He takes this into account when deciding where to keep the oldest boards.

***

The people are quiet outdoors, but they are not solemn.

Their jokes are brief because an elaborate joke is likely to be interrupted by something needing to be moved. They imitate the grazers exceptionally well. They have a rude gesture performed entirely beneath the brim of a visor, visible only to someone standing close.

Children play at making a route impossible. They lay sticks across a dry courtyard, place stones where a foot ought to go, and challenge one another to cross carrying a full cup. Older children may secretly alter the course while the crossing is in progress.

Adults object when this is done with real spillways.

Indoors, where everyone is near enough to hear, people argue and sing. Songs tend to have short lines. Some verses are held until a late-arriving person has taken off their boots and joined in. Nobody finds the pause awkward.

The returning noises in the walls are less welcome.

Most are meaningless: a knock, a dragging sound, something like a cough. Occasionally a voice is distinct enough to recognize. A woman hears her grown son asking for a cup from a wall repaired when he was small. She puts down what she is holding and listens. It does not happen again.

There are masons who can tell when stone is likely to give back a noise, though none can promise which noise it will be. They warn householders before opening an old seam. Some people leave the room. Others stay very close.

***

Not everyone becomes good at living here.

There are absent-minded people, impatient people, people who cannot tell one white bird from another. The town accommodates them imperfectly. A neighbour checks their thresholds. A spouse ties the mallet to their belt. Someone spends thirty years being reminded not to empty a basin marked with wool and resents it every time.

Children are not uniformly fearless. One will cross anything; another will wait, angry and ashamed, beside a plank she has crossed a hundred times before. Usually someone comes back for her. Occasionally she has to find a different way.

The town contains generosity, carelessness, grudges about damaged roofs, and quarrels over whose animals ate whose drying cord. It also contains rooms prepared for people whose own rooms have become inaccessible. Most houses have one narrow sleeping place that is kept clear.

Tonight the child returns her panes late. Her grandmother has made her wait while the kitchen channel settles.

At the basin, there are animals crowded around the usual crack. She cannot put the tray down where she did before. She walks along the edge until she finds a bare stone, crouches, and lowers one end into the water.

Ten creatures slip away immediately.

The last clings to the grain of the tray. She waits with her fingers growing numb. Behind her, the birds begin leaving the upper ledges, but only a few, and she watches them without standing.

At last the little body releases. For a moment she can still see its dark points against the submerged wood.

She lifts the empty tray, tucks it beneath her coat, and takes the steps home.
