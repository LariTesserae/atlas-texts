The smallest inhabitants are not hidden so much as mistaken for the surfaces they occupy.

What looks like powder on the underside of a leaf is a multitude of pale, broad-backed animals. Each has a shallow notch behind its head and four limbs folded beneath the rim of its body. Their backs are rough; their bellies are smooth and faintly green. They fit against one another without quite touching, leaving narrow black channels through the apparent dust.

Under sufficient magnification, those channels are crowded too.

There are slender animals there, transparent except for a dark bead near either end. Their bodies lie bent around the feet of the larger creatures. Some clasp a single grain of mineral. Others have their heads pressed into the tiny cavities where the leathery leaf meets its powdered underside. No cavity is empty. At the bottom of each is something smaller, with a body suited exactly to the space.

Size is not a useful way to distinguish importance here. A creature too small to resolve with the eye may bear more of the chamber’s deep note than the hunter stretched along the root.

The hunter is handsome at close range. Its fired-clay color is mottled with gray, and its narrow back carries three blunt ridges. Beneath the raised forelimbs its mouth divides sideways into a pair of fine combs. The smaller animal held between its hooks is green, nearly round, with its legs tucked tightly against its body.

Neither has pierced the other.

There is a minute space between the hunter’s mouth and the smooth shell of its prey. Within that space lies a brightness not visible from any other angle. The hunter’s hooks, the green shell, and the parted combs all face it. It would be easy to call this feeding, but nothing passes across the gap. Both animals are full.

The pale bodies among the tassels have no mouths that can be found. Each carries a fan of delicate ribs on its breast. These are spread to different widths, so that a single tassel contains hundreds of distinct arrangements: narrow fans, nearly flat fans, fans with one rib folded across the others. The sharp upper tones of the hollow are strongest here. They do not come from the ribs vibrating. A rib seen against the green light is as steady as a mineral edge.

The creatures hang heavily. The tassels bear their weight.

Higher up, among the swollen joints of the ceiling braid, live the things that resemble seed pods. Their feet are small and fleshy, with dark nails embedded in the foliage. The seams along their bodies are not divisions in a shell but closed folds of skin. Between these folds are sheltered hollows containing eggs.

The eggs vary greatly. Some are opaque and white. Some are clear enough to show a curled body with its feet already braced against the inner surface. In one, an animal’s black eye faces outward through the shell, unobscured and apparently attentive.

There is no difference in vitality between it and its parent.

The builders are harder to see.

One is beneath the lip of the little clay bowl. Its body is the color of the bowl’s unglazed underside, and for a while it looks like a thick place in the clay. It has a low head, a broad trunk, and six short limbs. The rear pair hold the floor. The middle pair rest against its breast. The foremost limbs end in several unequal fingers.

Those fingers are empty, but clay lies in the creases of their tips.

Its face is not human. Two glossy pits sit far apart above a mouth like a short, horizontal cut. Between them is a raised patch of skin bearing a pattern of small depressions. The same pattern, enlarged and less regular, occurs inside the bowl. It may be a mark of ownership. It may be what a bowl is for.

The builder’s head is turned toward the black seed.

Seen from below, the seed has a seam and a pale point at one end. Seen from above, it appears perfectly round. The bowl is tilted just enough to hold it against one side, where seed and clay meet over a very small area.

The builder attends to that contact.

It is not waiting for the seed to open. Its posture has none of the slackness of waiting. The four fingers of one hand are spread against its chest, each resting in a separate shallow groove. Its feet grip the mineral with their whole undersides. Its attention is as substantial as the bowl.

There are others of its kind, though none has quite the same proportions. One fits within the crossing of the dark fiber binding. Only its head and one thick wrist are visible. Another occupies the inward-facing side of an upright salt chip, its back flattened against the crystal, its fingers extending around both edges. A third lies along the handle of the little tool. The winding of root passes over its abdomen without pinning it; its skin bears a groove made for the winding.

Their constructions leave room for them. Sometimes that room is so exact that body and object are difficult to separate.

There are no paths between the five green islands, no worn approaches to the bowl, no discarded lengths of fiber. Yet the builders do not seem isolated. A shallow hollow in one creature’s palm has the same shape as a knob on a distant creature’s wrist. The angles of their raised heads agree with the angles of the inward-facing salt chips. The tool’s crystal point rests upon a fracture that runs, unbroken, beneath two separate masses of roots.

They belong to one another through these correspondences. Distance does not interrupt them.

For all their strangeness, they are animals. Their bellies are soft. Their feet take weight. The little one beneath the bowl has an uneven fold of skin at its neck, and one of its fingers is shorter than the others. Beside its mouth is a pale scar.

Nothing here is flawless. The leaves have torn margins. One egg has a dent. A ceiling creature grips with five feet where its neighbors have six. These irregularities are held as completely as every other feature.

There is even shelter. Beneath a broad crimson-backed blade, several flat creatures press close around a much smaller one. The outer bodies expose their ridged backs to the chamber; the little body faces the leaf. All their eyes are open. It is unmistakably protected, though no danger approaches.

And throughout them is the sound.

It thickens in the joints, in the parted mouths, in the spaces enclosed by feet and roots. Near the builder beneath the bowl, the wooden resonance is so strong that the clay seems much larger than it is. Near the clear egg, a thin, fierce tone stands inside the shell.

These creatures need no motion to make their presence felt. Under the low stone, each bears its weight, occupies its shelter, holds its contact with another thing. The small builder’s palm remains open against its breast. Its face remains directed toward the place where the black seed touches the clay.
