The people who live here can tell which pump has started by the vibration in a table.

They may not know they are doing it. A spoon rattles against a saucer, and someone pauses halfway through a sentence, listening through the floor. Then the conversation continues. It is only the east station coming on. A different vibration would have sent someone to the window.

They live above shops, in the upper rooms of former offices, in short rows of houses behind the ridge road. Their downstairs rooms contain things that can be lifted or washed. There are hooks where another household might have sockets. The lowest shelves are often empty. A good chair is one person can carry upstairs.

Their shoes wait by the door with pale salt lines across the leather. Even shoes worn nowhere near the sea acquire these lines.

Some work at the salt sheds. Some maintain valves, refrigeration units, conveyors, ferry engines. Others cook for the night shifts, clean the rooms where crews sleep between sailings, or sell cigarettes, charging cables, soap, and particular sizes of washer. Their jobs have narrow names and long consequences. A person who changes a seal may prevent a district from flooding. A person who forgets to change one may never hear the last of it.

There is a woman who walks the waterfront shortly before the night shift begins. Her work clothes have bleached unevenly: the shoulders almost white, the folds beneath the pockets still blue. She carries a small torch even in daylight and keeps a screwdriver in a sheath repaired with fishing line.

She stops at covers other people step over.

Sometimes she crouches and places two fingers on the iron. Sometimes she opens a cabinet and stands without moving, studying something too small to see from across the road. She has learned that a leak is not always wet, that a failing bearing can first announce itself as warmth, that silence can mean a machine is working properly or that it has stopped doing something essential.

The crabs retreat when her shadow crosses them. She waits for them to get out of the way before setting down her tool bag. This is not a ceremony. A crushed crab smells inside a bag, and there are already enough unpleasant smells in her working day.

She knows which ladders still have sound bolts beneath their shell growth. She knows where a person who fell in could climb out.

There are people she no longer knows how to imagine except in their work clothes.

***

The rats know the port differently.

They know it as connected darkness: the space behind a kick plate, the underside of a ramp, the dry upper lip of a drain. An open yard is an interruption to be crossed only under pressure. A warehouse wall is a road.

Their coats are coarse and often damp along the belly. Some have pale scars on their tails. They drink from leaking freshwater pipes and from pockets of rain caught above the reach of the tide. They feed on grain shaken from pallets, scraps in bins, pigeon eggs, and small creatures stranded as the water leaves. A dead fish beneath a trailer can support several nights of argument.

When the oldest street floods, they move upward. One appears on a wheel hub; another climbs a chain. Inside the warehouses, certain beams become briefly crowded. Then the water falls and they descend again, leaving the upper places to dust and pigeons.

The pigeons inhabit the remaining room. Under roofs, beyond the sweep of fans, they raise young in nests so slight that the eggs seem laid directly on steel. Their calls sound larger inside an empty shed. A bird hidden high in the rafters can fill the building with its throat.

Outside, the gulls watch doors. They distinguish a worker carrying tools from a worker carrying lunch, and notice the distinction before the worker has reached the steps.

***

Most of the inhabitants have no use for a door.

Mussels grow around one another until opening their shells requires the cooperation of a crowd. Within their dark, closely packed bodies, water is continually being drawn in, sorted, and expelled. They cannot leave a bad place. They can only close.

Barnacles wait with their plates shut until the tide reaches them. Then the rough white surface becomes a field of small moving fans.

Among them are animals rarely noticed from above: pale worms in tubes, flattened things beneath stones, translucent bodies clinging to the shaded sides of floats. Some are visible only when a piece of equipment is lifted for cleaning. For a few minutes, a whole neighborhood hangs dripping in the air. Then a scraper removes it.

The young fish use the quay as a long, broken shelter. They hold beneath steps, behind ropes, inside the wavering shade of a hull. Their bodies are almost transparent until they turn together. Then a patch of darkness flashes silver and is dark again.

Larger fish pass outside them.

One has a damaged mouth. One carries a length of old line that has grown into the flesh beside its jaw. There are fish thick enough that their approach displaces the smaller ones before their bodies become visible. They wait below outlets where the current delivers food, adjusting their fins so slightly that holding position looks effortless.

When a bucket is emptied from a doorway, they rise.

Sometimes it contains fish scraps. Sometimes mop water. The first movement below the splash is quick and undiscriminating; what follows is more cautious.

***

Children grow up recognizing the places where animals appear, rather than the animals themselves.

There are the crabs under the third trailer, the gecko behind the drinks machine, the fish at the warm outlet. A child may watch the same patch of wall for weeks without knowing that several geckos have occupied it. The place remains dependable even when its occupants change.

They learn not to put a hand into a recess before looking. They learn that a smooth surface can be slick, that a shallow puddle may cover an open hole, and that water coming up through a drain deserves more attention than water falling from the sky.

On evenings when the street floods, they stand on the ramps and watch little fish pass the warehouse doors. Adults call them back before darkness. There are objections, bargains, a last requested minute. Eventually someone comes down the stairs to fetch them.

***

Farther out, the wading birds keep moving ahead of the tide.

Each lifted foot leaves a small well that fills behind it. Their bills find animals hidden beyond human sight: worms withdrawn into mud, shrimp nearly the same color as the bottom. As the available ground narrows, the birds become less tolerant of one another. A stretch of mud that held them quietly an hour ago begins to fill with short cries and raised wings.

At last they leave it.

They settle on higher banks, on abandoned aprons, on the roofs of low structures beyond the lamps. Some sleep with a bill buried in shoulder feathers. Others remain awake. Beneath them, the tide covers the places where they fed.

The night workers are eating now. A kettle boils in a room above a pump house. Someone has taken off their boots. Someone else is examining a swollen finger beneath the table light.

Outside, a bat turns between two buildings. A rat waits for a door to close. Along the flooded curb, a crab investigates the tread mark left by a cart.

Under the quay, where none of the light reaches, the mussels open a little wider.
