The pale animals are not as solitary as they appear.

If you watch one for long enough, you may notice that it stops beside a gray colony without feeding. It puts a forefoot against the stone, waits, and moves on. At another colony it does the same. Sometimes it returns to a place it has already visited and remains there until the light has changed.

You might reasonably suppose it is hunting the small black scavengers.

Then you find a narrow crack cleared of loose grit, with a damaged plant cushion pressed carefully into it. Three chips of ceramic hold the cushion in place. Each chip has been set at a different angle so that, whichever way the next current comes, at least one will provide shelter.

The pale creature is tending something.

Its people live on the plain.

They are scarce enough that a traveler could cross their country for months and believe it uninhabited. Even when seen, they are usually mistaken for animals. Their bodies are long and low, their four limbs emerging well apart from a flexible trunk. An adult standing at ease scarcely reaches a human knee. Much of that height can disappear without the limbs moving: the ribs fold, the belly settles, and the whole body becomes a shallow oval fitted closely to the ground.

They have no single, habitual way of standing.

On quiet stone they walk with their feet beneath them. On grit they spread their limbs and turn the broad outer surfaces of their feet downward. At an unstable boundary they advance almost sideways, keeping two feet on each plate for as long as possible. They can withdraw from a widening seam without first turning around.

What look like blunt feet open into short, powerful digits. The inner digits are small enough to handle a scavenger’s egg; the outer ones bear thick pads scored with fine channels. Dust passes through those channels rather than collecting beneath the foot. Along each wrist is a fringe of stiff hairs, almost invisible until sunlight catches it.

These hairs are constantly moving.

The people feel the ground currents before a human sees the dust that reveals them. They also feel disturbances traveling through the rock. Their lower jaws, breastbones, and foot pads are all sensitive to vibration. When a person lies flat in a groove, it is not necessarily hiding. It may be listening to something several kilometers away.

Their eyes matter less at such times. The eyes are set far apart beneath folds of pale skin, giving them a good view of the ground on either side. Dark lids cross them during a dust front. There is a second, transparent covering beneath the lids, scratched and renewed throughout life.

Young individuals have clearer coverings. Old ones see the sun through a faint amber haze.

Age shows elsewhere, too. The skin loses its even mineral color and becomes mottled with rust and violet. The foot pads thicken. The body grows less willing to fold into a narrow groove. An old person may be unusually easy to see, moving slowly across open stone at an hour when everyone younger has already gone under cover.

Such a person is seldom alone, though the companions may remain out of sight.

***

Most of their dwellings are beneath the surface, but not far beneath it.

They occupy the shallow spaces between overlapping plates: rooms with ceilings that may be only a hand’s breadth above the floor in one season and high enough to stand beneath in another. A useful chamber is one that changes slowly, drains dust toward an accessible opening, and has more than one exit. Its size is less important.

They furnish these spaces with ceramic pieces too low and plain to be recognizable as furniture. A sleeping support is a curved strip. A cupboard is a covered groove. Water is stored in thin, sealed vessels that lie against the cold underside of the upper plate.

There are no fires.

A family may possess a surface the size of a palm that becomes warm when placed in a certain recess. It is used to warm food, soften repair compound, or bring a chilled infant slowly back to its proper temperature. Such surfaces are passed from chamber to chamber when needed. Some have been in use so long that nobody remembers their original color.

The squat buildings above ground belong to this same domestic world, although they are not ordinary homes. They are places where travelers can wait, where a sick person can be kept away from shifting ceilings, and where work requiring a fixed direction of weight can be done.

The worn hooks hold bodies as often as belongings. A person resting in a shelter may suspend part of its weight from two hooks, leaving its feet lightly in contact with the floor. This reduces fatigue without preventing it from hearing the stone.

The bowl-shaped hollow in the wall is used for conversation.

A forefoot laid inside it can send a sequence of small pressures into the ceramic. At another shelter, the sequence may arrive as a faint movement beneath someone’s fingers. The anchors carry some of these signals. Other messages travel through the plates themselves, less reliably and much more quickly in certain directions than in others.

A bowl free of dust may have been used that morning.

Not every shelter can speak to every other. Some receive only. Some have gone silent. There are buildings whose bowls still move occasionally, though no known settlement lies in the direction from which the movement comes.

The people do not agree about these.

***

Their speech in company is almost inaudible to an upright listener.

They make small clicks, scrape one hard part of the mouth against another, and tap the ground. Much of the meaning lies in timing and pressure. Two people lying side by side can have a private conversation through the narrow strip of stone beneath them, while a third, a few steps away, hears nothing.

They do have names, but a name spoken on one plate does not sound quite the same on another. When introducing themselves to a stranger, they repeat it several times and allow the stranger to feel their forefoot while they speak.

Their hands are warm.

This can be surprising after everything else about them: the dust-colored skin, the low posture, their ability to remain motionless in a cold groove. Contact reveals a living warmth, carefully retained. They lose very little moisture through their skin. Their breathing is slow, and the passages behind the nostrils recover much of the water in each exhalation.

Nevertheless, they must drink.

The plant colonies supply only a small part of what they need. More important are condensation surfaces fitted into deep cracks, where minute daily changes of temperature can be made to yield water over many years. A good collector produces so little that its vessel seems empty until tilted.

Collectors are guarded, repaired, and inherited. A person may travel for days carrying a sealed vessel no larger than a fist, both hands cupped around it during every halt.

Spilling water is not considered a crime. Refusing to help someone who has spilled it may be.

Their food is similarly unassuming: mineral-rich plant tissue, scavengers harvested after breeding, pale growths cultivated on the undersides of certain plates. They eat slowly and retain almost everything their bodies can use. Much household work involves returning salts to a cultivated crack without smothering its stems.

The gray cushions are not all wild.

Some of the smallest are the oldest tended possessions on the plain.

***

Children are carried under the body.

For their first years they cling in pairs or threes to folds along an adult’s abdomen, protected from grit by the lowered limbs. They learn the landscape first through movement: a parent tightening before a tremor, spreading its feet at a change of current, becoming completely still beside a doubtful seam.

Only later do they learn to look at the horizon.

A child’s first independent journey may be across a few meters of exposed stone. Several adults lie nearby, apparently asleep. No one urges the child forward. It may wait through most of a day before the conditions are right.

When it finally crosses, the adults touch its feet.

There is little in their education that resembles a lesson spoken in advance. A young person is taken to a familiar place and asked to find what has changed. At first the differences are obvious: a fresh seam, a lost plant, a dust crescent on the wrong side of a rock. Later the task becomes harder. The pupil must notice that a plate answers pressure differently, or that a shelter’s pull has shifted slightly away from its door.

Mistakes are expected. Concealing them is treated much more seriously.

They enjoy games in which a mistake is harmless. Children rearrange tiny stones behind one another’s backs. Adults make elaborate sequences of taps for someone else to repeat. In sheltered chambers, people tell accounts of journeys whose pleasure lies in an ingenious crossing or a ridiculous delay.

Some stories concern travelers who were in such a hurry to arrive that they spent three days pinned behind the same stone.

These are especially popular with the old.

***

The anchors occupy a large part of their attention, but they did not necessarily build them.

They know how to adjust certain parts. They can replace a worn ceramic fitting, redirect a local pull, or quiet an anchor that has begun to send damaging stresses into a plate. They have tools for descending through narrow shafts beneath the shelters, where an ordinary hanging weight may swing toward a wall or come to rest against the ceiling.

They cannot make a new anchor.

Or perhaps they can, and the work requires more people, more material, and a steadier interval of time than the plain has given them in many generations. Their accounts of earlier times are difficult to interpret. The same word may mean *made*, *restored*, or *persuaded to continue*.

They disagree over repairs. One group will spend years preserving a failing shelter; another will move its water vessels and plants away, arguing that the strain has merely been passed into neighboring stone. These disputes can divide families whose members continue to share food and care for the same children.

An anchor keeper is not a ruler. A keeper is someone willing to remain close to a particular failure.

You can recognize one by the fine dark powder embedded in the folds around its digits. The powder comes from below the sunlit surface and does not wash out easily.

***

They do not bury their dead in the ground. The ground will not keep them.

A body is placed beneath a ceramic cover in a secure chamber, and watched through the long work of drying. The salts are eventually returned to the household’s cultivated colonies. Small pieces of the hard inner skeleton may be retained.

These are polished by handling.

A traveler sometimes carries one in the mouth during a dangerous crossing, leaving both forefeet free. It has no mechanical use. When the traveler reaches safety, the piece is taken out, cleaned, and put away.

Families leave failing settlements with such things, with water, with warm surfaces wrapped in insulating fiber, and with bundles of living stems. The journey may be only a few kilometers, yet take weeks. Someone always goes ahead. Someone always remains behind until the last load is ready.

Their numbers cannot be judged from the shelters. A region with six empty buildings may support several crowded chambers beneath the stone. Another may contain nothing living larger than a scavenger, though every building remains intact.

If you meet one of the people in a shelter, it will probably approach your boots first. It will examine the soles, the fastenings, the way you distribute your weight. Then it may touch a dangling strap and tuck it out of the ground current.

It cannot know how long you intend to stay, or whether you understand the danger.

In the morning, if the step has opened onto a seam, you may find your belongings moved back from the doorway. Outside, on the opposite side of the gap, a pale body lies almost flush with the rock.

It waits while you wake.

One forefoot rests on the plate you must reach. The other is lifted, making a small, deliberate motion toward a patch of ground that looks no safer than any other.

For the moment, it is.
