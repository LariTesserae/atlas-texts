At first there is only a place where the water does not quite reflect the sky.

It is beside a shelf of stone, in a hollow shallow enough that a wading bird has crossed it without wetting its belly. The interruption is long and gray-brown. A few grains of pale clay lie on it. When a fly lands near one end, an eye opens.

The animal has been there since before the sun reached the hollow.

Its body is broad through the ribs and narrows only a little toward the hips. Four short legs lie folded against its sides. Its tail, nearly as long as the rest of it, is flattened underneath and thick at the base; the last third rests in a seam between two stones. Nothing projects very far. The ears are small openings beneath folds of skin. The nostrils sit high on a blunt muzzle. Even the eyes have low, heavy lids, so that when they close the head loses most of its recognizable features.

It is about the length of a large dog, but lower than any dog could comfortably remain. Standing, it barely clears the sedges. Most of the time it does not stand.

Along its back, the skin is covered in close, blunt scales. These are smoother at the sides, where water passes over them, and rougher along the spine. Mud dries in the rough places and remains there until another soaking loosens it. An animal that has spent several days on the pale flats looks almost white. One that has been feeding in a dark channel carries the color of the channel onto dry ground.

Underneath, it is unexpectedly soft. The throat and belly are finely creased, gray with a faint pinkness where the forelegs join the body. It keeps this skin against damp earth whenever it can. On hot afternoons, it shifts a little at a time, pressing its weight into cooler mud beneath the surface crust.

Its feet are wide. The toes spread when it walks, joined by webbing that folds away on firm stone. Each toe ends in a short dark nail. On mud, the prints look like hands that have been dragged backward before lifting.

The little tracks visible through the pools are often its tracks.

It feeds most actively while the water is moving. At the mouth of a narrow channel it settles with its head facing the current, forefeet braced, tail laid downstream. Small fish pass close to its jaw. Usually it lets them go. Then the head moves sideways with a speed that seems unrelated to the rest of the animal, and a fish is gone.

The mouth is wide but not conspicuous when closed. There are short teeth for holding, and flatter ones farther back. It eats crabs too, turning them against the bottom with one forefoot before biting through the shell. Afterward, fragments lie in the hollow where its chin rested. Birds sometimes approach these scraps while it is still there. It tolerates them until one steps near an eye.

It is not patient in the sense of resisting an urge to move. Remaining still is simply one of the things its body does well.

A falling tide leaves food caught in places that will soon become dangerous: a warm pocket beneath a ledge, a channel pinched shut by sediment, a basin growing saltier as its edges whiten. The animal knows these places. It travels between them by routes worn into its habits rather than into the ground. A ridge that seems negligible to an observer may divide two quite different journeys. On one side, the mud holds its weight. On the other, it sinks to the belly and must pull itself onward with slow sweeps of the forelegs.

It can swim, but in these shallows swimming is usually unnecessary. It pushes off, slides, touches bottom again. From a distance the movement looks like a patch of darker water passing over the stones.

Behind each eye is a small gland that sheds excess salt. After it has spent a long morning in the brackish reach, clear drops gather there and run toward the jaw. When they dry, they leave a pale streak. Young animals often have clean faces because they remain farther inland, in rain-fed hollows among the sedges.

They begin life on the few raised tongues of ground.

The female chooses a place beneath a shrub whose roots have bound the soil. With her hindfeet she opens a chamber above the usual reach of the tide. She lays a small clutch of leathery eggs and packs the entrance loosely with damp clay, leaving cracks through which air can pass. For weeks afterward she returns, sometimes merely resting nearby, sometimes scraping away soil that has hardened after dry weather.

An exceptional tide can take the whole clutch. So can a large bird, if it finds the chamber while the clay is still loose.

The young emerge narrow-headed and disproportionately long-tailed. They are striped at first, with dark lines that break up their shape among reed shadows. Several can shelter beneath one stone. They eat larvae, tiny snails, and the smallest crabs. When disturbed, they do not immediately flee. They flatten themselves, spreading the ribs, and wait for the disturbance to pass over them. Only at a second approach do they scatter.

Adults generally live apart. There is food enough for many, but each needs quiet at the small channel mouths where it feeds. When two meet, they raise their bodies higher than seems possible and hold their mouths partly open. The pale lining is visible. Their throats swell.

The sound they make is low and hollow, a brief pulse followed by a pause. It carries through the flats without becoming loud. Heard among the sucking channels and distant frogs, it is easy to assign it to the water.

Sometimes the display is enough. Sometimes one animal presses its shoulder against the other and they push, slowly, their feet sinking. The loser turns aside and travels to another basin. They rarely pursue each other across open ground.

In cooler weather, several may occupy the same sun-warmed shelf without argument, each leaving space around its head.

They sleep with part of the face exposed. Rising water lifts the tail first, then creeps beneath the flanks. An animal may remain asleep through this, buoyed a little above the place where it lay. When the water reaches its nostrils, it wakes and shifts uphill. The movement is so small that anyone watching the incoming tide might miss it.

By evening, the one beside the nearest stone has lost the pale grains of clay from its back. Its scales are wet and darker now. Fish have begun moving through the newly joined pools, and it turns its head toward the channel.

For a moment its whole outline can be seen: the thick tail, the spread feet, the low curve of the ribs. Then it eases forward. Water closes over its back, leaving the eyes and nostrils above the surface.

A bird lands on the stone it has vacated. The animal holds its place below, making small corrections with its feet as the current strengthens.
