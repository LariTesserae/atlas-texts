The animal beneath the pump-room step is usually mistaken for part of the step.

It lies along the underside of the lowest tread, where the iron folds back on itself to make a lip. There is always something caught there: fibres from a belt, a little rust, the grey pulp of a drowned moth. The animal has the same uneven edges. Water beads on it without making it shine.

The caretaker knows it is present because the step closes badly when it is there. The last inch requires a little pressure, then gives with a soft resistance that should not exist in the hinge. She leaves the step down now. In the maintenance book, against the instruction to fold it after use, she has written: **Occupied beneath.**

There are others in the district, though it is difficult to say how many.

Seen away from shelter, one is about the length of a hand. Its body is low and broad, narrower at one end, with a row of short appendages tucked underneath. These are not quite legs. Each ends in a soft divided pad that can close around a wire, spread across a stone, or enter the smallest loss of mortar between two bricks. An animal crossing the court uses them in no fixed order. Some hold while others reach. Occasionally the whole body advances without any visible movement beneath it.

Its upper surface carries fine mineral grains. They are embedded rather than stuck there. On the iron steps it looks rust-coloured; beside the canal it is almost black. One found inside a pressure-instrument case had acquired a scattering of pale green flecks, matching the corrosion on the dial.

Underneath, it is colourless and unexpectedly delicate. A mechanic once lifted one on the blade of a scraper. Through its belly he could see a series of clear chambers, each containing a small bubble. The bubbles shifted when he tilted the blade, but not toward the upper end. They moved toward the pump room.

He put it back before finishing his inspection.

At the narrow end of the body are two folds which open in water. Between them, something moves that might be a mouth. The animals feed where the district is least clean: beneath felt, behind hinges, in the soft brown margins of the old flood marks. Their grazing leaves neither the broad polished path of a snail nor the scraped spot made by a fish. They remove individual grains, making small pale points in the deposit.

Given time, the points join.

The caretaker can identify a regularly occupied surface by its stippling. She does not use this to find the animals themselves. Looking directly at a wall is a poor way to find one. It is better to put a fingertip against the brick and wait. Sometimes, several inches away, a loose edge will lift.

They seem to notice pressure before light or sound.

When a cart is moved on the street overhead, the nearest ones flatten. When the northern stair is crowded, they gather in the joints beside its lowest landing. When the pump begins its intake, every animal that can be seen puts its pads down firmly and draws its edges close against the surface beneath it.

During the rising part of the stroke, they become thicker.

The change is slight. An animal that looked like a peeling flake of iron becomes a small, rounded body. Its mineral grains separate, exposing pale skin between them. For a few seconds it is plainly alive, with a shape that could be held in the hands.

Then the water settles. The body spreads again.

There is an old entry in the maintenance book: **Do not detach during stroke.**

No consequence is recorded. The instruction has been copied into every replacement book.

They do not restrict themselves to wet surfaces. One of the wandering flood marks passes above the valve-repair door, well out of reach of ordinary water, and several animals live along its arch. They cross the dry lintel slowly, feeding as they go. If one reaches the end of the brown line, it turns back.

A woman cleaning the lintel once left a gap no wider than her thumb. By dusk, an animal had stopped on either side of it. They remained there for three days, their front folds opening and closing against bare brick.

On the fourth day, a fine dark stain appeared between them.

There had been no flood. The caretaker drew the stain in the book, with the date and the pump reading. Neither animal was there the following evening.

For most of the year they occupy separate places. The one under the pump-room step tolerates a smaller animal along the back edge, but presses against it when it approaches the hinge. Three share the underside of the brass-fitting shelf, distributed precisely among the shelf brackets. They do not disturb the fittings. The felt below them is clean in three narrow strips.

In late winter, after several days of delayed rain, the animals begin to move toward the loading court.

They arrive by routes that are not obvious from the landing. One follows the inside of a cable sleeve. Another descends the back of an iron shutter, stopping at every rivet. Those beneath the platform come up through the spaces between its boards. At first they appear to be feeding. Then, around a shallow basin in the stone, their bodies form a broken ring.

They leave gaps wherever an old groove enters the basin.

The ring may remain for a week. Shrimp pass between its members. A bird sometimes steps over them to fish in the middle. No animal turns toward the disturbance unless a foot presses down on one of the grooves.

At some point the basin acquires a fringe of transparent grains. Each grain is fastened just above the ordinary waterline, close enough for the pump’s rise to cover it. There are hundreds, and it takes a good lamp to distinguish them from droplets.

Inside each is a short dark stroke.

The young appear first on the undersides of stones. They have no mineral coating and are so thin that their internal chambers look like an extra row of pores in the masonry. For a while they move together, advancing a fraction of an inch with each pump stroke.

Most disappear before growing large. The small fish search the joints where they gather, and the birds become especially patient around the basin.

Those that survive establish themselves near some regularly moving piece of machinery: the step hinge, a belt guide, the float in an inspection chamber. They stay there until their backs have darkened. An apprentice once transferred six young animals to a quiet tray of canal water. They remained alive for several weeks but did not grow. When he tipped the tray beneath the loading platform, they left it before the water had finished draining.

The adults can be moved, although they rarely remain where they are put.

A full-grown one was once discovered halfway up the eastern stair, fastened to the bottom of a bucket. The bucket was empty. It had been used to carry broken valve seats, and the animal had travelled among the flakes of iron left beneath them.

The porter noticed it while resting at the turn. He set the bucket down.

Its pads had reached over the rim of the base. They were pressed so firmly to the stair that he could not lift the bucket without lifting a thin scale of stone with it.

A caretaker brought down a length of damp felt, slid it beneath the pads, and waited through two pump strokes. The animal let go. She carried it back on the felt, holding the whole length level.

It spent the next month beneath the eastern landing. After that, it was no longer distinguishable among the animals there.

They are not tame. They show no interest in the hooded light, and an offered finger receives no investigation. Yet they seem to become accustomed to a particular arrangement of things. The fresh plank on the central landing was avoided for eleven evenings. On the twelfth, one appeared beneath the brick weighting its canalward end.

Now, when the caretaker lifts that brick to check the wood, she does so slowly.

She has never given the animals a collective name. In her book they are recorded by location: **under tread**, **north joint**, **behind winding-shop sill**. When a sill is replaced, its entry remains until something occupies the new one.

Some entries have lasted longer than she has been making the circuit.

At dusk she passes the pump-room step and lowers her lamp. The animal beneath it is visible as a rough strip against the folded iron. Its edges lift once, then settle.

She touches the measuring rod to the pool below, reads the ordinary depth, and waits for the stroke. As the piston advances, the animal rounds against the underside of the tread. Water rises around her boots. A clear drop gathers on each of its folded front edges, held there until the mechanism withdraws.

Then its pads loosen, one after another. It shifts a little farther beneath the iron.

The caretaker enters both readings in the book and leaves the step down.
