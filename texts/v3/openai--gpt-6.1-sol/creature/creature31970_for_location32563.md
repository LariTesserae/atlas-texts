At first it looks like something the crews have left behind.

There is a folded length of gray material beneath the lowest step of a maintenance stair: perhaps a discarded sleeve of insulation, soaked through and stiffened with grit. One edge lies in the water. The other has been drawn up against the warm foot of the stair, where a heater prevents ice from distorting the bolts.

It remains there through three shifts.

Then the heater fails, and the material is found beneath the next stair.

The people who work the basin call these things folds. The name is used on maintenance reports as well as in conversation. A fold obstructing a drain is a fold, just as ice obstructing it is ice. There is a box to check for either.

An adult, spread flat, would cover most of an insulated door. But they are almost never spread flat. Their bodies gather into overlapping pleats, with the wet outer surfaces pressed together and the softer inner surfaces concealed. A resting fold is no larger than a tool bag. A cold one can become smaller still, tightening around itself until it resembles a thick, badly wound gasket.

Its exterior is gray, sometimes nearly black, and studded with whatever has adhered to it: ceramic splinters, rust scales, short lengths of insulation fiber. An old fold may carry a washer in its skin. There are specimens with scraps of orange flagging incorporated along one edge, the numbers still legible.

These objects are not decoration. On fresh composite, the grit gives it purchase. Beneath a falling curtain of water, the fibers hold a layer of relatively motionless water against its surface. The larger fragments take the first impact of freezing drops.

Underneath, it is a pale, jointless animal.

The underside has many narrow ridges, each divided into thousands of soft points. They adhere and release in sequence. On a smooth replacement panel, a fold moves with an almost imperceptible rippling, advancing the width of a fingernail while appearing perfectly still. Over a bolt head it opens, flows around the obstruction, and closes behind it.

It cannot cross every surface. Freshly cast banks are too hot. Dry ceramic dust clogs its underside. Ice offers no purchase unless there are bubbles or scratches in it. A gap wider than the body’s longest fold is impassable.

This gives the animal a peculiar geography. A person looking across the basin sees stretches of water divided by terraces and pump houses. A fold encounters wet undersides, narrow seams, strips of tolerable warmth. A single cold handrail may connect two places that, for it, would otherwise be impossibly far apart.

There are folds that have lived their whole lives on one pump house.

They occupy the tall feet, traveling around them as the warmth changes, sheltering behind the bolts when the pumps engage. Their tracks make dull bands through the rust. Workers sometimes use those bands to judge whether an inaccessible heater is still functioning.

One pump house has been cold for eleven years. A fold remains on its southern support.

It feeds there, slowly, and contracts during the worst of the cold. Twice a cycle, warm waste water passes through a return pipe behind the support. The animal opens for those few minutes. No one has seen it descend to the floor.

***

A fold has no obvious head.

The edge that leads during travel is thicker than the others and carries a row of shallow pits. These detect warmth, vibration, and certain dissolved substances. If the animal reverses direction, another edge thickens over the next several hours. The pits on the former leading edge close.

It does not see the amber lamps, although light falling directly on its underside makes it withdraw. It knows a running pump through the metal well before water begins to move. It knows the casting gantry as a traveling succession of tremors, some useful, some dangerous.

At feeding places it opens slightly.

Inside each pleat are fine dark threads. They extend into the water and trap the films shed by roots, the bodies of small organisms, and the soft material accumulating where rubber dust stays wet. A healthy fold feeds with most of its body closed. Only the threads show, like the frayed lining of an old coat.

The white crustaceans graze around it. Sometimes they enter its pleats and emerge again. Sometimes they do not.

A fold is not a swift predator. It closes too slowly to catch anything alert, but an injured crustacean or one stunned by a pressure change may be drawn inward. The animal holds such food against its inner skin for a long time. Afterward, a transparent shell works its way out along the lowest crease.

Beneath occupied stairs, these shells accumulate with the ceramic grit.

There is usually very little waste. Life in the basin passes through several bodies before reaching the intake.

***

The crews have learned to lift folds with a warm scraper.

A cold scraper tears them. A gloved hand is worse: the animal fastens to the fabric, and pulling it loose strips away the soft points beneath. If it has to be moved, the scraper is warmed against a pipe, slipped under one edge, and left there until the body begins to gather onto it.

This can take twenty minutes.

Experienced workers leave the scraper in place and do something else. New workers tend to hurry. There are pale scars on the supports where folds have been removed in pieces.

Once freed, the animal is carried in a shallow tray with a little basin water. It must be set down against a seam, not in the middle of a panel. On an open surface it will travel until it finds an edge, spending reserves it may need later.

Some workers resent this. Others keep a particular scraper for the purpose.

The same fold is often encountered year after year. It can be recognized by its embedded fragments, by an asymmetrical pleat, by the old damage that makes it turn in one direction more easily than the other. At the third pump house from the intake, a large individual has a strip of white ceramic along its back. It has been moved away from the door seal so often that it begins to gather itself when the warm scraper touches the step.

Whether it recognizes the procedure or simply the temperature is disputed.

It has never gathered itself for a cold scraper.

***

The folds respond to the bells.

Well before the first bell sounds, they draw their feeding threads inside. Those on exposed surfaces move toward joints. Those already sheltered loosen their attachment without leaving their resting places.

This is difficult to observe unless you know what to watch for. A crease widens. A scrap of flagging changes its angle. Water appears under an edge that had been tightly fixed to the metal.

During the silence, when the clear films advance uphill, some folds open.

The underside is then visible: pale cream, crossed with delicate branching channels, dotted here and there with small amber swellings. The water moves through the exposed pleats. It carries away accumulated grit and, in season, the amber bodies.

These are the young.

They are no larger than drops of solder and have only two folds each. For a little while they can swim, opening and closing around a pocket of water. Most are carried down when the pumps resume. They pass into the channels, through screens, into chambers from which nothing recognizable returns.

A few adhere above the ordinary waterline.

They settle in bolt holes and shallow casting flaws. There they flatten until they are nearly transparent. For the first several cycles they feed on what grows on the metal itself. Their gray outer layer comes later.

No one has established whether the adults need the climbing water to release their young. Folds kept in warm tanks reproduce poorly. Sometimes they shed an entire brood into water that is perfectly still; sometimes they retain it until the swellings are absorbed again.

In the basin, a brood may be released from one fold while another, centimeters away, remains closed.

The reeds fold early. The folds loosen later. Between these events, there is usually enough time for a worker to reach shelter.

***

The western shore has the largest population.

The new banks provide bare surfaces with shallow flaws, not yet occupied by the dense growth of the older terraces. As the composite cools, the first thin films of food begin to form around the leakage points. Young folds arrive there in the water. Older ones approach along the gantry rails or cling to panels being carried into place.

They wait below the fresh lip.

When the heat becomes tolerable, they ascend.

A newly settled fold is almost impossible to distinguish from a wet casting defect. Over time it develops an edge, then a crease, then the familiar gray bundle. It feeds into the cracks and keeps them damp. Its weight is negligible, but the moisture beneath it is persistent.

Maintenance engineers disagree about how much damage this causes. Some say a fold accelerates the failure of a surface. Others say it occupies failures already underway.

Both observations have been made on the same panel.

There are regulations requiring the western shore to be cleared before inspection. By the time an inspection party reaches the last marked terrace, folds have generally begun returning to the first.

***

They are not sociable, but they accumulate.

A good warm seam may support six or seven, their edges overlapping. Each feeds separately. If the heat diminishes, the outer animals tighten first. The innermost may remain open for hours, sheltered by the others.

During a long shutdown, such a group becomes one hard-looking mass. Ice forms across the exposed surfaces. Within, the folds remain pliable around a pocket of unfrozen water.

When the heat returns, they separate slowly. Sometimes one does not reopen. The others feed beneath it until only its outer grit remains.

Dead folds resemble abandoned material more convincingly than living ones. Their bodies collapse into tough gray sheets, difficult to tear and slow to decay. Those sheets catch fibers and dust. Seeds lodge against them. On an old support, a rosette may grow from the remains of an animal that spent decades climbing around the same four bolts.

Workers occasionally find a living fold underneath such a plant, feeding on what washes from its roots.

They leave these alone unless the growth threatens a seal.

***

There is a fold beneath the easternmost control kiosk that is too large to move with a scraper.

Most of it is hidden behind the wall lining. One part rests below the schematic display; another emerges beside the door. When the kiosk was still heated, workers assumed they were looking at two animals. During a power interruption, both portions withdrew, and the connecting pleats became visible through a gap beneath the wall.

It had grown around the base of the cabinet.

The kiosk has since been abandoned. Its lamps still work. Across the display, pale lines mark channels that may or may not exist yet.

The fold does not respond to the moving lines. It feeds in the damp space behind the cabinet, where the old cable insulation sheds a fine organic dust. When someone opens the door, it draws the exposed portion inward. In the sudden quiet, this makes a faint sound, a wet release followed by a soft dragging.

On some visits it is nowhere to be seen.

On others, one gray edge lies across the threshold. It has gathered a brass screw, two red fibers, and a flake of paint bearing part of an instruction. The visible word is KEEP.

The floor outside is settling. A shallow stream now passes beneath the door, and the kiosk’s lowest wall panel has begun to bow. Eventually the warm circuit behind the cabinet will break or become submerged.

The fold has already extended one pleat into the new stream.

A meter away, under the raised walk, there is a joint that remains warm through most of the cycle. Between the kiosk and that joint lie two bolt heads, a narrow strip of ice, and a patch of coarse replacement composite.

It advances a little after each bell.
