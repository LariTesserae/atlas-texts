The first inhabitant you notice is a fold in a leaf.

It is no larger than your thumbnail, pale along its hinge, with a clear bead of water caught inside it. When you lean closer, the bead moves against the slope. The fold tightens. Along the underside of the leaf, a row of minute bladders empties one after another, drawing the water inward.

Nothing looks back at you. But several meters away, beneath your other hand, a second leaf makes the same adjustment.

Then the surface under your boots begins to drain.

You have not found a creature sitting on the vegetation. You have touched a small part of something whose other parts are elsewhere.

***

The inhabitants do have bodies, although a body here is not necessarily a continuous piece of flesh.

One might consist of a cluster of sensing leaves on a high ridge, a pale knot enclosed in a trunk far below, several kilometers of specialized root, and a thin, almost invisible film spread across the underside of a floating mat. Ordinary tissue lies between these parts. Water passes through them. Other inhabitants pass through them too, in the form of fibers, dissolved compounds, pulses of pressure.

Cutting out any single part would not give you the creature. It would give you an organ, or a tool, or a temporary place where the creature was paying attention.

Most of their substance is unremarkable. Their roots mingle with the roots that hold up the country. Their leaves are lost among the leaves that drink the rain. The distinctive tissues occur at junctions: where three vessels meet, where a load changes direction, where cool surface water enters a warmer depth. There, the grain becomes exceptionally fine. Thin partitions subdivide a cavity into hundreds of wet chambers. A translucent membrane trembles without any wind reaching it.

These are the places in which a resident gathers itself.

A large one may have many such places. They do not all agree immediately.

***

Their senses are suited to a world that is mostly hidden from itself.

They feel the weight of water before they feel its temperature. They know a distant fracture by the change it makes in a supporting root. They can distinguish rain newly arrived from rain that has spent three days falling through foliage, tasting the minute additions collected on the way down.

What you hear as continuous uproar is, to them, full of gaps and particulars. They listen through their own substance. A concussion reaching a root junction tells them which vessel failed, how full it was, and whether the surrounding growth has already begun to close it. The grinding of two leaves matters less as sound than as friction transmitted through their stems.

Their finer leaf tissues measure light in narrow bands. Some faces turn toward brightness; others turn away, toward a dark portion of the sky through which heat can escape. A resident may keep a patch of new growth cool by altering surfaces so thin that they appear only as a change in sheen.

Much of this work happens without deliberation. A torn vessel seals as your blood would clot.

But not everything they do is automatic.

Sometimes a root begins thickening toward one pocket of water, stops, and turns toward another. Sometimes a newly raised terrace is left strangely bare along one edge while its neighbor receives an excess of light-catching foliage. There are delays, revisions, abandoned beginnings. You could watch the same ridge for a season and come to recognize which changes were merely growth and which were choices.

***

They move by making more of themselves ahead and letting go behind.

A small resident can cross a pool in weeks. It sends transparent filaments among the hanging roots, establishes a few new chambers, and gradually shifts its activity into them. Its old leaves lose their special sheen. The tissues that held its attention become food or plumbing or simply part of the mat.

A very large resident may take years to withdraw from a valley.

It does not have to move all at once. For a while it lives more fully in two places than in the space between them. Signals travel along several routes, arrive at different times, and are reconciled in slow exchanges of fluid.

This makes injury complicated. A severed portion may die. It may survive as a diminished continuation. It may become independent, especially if the break lasts long enough for it to establish different habits.

There are residents that began as wounds.

In a cleft where a great buttress split, two surviving networks might each have rebuilt the missing connection. By the time their new tissues met, they were responding differently to the same weather. One opened its pores early. The other stored water against a shortage that never came. They joined well enough to carry the load, but not well enough to become one again.

You would see a smooth green seam. Within it, two old selves would still be negotiating.

***

Their boundaries have practical consequences.

They exchange salts, water, sugars, structural compounds. They also withhold them. A ridge can be crowded with growth and still contain a narrow region of deprivation, where one resident has closed its junctions to another.

Competition here rarely leaves something eaten down to a stump. More often it produces unusual architecture.

A broad leaf grows just high enough to shade a neighboring sensor. A root collar hardens around a shared channel, leaving only a restricted passage. One inhabitant releases a substance that makes another’s tissues close too readily; the affected slope remains wet and dark while the first extends into the available light.

There are thieves in the colorless depths. They imitate the chemical markings of useful tissue and attach themselves to supply routes. Some remain small, drawing no more than their host can overlook. Others multiply until a whole support layer is honeycombed with soft, hungry chambers.

The great inhabitants are not uniformly benevolent custodians of the land. They have preferences, tolerances, persistent mistakes. One may preserve a troublesome neighbor because their intertwined roots make an excellent brace. Another may sacrifice healthy surface growth to protect a deep chamber that has been active for centuries.

The landscape continues to rise through all of this. Cooperation and conflict are both built into it.

***

Young inhabitants are easy to mistake for ordinary plants.

They begin in the tightly occupied water, beneath rafts of fleshy leaves. A seed case bursts somewhere above, and most of what falls never establishes anything distinct. It is consumed, incorporated, or reduced to a few cells waiting inside another’s tissue.

A successful young resident first makes a loop.

Two fine roots attach to separate stems. A third joins them beneath the water. Within that little circuit, fluid can pass around and around, altered at each junction. The loop samples its surroundings, retains some changes, erases others. It begins to regulate its own pressure.

Its first leaves are small. Its first decisions concern which existing root to follow and when to open a valve.

For a long time, an older resident may carry it without much cost. The young one learns the local rhythms of water and strain, responds to signals, performs tiny pieces of maintenance. Later it begins to ask for more than the older one supplies.

Sometimes it is given a growing margin.

Sometimes its connections are closed, and it must leave through whatever wet tissue remains accessible.

At the outer lobes of the continent, many of the pale tips belong to these young inhabitants. They have little stored experience and abundant room. Their construction is less regular. They raise shallow chambers that fill with water, then devise ways of supporting the unexpected weight. They produce leaves too closely packed to unfold, or channels that deliver pressure unevenly. Older networks extend beneath them, bracing some errors and allowing others to fail.

That is part of the noise at the frontier: not only expansion, but experiment.

***

What they remember is held in living arrangements.

A sequence of chambers that opens reluctantly may preserve the consequence of an old flood. A protected branch of vessels can keep the chemical trace of water no longer found anywhere nearby. Some memories are renewed continually, copied into fresh tissue before their original cells become ordinary wood.

Others disappear.

A resident that replaces too much of itself too quickly may retain its skills and lose particular histories. It still knows how to support a ridge. It no longer responds to a certain pressure pattern with the hesitation that once saved it.

Deep inside the oldest buttresses are narrow routes maintained at considerable expense. They carry little water and bear almost no weight. Signals travel through them slowly. When new growth becomes difficult to stabilize, activity sometimes returns to these routes, and a pattern unused for a very long time enters the working tissues again.

The resulting structure may resemble something built far away, before the present heights existed.

***

If you remain still, one may learn you.

At first your weight is just an irregular load. Your warmth is a local disturbance. The substances leaving your skin are unfamiliar additions to the water.

A resident closes the pores beneath you. It raises a few leaves to sample the air around your face. Several sensing surfaces withdraw when you touch them; one stays open.

Over hours, the little hollow around your boots stops filling. A narrow channel grows along its edge. The ground supports you more firmly, not because it has understood a visitor, but because it has found a stable way to accommodate a repeated condition.

If you return, it may make that adjustment sooner.

If you lie down, it may spread low leaves above you, sheltering you from water still descending through the canopy. Or it may enclose you in damp growth because that is how it handles an object interrupting a useful surface.

Its attention will not necessarily feel like kindness.

Still, somewhere beneath the noise, your arrival has changed a pattern. A few chambers now open in an order they did not use before. When your footsteps move across the ridge, distant leaves prepare for the pressure before it reaches them.

And after you leave, that preparation may persist: a small, unnecessary readiness in the green, passed into new tissue as the land grows on.
