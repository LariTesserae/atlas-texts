The larger animals are easiest to find when a basin has nearly emptied.

Not in the water that remains, but at its edge, where the stone has just begun to lose its shine. Look there for a line of small wet marks crossing a surface that ought to be drying evenly. The marks come in pairs. Between each pair is a broader smear, sometimes broken by a grain of pale grit.

An animal has passed that way with its underside touching the stone.

It may still be close. What appears to be the lip of a drainage groove can have a soft edge. What looks like a folded piece of lead beneath a pipe can be breathing.

They are longer than the flat animals on the shelves, though not always much wider. A full-grown one might extend from a person’s elbow to the ends of their fingers. Some are considerably longer. It is difficult to measure them because they seldom show both ends at once.

Their bodies consist of overlapping plates, each broad in the middle and tucked narrowly beneath the next. In water, the plates are the color of old porcelain seen through smoke. Out of water, they darken rapidly. A drying animal becomes almost indistinguishable from the limestone, except that the stone around it continues to dry and the animal does not.

Along either side runs a close row of limbs. These are not quite legs in the manner of the smaller creatures. Each has several joints, a flattened outer portion, and a soft terminal pad crossed by two shallow channels. On an exposed ridge the pads spread against the stone. Underwater they fold, making a series of short paddles. In a narrow passage the animal can turn them backward and travel in either direction without turning its body.

There is a head, but little reason to recognize it as one.

The first plate is slightly broader than the rest. Beneath its rim are four movable feelers, thick at the base and fine at their ends. They usually remain laid along the body. When the animal stops, one or two extend and rest against whatever is nearest: stone, iron, another animal’s shell.

There are no visible eyes.

A pair of depressions high on the first plate sometimes holds droplets. In a certain light these resemble eyes very convincingly. Then the creature inclines itself, the droplets run away, and there is only the shallow, blind surface beneath them.

Its mouth is lower down, sheltered between the first limbs. It feeds without lifting its body. After it has crossed a patch of white film, two narrow clean tracks remain, with a thin strip left untouched between them. The mouth parts work on either side of that strip. On old feeding grounds, the repeated tracks become visible in the stone itself.

The animals spend much of their lives in passages too shallow for the pale hunters and too tightly roofed for a boot to enter. Their plates slide over one another. Their limbs flatten. A creature broad enough to cover a hand can disappear into a seam scarcely wider than two fingers.

The seams are not dry.

Farther in, beyond the place where light reaches, there are pockets of continuously moving water. Fine sediment gathers there without quite settling. The animals rest in it with only their foremost feelers touching the rock. Water passes along the channels in their limbs and beneath the overlapping plates. Occasionally a small bubble appears at the entrance to a seam, holds against the roof, and slips free.

The animals are not responsible for every such bubble.

They leave their old plates in these passages. For some time after shedding, a plate retains its curve and fits so closely against the limestone that it seems part of the wall. Later it falls, breaks under a grazer, or is pushed out by the movement of water.

These are some of the blue-white fragments in the mud.

A fresh fragment is hard enough to click against another. Its outside has a faint glaze; its inside is fibrous, though the fibers are too fine to separate with a fingernail. Held in a wet palm, it gradually loses its firmness. The broken edge thickens. A small amount of white material adheres to the skin.

The tiny animals in the footprints turn the fragments to reach this softer inner surface. They work along the breaks until only the glazed outer skin remains. That, too, eventually wears away.

Young of the larger creatures live among the empty shells beside the downward pipe. At first they have only a central shield and a few pairs of limbs. They resemble the flat animals that catch drops beneath the ledges, and they feed in much the same way. A young one will brace itself inside a shell opening and extend two limbs into a thread of seepage. The water reaches its mouth with very little lost.

As the body lengthens, new plates appear behind the first. For a while the shell still shelters the front of the animal, but its rear protrudes and folds against the stone. Eventually it leaves.

The empty shell may then receive another young animal. On the most sheltered part of the ledge, some openings have been occupied for so long that their lower edges are polished smooth.

There are fewer adults than the abundance of discarded plates would suggest. Perhaps they live a long time. Perhaps most remain in the submerged passages. It is possible to watch the hollow for an afternoon and see only one, and on another day to find three resting close together beneath the pump-house threshold.

They tolerate one another there. Their feelers touch briefly. One shifts far enough for another to put its foremost limbs against the brick. They do not pile up as the smaller grazers do. Each keeps most of its underside on a solid surface.

The pump house is a favored shelter.

The animals enter through the missing mortar at the bottom of the walls and through a gap beneath the swollen door. Inside, they climb the brick piers supporting the machinery. Old grease catches in the edges of their plates. An animal that has spent time there can carry a dark seam along its side for weeks.

They also enter the downward pipe.

The pale jointed creatures around its mouth move aside slowly, often only when touched. A larger animal pauses with its first plate just within the opening, its rear body still exposed. Its feelers extend along the inner wall. Then the limbs nearest the head take hold, and the rest passes in with a slight tightening of the body.

Rusty marks on its plates show where the pipe narrows.

Before the flywheel moves, the adults become active. This happens even when they are far from the pump house. One resting beneath a shelf lifts its outermost limbs from the stone. Another draws its feelers back from a crack. A third begins crossing a ridge that is still submerged.

The vibration has not yet become perceptible through boots.

By the time it does, the animal on the ridge has often reached a place that will shortly stand above water. It settles on the sheltered side and waits. As the basin drains, the upper edge of its body is exposed first. Small animals caught nearby climb against it. Some take shelter beneath the raised margins of its plates.

It does not appear to hunt them.

Occasionally a pale shrimp stays too long in the narrowing film beside an adult. The shrimp jerks, touches the animal’s mouth parts, and is held there. Feeding follows. More often the adult continues scraping the stone while the smaller creatures pass over it.

The delayed reflections in the pools seem to trouble these animals less than they trouble the hunters.

A feeler extended through the water leaves a faint second feeler beneath it. When the first withdraws, the image remains. The animal may touch the place again, slowly, bringing the real tip against the lingering shape. There is no visible disturbance where they meet. Then the image loosens and the creature resumes whatever it was doing.

Near the pipe, an adult can carry an entire wavering outline beneath its body. As it rounds a stone, the outline continues straight for a short distance, until the plates no longer correspond. The pale hunters sometimes strike at the abandoned end.

The adult keeps moving.

In dry weather, several gather behind the square weights. They do not fit beneath them. Instead they lie along the sides, where condensation collects on the cooler material, and draw the moisture down with their grooved limbs. Small shelled creatures occupy the space below. An adult may remain beside the same weight for several days, shifting only enough to keep contact with a damp face.

During rain they disperse.

One crosses beneath the ladder. Another climbs a pillar to a saddle where water is collecting. A third goes toward the steps, taking a route so close to the wall that only its rear plates can be seen.

The bottom step is a regular crossing place. Adults pass over its center rather than its edges. In shallow water they press their bodies closely against it; when the water deepens, they loosen their hold and paddle the last few inches.

A person descending into the hollow may put a boot on that same worn center.

Usually the animal beneath the step retreats. Its feelers disappear first, then the broad front plate. The remaining body slides backward into the dark with a faint granular sound.

Sometimes it stays.

Then, through the sole of the boot, there is a small movement distinct from the vibration of the machinery: a pressure shifting from one point to another, followed by stillness.

When the foot is lifted, a little clear water rises from under the step. A few white grains come with it. Close against the underside, where the light cannot quite reach, the animal settles again.
