The woman in the end room sleeps with one foot outside the sheet. Even in sleep she finds the cooler part of the mattress, the strip beside the wall where the fan reaches after passing through the screen. Her name is Mara. Most people in the district call her by it; the newer drivers call her Pump, having mistaken an instruction to ask at the pump house for an instruction to ask for her.

She is not the engineer. An engineer comes twice a month, carrying a case that he keeps off the floor. Mara clears the intake screens, checks the sump, oils what the maintenance chart tells her to oil, and records the readings in a ruled book. She has keys to six doors and permission to open four of them. For the other two, she telephones.

Her hands are broad, with soft-looking palms and thickened fingertips. There is usually a narrow crescent of black beneath one nail, however carefully she washes. Small cuts take a long time to close here. She keeps them covered until the bandages grow loose with sweat, then replaces the bandages without looking much at the wounds.

She wears rubber sandals in her room and boots outside it. The boots stand upside down on two pegs beside the door. Before putting them on, she taps each heel against the concrete. Sometimes a cockroach drops out. Once there was a gecko, pale and indignant, which climbed her sleeve before she could shake it off.

Her room contains very little wood. A steel bed, a plastic chair, a narrow cupboard raised on bricks. Papers go in a biscuit tin with a sound lid. Rice goes in another. The distinction matters to her; visitors who lift the wrong lid are corrected immediately.

She has tried keeping photographs on the wall, but their edges curled and small dark marks appeared beneath the glass. Now the photographs lie between sheets of cooking paper inside the cupboard. She takes them out when someone asks after a person in them. Her daughter lives inland, above a shop that sells school uniforms. In the photograph the daughter stands on a road with a slope steep enough to be visible behind her. Mara always notices that slope before she notices the girl’s shoes or haircut.

She has lived here eleven years. Her first room was the middle one in the other row, where the roof leaked over the cooking shelf. When the end room became vacant, she moved in one afternoon with help from a freight clerk and his trolley. She likes having only one neighbor through the wall. She likes being able to see the pump-house door from her own.

At first she woke whenever the second motor started. Now she generally sleeps through it. What wakes her is an interval that lasts too long, a pause where there should have been a beat, or a new thin rattling laid over the familiar sound.

She does not leap up. She lies still and listens, separating the roof from the fan, the fan from the motors. Then she gets out of bed and puts on her boots.

In the pump house she touches the same things in the same order. Door frame, handrail, housing. Partly this is habit; partly she can tell through her palm whether a vibration has changed. She still takes the proper readings. If she reports a fault, she gives its number and the time she noticed it. She has learned that “it sounds wrong” travels badly through an office.

Her hearing is less good than it used to be. People sometimes mistake this for inattention. She turns her better ear toward them and watches their mouths. With someone she knows well, she lifts a hand to stop the conversation until the compressor has finished its hiss.

She is talkative during meals. She dislikes eating with anyone who treats a working lunch as another inspection. Given twenty minutes and a bowl of noodles, she wants news: who has left, who has been overcharged, whether the clerk’s mother has had her cataract done. She has definite opinions about all three.

The animals occupy smaller portions of her attention.

Pigeons are a nuisance because their droppings block things that should remain clear. Rats require action. Mosquitoes require action continually. The geckos she leaves alone, except when one settles inside the electrical cabinet. She coaxes those out with a folded maintenance notice, holding the paper flat against the wall until the animal steps onto it.

One gecko lives behind the cupboard in her room. It is missing the end of a toe. She recognizes it by that and by the way it waits beside the light switch, its head projecting beyond the cupboard’s edge. She has never named it. When visitors ask whether it is always there, she says, “Usually.”

Behind her room, she has placed an old shallow tray beneath the laundry. Clothespins fall into it instead of the ditch. After rain, mosquito larvae gather in the water, so she empties it every morning. The little fig’s leaves sometimes reach over the tray. When the maintenance crew cuts the tree back, she asks them to leave that branch if it is not touching the window.

She grows no vegetables. She tried once, in buckets. The leaves did well and the roots smelled bad. Now the buckets hold cleaning brushes and a coil of spare hose.

On her days off she takes the bus to see her daughter. She wears different sandals and carries food in a bag kept specifically for the journey. She enjoys the visit. She complains about the stairs. Late in the afternoon she checks the time more often, because missing one bus means missing the connection back.

She has considered moving. There is a room available near her daughter, with a window that opens over a courtyard. The rent is higher, though not impossibly so. She asked how much sun it received and whether the landlord repaired the roof promptly. Her daughter answered both questions. Nothing has been decided.

For now, Mara lives in the end room. She comes back from work, washes her feet, and sets a pot on the hotplate. Her trousers hang beside the door, drying slowly from the waistband down. Through the screen she can see a square of illuminated court and, when it is open, the dark mouth of the pump house.

While the food heats, she removes the photographs from their tin. Her daughter has sent a new one, printed at a shop: the upstairs room, still empty, with the window open. Mara looks at it for a while, then slides it under the others.

The gecko puts its head out beside the cupboard. She reaches past it to turn on the lamp.
