The people who live there can usually tell which level they are on with their eyes closed.

It is partly the air: the upper galleries smell of wet leaves and cold metal, the packing levels of bruised cabbage, the residential corridors of cooking and fabric that has not quite dried. But it is also the sound beneath those smells. A particular lift knocks twice before stopping. Water descending behind one wall makes a noise like someone sorting a drawer of cutlery. On another level, the pumps produce a pressure in the ears without any distinct sound at all.

Children learn these differences before they learn the district numbers. They describe their homes as being above the warm passage, past the shaking window, under the trees whose roots make the ceiling drip.

Their parents correct them when an address needs to be written down.

Most residents keep two kinds of footwear by the door. There are shoes for inside and boots for everything else, though “inside” is a matter of disagreement. A glazed stairwell is inside until its roof vents open. A greenhouse is inside, but nobody would cross one in slippers. Some apartment corridors have drains down their centers, and the people who live along them have developed small, persistent arguments about mats.

The apartments themselves are less damp than a visitor expects. Keeping them that way takes work. There are cupboards with warm backs, drying rails fed by otherwise wasted heat, little fans whose bearings everyone intends to replace next month. In older rooms, furniture stands a finger’s width from the walls. A chest pushed flush against concrete will leave a pale rectangular patch of mold behind it, and this is something a grandparent checks when visiting, sometimes without being asked.

People own bright things. Orange bowls, red blankets, a blue plastic stool repaired with wire. A window may be almost entirely occupied by a paper decoration faded on the outward side. Against so much gray and green, these objects seem emphatic, but they are not chosen for the landscape. They are wedding presents, purchases made on a trip, things children wanted.

Someone collects small porcelain animals and dusts them every Sunday. Someone has covered the drain inspection hatch in a bedroom with a rug, despite being told repeatedly not to.

***

A woman on the middle terraces grows berries for a living and roses badly.

Her crop beds are orderly, the ties adjusted before they cut into the stems, the nets repaired with small knots that scarcely catch the light. The roses stand in mismatched containers outside her kitchen. They get black spot. One has produced nothing for three years except a long, inconvenient cane that interferes with the window latch. She will not throw it out.

Her father brought the original cutting from somewhere beyond the rim. She does not remember the name of the place, although she remembers him washing the soil from his hands in their kitchen and being surprised that he had dirt under his nails. At home he worked on control systems and his hands were usually clean.

She knows exactly how much weight the terrace can carry. She has no idea what the rose wants.

Two levels beneath her, a boy is learning to repair electric motors. He dislikes the work so far. The experienced technicians touch a housing, listen briefly, and make useful statements; he listens and hears a motor. His instructor has stopped saying that the distinction will come with practice and now asks him to describe anything he notices, however foolish it sounds.

Last week he said a bearing sounded thirsty.

They took the assembly apart. He was right about where the fault was.

He has told this story several times, always with less hesitation.

There are residents who work outside the basin and leave early on the rim train. Their coats are lighter. They carry bags that would be awkward on a ladder. When they return, they sometimes stand a moment at the top of the descent, looking down as a visitor might. Then someone behind them asks to get past, and they move.

Not everyone who lives among the growing beds likes gardening. Not everyone knows how to clear a drain. Some people avoid maintenance shifts, complain about rail noise, or become genuinely angry when an emergency diversion leaves them taking four extra flights of stairs for a week. The basin’s old systems require cooperation, but they do not produce agreement.

There is a residents’ meeting where the same man raises the same objection to a proposed bridge. His objection is sound. Everyone is tired of hearing it.

***

The oldest inhabitants carry maps that no longer match the signs.

They remember a passage before it was partitioned into cold stores, a nursery where there are now filtration tanks, a stair that once continued down another three flights. When they give directions, they use these vanished places. Sometimes a younger person understands because the old name has survived. Sometimes it leads to a locked door.

One retired gate technician still wakes when the transfer cycle changes at night. He lies still, waiting for the second pump to start. If it does, he sleeps again. If it does not, he gets up and checks the public status display, though he has no authority to do anything about what it shows.

His wife sleeps through the pumps. She wakes at the soft scrape of a cup being taken from the kitchen shelf.

They have lived in the same rooms for forty-one years. Outside their window, a birch has been replaced twice. They refer to all three trees as the birch.

Children visit them because they keep sweets in an old fuse box with the terminals removed. The technician explains this removal to every child, every time.

***

Other residents are harder to count.

In the brought earth, worms live between the fibrous undersides of plants and the coarse drainage layers. They enter new beds in root balls and survive the periodic rearrangement of their world. A bed can travel half a kilometer on rails while everything within it continues eating, tunneling, and laying eggs.

Under loose bark, beneath stacked trays, in the warm insulation behind a poorly seated panel, there are creatures the inspections occasionally discover and usually fail to eliminate completely. Woodlice gather where two membranes overlap. A small spider maintains a web inside the sheltered angle of a signal housing. The web must be rebuilt whenever the cover is opened. It has been rebuilt many times.

The wagtail has a larger territory than anyone first supposes. People on several levels believe they see their own bird. It knows which channels expose insects when the gates change, and waits on the downstream side before the change happens. It has learned the brief click of a mechanism better than some of the trainees.

There are fewer animals than there would be in an unbuilt valley, but the ones present have intricate lives. A fly emerging near a heat exchanger encounters warmth, light, moving air, and danger in arrangements that did not exist for its ancestors. It finds a mate anyway.

In the tanks and filter beds, life is abundant enough to be measured in samples rather than sightings. Workers hold cloudy jars toward a light and discuss what is thriving, what is absent, what has become too numerous. Some organisms are cultivated with great care. Others are recognized only when they begin to obstruct something.

The basin depends on both kinds.

***

At the retaining wall beside the service road, the worker who clips the ferns is not trying to make them beautiful. She needs the inspection plate visible, and space enough around it to work without wet fronds pressing against her wrist.

She cuts a little farther back on the hinge side.

There is a small snail behind one stem. She moves it to the uncut section with the flat of her glove. This takes perhaps three seconds. She does not think of it again.

Later, after she has gone home, the snail begins working its way across the wet wall. The pump starts. The remaining fronds tremble, and a bead of water falls close enough to make it withdraw.

It waits.

Inside the wall, water moves uphill. Below it, windows brighten. Someone opens a cupboard to check whether a shirt is dry; someone turns a rose pot a few degrees toward the last of the light. The snail extends itself again and continues along the seam.
