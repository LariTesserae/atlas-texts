The largest inhabitant is easiest to mistake for something that has already been repaired.

At the foot of one wall, between a ceramic ring and the blackened end of a rib, there is a length of pale material. It resembles a strip of soaked leather pushed into a gap to stop a leak. Grit adheres to it. Three small shell-bearing animals rest along its upper edge.

Watch it through several rounds of knocking.

When water arrives in the seam above it, the strip thickens slightly. A crease opens along its length. The shell animals shift their feet but do not withdraw. After the water has passed, the crease closes, and the strip is again an unremarkable piece of packing.

Elsewhere, something similar lies behind a knot of fiber. Elsewhere still, a pale welt follows the underside of a slab. These parts do not move together. Sometimes one is swollen while another has sunk so far into its crack that only its gritty surface remains visible.

They belong to the same animal.

Most of it lives beneath the floor, in the spaces between the older tiles and the buried jars. Its body is broad and very thin, folded wherever the available space narrows. It has no single outline that could be drawn without first taking the court apart. A thick portion lies beneath the pool. From this extend soft, flattened lengths that pass around jar shoulders, through broken necks, and up behind the lowest courses of the wall.

Some end blindly. Others divide. A few can be withdrawn, slowly, and put out through a neighboring opening. These are the parts most often mistaken for lashings.

Its skin is not uniformly pale. Where it lies against fired clay, it takes on a rusty stain. Where fibers press into it, the surface develops narrow ridges. A portion sheltered inside a glazed vessel may remain almost translucent. You can sometimes see a slow contraction passing through it, pushing a cloudy fluid past little dark bodies embedded beneath the skin.

Those dark bodies are organs, though they have the look of grit swallowed by accident. They differ from one part of the creature to another. Some shiver when the tail-knockers call. Some turn faintly red when cold water passes overhead. Others do nothing that can be seen.

There is no obvious head.

At feeding places, however, the body develops temporary mouths. A wet fold presses against the underside of a busy seam and parts into several lips, each no wider than a broken thumbnail. The lips take what washes down: softened fiber, scraps of shell, dead grazers, the skins shed by the six-legged animals. They close around these things and remain closed for hours.

Living animals ordinarily cross the folds unharmed. The mouths are too slow to catch them, and the creature seems unwilling to make the effort. A tail-knocker can shelter in a hollow pressed into its side and beat against the wood above without being disturbed.

This arrangement is not always harmless. A shell animal caught beneath a swelling fold may be carried into a crack from which it cannot escape. And when the court has gone too long without a good fall of water, the little mouths remain open. The seam-walkers then take another route.

The inhabitant has no name among the other animals. They recognize particular parts of it: a warm shelter, a yielding obstacle, a surface that must be crossed quickly. None can encounter enough of it to recognize the whole.

You cannot encounter the whole either.

Even the exposed folds are misleading. The creature keeps a layer of collected material over much of its living skin. Clay dust settles into its mucus. Fragments of fiber lodge there. Shed shells overlap until a patch becomes almost firm. When water presses through a damaged joint, the animal pushes one of these coated folds into the opening and leaves it there.

This can hold a crack closed for years.

The repair benefits the animal first. Behind it are chambers the creature needs to keep wet, and passages through which its own substance must move. But smaller inhabitants soon occupy the repaired place. They scrape it, foul it, drag fibers across it. Sometimes their traffic wears through the coating and reveals the tender skin beneath. Then the fold withdraws, and the repair fails with a soft sound, much quieter than the collapse of clay.

The creature does not maintain everything. It has abandoned the dry side of several ribs. It cannot reach the highest lashings. Beneath the new ceramic flakes, one of its ends has been pinned and cut off from the rest. The severed piece still contracts when struck by a drop.

It may do so for a season.

At long intervals, the inhabitant moves more decisively. A portion beneath the pool loosens from the older tiles. Water becomes cloudy. Pale folds appear in holes where there were none before, while familiar ones shrink away. The small animals are displaced from their shelters and crowd into neighboring seams.

By the next long quiet, the court has acquired a slightly different interior.

No one sees the movement itself. It is too slow, and too much of it takes place underneath things. You notice only that a shell cluster now sits higher, that a formerly reliable drip has stopped, or that one slab gives a softer report underfoot.

The animal listens through much of its body. A knock conducted along wood reaches it differently from one arriving through a buried pot. It can distinguish where a sound began, whether water followed it, whether a passage has emptied or remained full. Its responses are slow enough that they seldom look like responses. A fold may rise into a channel long after the calling animal has gone.

It does not conduct the court’s noise. It depends on the noise continuing.

When the great pot at the doorway sounds, the inhabitant becomes still.

This is easiest to see at the pool, where a thin edge of its body lies beneath the lip of a slab. Usually that edge pulses, almost imperceptibly. During the pot’s note, it flattens. The grit on it stops shifting. Even after the other animals resume their scraping and knocking, it remains withdrawn for a little while.

There is a part of the pot it touches: the outside of the bowl, far back in the sealed doorway, where nobody standing in the court could see. A narrow fold lies against the ceramic there. It has no clay coating and no feeding lips. Its surface is dense with the small dark organs that shiver at sound.

Perhaps the pot tells it something the rest of the court cannot.

Its young are easier to recognize as animals. They appear occasionally in the central pool: pale, broad swimmers, each with a thicker middle and a thin, wavering margin. For a few days they live in open water. They gather beneath falling drops, turn over when a loud report passes through the floor, and feed on the cloudy matter stirred from the bottom.

Then they find edges.

One enters beneath a slab. Another slips into the hollow of a ceramic ring. Most disappear without establishing themselves. Those that survive flatten and lengthen. Their margins explore narrow gaps. Their temporary swimming bodies become bodies suited to a place.

There may be several adults beneath the court. Two folds meeting inside a wall sometimes adhere so completely that no boundary remains visible. At other times they recoil from one another, leaving a dry interval between them.

The handprints above the waterline are not theirs. The animal’s folds have no fingers, and its skin cannot bear the heat that fired those panels. Those marks belong to whoever made the court, when its clay was soft and its surfaces accessible.

Yet beneath one of the smallest prints, a pale end has pressed itself into the shallow palm. It rests there during the quiet between knocks. Water beads around it without washing it away.

When the channels begin to fill again, it draws back into the wall, slowly enough that the hand seems occupied for a moment after it has gone.
