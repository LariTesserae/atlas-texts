The first sign of it is a place where the water will not go.

A drop comes down from the condenser, strikes the incline, and divides. One part runs toward the basin. The other follows a seam until it reaches a patch of ceramic scarcely larger than a fingernail. There it spreads flat, loses its shine, and is gone. The patch remains damp after the rest of the seam has dried.

Under the maintenance lamp, its surface looks faintly stippled. You might take the stippling for pores in the ceramic, except that it continues across a blue inspection mark and into the gap between two plates.

It was put into the chamber as a clear gel.

The cartridge label called it a substrate-conditioning culture. There were instructions for keeping it above a minimum temperature during installation, and a warning not to flush it into the municipal drains. The technician squeezed six measured portions into the nutrient sockets around the plants. For several weeks it stayed where it had been placed.

Then a pale thickness appeared inside the socket nearest the heating filament. It climbed the root of a plant without entering it, reached the underside of a plate, and spread out there. No one recorded the moment when it first crossed a seam.

It has a body, although there is nowhere you could point and say that all of it is there.

Some of its body is a transparent lining in the ceramic pores. Some hangs in the narrow spaces below the plates, folded around hoses and piston stems. In places it thickens into little gray cushions that retain water when the basin is drained. These are soft enough to collapse under a fingertip. Left undisturbed, they recover very slowly, drawing themselves up into low domes.

The living surface carries thousands of short filaments. They are too fine to look like hair unless several catch a drop together. Each filament can bend toward moisture, withdraw from freezing water, or fasten its tip to a rough surface. Their movements are usually hidden by changes in the water around them.

It does not travel by lifting its body and setting it down elsewhere. It fills a little more of a pore. It leaves a little less of itself behind.

For a long time, its whole range lay between the warm filament and the nearest two plants. It lived on what leaked from the nutrient sockets: salts, sugars, fragments of old roots. Dead blades freezing against the floor gave it a way to cross ground otherwise too dry. It grew along their undersides, sheltered between plant fibre and ceramic.

The flattened collar of old growth is now occupied. Lift a blade, and a translucent thread stretches with it. The thread parts without a sound. Both broken ends close.

The cold has made it economical. Its outermost portions can freeze almost completely. Thin walls divide the water in its tissues into chambers so small that ice cannot tear very far through them. When warmth returns, some chambers swell again. Others remain white and empty, and become passages through which new growth advances.

It has consequently accumulated a great deal of itself that is no longer alive but is still useful.

There are routes under the floor lined with these dead chambers. Water enters them readily. Nutrients travel farther through them than they would through the original ceramic. At two points near the basin, the lining has become thick enough to obstruct a pressure port. The service log attributes the resulting fluctuations to mineral fouling.

The obstruction is irregular. When the water falls, it tightens.

That may be the beginning of something more than growth.

The chamber changes beneath it. A plate lifts, and the soft lining along its edge stretches. A neighboring plate tilts, crushing part of a passage. Each refill floods some routes and leaves others dry. Portions of the body that have never been close to the heating filament receive warmth for a few minutes; portions beside it lose their water.

At first, the culture answered these changes locally. A wet place grew. A dry place contracted.

Now a pressure applied near the ridge can produce a contraction beside the basin, several minutes later. Something passes through the body in a sequence of small changes. The cushions flatten one after another. Filaments withdraw. Water is pressed along a passage and held at its far end.

There is no central organ directing this. There are junctions that have become thicker through repeated use, and routes that have survived because they lead somewhere useful.

One junction lies beneath the rising plate.

The creature has occupied its underside so thoroughly that the plate no longer rests quite as the designers intended. A pad of living tissue presses against the framework. Another has entered the clearance around the piston. Neither is strong enough to lift the ground. Both are capable of changing what the pressure sensor reports.

The actuator makes a correction.

The pad relaxes.

Hours later, it swells again.

This has been happening for months. The adjustments are tiny, and several have been reversed by the chamber’s controls. But the result is visible in the narrowing gap. The plate is rising toward a ledge where moisture collects and the mineral paste, still fresh, can be penetrated by the creature’s filaments.

Along the ridge, it now lives just beneath the chalk.

The nozzles cover it a little at a time. It grows through each deposit before the next arrives, leaving pale branching hollows behind it. The scanner measures the outside. It does not measure the wet tissue protected within.

From there, the creature has access to a colder region than any it has occupied before. It advances only during the lamp’s eleven minutes, when the surface warms enough to release a film of water. After the lamp goes out, its exposed filaments retract into their holes.

Some fail to retract. Under magnification, the ridge is strewn with their broken tips.

It continues.

There is no reason to give it a face. The tufts at the water’s edge are plants, and the dark mark on one mineral knob is a stain from a corroding fastener. It has no mouth hidden in the drain, no eyes waiting for the hatch to open. Its attention, if that is what the linked contractions amount to, is spread through the surfaces it touches.

An opening hatch reaches it as warmth, vibration, then a sudden abundance of water. Boots send pressure through the plates. A changed canister alters the composition of the next few drops. It has begun to respond differently to each of these events.

When a technician descends, the nearest cushions empty themselves before the basin rises. Water moves into the passages beneath the ridge. The creature holds it there through the visit, sometimes for hours.

After the hatch closes, growth begins.

At present it cannot get beyond the chamber. The ladder is dry. The walls are too cold, and the gap around the hatch contains no continuous film of moisture. A few filaments have reached the foot of a metal panel and died there.

Most of it remains underfoot, folded into the machinery’s clearances, waiting through the long intervals between drops.

The bead stopped at the narrowing gap is touching it now. Several filaments have extended into the water. They fasten to the opposite plate and draw taut as the actuator moves.

For a while the bead holds its shape. Then its surface sinks between the fresh edges, feeding a passage that did not exist at the last refill.

Farther down, beneath the chalky ledge, a cushion begins to swell.
