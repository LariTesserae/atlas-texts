The oldest living thing in a garden is usually the part you step over to reach the fronds.

It lies close to the stone: a cushion of folded leaves, gray at its outer edges and almost black in the seams. Dust has filled its creases. Several other plants may have rooted through it, and a fallen slab may divide it into what appear to be separate growths. But beneath the slab, inside the rock, its living threads remain connected.

The upright stems are temporary. They rise where nourishment is plentiful, stand for a few dozen years, then lean and disappear among the lower leaves. The cushion continues. It can lose one side to exposure and extend the other into a crack without moving its center appreciably. In a sufficiently old garden, the tallest growth belongs to organisms younger than the small, compressed leaves around their feet.

No one leaf lasts very long. The enduring thing is an arrangement: sheltered folds, feeding threads, passages through which thick sap travels so slowly that a change at one edge may take days to reach the other.

When the wind begins stripping dust from the western side, the eastern leaves open to catch it. They do not respond to the wind directly. Something has passed through the cushion ahead of them.

***

The six-legged grazers come in several forms, though at a distance they all look like pieces of the plateau that have acquired the ability to choose their shade.

The commonest is broad at the front and narrow behind. Its plates overlap loosely enough to admit mineral powder, which becomes packed into their surfaces. A young animal is faintly translucent along the plate edges. An old one is opaque, chalk-white, stained green in the joints.

Its feet are more delicate than its armored back suggests. Each ends in two curved points and a soft central pad, which it places against the stone before committing its weight. On open ground it walks with three feet down at a time. Among leaves it may keep five anchored while the sixth searches ahead.

It can remain halfway through a step for a very long while.

There are no visible eyes. Along the forward edge of its body lie several dark pits, recessed beneath a shelf of armor. These detect light, but their more important work seems to be reading differences in heat. A sheltered crack, a cold leaf, the slightly warmer mouth of a vapor seam: the grazer distinguishes them before it reaches them. Two short feelers beneath its mouth continually touch the surface ahead.

At a violet sac it becomes unexpectedly precise.

The hooks enter on either side of the stalk. The animal holds still until the sac relaxes around them, then cuts a narrow opening in the skin. It takes the dense contents in small portions. Feeding may occupy most of an afternoon. When it withdraws, the empty casing is still attached, and the neighboring sacs have drawn close against the branch.

The shrub tolerates this loss. Some of its finest reproductive bodies pass through the grazer unchanged, wrapped in material that will protect them during a crossing. Others adhere beneath the animal’s plates. A grazer arriving at a new hollow carries a faint collection of the places it has already visited.

Its armor also carries less welcome passengers.

***

Under the leaf cushions live pale, jointed creatures no longer than a finger’s width. Their bodies are narrow enough to enter the grooves left by roots, and their backs are covered in fine bristles that hold a permanent coat of powder.

You almost never see one whole.

A hooked forelimb appears beneath a leaf. A length of body follows, then slides backward. Sometimes several emerge around a dead stem, working at its waxy skin. They cut it into strips and draw the strips into the dark.

They feed on the material that the larger growth discards: shed fibers, empty sacs, old leaf surfaces, the soft lining of a dead grazer’s joints. Much of this material is difficult to break down. Inside each small creature is a community of still smaller life that does the slow chemical work. A fragment of leaf can remain within the body for weeks.

Their dwellings are lined with compacted waste. Over many generations, the undersides of cushions acquire a network of smooth-walled tunnels. These hold warmth, intercept powder, and divert the bitter vapors rising through the rock. A plant growing above such a network often has broader leaves than its neighbors.

The little inhabitants defend their passages by plugging them.

At the approach of a grazer, they draw tight bundles of fiber into the entrances and retreat deeper. The grazer sometimes tears open the outer chambers with its hooks, taking a mouthful of inhabitants along with their stored food. Afterward, the survivors repair the damage. By the next morning the breached opening may be difficult to find.

***

The winged animals are the young of one of these hidden kinds.

For most of their lives they are long, flat creatures moving through the lining of the tunnels. Then, during a season of sustained mineral vapor, some become darker and cease feeding. They climb into the folded bases of leaves. There they open membranes which have been packed along their bodies in very fine pleats.

A newly emerged one looks too large for the space it came out of.

Its wings are broad and nearly colorless, supported by branching amber ribs. The body hangs between them like a small piece of charred fiber. It cannot rise far by its own effort. Instead, it waits at the edge of a frond for air moving upward, makes a few slow strokes, and lets the current take it.

In the cold morning light, a dozen may be suspended above a garden, each correcting its position with movements too small to hear.

They carry their nourishment with them in a swelling at the base of the body. Once airborne, they do not feed. Some descend within a few paces of where they began. Others are lifted beyond the hollow and vanish over bare country.

A dust front can carry them for days.

Most are lost. Some settle in old gardens whose feeding seams have gone quiet. A few reach living cushions and work their way beneath the outer folds. Their wings come off along prepared lines. The amber ribs remain caught among the leaves long after their owners have entered the stone.

On certain plants, those shed ribs make a delicate litter, like the remnants of many unsuccessful attempts to draw the same branching shape.

***

There are predators, but not many, and they are easiest to find by waiting near an empty sac.

One resembles a fallen length of frond. It is green on one side, silver on the other, with four limbs folded so closely against its body that they look like leaf veins. It attaches itself to a stem and turns when the surrounding blades turn.

Its mouth opens sideways.

It takes the small tunnel dwellers when they come to salvage the casing. After a successful catch, it may not move again for several days. During that interval the sac shrivels, the plant changes its angle to the light, and the predator adjusts with it, retaining its place in the silhouette.

Occasionally it chooses a grazer instead of a stem. It clings to the sheltered underside of a rear plate, feeding on the little passengers gathered there. The grazer gains nothing obvious from this arrangement, but seems not to notice it.

Beyond the gardens, beneath loose slabs, are heavier hunters. Their bodies are flattened and their limbs short. They wait beside the folds used by migrating grazers, where the rock releases warmth after sunset.

They do not chase.

A grazer can pass within a few body lengths and continue unharmed. The hunter spends its stored energy only when contact is nearly certain. A failed strike may cost it the opportunity to feed that season.

The old ones bear scars along their mouths where armor has broken against them.

***

Young grazers have no armor worth speaking of.

They hatch beneath the parent’s rear plates, in a shallow chamber lined with discarded leaf fiber. For a time they travel there, feeding on a paste the adult brings back from the violet sacs. When they begin walking, their backs are soft and yellow-gray, and their legs appear too long.

They stay inside the garden through several changes of the fronds.

Mineral enters their plates gradually. The first deposits form small islands, then join. At the seams the armor remains thin, allowing growth. A juvenile that ventures onto open stone before this process is complete can lose too much of its internal fluid to return.

Adults sometimes gather at the edge of a hollow before a crossing. They do not necessarily depart together. One leaves at dusk; another remains for several nights; a third feeds again and turns back into the garden.

Those that go follow the shallowest shelter available.

Their routes persist beyond the lifetimes of individual animals. Feet have roughened strips of polished stone beneath certain overhangs. On a long crossing, these footholds may be the only clear sign that another grazer has ever passed that way.

Some routes lead to basins that are no longer green.

An animal arriving there explores the root grooves, tests the scattered plants, and settles beneath a ledge. It may wait through an entire cold season before moving on. The remnants can sustain it, though not enough to rebuild what the journey has used.

In old, empty hollows, chalk-colored plates lie among the fallen stones. Wind eventually turns them over. The thinner pieces break first.

***

Late in the day, the living depth of a garden contracts.

The winged animals descend. The small scavengers draw their stores inward. Grazers find places where their plates fit against the rock, leaving almost no dark line underneath.

Above them, the blades turn and fold. Their pale undersides vanish one by one.

Below, the roots remain active. Sap passes through narrow living channels. A tunnel wall is scraped clean. Somewhere inside the stone, a growing tip reaches a boundary between mineral grains and divides, sending one thread along each side.

By morning, neither thread will have traveled far enough to see.

The cushion above them may have closed over the same crack for centuries. It still has room to grow.
