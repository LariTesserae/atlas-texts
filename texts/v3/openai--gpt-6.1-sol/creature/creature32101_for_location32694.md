The people who live here know one another partly by their footsteps.

There is a woman whose left heel comes down hard, a boy who takes the stairs two at a time until he reaches the flight that sounds beneath the court, and an old man who pauses before every landing. In mist, when the galleries vanish beyond their nearest posts, these habits tell the town who is abroad. A stranger is recognizable by stopping at the loud stair and looking down.

Children learn to cross that stair quietly. Later, carrying something heavy, they have to learn it again.

There are not enough people to occupy all the buildings. Some households use three or four of them: one for sleeping, one for cooking, a dry bay for wool, another for tools that would rust beside the channels. Even so, many roofs shelter only a bench or a length of coiled rope. A visitor can mistake this for abundance. The inhabitants know how far a roof’s usefulness depends on the wind.

A room may be comfortable all winter and become uninhabitable in spring when a rising fold of stone lifts one corner. Its occupants move their bedding across a bridge. They leave the door hanging, because the room may settle into usefulness again, or someone may want it for another purpose.

People speak of these moves as they speak of changes in weather.

“We were sleeping above the lower basin then.”

“That was before the kitchen tipped.”

Their memories have addresses that no longer exist in quite the same shape.

The woman splitting willow has lived here long enough to remember when there were no bridges. Her name is Sela. She keeps a knife with a handle worn thin on one side, and turns every strip of willow through her fingers before adding it to a frame. A damaged strip will sometimes hold for years inside a hardened wall; a sound one may snap while she is weaving it. She dislikes having either fact offered to her as wisdom.

Her youngest brother went down the mountain nineteen years ago. Occasionally he sends iron, lamp oil, or cloth in colors that look startling beside wet wood. She stores his letters in a shallow box whose lid has warped. She has built a room for him twice. The first is now a passage, and the second has stone nearly up to its window.

She does not call the present empty room his.

Others are less restrained. They allocate whole galleries to absent daughters, cousins, people who have promised to return with spouses. One man has been preparing a work bay for a cooper for so long that he has learned to repair barrels himself. He still thinks a cooper would do well here.

There are also structures whose intended occupants have never been named. Their builders can give practical reasons for them: the supports had to go in while the ground was firm; a rising surface would be easier to manage beneath a roof; timber already carried this far should not be left stacked indefinitely. These reasons are true. The roofs nevertheless make the town appear to be waiting.

Few of its inhabitants admit to waiting for the same thing.

The people who tend the stone are not a separate order. The person measuring a new fold in the morning may spend the afternoon washing cabbage or mending a sleeve. Certain hands are sought out for certain judgments, however.

Sela can judge when a wicker frame should be loosened. A broad-shouldered man who rarely speaks is good at placing his palm against a wet surface and deciding whether it will bear weight. The oldest woman in town remembers where water used to emerge, which is useful when a channel dwindles without warning.

They disagree often. A fence may remain around a patch of soft stone for an extra season because no one wishes to take responsibility for removing it. Elsewhere, people step over the cord every day until somebody quietly takes it down.

Their work leaves marks on their bodies. They have strong fingers, tender knees, and small pale scars across the backs of their hands. Some develop a stoop from inspecting the undersides of floors. Those who walk the channels have one shoulder higher than the other from carrying a scoop and bucket. They dry their socks near the cooking fires and occasionally burn them.

They are attentive without being solemn. Someone who examines a surface too long will be told that staring does not make it harden. Someone who fails to examine it may return to find a wedge laid conspicuously beside their bowl.

Meals are the best time for talk. Kitchens are small, crowded, and padded with hanging wool, so a voice stays near its speaker. There the town becomes more ordinary: people interrupt, complain about the cabbage, repeat news from below, ask who took a particular knife. Out in the court, where a dropped utensil can overwhelm a sentence, the same people seem reserved.

Their children spend a great deal of time in these kitchens. They also play in the empty market bays, assigning each other wares that do not exist. A girl sells handfuls of warmth. A boy has a stall of names for the grey bird. Disputes over prices are conducted at close range.

The smallest children are forbidden to push anything through the grilles beneath the terraces. Older ones know which openings return a pebble’s sound at once and which hold it long enough to make waiting unpleasant. They are occasionally caught conducting experiments. It is difficult to hide an experiment that the entire town can hear.

Something also lives in the mountain, though the inhabitants do not agree on what that means.

The black wet stone is warm in a few places through winter. There are folds that rise steadily for decades, and others that remain unchanged until a wooden support beside them is removed. A hollow under the eastern terrace sometimes draws air inward during perfectly still weather. Moss at its mouth lies flat against the grille; a loose thread points into the dark.

None of this establishes a creature.

The inhabitants have examined fallen pieces, when pieces can be obtained. These dry, harden, and become much like other stone. There is no blood, no fiber that can certainly be called flesh. Split open, a piece may contain a pale seam or a small water-filled pocket. People keep such pieces on shelves, then gradually put them to use as weights.

Yet the growth is difficult to regard as entirely indifferent.

Near the central swelling, frames filled from the same mineral seep have hardened at markedly different rates. A stair repaired three times has continued to lift at one end while the adjoining wall scarcely changes. Water has appeared beneath a new basin before its channel was completed. The person who built the basin considers this good fortune. Someone else says the site was chosen because it had always been damp.

The mountain gives them ample time to argue. Most changes are too slow to witness. A person presses a thumb against a surface in childhood and returns years later to find the hollow of that pressure still there, carried upward beside the imprint of a leaf.

Those marks trouble people more than the sounds.

Sound can be explained by chambers, slits, falling water. The marks preserve occasions no one meant to preserve: a hand braced against wet paste, a knee slipping, a reed laid crooked by a tired worker. Old walls contain the gestures of people whose tools have passed to grandchildren. Sela can identify her father’s palm in a wall behind the long building. She puts a basket in front of it when visitors ask to see.

There are inhabitants who talk to the stone while working. Usually they ask it to stay still until a fastening is done. Some apologize when driving in stakes. Others object to this habit, but speak sharply to a stubborn door themselves.

Whatever is alive within the cup receives all of it: the stakes, the water channels, the scraps of willow enclosed in walls, the weight of sleeping households. Its hollows carry their dropped buckets and their hammers. It rises beneath their rooms.

If it has a pace of its own, a human lifetime is enough to notice only a little of it.

At evening, the person clearing the channels is usually accompanied for part of the round by a child carrying an empty bucket. The bucket is unnecessary until something blocks a grille or a junction must be drained. The child carries it seriously anyway.

They work inward as the mist comes over the rim. Leaves go into the bucket; water resumes its narrow course. At each junction the older person waits to see that the flow continues before moving on.

Across the court, lamps appear in a few of the many doorways. Supper smells reach the galleries. Someone calls a name, receives no answer, and walks closer to call again.

The central stone is left bare. No lamp is set on it, though it would be a convenient place for one. The last person crossing the court sometimes rests a hand there, testing the dampness or the temperature, then goes inside.

During the night, water continues falling into the chambers below. The timber cools. A sleeper wakes at a sound from beneath the floor and listens long enough to decide whether anyone else has risen.

Usually no one has.

In the morning there will be channels to clear, a door to ease upward on its lashings, perhaps a new space beneath a beam where yesterday there was none. People will bring their tools out into the bright court. They will look, confer, and begin adjusting what they have made around what has grown.
