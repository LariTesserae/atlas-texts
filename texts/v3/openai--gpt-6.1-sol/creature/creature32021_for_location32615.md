The first person you meet has a cord tied around her waist.

It trails behind her over the frost, vanishing into a groove. She stops when she sees you, puts down the bundle she is carrying, and pulls twice on the cord. After a moment it goes slack. Someone out of sight has answered.

She is not particularly small, but everything about her has been gathered close. Her sleeves narrow at the wrist. Her boots have no loose straps. A hood covers her ears and the sides of her face, leaving an opening just large enough to breathe through. Even her bundle is wrapped so that no corner can catch the wind.

When she approaches, she does not look first at your face. She looks at your feet.

You are standing across a faint line in the ground. It resembles a scratch. She takes your elbow and moves you away from it. Only after you have stepped aside does she kneel and brush off the frost.

The line is opening.

There is no alarm in her manner. She takes a little fork of horn from her belt and presses it into the seam. It enters farther than she expects. She leaves it there, ties a pale knot to its handle, and retrieves her bundle.

The cord tightens. She follows it home.

***

The people here are not born knowing how to live at a height.

Small children crawl toward an edge as readily as toward a warm lamp. They pull at warning knots. They put their fingers into the soft red tissue of a new split.

For their first years they wear harnesses woven from the toughest fibers their families can obtain. The fastening lies between the shoulder blades, where the child cannot reach it. At home the line is attached to a roof rib. Outside, an adult holds it. In a busy household there may be three children moving around the same anchor, their cords lifted and exchanged by someone who continues talking throughout.

Children learn to untangle themselves before they learn to tie a reliable knot.

They are taught to fall on their bellies, arms wide. They practice on slopes where the frost has been scraped away. Later they practice with mittens, with a burden, with their eyes closed. An older child may knock them sideways without warning. The game ends as soon as anyone begins crying; it resumes another day.

Adults fall this way too. A person who slips in conversation can be flat against the ground before the listener has noticed anything wrong.

Their palms are thickened. Their knees rarely heal entirely. They keep their fingernails short because a torn nail can make a climbing hand useless.

Most have an old injury that is troublesome in cold weather. The injuries are often mundane: a cooking burn, a cut from a stone blade, a shoulder damaged while hauling water. Falls severe enough to leave remarkable scars seldom leave survivors.

***

A household may contain people who are not related, and be missing several who are.

Routes determine who can live together. A marriage can begin with a seasonal crossing and continue for years through shouted conversations across a widening gap. Children are sent to stay with relatives before a passage becomes uncertain. Sometimes the passage remains open. Sometimes a visit becomes a childhood.

People distinguish carefully between someone who died, someone who fell, and someone whose way closed.

A woman in one settlement has not touched her brother for eleven winters. She can see his shelter on certain clear mornings. He has repaired the roof twice. Once she watched him carry a child outside.

When the air is still, they communicate with pale cloths stretched on frames. Most messages are simple enough to recognize at a distance: illness, birth, visitors, need for cord. Longer ones require a sequence, and patience. A shift in the wind may end a conversation halfway through.

She keeps the unfinished messages on strips of bark.

Her brother has asked about their mother’s death three times. The account is difficult to send across so much air.

***

There are occupations that would be hard to name elsewhere.

Some people tend drinking hollows. They remove dead insects before a thaw, shelter the ice from drifting dust, and notice when the bitterness changes. A good pool keeper can detect a leak from the leaf before the water acquires a color.

Some walk the paths solely to examine them. They carry wedges, cord, and a narrow scraper. They renew marks and take away misleading ones. On returning, they describe a route by what it is doing: lifting at the third rib, sinking beyond the dark pool, drawing apart where the moth cracks begin.

One elderly man remembers paths that have not existed for forty years. He is useful because the plant sometimes repeats a movement. A crease that closed in his youth may open again.

He cannot always separate the old route from the current one. Someone younger walks with him and writes down both.

Other people enter the stems.

They go through old scars or openings enlarged by burrowers. Inside, they collect resin, pale fungi, and tough sheets of membrane useful for waterproofing. They work in warmth that makes their outdoor clothing unbearable. The air smells sweet at first and then sickening.

They listen for chewing.

A feeding burrower may pass within an arm’s length on the other side of a thin wall. It is usually harmless. The danger comes when it opens a channel full of sap, or when the channel it has abandoned collapses beneath a person.

Stem workers return sweating into the cold. Their families meet them with dry clothes and hot water, and examine their hair for the small white animals that cling there.

***

The grazers are called by different names on different leaves. They answer to none of them.

Up close, their coats contain several kinds of hair: a dense dark underfur, pale stiff strands, and long sparse filaments that tremble even when the animal stands still. Their eyes face partly downward. The muzzle is divided by a deep groove, allowing the mouth to scrape along either side of a narrow ridge.

A feeding band proceeds slowly. Those at the front test the tissue. Those behind enlarge the exposed patches. The youngest eat last, taking softened material from the edges.

They sleep pressed together along warm veins, with their feet beneath them and their heads pointing in several directions. A sleeper on the outside is regularly nudged into the middle. By morning the band may have shifted many body lengths without fully waking.

People take their shed wool and their dung. They sometimes kill one, but not on an open feeding ground. Blood brings down the long-winged hunters, and a frightened band can sweep a person over an edge.

A few grazers tolerate people near their resting places. They will accept bundles of scraped tissue. They will permit a hand against the shoulder.

They still leave when the ground cools.

Families that have fed the same band for years can wake to find it gone, the hollow empty except for warm depressions and a little frost beginning between them.

***

Under the leaves live animals the people seldom see whole.

Their hooked feet can grip the smallest irregularities in the lower skin. They move from vein to vein with their bodies hanging beneath, testing each new hold before releasing the last. Their long tails can take their weight briefly, though they seem reluctant to use them for this.

In daylight they flatten against the underside and are difficult to distinguish from patches of dead tissue. At night they feed among the hanging mosses. Some eat insects. Larger ones strip the soft outer layers from the veins.

Their young are born with oversized feet. For the first weeks they cling beneath the mother, sharing her holds where they can reach them.

People know when a family is living below a house. There is scratching under the floor, an occasional thump, and a sour smell rising through cracks. In severe cold the animals gather directly beneath the hearth.

Most households allow this. They believe the animals notice failing tissue early.

When the scratching ceases, someone goes outside with a lamp.

***

The long-winged hunters look ungainly when perched.

Their wings fold into several overlapping lengths, the tips protruding well past the tail. They brace themselves against the bark with a short additional claw at each wrist. Their faces are narrow, with nostrils protected by flexible ridges.

They spend hours watching an apparently vacant volume of air.

A falling animal gives them time. A hunter may leave its ledge, descend below the body, and turn upward to meet it. Smaller prey disappear into the feet. A grazer is too heavy to carry, but the hunter can alter its fall, steering it toward a lower platform.

Some ledges beneath favored perches are littered with bones.

People examine these places when the birds are away. Among familiar remains they occasionally find a jaw or a joint that belongs to nothing living on the inhabited levels. Such pieces travel from settlement to settlement. Arguments follow them.

The hunters are reaching somewhere the people cannot.

***

There are fish in the enclosed water channels.

They are long, almost transparent, with blunt heads and little brushes around their mouths. In an exposed pool they lie against the bottom, scarcely moving. Lifted into lamplight, they show a narrow dark gut and a slow red pulse behind the gills.

Nobody has seen them breed.

After rain, children gather them from the grooves. They must be cooked promptly: their flesh becomes bitter as it cools. A few are returned to sheltered hollows, although they do not thrive there.

The fish disappear into the leaf whenever they can find an opening.

Sometimes, during a quiet meal, a person feels a faint movement beneath the bowl. Another follows it farther down the vein. For a moment everyone listens.

Then the movement passes.

***

Not everything here has chosen a good place to live.

There are little black birds that build on the old margins because the curled tissue shelters their eggs. The margins continue curling. In some years nests close inside them.

There are beetles whose chambers fill with sap when growth resumes. There is a moss that spreads over warm skin until it insulates the surface, loses its heat, and freezes.

People also make mistakes slowly.

A village prospers in the lee of a rising rib. More families arrive. Gardens are extended into hollows once considered too distant. A new pool is fenced, a meeting shelter built, a difficult crossing improved with three seasons’ worth of rope.

Meanwhile the rib keeps rising.

By the time its movement becomes unmistakable, the gardens on one side lie in colder shade. The crossing hangs at a steeper angle. The oldest houses have begun to buckle.

Leaving requires dismantling the place that has kept them alive.

They do this reluctantly and with great care. Door skins are rolled. Hearth stones are lowered on cords. The dead, wrapped and lodged in dry bark cavities, are usually left behind.

At the next settlement, the newcomers sleep close together until more shelters can be made. They tell their hosts which passages remain usable. For a while they speak of going back.

***

On the morning after the new leaf opens, the woman with the waist cord takes a child out to look.

They do not approach its edge. From a safe rib they can see only a portion of the pale surface, already marked by falling ice. Water has gathered in a crease. A flock is circling above it, trying the air.

The child wants to know when they can cross.

The woman watches a grazer standing at the junction of old and new tissue. It extends its muzzle, scrapes once, and withdraws. After a long pause it sets one forefoot forward.

She has brought marking cord, but leaves it in her pocket.

They stay until the child becomes cold. Then they turn toward home, where there is water to carry and a roof seam to mend. Behind them the grazer takes another step.
