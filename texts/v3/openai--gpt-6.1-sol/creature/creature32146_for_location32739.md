The woman who clears the catchment screen has a pale groove across each thumb where the wet willow has worn her skin.

Her name is Eda. She is broad through the shoulders and wears her hair cut at the jaw because she dislikes feeling it against her neck when she bends over the pool. In winter she binds her wrists with strips of old cloth. These are not to keep her warm. Cold water runs down her arms when she raises the screen, and the cloth catches it before it reaches her sleeves.

She can tell which household has scraped its roof by what collects in the weave. She recognizes the red thread used to mend one family’s baskets, the hard little chips left by another family’s copper knife. Sometimes she finds a bead, a tooth, a fastening carved from antler. She puts these on the ledge rather than carrying them around the town. People come to look, usually pretending they have some other reason to visit.

She does not watch the delayed reflections. She works above them all morning and says looking down makes her neck ache.

When a child asks whether the water remembers, she says, “It keeps things. That isn’t the same.”

She has been known to wait beside the pool after her work is finished.

***

Most of the people here have strong feet and troublesome hands.

Their toes spread well when they stand barefoot on stone. Children learn this before they learn to carry a full cup: how to put down the whole foot, how to feel the difference between a damp surface and a surface wearing a skin of water. Visitors step carefully and still fall. A local person may hurry through the same passage with a bundle obscuring the ground ahead, placing each foot against a remembered irregularity.

Their hands are less fortunate. The joints swell. Cuts whiten at the edges. A small split beside a nail can remain open for a month, healing a little each night and opening again the following morning. Nearly every house has a dry wrapping kept high in a covered vessel. Asking for it is an admission that an injury has become inconvenient.

They rub their hands with warmed fat when they have it. Some use a bitter paste made from the roof plants. One old man insists that the paste does nothing except make people stop biting their fingers. His daughter prepares it for him anyway.

Dry skin is something they notice in strangers, along with the easy way strangers pull off their shoes.

The town’s people dress in short layers that can be removed separately. A person whose left side has become soaked does not necessarily want to change the right. Hems are reinforced, cuffs are narrow, and pockets close with a fold rather than a flap. Loose ornaments are uncommon. Anything dangling will eventually catch on a peg in a passage where someone behind you is trying to get through.

But they like bright things. There is a woman who has sewn nine yellow glass beads inside her collar, where they can be seen only when she turns it down. A boy keeps a piece of blue pottery in his mouth while he works, despite repeated instructions not to. When he smiles, one corner of his teeth becomes briefly blue.

***

The gutter-maker beside the washing court is young enough that people still refer to his father’s work when judging his own.

His father made reliable gutters and ugly bowls. The son makes reliable gutters and bowls that people are reluctant to use because they look too thin. He has a habit of holding finished objects at eye level and turning them against the light. His father tested everything immediately with water.

They argue about the proper size of a breaking lip.

The father’s hands shake now. He steadies one with the other when he wants to show where a cut should go. The son watches patiently until the shaking settles, then sometimes cuts somewhere else. This causes more anger than a direct contradiction would.

Their house has the palest lower walls in its passage. Everyone remarks on this. Inside, a bowl has stood beneath the father’s bed for eleven years, catching a drip neither man has found a satisfactory way to divert.

The son is the one kneeling at the threshold in the evening. He hears the clean drop and remains still for a moment, his blade resting against his finger. Then he calls inside for someone to bring a lamp. There is another notch to make before dark.

***

Children here are given small, consequential jobs.

They lift bowls that are not too heavy, carry scraped grit to the outer shelf, turn drying reeds, and fetch a person who knows how to mend whatever has just begun to fail. Their work is checked, though not always before something goes wrong. A child learns the seriousness of a drain by leaving a rag beside one and finding it plastered halfway up a wall the next day.

They play in the scraps of space between these duties.

They float reed splinters along channels and wager buttons on which will reach a particular step. They make little dams with their heels, releasing the water when an adult approaches. In the washing court they imitate the notes of drops striking different vessels. One girl can reproduce the deep basin’s hollow sound so well that people turn to see whether its inlet has opened.

At the catchment pool, they test the reflections.

One holds up two fingers, then three. Another watches the reflected hand. They disagree about whether blinking makes the delay longer. They have tried shouting at it, though shouting has nothing to do with what they are testing.

There is a boy who once kept his hand raised until the reflection raised its own, and then lowered it very slowly. He says something below the surface touched his reflected fingers.

The others ask him to do it again.

He will not, which makes the story valuable.

***

The narrow pale fish have soft mouths and eyes like cloudy pinheads.

Some are no longer than a little finger. Others appear almost the same size, but have a folded looseness to their skin and a slight bend that does not straighten when they swim. This is the chief evidence offered by those who believe the fish are old.

They can remain in a shallow basin for hours without apparent distress. Their gills move too seldom for comfort. Laid against wet stone, they incline their heads toward the nearest seep, even when the seep is behind them.

People lift them with wetted hands. Not everyone is gentle, but nobody deliberately kills one. There is little meat on them, and they smell unpleasant when heated: not rotten, exactly, but like a vessel that has stood closed too long.

A fish carried to the catchment may rest beside the screen all day. Then it disappears. The openings in the willow are small, so someone usually concludes that it has gone out beneath the weave. Eda sometimes finds one on the ledge instead, in the shallow water caught by a flaw in the stone.

She puts it back without comment.

Several households call every fish by the same name. One names each separately. An elderly woman keeps a tally on the back of her door, with a short mark for each fish and a hook beside the ones she thinks she has seen before.

She has more hooks than anyone finds reasonable.

***

The gray birds on the rim are tolerated rather than welcomed.

Each has a thick bill, narrow dark legs, and a patch of feathers beneath the throat that separates when it bends forward. They stand so still that grit settles on their backs. When something rises at a drain mouth, a bird strikes once, swallows, and resumes its former posture.

The townspeople sometimes see what has been caught: a pale thread, a knuckled scrap, something that contracts around the bill. These are not usually fish.

No one has persuaded a bird to come down into a passage. Children have laid trails of food. A man once carried an injured bird indoors in a basket. It beat itself against the lid until he took it back to the shelf, where it became quiet at once.

That happened years ago. People still mention it when someone proposes catching one.

***

Not every inhabitant is useful.

There is a man who drinks more than his share at feast times, a woman who borrows good vessels and returns poorer ones, and two neighbors who have been arguing over a roof trough since before either of their youngest children was born. The argument concerns a finger’s width of board and a substantial quantity of remembered insult.

People avoid them when both are outside. They also make sure someone passes that way occasionally, because one is growing frail and the other cannot hear a cry from indoors.

Privacy is managed with considerable invention. A closed door does not mean much when voices travel through three walls and emerge clearly in the wrong kitchen. People conduct delicate conversations while scraping, washing, or tapping masonry. Some couples quarrel in whispers over a running trough. Others leave the basin altogether and return with mud to their knees, speaking courteously.

There are people who want to leave. They collect stories about inland rooms with earth floors that stay firm, about trees large enough to provide a beam without bargaining, about clothes drying in an afternoon. Some go.

Those who return usually describe the wind first. How it enters everywhere, how it takes hold of a sleeve, how it keeps moving after you have shut the door.

***

In the dismantled house near the entrance, the former occupants still come each morning.

There are four of them: two adults, an aunt, and a child too young to remember the room without the new ridge. They are sleeping in borrowed corners nearby. Their belongings have been divided among three houses, which means breakfast involves several journeys.

The aunt brings a stool and sits beside the exposed foundations. She objects whenever a builder proposes moving the hearth. The hearth has already been moved twice in her lifetime, but both times, she says, were necessary.

The child squats beside the stone ridge and lays pieces of reed across its crest. Water gathers under them. A piece sometimes lifts, turns, and slides down.

One morning the child finds a pale shoot growing from a notch near the top. Its two leaves have opened toward the white wall of the neighboring house.

The builders mean to pull it out before laying the trough. The child asks them to leave it, and the aunt supports the request because she dislikes the proposed trough.

By evening someone has cut a small accommodation into the wood. The shoot stands within it, untouched, its leaves scarcely wider than a nail.

The family will sleep elsewhere for another week. Each morning they come to see how the work is getting on. Each morning the child checks the shoot before anything else.
