It lives where the roots almost meet.

There is usually a little water there, too shallow to conceal a body and too dark to show one plainly. At first you see only the things resting on the water: a leaf tip, a pale strand of root, the reflection of a jointed stalk. Then one of the reflections moves without its stalk moving. A low head has turned beneath it.

The animal is longer than the space appears able to hold. Its forequarters lie between the roots, its middle follows the underside of one of them, and its hindquarters disappear into the wet leaves beyond. It does not coil. Its body takes a succession of gentle bends, each fitted to something else. When it leaves, the place looks unexpectedly spacious.

Its skin is dark where it is exposed and pale where it rests against another surface. Neither color has a clean boundary. Beneath the throat, greenish brown becomes a warm gray, then the faint yellow of a shoot that has not seen light. Along its flanks are narrow creases holding water. Tiny swimmers enter these creases when it lies submerged. They leave as it rises, spilling in clear threads from its sides.

The head is blunt, but not heavy. Much of its apparent breadth belongs to the soft corners of the mouth. These can draw inward until the face is narrow enough to enter a folded leaf, or spread against a root so that the mouth finds every small hollow in its surface. Two nostrils stand a little above the muzzle. Each has a fleshy lip that closes separately. The animal can breathe with almost all of its head underwater, and it often does, though it does not seem to need the concealment.

Its eyes are less easily found. They lie low on either side, under short translucent folds. In shade they are the color of the surrounding water. When the head turns, each catches a dull amber light. The folds can close across them without shutting out sight. Sometimes one eye watches the ground while the other remains covered.

It has limbs, though you might watch it for a long time before being certain how many.

A forefoot reaches around a root. Farther back, another foot presses against the same root from below. Something like an elbow lifts beneath its flank and settles again. The limbs lie so closely along the body that their joints are hidden in its creases; when needed, they open sideways, each ending in a broad pad divided by shallow cuts. On wet stone the pads spread almost flat. On stems they close, slowly and securely, without visible claws.

It moves by transferring small amounts of weight. No single foot seems to carry it. A shoulder advances, then a length of flank, then a foot you had mistaken for a loose piece of bark. A journey of several body lengths can happen before you have seen anything that resembles a step.

The younger ones are easier to count. They have narrow bodies, conspicuous joints, and too much pale skin around their mouths. They sit on the rims of pools with their feet spread, testing surfaces before trusting them. Even then, there is no awkwardness that damages anything. A young animal slips, catches a leaf with its mouth, and hangs there while the leaf slowly bends. It waits until its hind feet find the mineral rim again.

Adults allow them to travel underneath. Two or three small bodies may occupy the shelter between an adult’s belly and the ground, moving when it moves, feeding when it stops. The adult sometimes lowers its head to touch one with the side of its muzzle. At that touch the youngster becomes very still. Then both resume what they were doing.

They eat from surfaces.

A mouth rests against a stem. The lower jaw makes a faint sideways movement. When the head lifts, the stem looks freshly washed. Elsewhere they take a soft rim of mineral matter, a fringe of root hairs, the tender edge of a leaf. There are small teeth inside the mouth, closely set and translucent, but the animal uses them with such little force that feeding is almost indistinguishable from resting.

It also eats the tiny creatures that gather where it feeds. A shelled grazer vanishes beneath the broad lip. A larva is drawn from a seam in the stone. Sometimes a winged animal settles near its nostril and is taken with one quick movement, startlingly precise in a creature otherwise so deliberate. The eyes do not turn toward the prey. It seems to know what touches the surfaces beside its mouth.

Nothing follows it hoping for scraps. Several things follow it nevertheless.

Small flattened animals keep pace along nearby bark. Threads of translucent root incline toward places where it has pressed its feet. A dark, many-legged creature often waits beneath its throat and departs when the larger animal begins to move. These companions change from place to place. The animal does not shake them off.

Its voice comes from low in the body.

First there is a little tightening along the flank. Then a sound like air entering a wet cavity, followed by two or three rounded pulses. Nearby water trembles. A leaf touching its back gives a soft knock against a stem.

The answering sound may come from beyond the next stand of trees, or from immediately beneath your feet.

They call while feeding, while resting, sometimes while passing one another. Their bodies touch with no evident ceremony. One presses its cheek briefly against another’s shoulder. Two lie in opposite directions along a root, sharing the narrow ledge. They seem to distinguish each other, but they do not need to keep apart in order to remain distinct.

There are places they favor. A hollow under three roots. A warm seam with a particular bitter taste. The underside of a low branch where the skin of the belly can remain wet while the head rests among leaves.

An animal returning to such a place makes several small adjustments before settling. It places its forefoot into a depression, lifts it, and places it again a little farther inward. Its mouth finds a stem. Its tail, tapering until it is scarcely thicker than a vine, slides through the water and comes to rest.

Eventually you notice that the foot always fits.

The depression does not deepen. The root does not acquire a polished patch. The animal’s belly has no old scar from lying there, and its teeth have no worn edge. Yet its movements have the ease of long familiarity. It knows which leaf will bear its weight. It knows how far to turn its head without disturbing the shelled creatures on the rim.

When it sleeps, the mouth loosens. The translucent folds cover the eyes. Water rises and falls in the narrow creases of its sides.

Close to its throat, just beneath the skin, something pale can be seen moving with each breath. It might be a small inner bone, still soft. It might be the edge of an organ. The skin there is so thin that the shape seems ready to emerge, although it never comes nearer.

At the basin’s edge, one lies with its mouth against the furled shoot.

The shoot presses gently into the soft roof of its mouth. A little sap shines between them. The animal swallows, rests, swallows again. Its nostrils open above the water. One forefoot is tucked under a root; the other holds nothing, its pads partly spread.

When a call comes from the next basin, it lifts its head.

For a moment the shoot stands free, pale at the tip, tightly furled. The animal listens with water running from its chin. Then it lowers its mouth to the stem again, finding the place without looking, and settles its weight more comfortably between the roots.
