The first sign of an inhabitant is usually a place where the frost has failed.

On a morning when every pore in a chamber wall is white, there may be a dark oval near the floor, no larger than two hands laid together. Water beads there without freezing. If the chamber is quiet enough, the beads can be seen drawing toward the middle of the oval. They disappear one by one.

What looks like exposed wall is a back.

The animal fits itself so closely to the pale material that its edges are difficult to find. Its skin has the same shallow pits, the same scattered grains of grit. Along the lower edge, a fringe of fine folds enters the wall’s openings. These folds are not roots. When disturbed, they withdraw, slowly at first and then all at once, leaving a row of wet pinholes.

The animal comes away from the wall with a soft tearing sound.

It is broader than it appeared while attached. Part of its body has been folded beneath it, pressed into a crack scarcely wider than a finger. Released, this portion hangs down like a damp cuff. Two narrow limbs emerge, followed by a second pair that remain tucked close to the underside. For several moments it stands without going anywhere, its back still shaped by the surface it has left.

Then the pits deepen. A ridge subsides. The body becomes smooth.

There is no conspicuous head. At one end, the edge thickens into a blunt prow with a seam underneath. Above the seam are two small recesses, usually closed. In cloud they open, revealing surfaces the color of tarnished silver.

The inhabitants do not look up often.

They move through the streets with their bodies suspended a few inches above the ground. The two longer limbs take the weight. Their tips are hard and divided lengthwise, making the narrow paired impressions found near the pools. The shorter limbs touch down only on broken ground, or when the animal needs to brace itself against the wind.

Seen from above, one resembles a piece of wall carried on delicate supports.

From the side it is less simple. Its underside is dark, pleated, and constantly adjusting. Folds open to admit air, then overlap again. Small movements pass through them even when the rest of the creature is still. In cold weather the folds close so tightly that the belly seems solid. In a warm court, they loosen into rows of soft, pendant lobes.

They are not quick animals. On an open patch they can be overtaken by the shadow of a cloud. Yet it is difficult to watch one arrive anywhere. It goes behind a low wall and does not emerge from the expected passage. Minutes later it is attached inside a chamber reached by another street.

There are routes through the town that a standing visitor cannot see.

Some run under the creeping shrub. Others pass through breaks concealed by moss, or beneath old thresholds where the pale ground has lifted away from the soil. The inhabitants know these passages by contact. They lower the front of the body and test an opening with the thickened edge. If it is too narrow, they wait, pressing gently against it.

Sometimes the living material yields.

Sometimes the animal backs away and goes elsewhere.

Their relation to the walls is intimate without being entirely peaceful. Where they have rested for a long time, the branching cavities are wider and cleaner. Dead fibers have been removed. Water moves through those places readily. But the living gray rim is often thinned nearby, and in some chambers it retreats from a favored resting site altogether.

The animal feeds with the seam beneath its front edge. It opens against the wall, exposing a short brush of stiff filaments. These work over the surface in small, repeated strokes. What they gather is too fine to see.

It also eats the growth from drowned stems, the dark film beneath loose flakes, and occasionally the translucent grazers. A grazer touched by the feeding seam folds abruptly in half. The inhabitant holds it for a while without any visible effort, then continues along the rim.

Around the pools, their backs are darker.

They drink without lowering their bodies. One long limb is placed in the water, and the hard tip separates. A pale cord extends between its halves. It may remain there for several minutes, moving faintly with the ripples.

At the largest pool, an animal sometimes puts down both limbs and stands with its body over the deeper openings. The water beneath it clouds, then clears. When it leaves, its underside is fuller, the pleats pushed apart by something carried within.

They visit this pool most often before a dry spell.

What they carry sustains them through weeks in which nothing drips. Attached deep inside a vaulted room, an inhabitant grows progressively thinner. Its margins curl inward. Dust settles on its exposed back, and the resemblance to the wall becomes nearly complete.

It can remain like this after the moss has hardened and the grazers have vanished below ground. A little warmth persists underneath it. Tiny flies shelter along its edges. Once in a while the feeding filaments emerge and sweep the same small patch, finding whatever the porous surface still offers.

When cloud returns, the animal does not immediately move.

First the wall darkens around it. Then water fills the fringe of folds. Only after the chamber has begun to drain does it detach. Its limbs unfold stiffly. It makes several attempts before it can stand.

The first journey is usually to a pool, even if runoff is crossing the floor beside it.

There are more of them than a visitor would suppose, but fewer than the number of warm patches suggests. An individual changes resting places, leaving moisture and a little residual heat. Two may also lie edge to edge so closely that they seem to be a single broad animal.

They tolerate this closeness in the coldest chambers. In milder weather they separate.

Contact between them begins with the shorter limbs. These reach beneath the other’s body and rest against the folds. The animals may stay in that position for an hour. Occasionally one shifts and the other follows, keeping the points of contact unchanged.

Nothing audible accompanies this, though a hand placed on the wall nearby can sometimes feel a succession of faint taps.

They are sensitive to the town’s louder sounds. A deep bellow from a wall will cause every exposed individual to lower itself. The hard tips of the long limbs turn inward, and the belly settles onto the ground. They remain there until the vibration has passed.

The whistle of a narrow passage has a different effect. Animals resting nearby open their silver recesses. Some detach and move toward it.

This is most noticeable in the western part of the settlement, where the new rims are forming. During prolonged wet weather, several inhabitants gather around an enclosure that has no entrance. They stand outside with their fronts pressed to the soft wall. Their shorter limbs knead the base.

After they have gone, the rim is scored with shallow vertical channels. One section may be lower than the rest.

These gaps often become the entrances to new rooms. Other gaps close over, and some enclosures grow on undisturbed until there is no easy way into them.

The smallest inhabitants are found in such closed places.

They have almost no hard parts. Their limbs are short transparent pegs, and their backs show a dark branching pattern under the skin. In a wet chamber they spread themselves on the floor. As it dries, they gather along the base of the wall, fitting into the angle between two surfaces.

They cannot drink from the pools. They take moisture through their whole underside.

For a time, the gray growth advances around them. It climbs over their margins and leaves the middle exposed. A young animal may become difficult to separate from the room that contains it. Its skin grows pale and porous; the dark branches beneath draw closer together.

Some eventually pull free. The place they leave is smooth, shallow, and shaped like them.

Others remain.

In an old wall, among the fine branching chambers, there are sometimes two straight channels side by side, with a shorter pair behind them. These occur at different heights. They are most easily seen in broken pieces after a thaw.

The pale birds probe them especially carefully.

No one watching a young inhabitant can be certain which course it will take. Those that remain attached do not simply become still. Their folds continue to draw in water. Their backs thicken. For several seasons, a slight warmth can be felt at the center of the new material.

Above them, the wall grows.

The free-moving adults rarely enter those chambers once the rims have become firm. They pass nearby, pause, and touch the outside with a short limb before continuing.

Toward evening they leave their sheltered places and travel while the streets still hold some of the day’s warmth. They are easiest to see then. Low sunlight makes their pale backs shine, and their thin limbs cast shadows far larger than their bodies.

An animal crossing the central court stops whenever the wind rises. It lowers itself, waits, then stands again. Over the course of many such interruptions it reaches the opposite passage.

By dark, most have attached somewhere out of sight.

One may remain beneath the low arch at the third pool. Between drops, it extends its front over the water, opening the silver recesses. When a drop falls, the recesses close. The hollow note moves through the chamber and fades into the porous ceiling.

The animal waits.

After a while it shifts closer to the wall. Its fringe enters the damp openings. Its back takes on the roughness around it, and the distinction becomes harder to see.

By morning there is only a small unfrozen place, low down, among the salt-white pores.
