The people who live here usually know that someone is approaching before they see them.

A hand resting on a doorframe feels the arrival of a laden person differently from that of an empty-handed one. The wooden pins carry a little of the movement from the paving into the walls. Someone climbing the western steps sends a faint, uneven tremor through the bench in the nearest court. It is enough to make the bowl-maker lift her knife from the wood before her visitor turns the corner.

This is not an extraordinary faculty. Children acquire it badly, then well, then stop noticing that they have acquired it. Visitors are always putting things down at the wrong moment.

The inhabitants are human, although a stranger may spend a day uncertain of this. They hold themselves very still when attending to something. Their eyes follow movements too small for the stranger to have seen. In conversation, a person may pause with a finger laid against a basket rim, waiting for an animal hidden in the weave to finish passing.

Most have strong calves, narrow shoulders, and hands made rough in particular places: beneath the fingers from carrying damp leaf stems, along the heel of the palm from grinding, at the thumb joint from twisting cord. Their nails are kept short. Scratches heal slowly in the cold, and a cut is wrapped before any work is done among the trailing plants.

They wear their padded cloaks close to the body. Loose hems gather passengers. Clothing is hung inside out at night, and certain seams are sewn with removable thread so that they can be opened, emptied, and sewn again.

An old man named Senn keeps a little fork of bone tucked into his collar. He uses it to lift creatures from behind his ears.

He insists that this is a problem particular to him. His daughters disagree.

There are eighty-three people in the hollow, if the woman away on the southern ridge is counted and the infant born four nights ago is not yet given a separate share of water. The storekeeper counts both. The infant’s grandmother counts neither until they return or begin to drink from a cup. This disagreement has lasted through several births and departures. No one has found it necessary to resolve it.

There is much more exactness about jars.

Every household has a morning measure, marked by a notch inside a clay vessel. Water gathered from leaves belongs first to the household that tends the tree, but certain branches are reserved for the storehouse. A branch may reach over three roofs and be tended by someone living beneath none of them.

The rights are remembered through repairs, marriages, old exchanges, and the occasional judgment made in the long room. There are people who can recite the ownership of every collecting leaf on a tree. There are also people who remember the same history differently.

The arguments are quiet, but they can continue for years.

A woman called Tava tends one of the broad trees in the north. Her left arm will no longer straighten. She uses a hooked pole with a padded end, guiding the branches into positions from which their cups can be emptied without bruising them.

She knows which leaves will turn reluctantly, which will shed water if touched on the rim, which must be left untouched for a season. Sometimes she rests her forehead against a branch while holding it. This allows her to keep both hands free; it also tells her whether the branch has begun to move.

Her eldest son believes she attributes too much intention to the tree.

He says this while standing beyond its reach.

The trees can pinch. A branch turning under its own weight may catch a sleeve against a wall and hold it there until afternoon. Young branches are more troublesome, especially after pruning. People returning to the town ask whether the streets have changed, and are told about buildings, deaths, and three or four limbs that have become difficult.

A child once had to be cut out of a cloak. The cloak remained in the tree for two more days. Its owner, now grown, still takes the longer route around that court.

Children are plentiful in view even when adults are not. They fit into places the larger inhabitants cannot easily supervise.

They crouch along the dry borders of moss islands, conducting transfers with grass stems. They collect the little antlered animals in cups, although the animals climb out almost immediately. Their games involve making routes: from one doorstep to another without crossing a warm patch; across a court without disturbing a grazer; up a wall using only the empty cracks.

They do shout sometimes.

The sound surprises them almost as much as it surprises everything else. Then the nearest adult comes to see what has happened.

An alarm is supposed to be made by striking a hanging slab three times. Children learn this early. There are things in the foliage for which an adult must come running, and it matters that the signal can be distinguished from a dropped tool.

The long hunters are among those things.

They are tolerated high in the outer trees, where they take grazers and keep other climbers from becoming too numerous. Near a doorway, or above a route used by children, they are removed. This is done with forked poles, heavy cloth, and considerably more noise than anyone likes.

The hunters do not always cooperate.

Tava has a pale scar beginning at her jaw and disappearing into her hair. The creature that made it was barely as long as her forearm. She had mistaken a bent strip of bark for a loose piece and tried to pull it free.

When people tell this story to children, she corrects the telling. She had seen its feet. She had thought it too small to matter.

The grazers are less dangerous, though they can crush a hand against a wall without noticing. Some have names. Others are known by a missing tuft, a pale joint, the manner in which they fold their middle limbs.

They are not quite livestock. No one can reliably confine them, and breeding pairs chosen by people show little interest in remaining together. Still, the town takes their loose fur, gathers their droppings, and cuts feeding passages through the cushions of vegetation. A sick animal may receive three days of careful attention from someone who expects no particular return.

A few enter houses.

One gray female goes into the weighing room whenever the weather changes. She places her head beneath the balance beam and waits. The clerk has learned to work around her. On very cold mornings she exhales onto his bare fingers while he sorts the weights.

He complains about the hair.

The animals that live inside the buildings are harder to distinguish from the buildings themselves.

There is a small colony in the roof of the dispute room that consumes the soft material between the woven roots but leaves the roots intact. After some years, a roof containing these creatures becomes lighter and better insulated. After too many years, it becomes unreliable.

The roof-keeper listens to it through a hollow reed pressed against the matting. He is listening for movement, but also for the places where movement has stopped.

He keeps samples in shallow boxes. When he opens one, what appears to be a handful of dust resolves into pale, many-jointed bodies, each carrying a morsel larger than its head.

His apprentice dislikes them. She is very good at the work nonetheless.

There are smaller creatures whose presence is less useful. They eat cord at the point where it touches wood, or enter a grain jar by flattening themselves thinner than the lid’s unevenness. Some leave an acrid taste in flour. Others are found only when the flour moves.

No one in the hollow mistakes abundance for generosity.

The storehouse has a bare floor, whitewashed joints, and polished stone collars wide enough to frustrate most climbing things. Each morning the keeper examines a tray of fine ash set beneath the grain racks. The tracks tell her where to look.

She has a little book of them, drawn with charcoal.

Its pages include the print of a child’s thumb and a notation that the child had denied entering.

The keeper is the same grandmother who will not yet count the infant separately. She has put aside a clean jar for it.

People leave the hollow, though not often. They go for salt, for metal, to visit relatives in places beneath the cloud. Some return embarrassed by the noise of their new belongings. A clasp ticks against a bowl. A pair of hard-soled shoes announces itself throughout a court.

Others come back impatient.

They want higher roofs, more light, a stair that takes a straight course. They suggest clearing the southern growth, enclosing the square, capturing the grazers before dusk and collecting the moisture while the rock is wet.

These suggestions are discussed. Sometimes one is tried.

There is a straight stair behind the storehouse, built by a man who had spent seven years below. It is convenient. In certain weather the steps become crowded with flat blue animals descending from the leaves, and everyone uses the old turning path until they have gone.

The man still lives beside his stair. He sweeps it patiently with a soft brush.

Of the square, the inhabitants know surprisingly little.

They know when its veins will darken early. They know that a grazer prevented from reaching it becomes restless enough to climb a house. They know which creatures arrive from places where they have never been seen by daylight.

Once, a woman lowering a dish into the gathering crowd received a bite so slight that she did not feel it. Her arm swelled to the shoulder. She recovered. Her dish was found the next morning, empty and unbroken.

The townspeople keep back now, although some say the bite and the square had nothing to do with each other.

At dusk, children watch from doorways with adult hands resting on their shoulders. The smallest animals pass over shoes and between fingers. Occasionally something pauses against a person’s skin, as if taking an instruction, then continues.

The people do not bow.

They wait, and afterward they take what remains.

The dead are carried up the reserved stair wrapped in root cloth. Four people carry; a fifth goes ahead to clear the route. Even then, someone must watch the bearers’ feet. On the old lookout stair the longer step is taken by everyone together.

Up on the ridge there are low stone enclosures with gaps too narrow for the grazers. The wind keeps most of the small animals away. The cloth loosens slowly. Families visit when the weather allows, bringing another stone to add to the weight.

Senn dislikes the ridge. He says he has spent his whole life trying to keep warm and sees no reason to stop.

His daughters have heard this many times. They tuck his cloak more closely around his knees.

At night he sits inside his doorway, repairing baskets by touch. He does not remove every creature he finds in them. Some he puts onto the wall; some he settles back between the strands after the repair. He can identify several kinds without bringing them to the light.

From time to time he lifts the basket to his cheek.

In the next house, a woman nurses the new infant. Someone has coated the bed cords again, and the bitter smell fills the room. A covered cup stands within her reach. She listens to the baby swallowing, to the slight pull of the suspended bed, to a faint scrape from inside the wall.

She will inspect the wall in the morning.

For now, she puts one bare foot against it. The scraping stops. After a while it begins again, farther away, and she shifts the child to her other arm.
