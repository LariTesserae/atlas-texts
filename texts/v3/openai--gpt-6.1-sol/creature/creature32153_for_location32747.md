At first, you would take it for one of the old shrubs.

It lies lengthwise against the foot of the wall, where the wet leaves obscure its outline. Its back is dark and uneven. Fallen grit collects there, and a little moss grows in the hollows between its shoulders. It is scarcely higher than the vegetation. When the wind moves the leaves, you might notice that one part of the green band has remained still.

Then it reaches out and turns a stone over.

The hand is broad, with four short fingers and a thumb set unusually far back. Its palm is pale. It examines the underside of the stone, removes something small, and returns the stone almost exactly to its former position. A fern trapped under the edge is drawn free with one finger.

It eats without lifting its head.

There is only one of them in the fold now.

Its body is longer than you first expect, folded into three or four comfortable angles. The hind legs can tuck beneath it or extend sideways against the rock. Its forelimbs are good for holding things but poor for supporting its weight; when it crosses open ground, it goes low, using the sides of its hands. This gives its movement a peculiar discretion. It appears to be trying not to wake the stone.

Its skin is covered in fine, close hair, except on the palms, the soles, and a patch beneath the chin. Most of the hair is gray-brown. Along the spine it grows longer and catches debris. The moss is not part of it, though some of the moss has been there for years.

Behind the ears, the hair is unexpectedly soft and white.

You see this when it washes.

It washes every morning in the shallow stone bowl beside the shelter. First it drinks. Then it wets both hands and rubs its face, drawing its fingers backward over the eyes and along the sides of the head. It spends a long time cleaning the small folds of its ears. There is often a seed lodged in one, or a grain of sand.

The birds wait for it to finish. They have learned the order of things.

Its face is neither flat nor muzzled. A short, blunt nose sits above a mouth wide enough to seem expressive even when it is doing nothing. The eyes are dark, deeply set, and protected by heavy upper lids. In direct sun it closes them almost completely. In cloud, they open wide.

Nothing about it looks suited to watching distant mountains.

Most of what matters is within reach.

It knows where the snails gather after rain, which roots hold insect eggs, and the part of the pool where larvae become sluggish in the first cold of evening. It knows the shrubs individually. Under one, the soil is warmer. Another bears a few sour berries so late that they sometimes freeze before ripening. A third has a dead branch that traps goat hairs.

It collects those hairs.

Inside the shelter, behind a loose floor stone, is a bundle of them mixed with grass fibers and the pale felt scraped from the undersides of leaves. The bundle is used for bedding, but not carelessly. Damp material is brought out into the sun. Dry material is pushed toward the back. The creature sleeps curled around it, with one hand covering its nose.

There is room in the shelter for another body of the same size.

That space is kept clear.

It eats insects, snails, roots, and certain flowers. It takes very little from any one patch. Once a flower has been eaten, it usually leaves the neighboring buds alone. Whether this is knowledge or preference is difficult to say. It is capable of sitting before a closed blossom for most of a morning, waiting for warmth to open it, then deciding against it.

When the rust-winged bird comes to break snails, the creature watches from beneath the shrubs. Sometimes the bird leaves a broken snail unfinished. After it flies away, the creature goes to the battered slab and gathers what remains.

It puts the empty shell fragments into a crack.

The crack is full of them now: translucent pieces, white spirals, a few larger shells gone chalky with age. They have no evident use. On days when there is little sun, it takes some out and sorts them on the shelter roof. It seems especially interested in pieces with intact curves.

Its hearing is excellent, but the loudness of the falls does not trouble it.

When the wind brings the great rushing noise up from below, it settles more deeply against the wall. One hand rests on the stone. Through its palm it can distinguish a change in the stream nearest the shelter before the change becomes audible.

Sometimes it gets up and clears a blocked channel.

A leaf caught between two stones is pulled free. A drift of grit is swept aside. Water that has begun to spill into the bedding runs back toward the pool.

Other channels it leaves blocked. Behind one obstruction, a little basin has formed, warm enough in sunlight for larvae to gather. It catches them there.

The pale pebble at the pool’s outlet receives particular attention. When it is tapping, the creature sometimes sits beside it with its head tilted. Once, after heavy rain, the pebble was thrown out of its worn cup. The creature searched downstream, found it lodged among three darker stones, and brought it back.

It placed it in the hollow.

The tapping resumed.

For a while it stayed there, touching the wet slab beside the pebble with two fingers.

It is not fearless at the precipice. It approaches on its belly and keeps one hind foot hooked behind a stone. It will lean far enough to look beneath the lip, but never farther than its grip allows. Sudden gusts make it retreat at once.

Cloud is different. When cloud fills the fold, it moves freely across the bare center, touching down with its hands as if the whole place has become sheltered.

You may hear it then.

Its voice is quiet and rather dry: three or four clicks, followed by a soft descending sound made far back in the throat. It calls from beside the pool, waits, and calls again toward the footholds on the sloping rib.

Occasionally it climbs a short way toward them.

It knows the cut places. Its fingers fit into some of them better than a human boot would. Above the last patch of vegetation, however, it stops. There is a narrow shelf there where it can sit with its back against the mountain and face the old ice.

It waits until the light changes.

Then it comes down.

It is old. One of its thumbs no longer straightens, and the hair on its belly has thinned. It descends carefully on the side where its good hand can take most of the weight. If pebbles fall from the goats’ crossing, it draws its head beneath its shoulders and remains that way longer than necessary.

The goats scarcely notice it. A young one once stepped on the edge of its bedding while drinking from the bowl. The creature pushed the hoof away. The goat sprang backward, offended, then tried to eat the bedding.

That produced a sound the creature seldom makes: a sharp, open-mouthed bark.

For the rest of the afternoon it carried every loose fiber indoors.

It did not build the shelter, though it has repaired it. Small wedges have been pressed beneath a roof slab that shifted. The raised floor is cleaner near the entrance than at the back. The wooden pin in the wall is polished on its lower side, where it hangs wet roots to dry.

It has been here long enough to remember smoke.

When a summer storm breaks close overhead and the air takes on that burnt, mineral smell, it goes into the shelter and presses its face against the stained back wall. It stays there until the thunder has passed.

In winter it becomes slow, but does not sleep through the season. The water issuing steadily from the wrist-wide hole keeps a strip of stone free of ice. It spends much of each day there, folded beside the flow, taking what food it can from the wet margin. After a snowfall, it clears the shelter entrance with its hands and pushes the snow out over the edge.

On the coldest nights it draws all the bedding around itself. Even then, the space beside it remains empty.

By morning it is awake before the birds.

It drinks, washes, and checks the channel along the shelter wall. Then it makes its way beneath the shrubs, stopping where droplets hang from the leaves. It takes some directly into its mouth. Others it leaves for the sun.

When the first bird lands on the bowl, the creature looks up.

A little water spills over the chipped rim. It follows the familiar groove through the stone and reaches the creature’s resting place a moment later. The creature lifts its hand to let it pass.
