At first you take it for a bundle of cloth laid beside the unfinished stair.

It is the right color for something that has been wet and dried many times: gray in the folds, almost white across the raised surfaces. One end rests beneath the overhang of a landing. The other extends into the narrow channel that runs beside the steps.

Then the end in the channel bends.

A hand closes around the edge of a tile. There are four fingers and a thumb, though the thumb is set farther down the wrist than yours. The fingers are long and flat, with broad pads at their tips. Water wells between them as the hand lifts. It remains suspended for a moment, letting the last drops return to the channel.

The inhabitant draws its arm underneath itself and sits up.

It is smaller than you expected. Standing, it would reach your chest, but it does not stand yet. It sits with its legs folded sideways, one hand braced behind it, and looks at the stair.

Its skin is not cloth. Nor is it quite the same substance as the surrounding pottery. Across its shoulders it has the matte finish of an unglazed bowl, but at the elbow it gathers in thin, pliant wrinkles. A faint translucence shows at the edges of those wrinkles. When it moves, something darker travels beneath them.

Its head is broad and slightly bowed. Two eyes sit deep under a continuous ridge. They are clear, without visible pupils; each holds a small, distorted image of the steps. Beneath them is a short seam that might be a mouth. It opens once, releasing a bead of water, then closes.

There is no nose.

The inhabitant places its wet palm against the unfinished riser. It stays that way for a long time.

You think it is resting. Then you notice the change in the clay.

A slight bulge beneath its palm subsides. Water appears along the contact, carrying a pale suspension of fine particles. With a movement scarcely larger than a tremor, the hand shifts upward. What had been a rough patch is now smooth.

The next step has not merely been marked. It is being fitted to this particular body.

***

There are more of them than the stillness suggests.

One lies lengthwise in a roofed recess, its feet in a little pool. Another is crouched among the jars, so closely matched to their rounded shoulders that only the movement of its hands separates it from the wall. On a distant slope, three pale figures carry a length of gutter. They advance along the raised tile edges, taking small steps together. Whenever one pauses, the others pause too.

They do not seem to use the broad stairways unless carrying something. Alone, they prefer the spillways and the shallow drainage grooves.

Their feet explain this. Each has a narrow, hard outer edge and a soft central pad, divided by fine lines. On the dry borders of the paving, the hard edge bears their weight. In water, the pad spreads. You can see a foot become wider as it settles into a channel, conforming to the bottom without obscuring the little current altogether.

They stop often to wet themselves.

A wrist lowered into a cup. The back of a knee pressed against a flowing seam. A cheek resting briefly on a damp wall.

These are not gestures of relief. There is no sigh afterward, no visible pleasure. Yet an inhabitant that has crossed a broad, exposed court moves differently before and after placing its hands in water. The stiffness leaves its fingers first. Then its neck straightens. The dark movement beneath its skin resumes.

The landscape’s thousands of receptacles are within easy reach because they need to be.

***

Up close, no two bodies have quite the same surface.

Some are pale throughout. Others have patches the color of smoke, or a faint reddish band around a limb. One has a glossy shoulder attached to a dull upper arm. Another’s chest bears a shallow grid of lines, carefully closed but still visible in the wet.

They repair themselves.

Not casually, and not without help.

In a sheltered court you find an inhabitant sitting on the floor while another works at its ankle. A foot lies beside them on a folded strip of damp fabric. The seated one holds its lower leg over a bowl, keeping the broken edge submerged.

The worker has set out three possible replacements.

It turns each foot in its hands, checks the depth of the hollow at the ankle, and places one against the leg. The fit is wrong. It tries another. All this takes place with the patient attention you have seen given to the stair.

When it finds the right one, it does not attach it immediately. It lays the foot in the bowl beside the broken edge and leaves both there.

For the rest of the afternoon the two sit together. Occasionally the worker touches the rim of the bowl or adjusts the position of the leg. The injured inhabitant uses a wooden paddle to shape small channel teeth from a lump of clay.

By evening, the new foot is joined. Its owner tests it against the court floor, then returns it to the water.

The discarded foot is not buried or kept as a relic. Several days later you recognize its divided sole among the fragments packed into a new retaining wall.

***

Their youngest are not miniature versions of the adults.

You see one being carried in a shallow tray. It is soft enough that its weight has flattened one side. Its hands are tucked beneath its head; its legs have scarcely separated. The eyes are cloudy.

The carrier keeps turning the tray a little, letting water move from one end to the other.

Later you see the same small being beneath a hood near a firing court, with three adults sitting around it. One has a thumb pressed lightly into its palm. The others are motionless except for the occasional adjustment of a cord or a covering slab.

No one is shaping it now.

When it moves its hand away from the adult’s thumb, the thumb follows only far enough to keep contact. Then the adult waits.

There are many such hoods. Most are empty. Some contain bodies lying in positions you cannot distinguish from sleep.

The firing courts have narrow side chambers, smaller than the long enclosures used for blocks. Around their mouths, the clay is worn smooth at the height of a hand.

You never see anyone enter while a chamber is hot. You do see a figure come out after the walls have cooled, walking awkwardly, supported under both arms. Its back is brighter than the rest of it. For a while it cannot bend.

Its companions lead it to a pool and sit down beside it.

***

They are capable of haste.

Once a coping tile slips from a wall above a crowded collection court. It strikes a lower rim and breaks it. Water begins spilling across a surface meant to stay nearly dry.

An inhabitant several terraces away turns its head before you hear the break.

Others emerge from recesses, from beneath ledges, from places you had looked directly at without seeing anyone. They arrive carrying clay, cloth, a basin, a small curved tile. One holds its forearm across the damaged outlet while another packs the breach.

For the first time you hear their voices.

They are low sounds formed in the chest, with abrupt changes of pitch. Water gathers at the mouth seams. One voice stops; another continues its note from farther down the slope.

The repair is completed before much water is lost.

Afterward, the inhabitant that blocked the outlet remains kneeling. The current has scoured a patch from its forearm. Another pours cloudy water over the exposed place, slowly, from a cup.

***

At the central rise, they spend time doing very little.

One climbs to the summit carrying no tools. It examines several lifting cords on the way, pinching each between finger and thumb, but passes others without touching them. Near the top it settles onto one of the polished hollows in the bench.

Its body fits the hollow loosely. The seat has been used by many bodies, not made for this one.

For a while it watches the enclosing walls.

Then it lowers two fingers into the summit bowl.

A second inhabitant arrives much later. It has a narrow blue line at its wrist, a strand embedded in the skin and showing through where the surface is thin. It sits in the other hollow. Their shoulders do not touch.

Neither makes a sound.

Below them, water enters the high channels and travels toward the outlying courts. The first inhabitant lifts its fingers from the bowl and places them against the second’s wrist, just above the blue thread.

The contact lasts several minutes.

When cloud begins to fill the western gap, they remain where they are. Droplets gather on their heads and shoulders. One runs down the first inhabitant’s cheek and pauses at the closed seam of its mouth.

The second raises a hand and catches it before it falls.
