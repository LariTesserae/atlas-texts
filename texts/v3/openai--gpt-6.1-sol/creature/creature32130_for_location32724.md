The oldest inhabitants are the machines, though none is as old as all of itself.

A carrier retains its original nickel housing and one wheel. Its other wheel was cut from the rim of a discarded washer; its brush has been replaced so often that the socket holds a bundle of unequal filaments, some copper, some stiff fibers drawn from an insect’s wing. It travels slightly crooked. At one bend in the rail, it must stop and reverse twice before it can pass. The carriers behind it stop too, keeping the intervals their instructions require.

There is no machine assigned to repair a carrier. Repairs occur when a carrier becomes an obstruction.

Then one of the terminal arms reaches it, if it is within reach. A bent axle is straightened as though it were a contact pin. A loose housing is pressed shut as though it were a protective cover. Sometimes this is enough. Sometimes the machine is made tidier without being made functional, and the rail must eventually be extended around it.

Such abandoned housings are among the best shelters in the city. They retain a little warmth. Their entrances are small, and their interiors stay dark when the red lamp brightens. A carrier that stopped six winters ago holds a clutch of beetle eggs beneath its lid.

The beetles are the most settled of the living inhabitants.

They are dark, flattened creatures with a greasy sheen along the joints of their legs. Those born here are usually smaller than the arrivals from below. Food is plentiful in particular places and absent everywhere else; a larva may spend its entire development in a smear of lubricant no wider than the head of a screw. The adults can squeeze beneath the rails without lifting them.

Their backs bear the marks of the country. White ceramic dust collects in the shallow pits of their wing cases. Their feet darken with graphite. An old female has a scrape across her left wing where a cleaning brush once caught her against a bracket. The scrape has polished smooth through months of passing under the same low wire.

She moves confidently through places that appear impassable.

At the fractured block, she does not follow the ridge. She goes down its side, disappears beneath an overhang, and emerges beside the warm contact cover. There is a path there, but it consists of six separate footholds on surfaces facing different directions. She has used it often enough that her antennae barely pause at the gaps.

When the tower shudders, she stops with one foot still raised. After the movement passes, she touches the next foothold again before putting weight on it.

Other beetles follow odors rather than familiar routes. They arrive hungry, carried upward from bark, leaf litter, storehouses, nests. For hours they wander with their antennae spread wide, finding traces of food that end at bare metal. Some try to burrow into the white drift. Some keep returning to the seam, where the air carries a confused promise of everything outside.

The resident beetles meet them without ceremony. There are no guarded borders. There are only mouths too narrow for two bodies, food that can be stood over, and warm places already occupied.

A newcomer may shove an older beetle away from a dead fly, then lose the fly itself when a carrier gathers it into its brush.

The machines distinguish neither hunger nor possession.

They do, however, fail consistently. This makes them possible to live with.

A brush cannot enter the angle beneath a particular copper fastening. A carrier turns back when its forward feeler touches a living body, but continues pushing if it encounters only a leg. The repair arms pause during a voltage drop. The beetles learn these facts through contact, injury, and repetition. Their routes gather around the machines’ limitations.

The animals born here know very little open air. A moving carrier is ordinary to them; the blue distance outside is not.

Some have wings they never unfold.

The moths are less thoroughly at home.

Most enter from the mountain night, drawn first to warmth or light and then caught in the shelter behind the plate. Their wings arrive patterned with fine scales, but the passages wear them down. A moth resting between two wires has a bald stripe along each shoulder. Another has lost the tip of one hindwing to a turning axle. Folded upright, they resemble pale scraps caught in the machinery until a foot shifts or an antenna slowly uncurls.

They settle where the heated air rises.

On cold nights, their bodies become almost motionless. They remain attached to the metal by feet so fine that a single flake of paint can conceal one. As warmth returns, the first sign of life is often the mouthpart uncoiling and touching a film on the surface.

A few find enough to drink. Many do not.

There are places where moths have died holding the same posture as the living ones. Their neighbors rest beside them. Later, a beetle works at the base of a wing, or a fly lays eggs where the abdomen meets the thorax. Eventually the wings fall separately, and the body loses the outline by which it could have been recognized.

The little flies belong to these changes.

They emerge soft and pale from dark recesses, stand until their legs strengthen, and make brief, uncertain flights beneath the lamp. A flight across the whole chamber lasts less than a second. Some attempt it repeatedly, striking the same wire each time. Others walk almost everywhere, testing surfaces with their feet.

They are abundant after an influx of moths. Later, they become scarce.

The spiders persist through both conditions.

Each occupies a small height: the underside of a screw head, the corner of a raised solder bead, the place where a wire leaves the wall. Their webs are compact, made to catch what moves through a gap rather than what flies across open space. A thread may run from ceramic to nickel, another from nickel to a wire that trembles at every passing car.

The spiders continually adjust.

One has attached its lowest strand to an idle carrier. When the carrier resumes work, the whole web draws sideways. The spider crosses to the fixed wall, cuts two threads, and waits while the machine departs with the rest. Before the carrier returns, a new strand has been stretched above the rail.

Another spider lives near the seam, where it receives more food and more damage. Its web is rarely complete. It feeds while holding loose silk gathered under its body, as though there has not yet been time to put anything away.

Mites travel through all of this on the beetles.

Under a wing case, several can shelter in a fold that stays warmer and damper than the surrounding chamber. When the beetle stops to feed, one may descend. It examines the grease, the dead insect, the minute growth along a damp joint. It may return to its host or remain behind.

There are mites on the old female that have crossed the country many times without ever touching its floor.

For them, the beetle is the large, inhabited place. Its movements bring weather, food, collisions, periods of darkness. A cleaning brush passing over its back is a disturbance transmitted through another creature’s flesh.

At the central terminal, the repair arms work among all these smaller occupations.

One holds the damaged connection steady. One retrieves suitable fragments. One presses them into place. Their work has accumulated into an irregular bright patch, layered with shavings taken from surfaces elsewhere in the enclosure. At its edge, dried insect matter is trapped under a flattened strip of copper. The arms cannot remove it without disturbing the repair, so they have built around it.

A small beetle sometimes feeds there, within the space between two strokes.

It waits with its head under the fixed arm. The moving arm rises; the beetle advances. The arm lowers; the beetle withdraws. Neither has a complete account of the other. Their actions fit together for a few seconds at a time.

Then the tower moves.

Feet tighten against wire. Wings flatten. The machines halt at their measured distances. A crack opens beneath a drift, and the powder settles into it, carrying eggs, scales, a dead mite, a fragment of brush.

Afterward, the old female tests her six footholds. One is gone.

She reaches into the space where it was, draws back, and turns beneath the overhang. Her antennae touch the ceramic, then a rail, then the leg of another beetle approaching from the opposite side. Both pause. There is barely room for either to turn.

Above them, a repair arm carries a bright shaving toward the terminal. Below them, the fresh crack continues to shed dust. The beetles press past one another, and each goes on searching for a way through.
