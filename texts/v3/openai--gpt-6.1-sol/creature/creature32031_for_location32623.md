The smallest stone-backed animals are warm when you pick them up.

Not merely warmed by the sun. Beneath the hard upper surface there is a persistent heat, faint enough that you must cup one in both hands to notice it. The underside is soft and crowded: six short limbs, a pale mouth folded between the front pair, two dark grooves that open and close without quite becoming gills. The animal draws its feet inward but does not close itself completely. A little moisture shines along its belly.

Its back is not a shell in the ordinary sense. It grows in rounded patches, each patch separated from the next by a thin, yielding seam. Grains of the plateau’s stone are embedded in the surface. Younger animals are lighter and less convincing, with gray dust caught in a honey-colored skin. Age improves the resemblance. An old one can lie in the same place for so many seasons that the film growing on the surrounding rock continues across its back without a visible interruption.

They eat that film, but not from themselves. Their mouths cannot reach.

Instead, they graze one another.

Along the crowded creases you sometimes see a little procession of them, each resting its mouth against the back of the animal ahead. There is no leader worth naming. The first may be cleaning the underside of an overhang, or feeding on a larger sleeper, or simply waiting with its mouth closed. Eventually the line shifts, the animals exchange positions, and the backs exposed to light longest are brought within reach.

When frightened, they press down rather than flee. Their moist undersides adhere to the rock. You can mistake this for trust until you try to lift one.

The large folded animals carry whole neighborhoods of them.

These larger creatures are low and wide, with a head scarcely distinguished from the front of the body. What looks like overlapping shale is a series of thick skin folds hardened along the edges. Between the folds, the flesh is pale, finely ridged, and almost always damp. Small animals lodge there, feeding on shed skin and trapped dust. Some remain for their entire lives.

A full-grown host may span several yards. It has limbs, though you can watch for a day without seeing them: broad muscular lobes hidden beneath the outer margin. When it travels, it lifts one side, anchors the lobes, and pulls the rest of itself forward. The movement is less like walking than watching a heavy blanket being drawn across a bed.

Its head points downslope because its mouth lies beneath the foremost fold. Anything loosened on its back—dust, flakes of growth, the husks of its passengers—works gradually toward that mouth.

The arrangement is not clean. Food gets lodged. Passengers steal from the channels. Some bite into the soft folds. The host occasionally raises an edge and holds it open for hours, letting the dry air reach places that have become sore. The smaller residents retreat into neighboring grooves. A few are too firmly attached to leave.

The deep calls come from these animals. Beneath their backs are chambers that also serve as reservoirs, and air passing over the stored water produces the long, low notes. A nearly empty animal sounds different from a full one. So does one sheltering young beneath its body. Across a valley, the calls reveal things that sight cannot: which basin still holds water, which resting place has become crowded, where a familiar body has shifted out of reach.

An answer may take minutes because the answering animal must first adjust its weight, clear a vent, and draw enough air.

The long-legged grazers are lighter than they appear.

Their bodies hang between the legs like shallow bowls. The legs themselves are hollow for much of their length, braced inside by delicate partitions. Each foot divides into several soft toes that spread before accepting weight. This is why a step takes so long. A toe touches, withdraws, touches again. The other toes test adjacent patches. Only when the contact is secure does the body follow.

They do not merely avoid stepping on smaller creatures. They seek certain ones out.

A stone-backed animal makes a reliable foothold on dusty rock. The grazer rests a toe against its hardened back; the smaller animal presses itself down in response. Sometimes several little backs support a single foot. After the grazer moves on, its toe leaves a trace of mineral-rich moisture, which the animals beneath it spend the next hour collecting.

The grazer feeds with a fringe rather than a jaw. It lowers its body until the fringe brushes the ground, then draws the fine teeth inward through the surface growth. A feeding arc may take an afternoon. At its center the rock is clean; along its edges the fringe leaves narrow strips untouched.

Their young cannot reach the ground. They hang underneath the adults, clasped among folds of belly skin, and eat what gathers on the feeding fringe when it is drawn back into the mouth. A mother with several young looks unusually untidy, trailing little pale limbs beneath her.

There are hunters here too.

One resembles a length of grit caught in a crack: segmented, dull, scarcely thicker than a stem. It can remain motionless with its front end beneath a sleeper for days. Its prey are the softer animals that emerge to drink at dawn. When one settles against it, the hunter bends around the body and closes dozens of minute hooks.

The struggle is quiet, but not gentle. It may last until midday.

Another predator lives beneath the lips of stone. Its transparent wings are not used for sustained flight. They spread to catch condensation, and occasionally to carry it a short distance downwind. Most of its life is spent upside down, feet hooked into imperfections too small for a human finger to feel. It lowers a thread into the traffic below.

The thread smells of damp growth.

An animal following that scent encounters a soft knot at the end, something apparently edible. By the time it begins feeding, the knot has tightened around its mouth. The winged creature reels it upward a fraction at a time.

Under some ledges there are dozens of these threads. After rain, they glitter. The animals below learn the dangerous routes slowly, and forget them when a shower washes the scents away.

The soft bodies in the deepest cracks have fewer choices. Many cannot leave at all.

They begin as drifting specks in the shallow basins, then settle into stone pores or the porous walls of old buildings. Their bodies expand to fit the available chamber. One may become a narrow ribbon; another a bulb with three blind extensions, pressed into an irregular cavity. The exposed end opens against the air, gathering moisture and particles on a ring of minute hairs.

They can withdraw so completely that the recess appears empty. During the hottest weeks they seal it with a skin that dries to the color of dust.

Some survive inside a wall after the entrance to their chamber has been covered. Moisture still reaches them through the material. They feed on what seeps past, grow smaller, and wait. When a roof eventually cracks, bodies sealed away for years unfold toward the opening.

The building animals make room for them, though not deliberately in any simple sense.

The crust-makers are broad-mouthed creatures with short, powerful forelimbs. They chew grit with a secretion that turns cloudy when exposed to air, then press the mixture into place with their breasts. Each leaves a shallow body-shaped depression in the fresh surface. Later workers fill these hollows imperfectly, so the walls become layered with impressions.

The animals that maintain the chambers beneath are slender and moist. They carry water internally and release it against drying walls. Their food grows on those walls. They deepen the chambers by eating inward, and strengthen them with what they leave behind.

At the entrances live hard-headed little creatures with brushlike mouths. They feed on debris caught in the draft. An entrance stays open because closing it would deprive them of food. As the surrounding structure grows, they work farther outward.

None could make a lasting shelter alone.

The crust-makers sometimes wall off useful passages. The chamber-feeders sometimes hollow a roof until it falls. The entrance animals enlarge an opening enough for dry wind to reach the eggs. A compound contains repairs, abandoned rooms, sudden collapses, and new walls begun against the wrong surface.

Still, enough chambers remain cool. Enough eggs survive.

In some buildings, the current occupants have never encountered the kind of animal that laid the first courses. They follow old passages because those passages remain damp. They add material where water gathers and retreat from places where it does not. A house changes its inhabitants, then its shape, then most of its substance, while retaining a cool inner hollow that has never fully dried.

The trees support a different kind of persistence.

The leaf-shaped animals cling by a single broad foot. Their backs carry a thin growth that flourishes in light; their mouths feed at the foot’s edge, taking what the growth sheds and what settles from the tree. They move when the branch no longer suits them, but seldom far. On an old tree, the youngest occupy the newest leaves. Larger ones cluster around the branch forks, where moisture runs down the bark.

Their eggs are laid beneath actual leaves. At hatching, the young are nearly transparent and have to crawl to the light. Most vanish before their backs darken. Those that remain acquire the tree’s dull colors, though their edges are slightly too regular and their little breathing pores open along one side.

A heavily occupied branch bends under them. Over years that weight helps give the trees their crooked shapes.

Death leaves the plateau slowly.

A large animal stops calling before it stops breathing. Its passengers begin to move toward the margins. Some depart; others remain and feed deeper. The body loses its warmth, but its folds still collect water, and its hard back continues to shelter the things beneath it.

Eventually the broad lobes relax. The underside settles against the ground. Soft tissue is eaten away through narrow openings, while the mineral-rich outer layers weather into plates.

For a long time afterward, the animals passing over it still alter their steps. The place is useful in the same ways it was before: warm at evening, shaded beneath the rim, damp in the grooves at dawn.

Only close inspection shows the change. There is no slow yielding underfoot. The vents are full of dust. In one of them, a crust-maker has begun pressing the first wet mouthfuls of a wall.

By the next season, an entrance may stand there.

By another, something soft will have found the cool space behind it.
