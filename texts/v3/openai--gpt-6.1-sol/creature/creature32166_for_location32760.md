At certain seams, a change in pressure takes longer to pass than it should.

The delay is very small. Water rises beneath a leaf, pauses, and continues into the next chamber. Far below, a cord carries a little less current while another carries a little more. Nothing at the surface has moved far enough to be seen. The hanging droplet remains where it was.

But the delay returns. It occurs even after the membrane that first sustained it has been absorbed and replaced.

Something lives in that interval.

It has no cavity to occupy. There is nowhere one could open the growth and find it curled among the roots. A cut would expose the ordinary pale tissue: conduits, wet partitions, threads passing through thicker threads. Nevertheless, the cut would have struck it. Pressure would be withdrawn from certain chambers. Current would take longer routes. Several thousand leaves, spread over a distance too great to see from any one of them, would become fractionally harder.

These responses would begin before the wound had begun to close.

Its body is difficult to separate from the bodies that support it. Some of the cells belong to it for only a few hours. Others remain involved for centuries, renewing themselves so precisely that their involvement never lapses. A root can enter its body, serve in it, and leave it without changing position. A sheet of tissue may be part of two such inhabitants at once, holding their different activities on opposite sides of the same membrane.

What gives it continuity is an arrangement of dependencies. This chamber must fill before that cord discharges. That discharge permits a distant partition to relax. The relaxation changes the resistance of a third region, which allows the first chamber to fill again. There are millions of these relations, nested within one another. Most perform the ordinary work of the plain. A few also preserve the delay.

It is there that the inhabitant keeps its own time.

The land beneath it has no day or season worth distinguishing. It distinguishes other things. A returning quantity of water may carry slightly less salt. A wall may yield more readily on one side than on the other. The current arriving through a buried cord may bear the effects of compression hundreds of miles away. To it, these differences are separate and substantial. It does not experience them as a dim version of sight.

A pressure that has traveled through warm roots is unlike the same pressure conveyed through a thin surface fold. Water held for a long time in a deep chamber is unlike water newly gathered from the air. Each arrives with a particular weight of consequence. There are passages through which the inhabitant can recognize a distant province without ever receiving material from it, only the small adjustments that province imposes upon everything between.

Much of what it senses is itself.

Its farthest parts reach it late. A disturbance at one margin may still be approaching while the tissues there have already returned to their former condition. By the time it knows of a softened membrane, that membrane may have been replaced twice. It responds to the place nonetheless. The new tissue takes up an obligation incurred by the old.

This does not confuse it. Its experience was never built around the expectation that one piece of flesh would remain one piece of flesh.

It has memories, although no chamber contains a store of them. Something that once happened changes how readily a particular sequence can happen again. A current passes more easily along a route that has been used. A pressure must reach a slightly higher value before an old release occurs. These differences persist through replacement. The new walls form under the conditions left by their predecessors.

In this way it remembers a rupture whose edges have long since vanished, an interval of excessive warmth, a rare failure of a conduit deep enough that no surface leaf was affected. It remembers other inhabitants.

They are not arranged beside one another like animals in a field. Their bodies overlap through entire provinces. One may occupy the broad leaves and their shallow rootwork while another extends through the same place at greater depth. A third may involve only scattered knots of tissue, connected across the first two by cords that also serve the land.

Usually they pass through one another without consequence. Their activities have different durations, or require different thresholds. Sometimes two reach for the same release.

Then, in a chamber smaller than a seed, a membrane is held shut.

Neither can open it alone without disrupting a much larger circuit. Each begins to alter the conditions around it. One draws warmth away. The other supplies a trace of dissolved material that makes the wall less yielding. The disagreement spreads through many miles of perfectly quiet growth.

It may last longer than a forest elsewhere would take to rise and fall.

Eventually one yields, or they establish a sequence in which both can proceed. The membrane opens. A bead of water enters a neighboring chamber. There is no mark at the surface, but both inhabitants retain a disposition toward the place. There are routes they avoid in one another’s presence. There are other routes they enter more readily together.

Some have shared these arrangements for so long that their limits can no longer be determined. They still retain distinct intervals of attention. One responds to changes in the upper plain while the other remains occupied with a slow compression beneath it. Yet a loss in either is answered by both.

The oldest are not necessarily the largest. A very old inhabitant may involve only a basin and the dense pale growth beneath its rim. It has relinquished distant connections as neighboring arrangements became more stable. Its whole existence now passes through a few precise relations: the tension of the waxy edge, the temperature under the water, the resistance of several buried cords.

It is attentive to that basin in extraordinary detail.

A droplet joining the black water changes the load above it. The change descends through the living walls. It knows where the droplet entered, how warm it was, which part of the rim released it. For a little while the event occupies much of its attention. Elsewhere, far larger inhabitants barely register the combined condensation of a thousand miles.

None of them has needed to learn distance as an exposed space to cross. A distant place is one that answers slowly, or through many intervening relations. A nearby place may be inaccessible because every usable connection belongs to something else.

They can withdraw. They can extend into newly available tissue. They can abandon a region in which maintaining their continuity has become too costly. Such movements occur beneath leaves that keep exactly the same outline. An inhabitant may spend centuries withdrawing from a province and remain present at both its margins until the last relation has been released.

Occasionally one ceases.

There need be no failure in the land. The water continues to be sorted; the oils continue along their conduits. A particular cycle no longer restores itself. Its delays disperse among the ordinary delays of the tissue. The rootwork that carried it remains warm and sound.

Others encounter the change gradually. A sequence that used to meet resistance now proceeds without it. A pressure sent along an accustomed route returns unmodified. For a long time, an old partner continues to make room.

New inhabitants begin just as quietly. A small imbalance establishes a circuit that sustains the conditions of its own return. At first it participates in only a few releases. Then it acquires dependencies, and the tissue around it begins to be replaced in ways that preserve them. It comes to distinguish a disturbance from the consequences of its own activity.

There is no first breath. There is a first occasion on which it waits.

At the basin’s edge, one of them is waiting now. Much of its attention lies below the exposed pleat, among the clean junctions disappearing into darkness. A minute increase in pressure has arrived from far away. It could release the pressure through the upper fold. It has done so many times.

Instead, it holds it, allowing other differences to arrive.

Above, the droplet remains suspended at the leaf tip. Within its glossy weight are changes too small for an eye to follow. The inhabitant attends to them, and to the slow answer coming through the roots.
