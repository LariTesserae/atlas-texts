The one who lives in the shelter is not always inside it.

Sometimes you can sit at the doorway for a long time, listening to water drip from the roots overhead, before realizing that part of what you have taken for the roof bed has been breathing. A broad fold rests among the roots. Beneath it, something draws air through a narrow opening, holds it, and lets it out against the stone.

The breath is warmer than the air. It carries a faint smell of salt and crushed stems.

There is no need to move away. If you have sat down without trapping a limb or pulling a root loose, the breathing will continue. Eventually a hand may come out of the growth and rest beside you.

It is unmistakably a hand, although the fingers are unusually long and the palm has a second shallow hollow below the thumb. The nails are thick and smooth. They are used against stone, but not for digging. Their edges are worn into little curves, as though each has spent a great deal of time fitting itself around something.

The hand does not reach for you. It rests with two fingertips in the water.

Its owner is feeling what has changed.

***

Unfolded, the resident is longer than a person. Standing is possible, but appears to be uncomfortable and is seldom attempted. Most movement takes place with the belly near the floor and the limbs arranged to keep as much skin as possible in contact with the surroundings.

There are four limbs that bear weight. There are two smaller ones that ordinarily remain folded against the chest.

At first, the smaller pair might be mistaken for folds of loose skin. They do not help with walking. One is used to hold food while the other examines it, or to secure a leaf while the larger hands draw it close. They end in three blunt fingers each. During sleep they are tucked beneath the throat.

The larger limbs have joints enough to make the posture difficult to understand by touch. A knee may be where you expect a shoulder. What feels like the back of an elbow can turn out to be the underside of a wrist. Nothing bends abruptly. The resident changes shape by laying one part down and lifting another, allowing its weight to pass through a succession of arrangements.

The skin is thickest along the back. There it feels like the wet underside of a broad leaf, with a nap too short to catch between the fingers. Along the sides it is almost bare. Shallow furrows carry condensation toward the belly, where small muscular pockets collect it.

It drinks from these pockets. It also drinks from the pool, slowly, with its mouth below the surface.

The head is broad and low. There is a mouth, two breathing openings, and several soft ridges that lift when unfamiliar sounds arrive. There are no eyes to close.

On either side of the jaw, under the skin, lie firm oval plates. A fingertip resting there can feel them shift toward a sound. The resident does not appreciate being touched on these plates. It will remove your hand with considerable gentleness the first time.

The second time, it will leave.

***

It eats many things, but not very much of any one thing.

A wet film scraped from a root can occupy it for half an hour. It rolls the scraping between the small hands, adds a little water from a belly pocket, then presses the mixture into its mouth. Sometimes it takes the swollen end of a stem. Sometimes a winged insect that has settled nearby is gathered up with no greater haste than a drop of water.

The shelled animals are usually left alone.

Once in a while the resident lifts one, turns it over, and touches the rim of its shell with a nail. The animal answers by drawing inward and striking some hard part of itself against the shell’s interior.

The resident may tap again.

These exchanges are shorter than the calls that travel around the basin. They have a different rhythm, with long pauses during which the animal hangs motionless between the fingers. Eventually it is put down, often several paces from where it was picked up.

It is difficult to know whether this is conversation, examination, or merely a habit that neither participant has reason to abandon.

There are several such habits.

A strip of leaf is always left across the shallow channel nearest the shelter. The current carries it away; the resident replaces it. Sometimes a grazing animal follows the strip across. Sometimes nothing does.

Cold water is lifted from one of the sockets and rubbed into the creases of the smaller hands.

Before entering the shelter, the resident touches the roof, the left side of the doorway, and a place on the floor just inside. There is no obvious difference among these surfaces. It touches them nevertheless, even when retreating from an unpleasant disturbance.

The dry room holds a few possessions.

On the higher ledge is a bundle of fibrous strips, each knotted at one end. Some are supple. Others have stiffened almost to the hardness of wire. They are handled individually, not used to bind anything.

On the lower ledge are three shells, empty and intact, and a rounded piece of the mountain’s stone. The stone fits into the hollow below the thumb.

Nothing is stored in the center of the room. The resident sleeps there.

***

It knows the basin extremely well, but its routes are not always the routes a visitor would choose.

It may circle halfway around the fringe to reach a place a few strides away across open stone. On another occasion it will pass directly through the center, moving with a confidence that makes the same crossing seem briefly simple.

The raised floor within the socket ring is always avoided.

The resident can reach across it. Once, when a soft-winged insect became stranded in a bead of water there, it lay down outside the ring and extended one of its smaller hands until the fingers could free the insect. It then held the creature against a damp patch of skin until the wings loosened.

It did not put weight on the raised floor.

If both hands are placed on the central upright and the drops arrive together, the resident stops wherever it is.

The breathing openings close. The broad folds along its back draw tight. Every hand that can reach the floor presses flat against it.

It remains this way longer than the other animals remain quiet.

Afterward, it approaches the upright and touches the wet depression. It feels the rounded edges, then the floor around the base. If someone is standing there, it examines their hands as well.

There is nothing threatening in the examination. It turns each hand palm upward, runs a fingertip through the creases, and pauses over the places where stone has pressed against skin.

Then it goes to the pool and submerges its own hands.

For a while it will not accept food.

***

The groove beside the threshold fits its heels.

This is easiest to discover when it sits beside you. The hind limbs fold forward, the feet settle into the warm water, and the hardened pads at their ends rest against the doorway.

They do not fill the groove exactly. There is space beside them, and a wider worn patch beneath one. Perhaps the feet were once larger. Perhaps others have sat in the same posture.

The resident does not occupy the whole mark.

If you are already sitting there, it feels for the position of your legs and settles where it will not crowd them. A fold of its flank may rest against your arm. Moisture accumulates between the two bodies. Eventually it shifts a little to let that moisture drain.

It makes sounds, although not often.

Some come from the throat: low, breathy syllables that barely travel beyond the doorway. Others are made by pressing the small hands against the chest and releasing them. The skin beneath gives a soft double knock.

When the shells begin answering one another around the rim, the resident occasionally joins them. Its contribution is brief. Two chest sounds, a pause, then one more.

The sequence continues without any obvious acknowledgment.

At other times, one of the shelled animals near the doorway makes those same three notes when the resident is away.

***

It is not a keeper in the sense that everything depends on it.

Roots grow across channels it has cleared. Animals eat from stems it has carefully folded aside. A hanging frond can fall over the shelter entrance and remain there for days while the resident sleeps beneath it.

The place does not wait for its attention.

Still, it does things that leave the hollow more habitable.

It pushes accumulated fibers out of the pool’s narrow drainage grooves. It lifts a damaged stem so the water trapped in its joints can escape. It works a fingertip between a root and the rim of a socket, easing the root free without breaking it, then lays it in an adjacent seam.

These tasks are performed slowly. The resident often pauses halfway through one, rests its jaw against the ground, and listens.

Sometimes it never resumes.

A visitor who finishes the task may receive a touch on the wrist. A visitor who finishes it differently may receive the same touch.

There is no reliable way to read approval from this.

There is, however, a clear response to haste. If you pull a trapped root sharply enough to tear it, the resident places a hand over yours and holds it still. It does not squeeze. It waits until your muscles cease preparing to pull again.

Only then does it let go.

***

No one has seen it leave the low rim.

It approaches the boundary when the distant weather deepens. Its body rises slightly; the soft ridges on its head spread apart. Air moving up the mountain passes across them.

It can remain there through an entire change of weather.

When it returns, it sometimes carries things that were not in the basin before: a narrow seed, a scrap of dry membrane, an angular fragment too light to be stone. These are brought to the threshold and examined with the small hands.

Most are eaten or discarded.

A few are placed on the high ledge with the knotted strips.

If there were once others like it, the resident has retained ways of making room for them. It leaves half the lower ledge clear. It sleeps curled toward one wall. While sitting at the doorway, it occasionally lifts its flank and settles farther to the side, although nothing has approached.

This may have begun as a response to visitors. It may be older.

You can stay beside it without learning which.

Over time, it comes to recognize the sounds your body makes before you have noticed making them: the catch in your breathing before you stand, the movement of cloth as you reach toward a leaf, the small shift of water when your foot searches for a channel’s edge.

If you return often, a hand will sometimes be waiting at the difficult part of the crossing.

It offers two fingers. You may hold them until the floor rises beneath your next step.

Then the hand withdraws, and the resident continues whatever slow journey brought it there.
