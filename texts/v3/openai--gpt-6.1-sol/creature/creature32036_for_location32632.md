One of the pale disks on the back wall is larger than the others.

It sits below the inspection pane, a little to the left, where a run of old glaze changes from white to the faintest possible gray. Its outline is irregular. One side follows a shallow groove in the ceramic; the other spreads across it. Seen from the door, it looks like the mark left by something that was once bolted there.

It is an animal.

Its upper surface is covered in minute scales, though “scales” suggests something harder and more separate than these are. They overlap like the skins on cooling milk. Water enters beneath their edges and leaves through a slit near the animal’s center. The slit opens so slightly that its movement is visible only when a particle passes through it.

Underneath, the body is rose-colored.

There are hundreds of feet, arranged in imperfect circles. Each ends in a small cup. Some grip the wall while others release, reach, and grip again. The animal advances by passing a looseness through itself. A ripple goes around its edge, and when the ripple has completed its circuit, the disk is a little farther along.

It takes most of the night to reach the warm pipe.

This is not because it cannot move faster. When disturbed, it can gather its margin inward and run on a smaller number of feet, becoming a puckered oval, startlingly quick. But ordinary travel requires inspection. Its leading edge lifts over every change in the wall. Soft threads emerge from beneath it and enter pits in the glaze. Where they find a deposit worth eating, the animal stops.

Its mouth is on the underside, well away from the central slit. It is wide and shallow, with no jaws. A rough membrane passes over the ceramic, gathers a film, and folds back into the body.

The film includes bacteria, mineral dust, the waste of the silver swimmers, and substances brought up from the lower mountain in quantities too small to color the water. The animal takes all of it. What it cannot use is pressed into flat pellets and left behind, stuck to the wall. The snails sometimes eat these.

At the pipe, its feeding changes.

It settles beside the dark fronds, where warm water spreads over the ceramic before entering the room. Its scales rise. The spaces between them darken. Fine structures, previously tucked beneath the surface, stand out into the flow.

Now the whole upper body is feeding.

For an hour or more it holds this position. Bubbles lodge against it, making the animal appear blistered. A red-legged scavenger may climb across its back and pick among the scales. Usually the disk allows this. When the scavenger reaches the central slit, however, the body contracts sharply enough to throw it off.

By morning the disk has returned beneath the window.

It settles into the gray patch with considerable care. First the lower edge fits itself into the groove. Then the left side spreads. Last, the upper margin flattens, leaving no shadow beneath it.

The patch is not a nest. There is nothing built there, nothing stored. Yet the animal returns to it even when a nearer part of the wall offers the same light and temperature.

If it is moved a little, it goes back.

If another disk occupies the patch, both remain there for a while, one overlapping the other. Their feet touch. Small contractions pass between them. Eventually one leaves, though the larger animal does not always win.

There are scars across its underside from such meetings: pale crescents where cups have pulled against living tissue. The wounds have closed without becoming smooth. Some of its feet are divided. Others are missing. It travels competently with what remains.

It is sensitive to the machinery through whichever feet are holding the wall.

A normal valve knock makes its margin tighten. A pump’s hesitation makes it flatten so thoroughly that water can no longer pass beneath its scales. During a prolonged stoppage it begins moving toward the nearest bolt, where vibration from the mountain reaches the room through a shorter path.

Even after the pumps resume, it may stay at the bolt for several hours.

The bright side of the chamber matters to it, too, though it has no visible eyes. Beneath its milk-colored covering lies a branching stain of green. In strong afternoon light the stain broadens until it almost reaches the margin. When clouds cover the pane, it retreats toward the center.

A shaded disk survives. A disk kept in darkness grows thinner.

Nothing about its nightly journey explains why it also needs the day.

On the shaft side of the wall, directly behind its resting place, moisture gathers into a ring.

At first there are separate droplets, each seated in a tiny irregularity of the metal. Over time they join. The ring is wider than the animal, and slightly lower. Dust adheres to its wet edge, making it visible long after the water has evaporated.

No hole passes through the wall.

When the disk leaves, the ring dries from the top down. When it returns, the upper beads appear first.

A person touching those beads would find them cool. A person reaching into the chamber and touching the animal would find it warm, almost as warm as the pipe. The disk would draw in its edge beneath the finger. It would not flee immediately. Its feet would tighten one after another, testing whether the disturbance was still there.

Occasionally, small disks appear beneath the larger one.

For several days they remain attached to its underside, looking like additional feet without stalks. Then they separate and spread out over the wall. Some are no larger than the red cups on the ceiling. They have translucent bodies and conspicuously pink centers.

They do not know the route to the warm pipe.

The current carries a few away. Others wander toward the door and stop at the boundary between water and air. Here their edges curl upward. They extend the fine threads that adults use to examine the ceramic, touch the dry threshold, and withdraw them.

One young disk has remained near the screw hole where the shoot grows.

It shelters below the screw head during the pump stroke. In the interval it climbs far enough to graze the green film around the roots. It never reaches the clear leaves. When the shoot bends, its stem passes over the animal without touching it.

The larger disk continues its journeys on the far wall. Some nights it pauses midway, letting the warm current reach it there. On others it arrives at the pipe before the fronds have released their bubbles. Its route shifts by the width of a foot or two as deposits gather and are eaten.

Below the pane, its resting place is becoming smoother.

The shallow groove still receives its lower edge, but the gray glaze beside it is worn almost white. Each morning, after the final adjustment of its feet, the animal lies with a little more of its body exposed to the light.
