The inhabitant is usually mistaken for a patch of older stone.

It rests against the mountain wall, a little above the seep, where pale crust has built up around a long, shallow recess. From the basin, you see what looks like a fracture with several smaller fractures branching from it. There is a darker place at one end. After rain, water collects there.

Then the darker place closes.

The creature has been lying with one eye open.

Even awake, it is difficult to separate from its surroundings. Its back consists of overlapping plates, creamy white at their exposed edges and nearly transparent toward their centers. Mineral ridges continue across them from the rock on either side. Some of these connections break when it moves. Others stretch into threads before parting.

It does not pull itself free all at once. A shoulder loosens first. One long limb comes away from the wall, followed by another. The lower part remains attached while the upper part leans forward, as though it were testing whether the basin could bear its weight.

Under the plates, its body is dark amber.

There is no clear boundary between flesh and what looks like fluid held in narrow compartments. When it bends, the dark material travels slowly ahead of the movement, collecting in the part that will bear the load. Its joints do not occupy the same places every time. A limb that had an elbow while reaching can become an almost uninterrupted curve while resting.

It is roughly the length of the stone bench. That is a poor measure of its size, because it seldom extends itself. Folded against the wall, it occupies less space than a sleeping dog. Crossing open ground, it can spread across three channels without stepping in any of them.

It has two working hands.

Each carries three fingers, long enough to fold twice against the palm. The fingers end in soft, dark pads, not claws. On dry stone they leave marks that fade from brown to yellow and finally disappear. In a pool, the pads expand slightly, and fine pale filaments emerge around their edges.

There are other limbs beneath the body, but they are short and usually hidden. You hear them before you see them: a faint, irregular scratching, easily confused with mineral crust breaking as it cools.

The head is small and held low. Its two eyes face somewhat downward. Their surfaces are smoky rather than black, with little bright inclusions that shift when viewed from different angles. They do not blink together. Sometimes one closes while the other remains fixed on a channel for many minutes.

There is no mouth on the face.

The mouth lies farther back, beneath the foremost pair of plates. When the creature settles over wet stone, the plates separate just enough to expose a narrow, fleshy seam. It feeds there. A film that scarcely wets a fingertip is enough to occupy it for half an hour.

Afterward, the film is gone, and the stone beneath it has acquired a delicate comb of fresh white ridges.

It eats something that arrives in the water, or leaves something behind, or does both. The snails gather in its feeding places once it has moved on. It permits them to climb over its lower plates. Sometimes it stops with a foot lifted until a snail has passed beneath.

Its movements are not uniformly slow.

Most are so measured that you look away before they finish. But when a leaf empties above it, one hand can be at the falling water almost before the leaf has risen again. The fingers close around the splash, briefly holding an irregular mass of water without letting it spread. It carries this to a root, a crack, or one of the pools, and opens its hand.

It does not do this with every spill. Much of the falling water strikes it and runs off.

Nor does it tend all three islands of vegetation equally. The patch around the seep receives close attention. The central mound is usually left alone. Beneath the shelter, it removes small leaves before they have finished uncurling, selecting them by touch and placing them in the cool cup.

The leaves sink.

The creature keeps its fingers immersed after they are gone.

During these pauses, its amber underside grows lighter. Small dark flecks drift through the fingers toward the wrists. Its posture loosens, and the plates along its back lift apart. You can see fresh tissue beneath them, moist and finely folded.

This is the best time to notice how much damage it carries.

One hand is smaller than the other. A section of its back has no plates at all, only a thick membrane with white granules embedded in it. Several short limbs end abruptly. Along one side, an old split has healed around a piece of dark material that might be metal.

The injuries do not appear to trouble it. It accounts for them in every movement. The smaller hand never takes its weight. When it turns, it turns toward the plated side.

Under the shelter, where the rain cannot reach it, it sometimes peels away a loose portion of its back.

The piece comes off reluctantly, drawing out wet threads. It lays the plate on the bench and cleans the exposed place with its fingers. For a while the removed plate is translucent enough to show the stone through it. Then it whitens. By the following morning it is brittle, and the creature breaks it into flakes.

Some go into the central pool.

Others are pressed into cracks at the rim.

There are many such flakes among the roots. The basin’s white crust makes them difficult to recognize.

The three hollows in the shelter wall fit its fingers, though not comfortably. To use them, it must brace the smaller hand against the bench and reach across its body with the larger one. It presses one finger in, waits, and then presses another.

A tick comes from an upright.

The creature remains still.

Sometimes there is a second tick. Sometimes it withdraws its hand and goes back to the seep.

Whatever the hollows once did, they do not always do it now.

At the outer rim, it examines the surviving posts without touching their tops. It feels around their bases, following the places where roots enter the fittings. It pulls out dead root fibers. Live ones it leaves in place.

Once, after a shower, it reaches below the edge.

The arm lengthens as the dark fluid moves into it. Three fingers close around a suspended stone, turn it over, and release it. The stone stays where it is, now dry side upward.

Nothing else happens.

The creature waits beside it for some time before returning to the basin.

The small black bird does not seem afraid of it. On days when the bird arrives, the creature usually moves toward the leaning post, although it may take several minutes to get there. The bird watches with its beak open.

At close range, you can see that it has brought something: a narrow brown husk held against its tongue.

The creature raises a finger. The bird deposits the husk on the pad, shakes its head, and waits again while the creature turns it over. Occasionally the bird pecks it back up and leaves with it. More often, the creature places it in the water at the post’s base.

Their exchange has no obvious conclusion. The bird departs when it is ready. The creature may remain there until the next rain.

There are smaller inhabitants in the hottest pools.

At first they look unlike the adult. They are narrow and jointed, with dark bodies and no visible plates. Their limbs are arranged close to the head; the rest of the body trails beneath the silt. They advance by gripping the pool’s mineral lining and drawing themselves forward in short pulls.

The paired pinholes along the waterline are made by their forelimbs.

When the adult approaches, they retreat. It does not pursue them. Instead, it lowers its mouth to the water and remains there, its back gradually flattening.

After it leaves, the small ones emerge again.

They gather where its underside rested. Their bodies become difficult to see against the disturbed silt, but their hands remain visible: three tiny fingers spread against the pale bottom of the pool.

There are never many. Two in one pool, perhaps four in another. One sometimes appears in the channel beside the bench and is returned to the hotter water, cupped between the adult’s hands. The journey is made close to the ground. At every interruption, the creature wets its fingers.

It watches the central hollow most carefully.

As the afternoon seep strengthens, it follows the water outward. It clears one obstruction, leaves another, and lowers a leaf tip into a channel whose current has thinned. By then its plates are carrying a faint yellow light. This is easiest to see beneath its body, where the sun cannot reach.

When water finally surrounds the buried ring, the creature settles at the hollow’s edge.

Its hands enter the pool.

The light diminishes first in its fingers, then along its limbs, and last in the transparent centers of its back plates. As the basin cools, its body draws together. The uncertain joints become definite. For a few minutes, it looks more like an animal than it has all day: tired, folded on its limbs, with its head resting beside one hand.

It may sleep then.

The smaller creatures come nearer during this interval. One climbs partway onto its back and presses its mouth against an exposed plate edge. The adult does not stir.

When droplets begin gathering overhead, the little one slides back into the water.

The adult lifts its head.

If someone is sitting beneath the shelter, it will sometimes approach before the rain starts. It stops beyond reach and studies the visitor’s feet. Then it extends the larger hand, slowly, with its three fingers spread.

It does not offer anything.

After a while, it touches the warm bench, then the ground, then the visitor’s shoe. The pressure is slight. Through the sole comes an unexpected sensation: not more heat, but a brief coolness shaped exactly like the fingertip.

The creature withdraws.

It clears a narrow channel around the feet, carrying the loosened mineral paste away in its palms. When the first heavy drops fall, water runs along this new channel and rejoins the old one beyond the bench.

The shoes remain dry.

By morning, the channel will have a white border.
