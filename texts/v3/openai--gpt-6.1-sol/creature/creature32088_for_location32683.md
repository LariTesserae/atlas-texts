The broad leaves belong to something that has almost no stem.

Each emerges from a flattened joint just beneath the soil, thickens slowly, and spreads until its rim meets an obstruction. Against another leaf, the rim turns slightly upward. Against stone, it grows thin and follows the contour. Several of the oldest leaves have taken the shape of the places available to them: one has a straight side where it grew beside the ceramic ring; another bears a deep notch made by a root that has since withdrawn. The notch remains. Growth does not return to fill it.

Their upper surfaces are dull green, almost black in the chamber. Underneath, they are pale and finely ribbed. At the junctions of the ribs are small translucent patches, each no larger than a grain of salt. A lifted leaf shows them only when light reaches it from the entrance at an angle. They look like imperfections in the tissue.

These are the parts that receive what the buried apparatus supplies.

The leaf does not have to touch the ground everywhere. A little space beneath it is useful, and its ribs keep that space open even as the soil rises through the slow incorporation of older leaves. The underside remains close to the bed without being sealed against it. Where a mineral grain presses too firmly into the tissue, a shallow blister develops around it. Eventually the grain is held in a pocket, separated from the delicate structures by a thickness of dull, expendable cells.

There are leaves here carrying stones they enclosed decades ago.

The needle growth is different. Its stems are hollow and jointed, with a narrow channel running through each joint. It occupies places where the broad leaves cannot lie: between wall and soil, inside the bend of a root, along the raised edge of a water hollow. The fine needles fold tightly against the stems during a long dry interval. When moisture becomes available, they loosen so gradually that the change is difficult to witness.

A spray that appears dead may be conserving the contents of less than a drop of water.

Its tips are blunt under magnification. Each ends in a clear cap through which condensation can enter. The taller sprays reach toward the ceiling roots, but stop short of touching them. Contact would bring water occasionally; it would also expose the stem to whatever movement occurs in the tree above. The last few millimeters are crossed only by a drop when one gathers large enough.

The climbing plant has no such reluctance. It fastens itself into the mortar with pads that swell and contract slightly as the wall’s moisture changes. Along a stem, the leaves are spaced unevenly. Some stretches bear none at all. These bare lengths carry water past dry stones to places where a leaf can rest within reach of the field.

What appears to be a vine following a seam is often a vine abandoning one. Its oldest attachments remain in the mortar after the living stem has shifted away. They are pale, tough flecks, easily mistaken for part of the wall.

Under the garden, these different growths meet.

They do not share a single root system. Their roots retain distinct textures: smooth cords beneath the broad leaves, dark hairlike bundles beneath the needles, flattened strands descending from the wall. Between them is a film too thin to lift intact. It coats soil grains and the softened remains of leaves. In a damp hollow it becomes nearly transparent; elsewhere it contracts to a faint sheen.

The film takes apart what falls.

It enters a dead leaf first through the cut end and the smallest cracks along the rim. The waxy surface persists after much of the tissue beneath it has gone. For a while, an old leaf is a green cover over a shallow bed of released minerals. Roots enter this bed from below. By the time the cover finally splits, there is very little left to scatter.

The film also moves water, though slowly enough that a wet patch can remain visibly separate from the dry ground beside it for days. Its living threads cross those apparently dry intervals. A root may receive moisture from a hollow well beyond the reach of its own branches.

There is competition here, but few abrupt victories.

A broad leaf can cover the foot of a needle stem. The stem then lengthens at an angle, spending several seasons reaching the leaf’s edge. A wall runner can intercept a reliable drip and leave the moss below dependent on condensation. The moss thins. Eventually its exposed surface releases water less efficiently, the hollow stays wet longer, and a new set of roots finds it.

Much of the crowding consists of such unfinished adjustments.

The closed buds beneath the leaves are not waiting for sunlight. Each contains a folded growth capable of surviving separation from the parent: a little root plate, two rudimentary leaves, and enough stored material to establish itself. Most never open. They remain attached, receiving a minute share of whatever the parent can spare.

One has opened near the bottom stair.

Its parent was damaged where a boot pressed a leaf against the stone. The injury healed, but water no longer passed evenly through the crushed ribs. Over the following years, the bud enlarged and lowered itself into the soil. It now has three leaves, none wider than a thumbnail. The damaged parent still lies beside it, alive along one edge.

The pale shoot approaching the smooth stone belongs to this younger growth. Its tip has already begun to flatten. On the side nearest the stone, the cells lengthen less; on the other, a little more. It will turn without ever touching the obstruction hard enough to leave a mark.

No part of the garden can perceive the whole chamber. A root encounters dampness, a leaf encounters pressure, a stem responds to the cold surface beside it. Even the apparatus supplies no signal the plants can use beyond the energy they receive.

Together, those small local responses keep the narrow passages open, bring roots beneath the falling water, and leave living tissue over almost every surface that can support it.

At the edge of the thin moss, a new thread has crossed onto the smooth stone. It extends less than the width of a hair beyond the old growth. The exposed tip has dried and stopped there.

Behind it, still sheltered by the moss, another branch is forming.
