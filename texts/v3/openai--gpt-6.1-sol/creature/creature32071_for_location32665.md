Beneath the western wall, a little way inside the stone, there is a layer that has never quite hardened.

It is thinner than a leaf in some places and thick enough elsewhere to fill the space between two hands. Mineral grains lie suspended in it, held apart by a clear substance that changes its consistency with warmth. Where the layer reaches a pore, it swells into the opening. Water collects against it, passes through it, and emerges onto the face of the wall.

This is the nearest the inhabitant comes to a skin.

The layer extends beneath the floor, divides around the deeper pools, and rises again under the eastern rim. It does not follow the hollow’s outline exactly. One narrow part reaches far down into the mountain. Another ends just beneath a dry shelf, although wet stone continues beyond it. Its shape is irregular and has altered over time. Some of the pale, rounded rock lies inside it; some merely rests against it.

A broken piece taken from the rim would look ordinary. Even a piece containing the clear substance would give little away. Exposed to the air, it would cloud, tighten around its mineral grains, and become a faintly waxy seam. It would remain moist for a surprisingly long time.

What lives here cannot be found by turning over a stone.

It holds water differently from the mountain around it. Along most of its body, water is allowed to descend. In a few narrow places it is passed from one portion of the living layer to another, each taking up a little before yielding it onward. The uphill channel lies above one of these places. Its exposed film travels with the movement beneath it.

The ascent is costly. During a long dry spell it slows first. Then the upper basin loses depth, and the ferns beside it draw their new growth close. The largest leaves continue to hold water. Their stems remain firm. Only after several rainless weeks does the clear substance beneath the western wall begin to contract.

There is no central chamber where these changes are decided. A dry place tightens; an adjoining wet place gives. The adjustment travels through the body so slowly that one side of the hollow may still be responding to yesterday’s sunlight while the other has begun to cool.

It experiences the plants through their roots.

A growing root presses against the living layer, releasing minute traces of what the leaf above has made. The substance around it softens. Moisture becomes easier to draw. In time, the two form a contact so close that neither has a clean surface there.

The plants remain separate lives. A fern can die while the hollow stays healthy. A broad-leaved stem can flourish at the expense of another stem in its own cluster. Their roots, however, are admitted only into certain places. Beyond those places, the living substance becomes dense and faintly bitter. A root tip pauses, thickens, and turns.

This is why there is room between the green islands.

A seed sometimes arrives with rain. Most lie on clean stone until their small store of food is exhausted. A few put out a root into a seam and find it closed to them. Very occasionally, after a plant has died and its old contacts have loosened, a seed finds a yielding place.

For the first season its growth is slight. It makes two leaves, then waits. Beneath it, the living layer changes around the unfamiliar root. If the contact holds, a third leaf follows. The new plant may be quite unlike the one it replaces.

The hollow’s appearance has therefore changed, though slowly. There was once another cluster beneath the largest shelf. Its roots left shallow branching impressions in a pocket of stone that now holds only water. The inhabitant still delivers moisture there. That part of its body has not finished adjusting to the absence.

It retains things in this way.

A season of heavy rain leaves passages more open than they were before. A prolonged drought narrows them for years. Pressure changes the arrangement of the mineral grains, and the changed arrangement persists after the pressure is gone. Through these alterations, the body acquires something like memory: not a picture of what happened, but a lasting difference in how it can respond.

The warmth rising from the mountain is its oldest constant. Sunlight is more complicated. It reaches the inhabitant through heated stone, through the work of leaves, and through a sparse growth of translucent filaments beneath the pools. These filaments are difficult to distinguish from reflections. They flatten at midday and extend as the light diminishes.

In the darkest basin they produce a faint luminosity of their own.

The wavering light on the shaded wall comes from them. It is strongest after several clear days and dwindles during overcast weather. The fern that opens toward it has grown accustomed to a source that remains after the rim has cut off the sky.

There is little here that would feed an animal. The exposed water contains almost nothing suspended in it. Fallen leaves stay tough, then become thin without passing through the soft, rich stages of rot. Small flying things approaching from the lower slopes rarely descend into the hollow; the damp air carries a mineral taste, and scarcely any scent of flowers or decay.

The inhabitant has no use for their absence. It does not guard the place. If something heavy were to step into a pool, the pressure would reach it as a sudden tightening in the floor. It would draw away from the disturbed patch. Hours later, if the weight had gone, it would return.

Its movements are usually much smaller than that.

At evening, the portion beneath the western wall relaxes. Water that has been held within it all afternoon begins to pass outward. One pore fills before the others. The bead on its lip enlarges, resting against pale stone until its weight exceeds what the surface can hold.

Far beneath the floor, a narrow passage opens a little.

The drop falls.
