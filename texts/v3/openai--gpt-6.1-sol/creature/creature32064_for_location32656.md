The first inhabitant you find is standing on a staircase between the loading platform and the covered street.

You have passed that staircase twice already. From below, you saw the gray underside of the landing and a rectangle of light. From above, you saw eleven wet steps and the handrail descending into shadow. There was no reason to stop.

On the third passage, you notice a hand resting on the rail.

It is a broad hand, bare, with a pale scar between the thumb and forefinger. The fingers lie loosely around the metal. A little water has gathered against the smallest finger, held there by the pressure of the skin.

The person attached to it is midway down the stairs. One foot is planted; the other has left the step above. They wear a heavy blue coat, trousers with a repaired knee, and shoes whose soles are thicker than any you have seen in the station’s cupboards. Their head is inclined toward the loading platform.

They are not concealed. Once you have seen them, it is difficult to understand how you failed to see them before.

You say something.

They remain where they are.

There is no frost on their eyelashes. Their face is neither slack nor strained. It holds the mild concentration of someone carrying a full cup through a crowded room, although both hands are empty.

When you move closer, the raised shoe touches the lower step.

You stop.

So do they.

At first this seems like a mistake of perception, something the machine vibration has done to your balance. You take another step, deliberately. Their shoulder shifts under the coat. A fold opens beside the repaired knee.

It is not an imitation. They do not move when you move your arm, or turn when you turn your head. Only your approach appears to give them room to continue.

By the time you reach the landing, they have descended one stair.

They look at you then.

Their expression changes slowly, and with immense care.

***

There are people in the stations.

Some are in the residential blocks, where they sit at kitchen tables or stand beside beds. Some occupy the galleries above the pumps. Others are on the roads between stations, in the cabs of maintenance vehicles, or halfway across bridges so long that a person on them cannot be distinguished from a defect in the cable.

They are easy to overlook because almost everything that ordinarily announces a person is missing.

No sleeve swings. No heel strikes the floor. There is no shifting weight, no breath lifting a shirt, no small adjustment of the mouth before speech. Even when you look directly at a window, a resident standing behind it belongs to the arrangement of the room as thoroughly as the radiator does.

You begin to find them by their belongings.

A shopping bag held away from a wall. A lunch tin beneath a control panel. A wet comb beside a basin. Two cups on a kitchen table, one close to the edge because someone has drawn it toward themselves.

There are many cups.

The food in the kitchens is plain. Dense bread, white roots cut into cubes, a dark paste kept in covered bowls. There are plates containing meals at every stage of being eaten. A spoon may be suspended just above a bowl, its contents retaining a rounded surface. A piece of bread rests between parted lips.

Nothing smells spoiled. Nothing smells freshly cooked.

The residents are not waiting for the food to change. Most are not waiting at all. Their faces have the private, ordinary expressions of people who do not expect to be observed.

A woman at one table is smiling at a man whose chair has been pushed back.

In another room, someone has stopped with their forehead against a cupboard door.

***

They can see you.

You learn this from their eyes, which are the first part of them to alter. A glance travels more readily than a hand. Sometimes it takes the length of a room. Sometimes, on the far side of a window, only the smallest dark adjustment is possible.

You learn to approach from in front.

Coming up behind someone produces no response until you have almost touched them. Then the whole body seems to attempt its turn at once. The movement is slight, but there is something distressing in it: a shoulder lifted too far, a heel pulled against the floor, the muscles of the neck drawing tight.

The person on the staircase managed to look at you without pain because you came into view slowly.

They are middle-aged, perhaps. Their hair is cut close around the ears. A small brass key hangs from the coat’s upper button, secured with a loop of red thread.

When you hold out your hand, they study it.

Then their fingers leave the railing.

You feel each separation: thumb, forefinger, the broad pads of the other three. The water that had gathered against their skin remains on the rail.

Their hand is colder than yours, though not as cold as the leaves outside. It is heavy. It grips with the exact pressure needed to make you stay.

For several seconds nothing else happens.

Then they draw a breath.

It is shallow and rough. Their chest rises as if a stiff garment has finally loosened.

They say a name.

You cannot tell whether it is theirs or someone else’s.

***

Contact allows more than proximity.

With your hand around theirs, a resident can finish a movement. A technician can withdraw an arm from an inspection hatch. A woman can set down the kettle she has been holding tilted over a cup. Someone lying in bed can turn onto their other side.

They move cautiously. They seem to know, before you do, how far your presence will carry them.

The technician does not try to leave the machine hall. After pulling his arm free, he flexes his fingers once, looks at the dark marks the hatch has left on his wrist, and sits down on a low stool. He closes his eyes before you let go.

The woman sets down the kettle and uses the remaining movement to touch the cheek of a child seated at the table.

The child’s eyes turn toward her.

You are still holding her other hand. For a little while, all three of you are there together.

It is difficult afterward to stop thinking about this.

***

Their homes were built for a life that included movement.

The stair treads have shallow hollows. Cupboard handles are polished on their undersides. There are patched sleeves, blunt pencils, replacement seals stacked beside pumps. Someone has kept a collection of small stones in a cigar box, each wrapped in paper and labeled.

There are calendars, too, though you never find two that agree.

Some carry a single mark. Some have whole months crossed out. One has been taken down and turned to face the wall. The pin remains above it.

The people have not all arrived at the same point.

A boy is reaching beneath a bed for a shoe. An old man has his coat on and a suitcase beside his chair. Two workers face one another across an open valve cabinet; one has begun to raise a finger, and the other is already laughing.

You cannot bring both far enough into motion to hear what was said.

You try for a long time.

Their laughter, when a little of it comes free, is startlingly quiet beneath the engines.

***

They understand the machinery better than you do.

The person from the staircase takes you, eventually, into a pump hall. This requires several visits. Each time, you return to wherever your last departure left them. They never go back to the stair. The new position holds as firmly as the old one did.

At the pump hall they place your hand on a cream-colored housing.

You feel the familiar vibration. Through the guard window, the piston remains halfway through its stroke.

They press your palm harder against the metal, then point to their own chest.

You think they are trying to explain a likeness.

When you shake your head, they give a small, impatient smile.

They lead you to the far end of the hall, where a narrow door opens into a tiled room containing an armchair. Beside it is a low table, a glass of water, and a pair of spectacles folded on a book.

An elderly woman sits in the chair.

The resident’s hand tightens around yours.

They have brought you here for her.

***

Most of what they ask is small.

An opened curtain. A button fastened. A dropped object returned to a hand that has almost caught it.

They do not ask for the engines to be stopped. They do not gesture toward the horizon or point down through the clouds.

One man, standing in an empty loading bay, wants you to move a wooden crate six inches to the left. He watches while you do it. When it is in place, his face relaxes.

You find a broken handle underneath.

Another resident asks to be taken outside. Her room faces the wrong way to receive the sun. With her arm over your shoulders, you get her as far as the covered street, then onto a service balcony. The effort is considerable. She is not infirm; she has to consider every step, and twice she loses the movement before her foot is properly settled.

At the balcony she lifts her face.

You wait with her until your own legs begin to ache.

When you leave, she is still there, one hand around the railing, eyes closed in the daylight. In the weeks afterward, you sometimes see her from the road.

She never looks unhappy.

***

There are limits to what you can give them.

Your touch will not carry through a chain of hands. You cannot wake a household by holding the youngest child. You cannot stand among a crowd and make a street begin.

When several residents are close, their eyes may all turn toward you. That is all.

The first time this happens, in a station waiting room, you almost run.

There are forty or fifty of them on the benches. A few stand by the timetable. A porter is leaning over a luggage trolley. Every face contains a different degree of recognition, alarm, curiosity, hope.

Near the doors, a woman has raised her hand.

You go to her because you have to choose someone.

She uses her breath to ask whether you have seen a child in a yellow coat.

You have not.

Later, on the bridge beyond that station, you find the child. The coat is unmistakable. He stands beside a maintenance barrier, turned toward the view, with one hand in his pocket.

You cannot hear him until you kneel and take his other hand.

He is not frightened. He wants to know whether the clouds are solid underneath.

Getting him back takes most of a day.

***

After a while, the country becomes inhabited to you.

You know which windows contain people. You know the man beside the switchyard gate, and the woman in the west gallery who has a needle caught halfway through a piece of cloth. You know where to find someone who can open a locked door, provided you can first reach their hand through the gap.

The long roads remain empty. The waterfalls retain their bright creases. There is still no birdcall.

But the lit rooms are no longer blank.

From the observation platform, through the coin-operated telescope, you can make out a person in one of the tiny gold windows across the gulf. They stand close to the glass. One arm rests against the frame.

You keep watching.

At that distance, you cannot be sure their eyes have found you.

Before you leave, you raise your hand anyway.
