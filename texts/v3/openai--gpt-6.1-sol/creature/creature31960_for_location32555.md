The first sign of one is usually a clean place on the rock.

Not a scrape. Scrapes have a direction, and this is a pale oval, sometimes as broad as a loading door, from which the mineral glaze has been lifted without disturbing the basalt beneath it. There may be six or seven such marks along a ridge. They occur on opposite sides of fins, on the undersides of ledges, in places where a machine could not have reached to polish them.

At the center of each oval is a smaller patch that has not been cleaned. A little island of silver.

The animal that made the marks may still be there.

You look along the ridge and see what appears to be a cable laid between two pinnacles. It is difficult to follow against the water. Where it crosses dark stone it shows faintly white; where it passes into daylight it disappears, except for a narrow line of internal beads. Farther along, it divides. One branch descends the face of the mountain. Another seems to end in empty water.

Then the cable shortens.

Something draws itself over the ridge, taking several minutes to bring a part of its body into view. It does not haul its weight upward. The water supports it. The motion is more like a length of slack being gathered, with one portion tightening while another loosens. At last there is a broad translucent body above the summit, and the apparent cables resolve into limbs.

The station crews call them stays.

The name began with the animals’ habit of fastening themselves between pieces of rock. It also serves adequately when someone asks how long they remain there.

An adult has eight principal limbs, although it can be difficult to count them. Each divides near its end, and the divisions can divide again. They are not tentacles all the way down. Their long middle sections contain a series of stiff, overlapping rods, visible as pale strokes through the flesh. Only the last few meters are freely flexible.

At full extension, a large stay can place its feet on outcrops two hundred meters apart.

Its body occupies comparatively little of that span. The central mass is flattened, a long soft lozenge with a thick rim and a shallow depression along its upper surface. In clear light you can see channels inside it, some empty, some carrying a slow procession of white material. There is no single conspicuous head. A short, folded structure on the underside holds the mouth. Along the rim, dark pits open and close independently.

They have eyes, but small ones. Light tells them which side of a mountain they are on, whether another body is passing overhead, whether the ice has changed. It does not give them their most useful account of the world.

For that, they put their feet down.

Each foot spreads into a thin oval membrane. Its outer edge seals to the rock; the center remains slightly raised. Within that protected space, a small hard organ rests against the basalt. The animal can increase or ease its pressure with considerable precision.

The isolated silver patch in an old footprint is where this organ stood.

A stay settles with its feet at different heights, often on different faces of the same pinnacle. One limb may lie against a column of basalt while another reaches over the ridge to touch the far slope. A third is held free in the water. The arrangement changes slowly, but it matters. Through one foot comes the vibration traveling up the mountain. Through another, the vibration that has crossed the basin. Through the loose limb comes the movement of the water itself.

There is no interval in which the animal hears silence.

Its sensory organs work within the roar, registering small changes in the strain of their own tissues. A shell cracks under a grazer’s mouth. A loose stone shifts beneath a hunter. Far away, something attaches itself to the same ridge.

The stay does not turn toward these events. Somewhere inside its nearly transparent body, a passage narrows. Fluid moves into a limb. A foot lifts.

Watching from a vehicle, you can miss the entire response. The animal’s outline remains almost unchanged. It has merely transferred its attention from one point of contact to another.

They feed where the smaller life collects.

A narrow fold under the body can be extended into a long feeding ribbon, much softer than the supporting limbs. At its tip are rows of tiny hooks and a lip that seals over irregular stone. The ribbon works into cracks, draws out clusters of pale animals, and conveys them inward beneath a moving cover of flesh.

The hooks do not excavate the basalt. A stay can spend weeks at a fissure that appears empty to a human observer, taking organisms from deep inside while leaving those nearest the entrance untouched. After it departs, the mouths of the cracks are still crowded.

On the shell beds around pumping stations, the feeding ribbon is kept folded. The animals there are too heavily armored, and the grazers are liable to bite it. A stay living near a station instead lays its ribbon through the open spaces behind the intake collar. Small animals shelter there from the flow. Eggs lodge there. Fragments loosened by the grazers pass inward.

Occasionally an engineer sees the ribbon pressed against the inside of a grille.

It withdraws when touched, with no obvious haste. By the time the engineer has fetched a light, it is usually somewhere else.

The largest stays do not live at the stations. The flow requires too much continual correction of their posture. Young ones flourish there, anchored beneath gantries and behind intake towers, where their limbs are still short enough to resemble pale straps.

They fasten to bolts, hinge recesses, the grooves between ribs in a housing. Their presence is often discovered during maintenance, when a cover proves unexpectedly difficult to remove.

A juvenile will let go of most of its attachments but retain one.

You can lift a plate away from a machine and find the animal stretched between the plate and the casing, a hand-sized body suspended from a thread that grows longer as you move. Its feeding fold closes. The dark pits around its rim contract. The last foot remains in place.

Crews have learned to set such plates down nearby rather than pull.

Given time, the animal chooses another foothold. Sometimes this takes the rest of the shift.

Their eggs are held in the shallow depression on the parent’s back, beneath a film so clear that the brood looks like a patch of warmer water. There may be several hundred at first. As they develop, the eggs become individually visible: little white knots, each already folded around its own limbs.

The parent moves its feet more frequently while carrying them. It avoids the exposed tops of ridges and settles in saddles, with its body low enough for the current to pass over it without pulling at the brood membrane.

The young leave one at a time.

A slit opens in the covering. One small stay unfolds, attaches a foot to the rim of its parent, and tests the water with the others. It may remain there for days. Eventually it lets go.

Most become food before reaching rock. Their limbs spread during the descent, slowing them so much that a juvenile can take several hours to fall a hundred meters. Transparent swimmers arrive from seemingly empty water and take them with small, precise movements of the jaw.

Those that land begin feeding almost at once. They have enough stored nourishment to explore only a few body lengths before choosing their first permanent attachments.

A large adult bears old damage. One limb may end halfway along its length, the stump divided into several thin new branches. A section of the body rim may be opaque, with a scar extending inward toward the feeding channels. Sometimes a foot contains a chip of basalt enclosed in tissue. The foot tore free before the rock did.

The animals repair themselves slowly. A new supporting rod begins as a cloudy line and may take years to acquire the density of those beside it. During that time, neighboring limbs carry a different share of the work.

There are stays whose bodies are no longer symmetrical enough to fit the diagrams in station manuals. They occupy particular saddles for decades, extending a shortened limb to the same notch, folding a damaged foot beneath themselves when they move.

Their departures are rare enough to be recorded.

A maintenance log may note that the large stay above the eastern intake has shifted to the next ridge. Three pages later are measurements of shaft temperature, a seal replacement, a lamp fault. Two years later someone notes that the animal has returned.

When a pump stops, the nearby stays respond before the crew finishes checking the alarms.

They flatten against their attachments. Free limbs settle onto the rock. Feet spread beyond their usual edges, and the sensory organs at their centers press down hard.

The altered vibration continues to arrive from the other stations. Returning sound still fills the basin, but one contribution has gone missing. The animals remain drawn tight around this absence.

If the pump restarts, they may hold that posture for days.

If it does not, they eventually begin to move. Some leave the building and descend along its buried pipes. Others climb to the summit above it. A few remain on the silent housing, feeding in cracks around the doors while the great sound of the working stations passes through the walls beneath them.

An adult traveling between ridges lets go completely.

First the feeding ribbon disappears into its fold. Then the farthest feet release, their membranes closing into small white cups. The stiff portions of the limbs are drawn alongside the body. The last foot holds until the animal has taken a little of the current against its underside.

It opens.

For a while, the stay hangs above the mountain, fully spread. Without the rock to show its scale, it looks slight: a translucent central body, eight pale lines, a few darker points.

Then it gathers the limbs into a long bundle and begins to cross the valley.

There is very little movement to see. The thick rim of the body makes a shallow undulation. The animal corrects its course by opening one foot, feeling the drag, and closing it again. It can take most of a day to reach the next ridge.

Beneath it, the mountain falls away into depths it will never visit. Above it is the ice, which it will never touch.

As it approaches the far wall, one limb extends ahead of the others. The foot unfolds before reaching the stone.

Its edge finds a place to seal. The little organ at its center presses against the basalt, and the limb becomes taut. For some time the rest of the animal remains suspended in clear water.

Then, foot by foot, it settles onto the mountain.
