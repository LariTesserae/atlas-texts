The first inhabitant you notice is standing where a side street enters an avenue.

You have passed that opening before. There was nothing in it then except a low mineral shelf and water moving over the paving. Now there is a narrow obstruction in the warmth. The current divides around it, though nothing you can see accounts for the division.

It is taller than you, but not by much. Its lower part is spread across the street. Above that, it becomes progressively narrower, ending somewhere near the height of an ordinary doorway. You cannot find its outline by looking directly at it. You find it by moving a little to one side and noticing which portions of the street fail to return.

After a while it crosses the avenue.

It does not lift anything resembling a foot. Its weight passes from one place to another through a series of small adjustments. Water gathers along its forward edge and thins behind it. For several moments after it has gone, the crossing remains warmer than the rest of the street.

There is another beside a wall farther on. You had taken it for the wall’s thickness.

These are not uncommon. They are simply difficult to distinguish from places where nothing is happening.

Most are broad below and loosely gathered above, but their proportions vary. Some scarcely rise out of the flood. Some reach the lintels of the larger halls. A few have an interval in their middle through which the warmth of the street passes almost unchanged. You can see an arch beyond them more readily than you can see their upper or lower portions.

Their bodies contain water, although not in the way a basin contains it. The water remains continuous with the street. It can flow through an inhabitant without carrying the inhabitant away.

What stays is a disposition of heat and pressure, held with considerable firmness. It has a front, though not always the front it had a moment ago. It has an interior. If you put your hand into the place where one stands, the water closes around your wrist with a slow, muscular reluctance.

There is no skin. There is nevertheless a boundary.

They avoid being entered.

One will draw itself sideways before you touch it, leaving a brief depression in the water. Another will remain still until your hand is very close, then contract into a shape so narrow that you lose it against the masonry. This contraction seems costly. For a long time afterward it stays near the wall, gathering warmth from the stone.

You learn to give them room.

The width of the avenues begins to make a different kind of sense.

An inhabitant travelling alone may occupy no more than a shallow crescent across the paving. Two travelling together require much more space. They leave an interval between them, and that interval must not be broken by a wall, a plant, or another moving body.

The interval changes as they proceed. Sometimes it is no wider than your shoulders. Sometimes it stretches across the avenue, with one inhabitant moving beside each bank of the central channel. They slow at junctions, draw closer, then open apart again.

Something is passing between them.

You cannot hear it. You can feel it if you happen to stand in the interval: a succession of small changes in temperature, each arriving at a different depth in your body. Warmth at the breastbone, then beneath the tongue, then along the backs of the knees. The order repeats, with variations.

They stop when you cross between them. One usually turns toward you. The other holds its position until you have moved away.

Their attention is unmistakable, though there is no feature upon which to fix it.

They do not attend to everything. Rain passes through them. A wall may shed a sheet of softened mineral a short distance away without attracting any response. But they notice a cup plant beginning to bend, and they notice the instant when a new room acquires its final corner.

At such times, several may arrive from streets that appeared empty.

They wait outside the threshold. The water is waiting there too.

When it is admitted, they follow it in.

For a newly formed room, the first occupant is usually small. It rests against one of the corners, extending a little way along both walls. During the next several visits you may find it in precisely that position.

Eventually the room begins to differ from the rooms around it. Its warmth has a recognizable order. Near the entrance it is mild; halfway across, it presses against your legs; beside the occupied corner, there is a narrow coolness that does not belong to the stone.

The inhabitant moves once this order is established. It may cross to another corner or venture into the courtyard. The room retains the difference in its absence.

This is how you begin to recognize where someone lives.

Not every resident has a room. Some remain beneath the open arches. Others favour a length of channel or a particular submerged flight of steps. One inhabits the suspended water in an upper storey. From the avenue below, you can occasionally discern it pressing outward against the exposed surface, making a rounded protrusion that slowly travels from one end of the opening to the other.

When rain comes, it descends by the outer wall.

Its return is much slower.

There are habits particular to individuals. Near the immense facade lives one that always crosses a channel three times before going through an arch. Another pauses beside the same isolated plant, even when the cup is empty. A low, wide inhabitant moves around the edge of a square and stops at each place where a building once stood.

You recognize them by these things, and afterward by smaller things: the depth at which the water divides, a lingering warmth on one side, a slight delay between the arrival of the body and the arrival of its weight.

They also recognize one another.

At a meeting, the first alteration is often so slight that you would miss it without practice. One inhabitant flattens a part of itself against the paving. The other opens a narrow space in its middle. Then both become still.

Sometimes they separate immediately. Sometimes they remain together for what feels like most of a day.

There are meetings after which one travels differently. It takes an unfamiliar turn. It pauses at a threshold it previously passed. Once you see a small inhabitant leave a larger one and proceed alone toward the outer flats. The larger one follows for part of the way, then stops. The smaller one continues until its passage can no longer be distinguished from the slow motion of the shallow water.

For a long interval, the larger one stays there.

You cannot tell whether the small ones are young.

Some occupy rooms whose walls are very old. Some of the tallest live in courtyards barely deep enough to shelter them. Size seems to have more to do with what an inhabitant can hold than how long it has existed.

The large ones are difficult to approach. The warmth around them contains successive layers, and crossing those layers makes you briefly uncertain of your position. A doorway seems closer than it was. Your hand feels immersed before it reaches the water. A sound from across the square arrives with the peculiar intimacy of something occurring inside your mouth.

They usually withdraw from the streets during rain.

The smallest remain outside. They collect in the shallow hollows left by individual drops. When those hollows close, they move to the next.

There are places the inhabitants will not enter.

A room dissolving from within may remain traversable for years, but once its corners begin to lose their distinction, its occupant leaves. It does not go far at first. You may find it just beyond the threshold, spread thinly across the courtyard.

Other inhabitants visit it there.

As the building subsides, the former occupant becomes harder to locate. Its warmth spreads. Its habits persist for a while without a body large enough to account for them. Water pauses where it used to pause. A narrow current turns aside at the entrance to a room that no longer exists.

The visitors continue to come, although less frequently.

In one courtyard, three inhabitants arrive separately and stand around a patch of paving. Nothing is present between them that you can distinguish. Nevertheless, they leave the middle clear.

At the largest square, the residents gather when the water lowers.

They do not fill the platform. They arrange themselves along the emerging steps, leaving intervals that extend down through the lower arcades as those are uncovered. More arrive than you would have thought the city could contain. Some come out of gutters. Some detach themselves from the undersides of causeways. Several rise through the paving, bringing no stone with them.

Their differences become clearer in the shallower water. There are bodies folded into themselves, bodies spread in long bands, bodies with several separated portions that move together. One has a hollow large enough for another inhabitant to stand inside, although none does.

For a time, all movement ceases.

Then a change passes from the lowest exposed step to the highest. Each inhabitant receives it, holds it briefly, and releases it toward the next. Along one side of the square the change moves quickly. Along another it waits so long that you think it has stopped.

No one occupies the platform.

But they keep making room beside it, adjusting their positions with care.

When the water returns, the gathering loosens. Residents leave in pairs or singly, entering the broad avenues before the steps disappear. A few remain until the square is fully flooded. The last is scarcely more than a firm place in the water beside a causeway.

Afterward, the streets are quiet again.

You pass the side street where you first saw one standing. It is there, or another very like it. This time, before it crosses, it draws its lower edge inward and leaves a clear strip of paving in front of you.

It waits until you have passed.

Then it continues across the avenue, taking back the space it needs.
