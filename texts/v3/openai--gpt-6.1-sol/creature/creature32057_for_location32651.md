The people have broad feet, or seem to. Their boots are made with several layers of woven sole, renewed one at a time, and a little extra width keeps a person from sinking into a damp inspection path. Indoors they are smaller than they looked in the street. They take off their coats and stand in their stockings beside the stove, rubbing their hands, ordinary narrow people with red ears and badly cut hair.

Most are not born with any special sensitivity to the ground. They learn it. A child learns that the dark patch outside the washhouse is slippery, that the pale ridge beside the tram stop will hold a hopping game, that a certain doorway gives splinters unless approached sideways. Only later does someone explain that the dark patch is sick, the ridge is a graft, and the doorway has been trying for forty years to make a branch.

There are children who put their cheeks against the floor to hear the pipes. There are also children who refuse to walk barefoot anywhere, even in summer. Both kinds generally grow out of it.

The city’s oldest families are not necessarily its wealthiest. Some have lived for generations in rooms that are slowly being squeezed shut. They lower their tables, cut the legs off their beds, and eventually move the bed into the kitchen. The ceiling descends too gradually to provide an occasion for leaving. One winter a grown son comes home and cannot stand upright where his mother is preparing tea. That becomes the occasion, though she resents him for it.

Other families live in the outer districts, in large, cold apartments whose windows have never quite finished forming. They hang blankets over the thin places. These are desirable homes because there is room to divide them, and because the plumbing can still be reached without going through someone else’s bedroom.

A room with reliable proportions costs more than a room with a view.

People save for such rooms. They belong to housing associations, put money aside in envelopes, argue over whether an aunt can be persuaded to surrender her lease. A person who has spent twenty years tending the same cylinder may dream of nothing more extravagant than a kitchen where the cupboards remain the same distance from the stove.

They eat thick porridge, sour preserves, steamed loaves, and a pale tuber cultivated in chambers beneath the warmer workshops. The tubers have to be lifted before they begin taking nourishment from the machinery. Left too long, they become fibrous and taste of lubricant.

Green vegetables come up from lower country in freight baskets. Fresh fruit is expensive enough to be carried home in a coat pocket. The smell of scorched apples from a passing tram can make a hungry person stop talking.

There are public eating rooms near the factories. Their cooks arrive before dawn and keep a pot of broth hot through all three shifts. A worker can buy a bowl on credit, but not spirits. The proprietors know which customers need an extra piece of bread and which will take offense if it is given openly. They leave it under the bowl.

At the tables, people complain about foremen, children, rent, the inconsistency of tram service. They discuss the condition of a wall much as people elsewhere discuss the condition of an elderly relative: with practical concern, old grievances, and a certain unwillingness to say plainly what comes next.

“It’s still taking water,” someone says.

That may settle the conversation for another month.

***

Much of the city’s knowledge belongs to people whose hands are occupied when they explain it.

A belt feeder can tell by the way the solution runs from a bucket whether it has been mixed properly. A window tender scrapes with the blade almost flat, leaving a wetness so thin that an apprentice mistakes it for a polished surface. The tender puts the apprentice’s finger on it and waits.

“Now?”

“Now.”

There is no secret afterward. There was hardly a secret before. But the apprentice has acquired something that a diagram could not give them.

These occupations leave marks. Window tenders have soft, pale patches on their wrists where the irrigating cords rest. Tram mechanics develop thick nails from testing wheel teeth with their thumbs. The workers who inspect deep conduits often lose the ability to smell anything faint; at home they put too much seasoning in the food.

Surveyors are recognizable by how seldom they hurry.

The instrument carrier may be young. The route keeper is usually someone who has already been lost and come back. That qualification is not written in the employment regulations, but supervisors observe it. Route keepers remember who had washing hung across a passage, where a dog once barked—there was a dog, years ago—and whether the third stair after a turn had a split down its middle.

They are encouraged to talk while working. Silence makes it too easy for each member of a pair to assume that the other remembers.

Some maintain the habit at home and drive their spouses to distraction.

“Coming through the sitting room,” a man says, carrying the tea tray.

“Yes,” his wife says. “I can see you.”

The city has doctors, though more of them are concerned with lungs and joints than with dramatic injuries. Cold gets into the work. People spend their days beside warm machinery and cross a freezing plaza to go home. Their coughs persist. Their knuckles enlarge. A doctor who tells a belt feeder to avoid damp places is considered an incompetent doctor.

There are also physicians for the buildings. The two professions share instruments and occasionally patients. A rash along a sleeper’s shoulders may be the first evidence of disease in the wall behind the bed. A house that has stopped closing its wounds can sometimes be helped by removing the family whose stove leaks into its drinking vessels.

No one finds this especially mystical. Usually it results in a dispute over who should pay.

***

The inhabitants are not quiet people, although visitors often think they are.

Outdoors, conversation has to compete with the factories or travel across too much space. People save it for the tram, the stairwell, the kitchen. Then they talk loudly and interrupt one another. A party in a low-ceilinged room can be heard through six adjoining apartments.

They dance. They play stringed instruments made from seasoned pieces the city has shed. There is a kind of little drum whose skin must be moistened during a performance, and the drummer keeps a cup beneath the chair. Popular songs mention departures, bad employers, and lovers who live inconveniently far away.

The long distances matter. A courtship can fail because the last tram leaves too early. Friends arrange to meet for months without managing it. People who live within sight of each other across a plaza may become strangers after one changes shifts.

When cloud fills the streets, visits become easier in one respect. Nobody can see how far there is to go. Lamps appear one at a time; the next doorway arrives before the walker has finished measuring the emptiness.

Some people like those evenings best.

There are gardens, but they are kept in containers with sealed bottoms. Growing something uncontrolled inside a house is irresponsible. A young couple may receive a shallow tray of flowering plants as a wedding gift, along with instructions that are longer than the marriage certificate.

The flowers are small and mostly unscented. People sit close to them.

They keep few pets. Sometimes a worker brings home one of the white insects from a power house and gives it a box warmed by a lamp. It hides in the joints, eats almost nothing, and may live for years. Children name it and then forget it for weeks. Their parents quietly tend the lamp.

A bird’s arrival causes a more general disturbance. Factory doors open. Someone who claims to recognize the species is sent for. The children want to feed it, but no one agrees on what it eats, and bread left on the roofs dries untouched.

After the bird has gone, its description changes in the telling. It acquires a brighter breast, a longer tail. For a while, people listen for it whenever work stops.

***

New residents arrive in the usual ways and in others.

There are migrants from the lowlands, who find the city bleak and the light painful. They bring soil in pots, boots with hard heels, ideas about building on firm ground. Their first winter is difficult. They wake at every movement of the house.

Some leave. Some discover that they like the broad spaces, the absence of mud, the way a tram announces itself long before it comes into sight. After a few years they stop asking where the plateau ends.

Others arrive with their apartments.

When a street acquires a block, a housing clerk goes there with a water inspector and a basket of bread. The bread is an old custom. It was once intended to prevent the residents from drawing too heavily on the local provisions before their needs had been counted. Now it is simply what one brings.

The new residents may be suspicious, grateful, irritated. One is invariably in the middle of something and asks the officials to come back. There are schoolbooks on tables, coats worn shiny at the cuffs, jars with labels from shops that no one elsewhere has heard of.

The clerk asks for names.

The water inspector checks the taps.

If the residents give a history that cannot be fitted into the city’s records, it is recorded separately. Officials learned long ago that questioning an old woman about the authenticity of her dead husband accomplishes nothing useful.

Occasionally someone recognizes a newcomer. A cousin who vanished on an inspection route. A former teacher. A person who resembles neither a missing person nor their younger self quite closely enough.

Such recognitions are handled privately when possible.

There have been marriages after them, and divorces. There have been people who went daily to look at a stranger and never spoke. The city cannot provide a rule adequate to all these circumstances. Its procedures concern water, wages, schooling, and the delivery of letters.

Children from added streets are admitted to school at once. Their handwriting is sometimes old-fashioned. They know songs with familiar tunes and unfamiliar verses. Usually they are caught up within a term, though they may continue for years to name a neighborhood that none of their classmates can find.

Their parents tell them not to discuss it.

Their teachers tell them they may discuss it, but not during arithmetic.

***

What else lives there is harder to describe.

Maintenance crews sometimes encounter movement in sealed chambers. It does not appear at the edge of a lamp beam like an animal. It is closer to the movement of someone turning over in bed, conveyed through several walls.

They put down their tools until it stops.

There are warm cavities too far from any engine, and passages where moisture gathers only at the height of a person’s mouth. A worker may find a smooth depression in a wall that fits a shoulder exactly. Resting there feels so comfortable that experienced crew leaders forbid it.

No one is certain whether these things indicate occupants, old arrangements for occupants, or the living material attending to itself.

Names are used nevertheless. A crew will refer to the tenant below a pumping station, or the sleeper behind a stair. The words make instructions easier.

“Don’t hammer there. You’ll wake the sleeper.”

An apprentice may laugh. The crew leader does not object to the laughter, only to the hammering.

Some cavities contain possessions: a bowl, a length of cloth, a chair pressed almost completely into the wall. Others contain pale fibrous forms that look unfinished, as though a branch began to grow around an absent object.

They are left alone unless they obstruct essential work.

The city employs a small number of listeners. Their title sounds ceremonial, but the work is badly paid and frequently tedious. They sit beside idle machinery, compare knocks with maintenance records, and try to determine whether an unattended sound requires a repair crew.

Most does not.

A press may repeat the rhythm of a shift that ended fifty years ago. A drain may continue making the sound of water after it has been dry for a season. In one factory the foundations give a short creak every afternoon at the hour when the former superintendent used to stand up from his desk.

Listeners disagree about what this means. Some believe pressure travels through tissues in ways not yet properly understood. Some believe repeated work leaves structures that can carry on without the work. A few have more troubling ideas, which they generally keep out of the reports.

The listeners themselves have ordinary households. They mend clothes while waiting. They fall asleep. One brings examination papers to mark. Another has to leave at seven, regardless of what is sounding, because there is no one else to collect her daughter.

Whatever occupies the deeper places has lived beside these obligations for a long time.

***

The watchman who hears the press before morning is named Iven.

He has held the post for eleven years. Before that he worked on the cutting floor, until a falling frame damaged his knee. The long walk across the yard is good for it, according to his doctor, though the doctor has never had to make the walk in cloud.

Iven carries his supper in a tin. He eats half at midnight and saves half for the hour before dawn. His wife knows this and packs two separate parcels; he still opens both to see whether they are the same.

He does not approach the knocking with unusual courage. He checks that the flywheel is still. He checks the belt. He lays two fingers against the wall and feels a faint, regular tremor.

Once, years earlier, he called a mechanic out for it. The mechanic listened, inspected everything, and sat beside him until the knocking ended. They were both cold. Nothing needed repairing.

Now Iven waits alone.

He has learned that it is worth remaining until the last stroke. Twice the press has paused long enough for him to start away, then resumed. He dislikes turning his back and having to turn around again.

When the sound finishes, he enters the hour in his book. In the next column he records the weather. Then he returns to the watch room, puts water on the stove, and unwraps the rest of his supper.

Above him, in apartments grown around warm shafts, people are beginning to wake. Someone opens a cupboard that has stuck overnight. Someone feels for a child beneath an excess of blankets. A tram driver steps out and tests the ground with the heel of a boot.

Far below the yard, the pressure passes onward.

Iven eats slowly. He has a little time before the day watch arrives, and the tea is too hot to drink.
