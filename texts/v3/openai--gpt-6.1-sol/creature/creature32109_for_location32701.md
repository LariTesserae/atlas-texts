At first, there is only a delay.

A thread of mineral grains holds its alignment slightly longer than the others. Around it, the small connections form and vanish, carrying weight onward. This one remains after its work should be finished. A current passes. The thread bends, releases one end, and attaches elsewhere.

Nothing visible has moved very far. But the delay has moved with it.

Over the next several hours, it travels a few meters through the clear water. It does not follow the current. Sometimes it advances against it; sometimes it stays where it is while all the grains that first held it are carried away.

This is the smallest state in which the inhabitant can survive.

It has no permanent material. Water passes through it. Minerals enter its arrangement and leave again, without becoming possessions. If you could mark every particle involved at one moment and return a day later, almost none of the marked particles would still be there.

Yet something would remain in the same part of the ocean, attending to the same ridge.

***

It usually lives beside unfinished stone.

There is more to work with there: connections that have not settled into their final angles, small uncertainties in the passage of weight. Around an old massif, the field has become regular. A disturbance can travel a great distance without finding an opportunity to turn. Beside a growing shelf, a minute alteration can persist, branch, return.

The inhabitant extends into these irregularities.

Its extent is difficult to measure. Most of it may occupy a volume smaller than a room, while a narrow part runs along a mineral seam for several kilometers. It can withdraw from a large space without moving toward a smaller one, simply by ceasing to repeat itself there. Conversely, it can occupy the opposite side of a ridge before anything has visibly crossed between.

Under strong sunlight, parts of it become perceptible.

The straight threads gather into overlapping, shallow curves. For a few seconds, a patch of water resembles the grain of pale wood. Then the light changes, or the particles exchange places, and the resemblance disappears.

There is no outline to follow. A person swimming through it would notice a small resistance, unevenly distributed: a pressure against one shoulder, a loosening around the ankles. Turning to find the source would change it. Holding still would give it time to change again.

It does not enclose the swimmer. Most often, it withdraws.

The water needs several minutes to recover its former balance.

***

Its senses are inseparable from the work of holding itself together.

A distant shelf contracts. The report passes through the inhabitant and alters the intervals between its connections. It restores them, but not exactly. Some part of the sound remains in the restored arrangement.

The next report arrives into that difference.

In this way, it can distinguish a ridge that has begun to cool from one that is coming apart. It can recognize the long, uneven settling of a mountain still forming beyond visibility. Currents bring changes in temperature, pressure, and dissolved minerals; light alters how readily certain grains bind. None of these arrives as a separate sensation. Each changes what the inhabitant must do to continue.

A nearby event is not necessarily the most important one.

A loud fracture may cross it without leaving much behind. A much fainter vibration, arriving from a familiar shelf, may cause it to draw in its distant extensions and remain almost motionless for days.

There are places it returns to.

One is a hollow ridge with three nested surfaces inside it. Another is the sheltered underside of a shelf where cold water passes at regular intervals. The formations need not offer warmth or nourishment. Sometimes the conditions there are worse than in the surrounding sea.

Still, when the currents permit, the same particular arrangement gathers beside them again.

***

It has a use for the vegetation, although it does not eat it.

The dark ribbons slow the water immediately above new stone. Under their folded leaves, dissolved minerals accumulate in slightly different proportions. Tiny bubbles interrupt the field. These are troublesome places for an ordinary connection to persist, but useful ones for a connection capable of adjusting.

A young inhabitant often begins there.

For many days, its entire existence may be confined beneath one leaf. The plant opens and closes. Warmth seeps from the stone. Each repetition requires a slightly different response.

Most such arrangements last only until the next cold stream arrives.

A few persist through the closing of the leaf, the loss of its bubbles, the gradual hardening of the surface beneath it. Eventually, they maintain themselves beyond the shelter in which they began.

The ribbon may later detach. What has grown beneath it remains.

There is no moment when the plant could be said to have given birth, and no visible young to find. A patch of vegetation can look exactly as it did the previous day while something newly separate occupies the water above it.

***

Older inhabitants become less dependent on a single site.

They learn the limits of nearby channels through repeated failure. An extension enters colder water and breaks down. It enters again, more narrowly. The third time, a different sequence of connections holds.

After many such attempts, the inhabitant can cross.

Its travel is slow in still water and sometimes extraordinarily fast during a disturbance. A storm may carry viable fragments hundreds of kilometers apart. Most lose the continuity that made them part of anything. Others keep adjusting, drawing on the strained field around them until they can persist independently.

For a while, these separated inhabitants respond alike.

They pause at the same intervals. They extend preferentially toward the same kinds of stone. A sound that resembles the settling of their former ridge causes each to contract, though they can no longer receive any signal from one another.

The resemblance diminishes over time.

One finds a warm shelf. Another spends years in a cold channel without vegetation. Another attaches itself to a forming mountain and grows through its outer layers. What they retain ceases to match.

If they meet again, they do not merge readily.

Their connections interfere. Each adjusts to the other’s adjustments. A fine trembling can spread through a volume of water that was quiet a moment before.

Sometimes one withdraws. Sometimes both remain, each occupying the intervals the other leaves unused. Along the boundary, their mineral threads become dense enough to catch the light.

That boundary can last longer than either of the shapes that first established it.

***

An inhabitant can affect the growth of a mountain, but only gradually.

It cannot raise a ridge at will. It can keep a few grains in place longer than they would otherwise remain. It can divert a small fraction of a load into a neighboring part of the field. Repeated over years, these changes leave a shallow passage through stone, or preserve a chamber that would have filled.

Inside some hollow formations, the successive surfaces are unevenly spaced. A broad interval narrows to a slit, opens again, then continues far into the ridge.

The inhabitant may still occupy it.

There, its movements are restricted but reliable. Each surface supplies a place where the pattern can be renewed. Passing currents reach different chambers at different temperatures. A sound enters through several thicknesses of rock and arrives in a sequence the inhabitant has encountered countless times.

It spends less effort correcting itself.

In long calm periods, it extends through the ridge and out into the water. Its older arrangements persist in the chambers while newer ones explore the growing edges. Some are abandoned within hours. Others return, slightly altered, and are repeated.

The stone accumulates around these repetitions.

Centuries later, a broken face may expose a series of pale, curved seams. They are not pictures of the inhabitant. They record places where it repeatedly did something too small to see.

***

Storms are dangerous because they supply more possibilities than it can accommodate.

The supporting field changes across an immense area. Familiar routes fail. Connections that have always borne a particular load begin bearing another. The inhabitant responds, and its response arrives into water that has already changed again.

A small one usually vanishes.

A larger one may survive by giving up most of its extent. Along a ridge, the visible grain in the water contracts toward a sheltered seam. The outer parts cease. Their grains resume ordinary alignments.

If the ridge itself is uprooted, the remaining pattern travels with it.

For hours, it occupies the chambers of a mountain rotating slowly through open water. As the stone softens, each chamber loses the conditions that made it dependable. The inhabitant retreats through nested surfaces, maintaining itself in less and less space.

Sometimes the last crest dissolves before it finds another support.

Nothing falls out of the water afterward. There is no residue distinguishable from the materials already there. The connections continue forming and breaking, but the particular sequence that corrected them is gone.

***

After a storm, a surviving inhabitant may remain contracted for a long time.

The water clears. Sunlight reaches the old ledges again. New stone begins gathering along a broken edge.

Eventually, a few connections hold beyond the sheltered seam. They extend a short distance into the open, fail, and are tried again.

There is very little to see.

A mineral flake turns beside the ridge. Far below, clouds move beneath the hanging sea. The new edge acquires another thin increment of stone.

For a moment, the water at its tip shows a faint curved grain.

It disappears, then returns slightly farther out.
