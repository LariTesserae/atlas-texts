The people who live here are good at noticing whether something has moved.

Not movement itself. A sleeve shaking in the wind, a bus coming around a corner—those scarcely need noticing. They notice that a washer left beside a drain yesterday is now a finger’s width nearer the wall. They notice a dark seam appearing below a window, or a bolt whose exposed threads have acquired one more white ridge. A child can look at a doorstep and tell you which part dries first. An elderly woman, asked how long she has lived in her house, may point to a mineral bulge beneath the kitchen sill.

“When we came, that was flat.”

They are not all engineers. There are shopkeepers, schoolchildren, clerks, people who clean hotel rooms, people who have never gone below the first level of the service tunnels. But everyone lives inside a set of conditions that must be read. Water is a household fact before it is an industrial one.

Someone who knows very little about the pumps may still know that a certain stain means a bowl should be moved, while another means the cupboard must be emptied.

Their hands are often occupied. They carry folded cloths, little brushes, pencils protected in waxed sleeves. Rings are less common than bracelets: fingers have to fit into narrow places, and mineral grit catches under a band. Many people have a smooth patch on one thumbnail from scraping deposits off things without fetching a tool. The backs of their hands may be dry and split while their cuffs are damp.

They hang coats with the sleeves turned outward. They never put a book directly against an outside wall.

In the street, they appear reserved until you understand where the conversation is happening.

A man waiting for the bus touches two fingers to his collarbone, then turns his palm toward a woman across the shelter. She replies without looking up from fastening her bag. A girl leaning from a balcony makes a long, impatient movement with one hand, and someone below answers with an expression sufficiently elaborate to make her laugh.

There are several ways of speaking with the hands. The quick gestures used around moving machinery are only the bluntest part of them. At home and in school, people learn a fuller language, with room for uncertainty, jokes, recollections, lies. Different districts have slightly different habits. Along the ridge, questions are held higher, nearer the face. In the southern wards, where glare comes off the wet paving, people turn a little sideways to keep their hands visible against their clothes.

Visitors often mistake a conversation for a series of instructions. They see the direction being indicated and miss the accusation.

Children learn early that attention has to be obtained before anything can be said. They wave, stamp where vibration will carry, or touch a wrist. Shouting after a departing person is useless. This gives even angry encounters a peculiar opening: first, someone must consent to turn around.

Once they do, the anger can be perfectly ordinary.

There are people here who speak beautifully aloud. The city has not made voices unnecessary. It has made places for them.

The padded room at the center of a house is usually too small for all the furniture its occupants want to put in it. Cushions are piled beneath the bench. A table folds into the wall. Someone’s sewing basket occupies the best foot space. Children complain about being made to come inside, then stay long after the matter that summoned them has been settled.

Voices sound intimate there even when the subject is tedious. A tax notice. An aunt’s disappointing visit. Whether the bakery has made its loaves smaller.

Some families sing in these rooms. Others dislike singing so close to one another. In one house there is a grandfather who tells stories with his eyes shut because, for once, nobody needs to watch him.

People who leave the shelf sometimes find that they cannot sleep in a quiet town. They hear plumbing, insects, another tenant turning over. Every small sound arrives separately and demands an explanation. They go home and sleep beneath the continuous pressure of the works, which has room inside it for their own breathing.

Others leave precisely because they cannot bear that pressure anymore.

There is a woman in the tree district whose son sends her photographs of woods. She examines them carefully, admiring the untended ground almost more than the trees. He keeps asking her to come. She keeps finding reasons not to. She has friends here, a room she likes, a husband buried on the ridge. Also, the young man renting her upstairs rooms has not yet learned how to clear the ceiling gutter without cracking its rim.

She would not say this last reason aloud. It would sound too small.

***

Much of the work is done by people who know a very narrow portion of the city extremely well.

A worker may have charge of six valves and two overflow chambers. His route is long, his duties repetitive, and there are components beyond his jurisdiction whose purposes he could not confidently explain. But he knows the drag of each wheel through his shoulders. He knows which gauge glass clouds from inside and which only looks clouded because of the light. He knows that the eastern chamber feels warmer under the left palm in late winter.

He has a replacement coming on Monday.

The replacement has excellent training and will write down everything he says. This reassures him less than he had hoped.

In the workshops, people make objects intended to disappear into other objects: seals, sleeves, collars, bearing surfaces. Their care is not especially visible in the finished city. A well-made part may spend twenty years behind a bolted cover. A badly made one may announce itself only as a damp patch in somebody’s bedroom.

Workers sometimes visit such bedrooms. They remove their boots, sit awkwardly on the edge of a chair, and ask when the stain first appeared. A resident fetches a notebook. Between entries about shopping and birthdays are little drawings of wet walls.

There are arguments over whose observations count. There are supervisors who trust a table more than the person standing beside the table. There are householders who have become attached to an explanation long after the water has disproved it. Maintenance does not make people humble by itself.

Still, most have had the experience of doing something carefully, seeing no result, and returning the next day to do it carefully again.

The tree keepers have this experience more often than anyone.

They carry rainwater through the streets in covered carts. They loosen soil with tools that look almost indecently delicate beside the equipment of the works. They examine leaves individually. A damaged tree can become a neighborhood concern without becoming a public spectacle; people simply alter their routes to pass it.

Children volunteer to collect fallen leaves, then quarrel over who gets the intact ones.

A few residents keep plants illegally in improvised beds fed from household drains. Most of these die. Now and then one survives, and its owner becomes evasive about what they have been doing differently.

***

Other inhabitants live where people look only when something needs attention.

Behind warm inspection plates are pale insects with flattened bodies and long, searching legs. When the plate is removed, they pause before retreating. Their blindness is not absolute; they seem to perceive the new exposure, though perhaps it is the movement of air that troubles them. They tuck themselves into the edges of the gasket, where a hurried worker can crush them while fastening the cover.

Children who accompany a parent on a short inspection sometimes insist on waiting until every insect has withdrawn. Adults tolerate this more readily when the shift is nearly over.

The birds at the settling pools are not residents in quite the same sense. They appear from somewhere beyond the shelf, eat what they can, and leave. Their feet make delicate dry impressions on the sediment for a few minutes before moisture fills them. People recognize individuals by a damaged feather or a particular way of running. A bird that has visited for three afternoons can acquire a name, although nobody agrees on it.

The small translucent animals in the tunnels are harder to distinguish.

At first they resemble scraps of softened seal material. Then one extends its front edge and draws the rest of itself forward. Seen against a glove, its body contains a fine branching darkness, like a crack viewed through cloudy glass. The branches contract slowly. It has no obvious eyes, and what might be a mouth is usually pressed against the wall.

They graze where the mineral film is thin and newly formed. Old, hard crust does not interest them.

They follow the water wherever it goes.

On a passage floor they move across the slope. On a wall they move upward. Beneath an arch, they collect along the narrow track by which droplets reach the upper drain. Whether they are adhering to the stone or being held there by the same conditions that hold the water is a question on which the workers have strong opinions and the surveyors have cautious reports.

In some passages they are solitary. Elsewhere there are dozens, each no larger than a fingernail, arranged along a wet seam with small spaces between them.

One tunnel worker has been marking their positions on a plan for eleven years. Her colleagues call it her census. She says it is not a census because she cannot tell whether she is seeing the same animals.

She has learned that they disappear before certain seams change direction.

Not always. Not enough to put in a maintenance schedule. But often enough that when she finds a familiar patch empty, she returns with a lamp and looks at the ceiling.

She is due to retire soon. The plans will remain in the cabinet. Whether anyone continues them is uncertain.

***

Children are the residents least impressed by the city’s strangeness.

They float slivers of wood in shallow channels and run beside them. They make games out of getting across a yard without stepping on a dry stone. They know which rails will leave a wet mark on the palm and which walls conceal a hollow where two people can sit facing each other, knees interlocked, and hear a whisper.

Their forbidden places are not necessarily the deepest or darkest. A bright settling yard can be more dangerous than a tunnel. A dry crust may cover water; a harmless-looking outlet may begin discharging without any visible preparation.

So they are taught routes, not merely prohibitions. Go by this stair. Keep the red bracket on your left. If the paving is warm beyond this line, turn back.

As they grow, they discover how much of an adult’s apparent confidence is memory.

Some will inherit rooms, jobs, duties, grudges. Some will take the ridge bus with luggage and feel, beyond the last buildings, that the roads still belong to the city. Some will return years later and find the immense yards smaller than they remembered, though no smaller on any plan.

At evening there are people at the southern parapet who have no business there except to be outside before supper. They lean on the warm stone. They watch the outlets and the valley. If someone beside them points, they follow the hand.

Far behind them, a gate is being adjusted. A dog wakes in a padded room. In an upper kitchen, a woman sets a bowl on top of a cupboard and checks that it sits level. Under the shelf, a translucent animal encounters a newly wetted stretch of ceiling and begins to feed.

The sound changes.

Several people at the overlook lift their heads. One looks back toward the pump houses. Another waits a little longer, feeling the parapet through both palms.

Then she taps her companion’s wrist. It is time to go home.
