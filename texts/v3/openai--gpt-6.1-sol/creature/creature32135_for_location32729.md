The people who live here carry very little that cannot be folded.

This is less noticeable than it sounds. A woman on her way to work has a purse, a food tin, a pair of gloves tucked into her belt. Inside the purse is a strip of waxed cloth with six addresses written on it. Two belong to her employer. Three belong to her mother. The last is a place where messages can be left if none of the others can be reached. She checks the cloth while waiting for a tram, then puts it away without concern.

Most residents have such a list. They keep it near the body, where the ink stays warm.

Their clothes are made for narrow passages and frequent waiting. Coat sleeves close firmly at the wrist. Buttons are recessed or covered; an exposed fastening can catch on a branch, a cable, the lip of a moving door. The soles of their boots are flexible, though the toes are reinforced. Walking on fresh ground requires a slight spreading of the foot, a reluctance to commit all one’s weight at once. Visitors find this tiring. Residents do it even on floors that have been stable for years.

People who work below street level can often be recognized by the back of one hand. They use it to test surfaces, and the skin there is thickened, pale, almost glossy. A palm gives too much warmth to the thing being examined. It can confuse the impression.

There are many distinctions among inspectors. A rail inspector does not necessarily know anything about a wall. A wall inspector may decline to offer an opinion on a curb. The best foundation listeners are rarely the people with the best hearing. They are the ones who can remain kneeling long enough to separate an old noise from a present one, and who will admit when they cannot.

The surveyor beside the new curb is named Mara.

She has three brass pins in her mouth and one between her fingers. Her father kept pins this way, and she has inherited the habit along with his instruments, though not his belief that the western districts would settle in his lifetime. When she was a child, he showed her a square of pavement that had stayed level for nine consecutive years. There was a bakery on it. People came from other districts to stand outside the bakery and say that they could feel the difference.

The pavement eventually tilted. The bakery remained open.

Mara remembers her father being unusually angry about the tilt, as though someone had broken an agreement. She does not expect the ground to keep agreements. She records what it does.

At home she is considerably less patient. She dislikes cups left near the edge of the table and will ask twice for them to be moved, the second time sharply. Her daughter says she worries more about the cups than about the building. Mara says the building has fittings.

Their apartment consists of three joined sections. On cold evenings, a little air comes through the black seals between them. They stuff these with wool offcuts, taking care not to pack them so tightly that the joints cannot work. The kitchen window opens onto the branches of a tree growing from a courtyard four floors below. Once a year, someone climbs up and cuts away the growth around the hinge.

Mara keeps a chipped blue plate that belonged to her mother. There are safer plates available: thin metal, resilient glaze, pressed fiber. She keeps this one in a padded drawer and takes it out for ordinary meals.

Many houses contain something like it.

***

Children learn the routes through their neighborhood as a set of choices.

They know which shop has a back door, which stairway continues onto a roof, which caretaker will let them pass through a boiler room. They are taught never to enter a newly opened passage merely because an adult has gone in. The adult may be a specialist. More importantly, the adult may be wrong.

At school, the younger children practice leaving a classroom by each available exit. Sometimes the exercise takes them around a corridor and back to the same room. Sometimes lessons resume elsewhere. There is usually a little excitement and then disappointment when the arithmetic continues.

They like the conveyor belt. They give names to certain mineral blocks and watch for them on later days, although the blocks almost never return. Occasionally a child claims to recognize one by a vein or a chipped corner. The teacher does not encourage this, but she does not forbid it either.

In the playground, games begin with negotiations over what will count as fixed.

A railing may be fixed. A tree may be fixed for the length of the game. A painted line is fixed unless someone sees it move. Children who insist on too many guarantees are left out until they learn to make do.

They are not without fear. Some dislike the brief dripping at dawn. Others are afraid of the rooms revealed between building sections: familiar rooms seen from a direction that should not exist, with a bed or a chair exposed in the slit. One boy will not go near a speaking wall because it once produced his own cough.

He had coughed there several days earlier. Everyone explained this to him.

It did not help.

***

The city has residents whose chief occupation is making other people’s lives easier to move.

They dismantle cupboards, transport aquariums, shift ovens while the ovens are still warm. They know how to lift a sleeping person without waking them and when it is better to wake them. Their carts have many small wheels, each turning independently. They carry spare handles because door handles are often the first things lost during a hurried removal.

There are cooks who follow repair crews from district to district. They set up beside whatever has failed and remain until the work has become ordinary again. A good cook learns how long a particular crew will tolerate thin soup, which workers cannot eat with gloves on, and who will quietly pay for someone else.

There are also people who refuse to move.

They live in apartments that have acquired alarming angles, shops with entrances below the pavement, houses whose chimneys have been detached for years. Some stay from poverty. Some have spent so much effort making one place habitable that they cannot bear to begin again. Others are waiting for someone who knows only the old address.

Neighbors leave them food and advise them badly. Inspectors visit. Notices accumulate.

Occasionally such a house settles into an excellent position, gaining light, drainage, and a patch of open ground. Its occupant is congratulated on a stubbornness that, elsewhere in the same week, has killed someone.

The dead are buried in ground that has already endured several winters. Even there, the markers are attached with adjustable bolts. Families keep copies of the names and dates at home. In some districts, tending a grave includes checking its position against two nearby trees.

The trees are not permanent either. Two are better than one.

***

Of the animals, the long-bodied rodents live most intimately with people.

They know the heat schedule of a building better than its tenants do. Before an office loses power, rodents may leave the cable insulation and appear along the skirting boards, carrying young. This is useful information, but it does not make them welcome. They gnaw seals, steal fruit, and pack ventilation ducts with seed husks.

Their nests are lined with things that hold a crease: paper labels, coat linings, the corners of old maps. When exposed, a nest may contain a small, densely folded history of the surrounding streets.

The foxes take advantage of human patience. They have learned that workers standing beside a stopped conveyor will usually remain there for some time. A fox can pass within a few feet of their boots, searching for dropped food. It is more cautious around anyone walking briskly.

The deer are less accustomed to people. They enter occupied buildings only when the routes through them have become easier than the routes outside. Their flattened antlers bear small scratches from masonry. Moss sometimes grows in the deepest scratches, close to the skull.

No one is certain what a deer hears in a lift shaft. Maintenance crews note their presence, but do not evacuate a building merely because one has gone away. Deer leave for many reasons. A falling apple can draw one out of a place it has occupied all morning.

The birds at the walls are more dependable. They arrive before a stored sound emerges and press close to the surface, their feet spread against it. When the sound is strong, they flutter from point to point, following it through the material.

Some residents feed them. Others object that this makes it impossible to know why they have gathered.

***

The beetles are found wherever the ground is newest.

A broad-backed beetle carries a piece of leaf much larger than itself down the clean edge of a fissure. It returns without the leaf, waits, and descends again. Several may work the same opening without touching one another.

Under magnification, their backs are furrowed. The furrows contain pale grains. These were once thought to be eggs, until eggs were found elsewhere, laid in the dry spaces beneath rail fastenings. The grains on the beetles’ backs are mineral, though softer than most mineral matter ought to be. They harden after the beetle dies.

A dead beetle left on fresh ground is difficult to remove by the following day. After a week, usually only the legs remain visible.

Children are told not to keep them in jars. The warning is mostly for the beetles’ sake. They do poorly away from unfinished surfaces.

There are organisms smaller than these, living where roots meet the pale material. Samples brought into laboratories change quickly. A smear that appears fibrous under the microscope may become granular while the observer adjusts the light. Researchers use cooled slides and work in coats.

They have established that roots grow more readily through ground visited by beetles. They have established that certain shrubs precede a rise in the surface. Beyond this, findings are difficult to repeat.

The gardeners still want the reports.

***

The city’s inhabitants are familiar with events for which they have no satisfactory explanation.

A worker hears a lunch spoon striking a bowl inside a wall. She recognizes the rhythm as her brother’s; he has a habit of tapping the rim before eating. His factory is eleven kilometers away. She sends him a message asking whether he is well. He replies that he is, and asks her to stop doing this.

A night watchman finds warm footprints across a cold floor. They are his own, made on the previous round, though by then his boots should have cooled.

In an empty upper story, a tree puts out a branch along the route formerly taken by a corridor. The gardener cuts it back. It grows there again.

Such things are discussed at meals, entered in notebooks, used to support arguments. Not every event is considered important. People have preferences about the inexplicable, just as they do about weather.

Mara’s father believed the ground learned from what stood on it. He pointed to the speed with which a second workshop could be erected where a first had been lost. He said the material acquired habits.

Mara has seen enough failed replacements to doubt him.

Yet she sometimes finds a pin upright in ground that has turned over completely. The surrounding measurements are gone, the curb displaced, but the pin remains with its head just above the surface, ready to be used.

She records this without assigning a cause.

At sunset she returns to the curb. The pin she placed there is still straight. A beetle has reached it and is resting against the brass. Above her, windows are being shut, and somewhere beyond the warehouses a shift of workers is coming out into the cold.

Mara waits until the beetle moves away before taking her measurement.
