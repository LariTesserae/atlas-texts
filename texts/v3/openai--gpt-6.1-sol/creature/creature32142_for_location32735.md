What lives here has no place where it can be seen all at once.

There are parts of it beneath the station, where the old intake divides into two pipes, and parts under the central mound. A thinner portion occupies the gravel below the eastern channel. These are joined, though not by anything you would recognize as a neck or a limb. In some places the connection is a cord of soft tissue laid along the bottom of a culvert. In others it is only a lining on the stone, scarcely thicker than the green film on a brick. It can draw this lining together, make a cord of it, and withdraw through an opening that seemed too small to admit anything alive.

The water moving between these portions is not its blood. It needs water as an animal with joints needs room to bend.

Most of its flesh is a dull cream color. Where it touches iron, it darkens; where sediment continually rubs against it, it develops a gray, leathery surface. There are folds that look like the undersides of freshwater mussels, and broad, nearly transparent areas through which a slow contraction can be watched passing. None of these surfaces ought to be exposed to daylight. When a piece of mud turns over and shows something pale underneath, the paleness sometimes withdraws before the mud settles.

It has no shell of its own. It uses what is here.

A section of pipe protects one of its thicker parts. The space under the submerged ladder shelters a fringe of delicate tissue. Beneath the station, where a fallen roof has made a low chamber, it rests against the underside of the rubble and holds several stones slightly apart. Those stones have been apart so long that mineral deposits have formed in the gaps. If it were to relax completely, a substantial part of the building would settle by less than an inch.

That is enough weight to keep it occupied.

It did not arrive at its present shape deliberately. As it grew, the basin gave it passages, stopped passages, pressed it flat, divided it around obstructions. There is an old brick embedded in one portion that cannot now be removed without tearing it. There are roots passing through another. Its oldest tissues have the texture of soaked packing leather and almost no sensitivity. New growth appears in the sheltered seams, soft and finely ridged, tasting the water before venturing farther.

It is not spread evenly through the place. Some of the most promising cavities are empty. It has tried them and withdrawn. Something in the sediment was wrong, or the pressure changed too abruptly, or the river sent cold water through them for too long. Elsewhere it persists in a crack that appears to offer nothing: a few inches of darkness, a trickle, one surface against which it can hold itself.

Its feeding is difficult to distinguish from drainage.

Along certain narrow passages it has formed lips. They are small, numerous, and usually shut. When water is drawn through the masonry, they open against the current. Fine matter catches on their inner folds: bacteria, fragments of algae, the softened remains of leaves, organisms too small to notice. These are moved inward with an unhurried rippling.

Larger food is uncommon. A crustacean can live beside one of the lips for weeks. The fringe beneath the ladder bends away when touched, and the little pale animals flick in and out of it as if it were vegetation. Then a passage opens elsewhere, suction increases, and a few are gone.

It does not pursue the beetle. It has no useful way to pursue a beetle. But it can keep a margin wet, and the beetle’s retreat may bring it to a joint in the concrete where the water suddenly rises.

Its senses are better suited to pressure than to surfaces. Through the soles of your shoes, you feel two pulses that do not quite coincide. Through its tissues, it feels those pulses everywhere, changed by distance, masonry, water, and the condition of the pumps. It can distinguish a blocked pipe from an empty chamber by what comes back through its body after a contraction. It can feel a stone loosen before the stone moves.

Footsteps reach it as brief, enormous events.

One foot on the pale slab compresses a pocket beneath it. Water is displaced; a fold of tissue is pinched; several remote passages tighten. The animal withdraws from that pressure, and the slab rocks. On the dark ledge, your weight is carried through concrete into the gravel below. It feels you there too, but nothing needs to move.

If you stand still long enough, the disturbance ceases to be alarming. It leaves the tissue nearest you contracted while the rest resumes its work. When you lift your foot, water enters the released space. That is sometimes the seam that begins to well beside your footprint.

It has learned the pumps.

At first their operation must have injured it. Even now, an unusual surge can strip a soft portion from the inside of a pipe. But the regular pulses have become part of the way it moves. It opens a passage just before pressure rises, closes another as pressure falls, lets the machinery draw water through a feeding fold or lift material off a passage it needs.

The pumps keep working because someone still checks them. The animal has no understanding of the amber lamp, the padlock, or the painted marks on the jamb. It knows the visits by a sequence of pressures: several heavy steps, a vibration through the doorframe, a period of changed flow. Once, after such a visit, a chamber that had been quiet for years began to flush again. It lost a great deal of tissue there. For months afterward, it kept away from the chamber.

The warm water comes from a deeper part.

Under the eastern side of the basin, beyond a bend no hand could reach, there is a compact mass of flesh protected from the strongest currents. Water passes through its folds slowly. The tissue there is richer in color, faintly pink beneath the cream, and crowded with organisms that live inside it. When that portion contracts, it expels a little water warmer than the water around it.

The warmth is slight. A hand notices it; a thermometer might miss the pulse if lowered too late. On cold mornings, it becomes visible for a few feet.

There are young in that sheltered mass, though most will never leave it.

They begin as tiny folded pieces, each holding a droplet in its center. For a while they remain attached. Then they loosen, enter the outgoing water, and catch wherever the current lets them. Nearly all fail. They are carried into the river, buried in silt, stranded when a pan drains. A few stay alive in cracks, no larger than the head of a match, closing whenever the water turns cold.

The adult does not tend them. Sometimes it draws them back in with everything else it feeds on. Sometimes one survives close enough that its growing edge meets the larger body. The tissues join, and a passage that was once separate comes under the same slow contractions.

So there may be one creature here, or there may be several whose boundaries have become difficult to follow.

In dry weather it gathers itself below the station. The basin then looks particularly deserted. Mud stiffens around the stones, the ladder becomes exposed, and the few films left in the upper channels are mistaken for stains. Below, it waits in a reduced volume of water, pressed fold against fold. Parts die. Other parts digest them. It can endure a considerable loss without needing to leave.

When the water returns, it does not immediately spread. First a narrow edge advances along a buried sill. Then tissue loosens beneath the mound. A channel begins carrying water that had previously soaked into gravel. Over several days, the basin resumes the arrangement in which you found it.

It seldom rests everywhere at once. Even during the quietest hour, one portion is yielding while another braces. A loosened stone has to be caught or allowed to fall. A feeding surface needs fresh water. Somewhere a fold has filled with grit and must turn itself inside out to clear it.

Seen from the road, none of this has a shape.

Below the embankment, it has the shape available to it: broad where a chamber permits breadth, narrow through a pipe, divided beneath a slab, gathered close in the dark. When you leave, it remains in contact with the place where you stood. The pressure of your foot is gone, but the water has not yet finished returning. A lip opens under the concrete. Fine sediment lifts, passes inward, and the seam clears.
