The animal in the hollow is not waiting for you to leave. It has been there since before you reached the ridge, and will remain after you have passed, unless the water stops gathering.

At first you would take it for a low bank of rubble. Its back is broad and uneven, powdered with the same dust that fills the cracks around it. Several loose-looking edges stand above its shoulders. One of them moves when it breathes.

It is about the length of a person lying down, but much lower to the ground. Most of its height is in the forequarters. Behind them the body narrows, ending in a blunt, muscular tail tucked along one flank. It has six limbs. The rear four are short and carry its weight; the foremost pair can reach well beyond its head. Those long forelimbs lie folded beneath it now, their joints hidden, their flattened ends resting against the stone.

Its head is small enough to be overlooked.

A dark wedge projects from under the front of the body. There are eyes, recessed beneath heavy brows, and two nostrils that open sideways. Around the mouth hangs a fringe of pale, stiff hairs. Some touch the rock. Others are held a finger’s width above it.

Every so often, one hair bends.

The animal feels the drop that has fallen into the crack before the drop makes a sound you can hear.

Then the head advances. The mouth fits against the opening, and a narrow tongue passes into it. There is a soft, sustained clicking, almost identical to the clicks coming from beneath the hollow. When the head withdraws, the fringe is wet.

It does not take all the water. Its tongue cannot reach the bottom.

***

The apparent rubble on its back is a coat of overlapping scales, thickest along the spine and shoulders. They are neither smooth armor nor separate plates of stone. Each grows from a living base, and each is worn down at its outer edge by the animal’s movements through narrow shelters. Mineral dust lodges in their ridges. Scale from the vents accumulates between them.

An old animal can carry several pounds of the country on its back.

Beneath this rough covering is dense, short fur, brown near the skin and almost colorless at the tips. The underside is softer still. When the animal rises, you may see a pale fold between its legs, clean except for a dark line where it has been lying against the ground.

The scales keep the wind from working through its coat. In a warm hollow they lift slightly, allowing air to move beneath them. On an exposed shelf they draw close. An animal crossing a ridge can seem to shrink without slowing down.

It walks on the rear four feet, using the long front pair chiefly to examine what lies ahead. Each forefoot has a broad central pad and three spreading fingers. These are not claws for breaking rock. Their tips are blunt, with fine ridges that grip a surface without scraping it.

Before committing its weight, it lays one hand down.

Sometimes it leaves the hand there for several breaths.

A person following would find little to explain the delay: an ordinary shelf, a dust-filled seam, perhaps a patch slightly warmer than the rest. But the animal is feeling the passage of vibration through the stone. A lifting plate gives a different tremor from a cooling one. Water dripping into a buried cavity produces a repeated impulse. Another animal moving in the next hollow sends a faint, irregular succession of shocks through the connected shelves.

It cannot read the whole plateau this way. Broken ground defeats it. Deep dust muffles the stone. At such places it must raise its head and use its nose, or go forward without knowing.

It goes more slowly then.

***

Its food is mostly small.

At a damp vent collar it presses its mouth against the mineral and draws the lower lip backward. The lip turns out, exposing a tough, finely ridged surface. With this it rubs away the films growing on the stone. Afterward, the collar bears a clean oval patch.

Under loose flakes it finds the flat grazers. One long forelimb holds the flake up while the other passes underneath, its fingers spread. The tiny animals slide toward the nearest crack. The hunter catches some against the soft pads of its hand, then brings the hand to its mouth.

Many escape. It rarely bothers to turn the same flake twice.

It also eats fallen berries, the fleshy bases of vent grass, and roots exposed by recent runoff. After rain it may spend hours in a channel, nosing through the small banks of seeds left where the water slowed. Its mouth is built for patient work close to the ground, and even in good feeding it makes few conspicuous movements.

A hollow that looks bare can occupy it for half a night.

The small furred animals avoid its hands but do not always abandon a water pocket when it arrives. They wait on nearby ledges while it drinks. Occasionally one comes down and licks a bead of water from the fringe beneath its chin.

The larger animal tolerates this until the little one touches its mouth. Then it turns its head, sharply enough to scatter them.

***

It does not live alone, although you will usually see only one at a time.

Five or six adults may use a chain of hollows stretching for several miles. They feed separately and sleep in different shelters. Where their routes meet, they leave faint greasy marks on the undersides of stones: the animal bends, presses a gland beside its jaw against the rock, and moves on.

The mark survives wind better than droppings would. Another member of the group finds it by lifting the edge and drawing air across the sheltered surface.

There are also places where they touch the ground to announce themselves.

One is a thick shelf near a junction of ravines. An animal arriving there lays both forefeet flat, raises its chest, and gives three slow blows with its breastbone. The impacts are scarcely audible in the air. Under a resting hand, they feel distinct.

An answer may come from somewhere farther down the ravine.

Then the first animal waits, or continues toward the water, or turns aside into a shelter. The same three blows do not always produce the same result. The intervals vary. So does the answering pattern. Young animals sometimes strike too quickly and receive no answer, then try again.

When a vent begins to fail, these meetings become more frequent.

The oldest animals return to the thinning moisture, testing the stone with their hands and licking the mineral mouths. They may visit a dry hollow repeatedly after the others have ceased to use it. Eventually they leave too. New marks appear beneath stones along another route, and a shelf that received evening blows for years lies undisturbed.

***

The young are born without scales.

For their first few weeks they remain in a chamber lined with dry grass and strips pulled from the margins of the broad ground mats. Their skin is dark; their fur is thin; their forelimbs seem disproportionately large. Even before their eyes open, they spread their fingers against the floor.

An adult sleeps curled across the chamber entrance. Usually it is the mother, but not always. Another member of the group will sometimes take her place while she feeds. The young crawl toward whichever body occupies the opening and lie beneath the warm fold between its legs.

Their first journeys are short. They follow the adult from shelter to water, keeping so close that their heads repeatedly strike its heels. At an exposed crossing, the adult stops and lowers itself. The young climb onto its back, fitting between the still-clean ridges of its scales.

A carrying animal moves with unusual care. It avoids lifting plates and pauses before stepping down even shallow ledges.

By the time the young can cross a ridge unaided, they have begun to acquire the plateau’s dust. Their new scales have pale margins. Seen together, they look like fragments freshly detached from a much older animal.

Predators take them whenever they can.

The adults have little speed, but a group with young can close into a low cluster, heads inward, broad shoulders facing out. The long hands hook into cracks. A predator attempting to turn one over must pull against both its weight and its grip.

After such an encounter, the adults may remain gathered for hours. The young move beneath them, emerging briefly at the edges and being drawn back by a forelimb.

***

At the cliffs, where warm air escapes into the cloud below, these animals sometimes gather without feeding.

There may be a dozen along a sheltered lip, their backs nearly touching, all facing into the slow upward movement of air. Condensation beads on their mouth hairs. They drink by drawing the wet fringe between their lips.

Below them, unseen flyers pass through the vapor.

An old animal may stay after the others depart. Its scale edges are worn almost smooth. One hand has healed crookedly and no longer lies flat. It spends longer testing each new surface, shifting its weight until the damaged fingers make contact.

If you approach, it raises its head.

For a moment its nostrils open toward you. The pale hairs stand away from its mouth. It is gathering what it can from your warmth, your smell, the unfamiliar spacing of your steps.

Then a drop forms on the underside of the cliff lip.

The animal lowers its head, reaches with its good hand, and draws itself a little farther into the dark.
