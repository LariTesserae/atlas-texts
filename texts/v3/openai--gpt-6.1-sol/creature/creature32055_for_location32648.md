There are forty-three inhabitants, according to the newest census. The older census gives forty-one, but includes a woman who has not yet arrived and counts the pumping-station attendant twice, once at his place of work and once at home.

He objects to having the second entry removed. At home, he says, he is quite different.

Most of the inhabitants could be mistaken for people in poor light, which is the only light the town has. They wear coats, carry parcels, stop to adjust their cuffs. Their faces have the ordinary arrangement of features. It is chiefly when they wait that something becomes noticeable.

A person waiting at a corner grows very still. Their weight settles evenly through both feet. The hands turn slightly outward. After a few minutes, the cuffs begin to lift away from the wrists, exposing pale bands of tissue that open into fine ridges.

This is embarrassing rather than alarming. A passerby will cough; the waiting person will stir and pull their sleeves down.

Children are taught not to stand too long in one place.

Their bodies vary considerably. Some have skin as opaque and dry as paper. In others, a greenish vessel can be seen passing beneath the jaw, or a slow movement of fluid across the temples. Their joints are firm. Their nails are narrow, blunt, and often stained with wax. They have no difficulty lifting tools, climbing ladders, or sitting in chairs, although a chair used by the same person for several years must occasionally be separated from them.

They are warm in patches.

A hand may be cold except for the last joint of one finger. A face may hold warmth on the left cheek and none on the right. Physicians take these temperatures carefully. Warmth that stays in one place is usually harmless. Warmth that travels needs watching.

They bleed little. A cut releases a clear, adhesive fluid, followed, if the wound is deep, by something bitter and cloudy. Work clothes have stiffened places at the elbows and knees. The pruners’ aprons are almost rigid.

Their internal anatomy is known mostly through accidents. There are chambers, bundles, thin partitions. There is no single organ that the physicians confidently call a heart. A pulse can be found in several places, but the places change with age and occupation.

The pump attendant has a strong pulse beneath each heel.

The map clerk has one in the soft tissue behind her front teeth.

She says it began when she was promoted.

***

They eat in the late cool hours, sitting close to their bowls.

Their staple food is shaved tissue from the municipal crop, steeped until it loosens into pale curls. There are cultivated nodules with a flavor like chestnut, strips of fermented rind, and little translucent eggs gathered from the undersides of approved stairs. Salt is valuable. Bitter things are common enough that sweetness is treated with suspicion.

Fresh water is served separately, in cups with lids.

A meal takes a long time. Between mouthfuls, people rest their palms against the tabletop. Some keep a hand on the side of their bowl, absorbing the warmth. It is rude to comment on how much a guest drinks. It is also rude to refill a cup without asking, since a person may have spent the entire day making room for that amount of water.

They cook for one another more often than their population would seem to require. Someone is always recovering from a cut, returning from a distant block, or simply too dry to manage a stove.

The woman who runs the only eating house keeps six tables in a room that currently has four corners. The fifth corner was removed last season after it began producing shelves.

She is broad through the shoulders and has a very small voice. When she is angry, the veins in her neck turn nearly black. She never raises that voice. Instead, she puts down whatever she is carrying and waits until everyone else has become quiet.

Her name is Sella. She knows which customers will eat an egg and which will inspect it for movement first. She keeps the best water for a pruner with damaged hands, who pretends not to notice.

***

There are seven pruners.

The youngest has been apprenticed for eleven cooling cycles and is still not permitted to cut a doorway unattended. She carries the water flask, the binding membrane, and a little padded case of metal probes.

Her name is En. She is impatient with small repairs and extraordinarily gentle with large injuries. Once, helping recover a man from an unfinished street, she held the loosened edge of his shoulder for six hours. During that time she spoke to him about a defective buckle she had bought, describing every attempt to make it close.

Afterward he said this had been helpful. It gave him something narrow to return to.

The senior pruner is difficult to see even under a lamp. His skin is dark and finely furrowed; his clothes are dark too, but smooth. He has lost the smallest finger of each hand. Neither finger has regrown, although both occasionally ache in wet weather.

He inspects a building by putting his ear to it.

What he hears is not simply growth. A stair forming a new step sounds different from a stair thickening the steps it already has. A wall repairing a crack makes a dry, intermittent noise. The beginnings of a mouth can be almost silent.

He spends a long time at the poorer houses.

There, a lintel may be allowed to keep its lips through the dry season. The residents feed it damaged crop tissue, scraps of rind, occasionally a dead grazer. In return, the doorway closes tightly against drafts and opens readily for familiar hands.

No one calls this a bargain in front of the council.

The senior pruner does not always cut. Sometimes he marks the doorframe and returns the next morning. Sometimes he sits on the doorstep and asks what the family has been eating.

He has a reputation for severity because he will not let children feed the doors.

***

Children begin in the nursery, but not all of them are born there.

Some arrive wrapped in layers of pale membrane. Some are found beside an established household’s bed, small and exhausted, with no visible place of attachment. Occasionally a child is discovered in a room that had been empty the previous evening.

Such children are not considered ownerless. The nursery asks who has space, who has water, and who can be relied upon to get up during the cooling hours.

The adults discuss these questions gravely. The child sleeps through most of it.

At first, children have very little depth. Seen from the side, a newborn may seem no thicker than a folded glove. They must be turned regularly and supported in soft, curved beds. Their rooms are kept dim, but not completely dark. Too much darkness encourages the wrong sort of growth.

As they develop, they acquire weight unevenly. A hand may become substantial before the arm. Their noses are often the last features to settle. They are hungry, irritable, and fiercely interested in thresholds.

The nursery keeps its doors blunt-edged.

By the time children can walk, they have learned several things the adults cannot remember learning. They know which shadows contain birds. They can distinguish living dust from an ordinary piece of dirt, though no one knows where the ordinary dirt comes from. They recognize a room that is about to divide.

For a few years, they also hear something beneath the central square.

Asked about it, they usually become bored.

“It’s putting that one there,” a child will say.

Which one?

“That one.”

Then they want their cup or their toy or to be allowed outside.

The toys are simple: jointed seed cases, hoops of dried vascular tissue, little figures made from shed bark. One boy keeps a doorframe predator in a box. The animal is scarcely longer than his thumb and spends most of its time pretending to be the seam in the lid.

He feeds it with tweezers.

His mothers do not know.

***

Households are formed for many reasons, and affection is only one of them.

Two people may live together because their moisture needs are complementary. One releases water during sleep; the other wakes painfully dry. A household may include an older relative whose presence discourages the walls from thickening, or a neighbor who has lost the ability to cross a particular street.

Sharing a room is consequential. Over time, sleeping bodies affect one another. Their pulses become easier to find in the same places. They wake at similar temperatures. A faint taste familiar to one may begin appearing in the other’s mouth.

People therefore do not offer a bed casually.

The pump attendant and his husband have slept apart for years, in adjoining rooms. They pass a cup of warm water through a small opening in the partition each morning. The husband drinks first. The attendant finishes it.

Neither would describe this arrangement as unhappy.

Privacy is maintained with fabric, good manners, and a certain amount of skilled cutting. Walls transmit more than voices. A person leaning against a partition can sometimes feel someone turning over on the other side.

New couples rent rooms in the oldest buildings, where the tissue is least impressionable.

***

Not everyone grew here.

There is a woman who remembers a sky. She describes it rarely, and then with a care that makes listeners uncomfortable. She says it had depth without walls and brightness without a cabinet. She remembers water striking the tops of her shoulders.

The children enjoy this part.

She arrived carrying a metal key, a wool coat, and a bag whose contents are still not entirely public. The coat has been patched with local fiber. She keeps the key against her chest in a sewn pocket.

For a long time she could not digest the food. Now she prefers the fermented rind.

Her body has changed more slowly than the physicians expected. Her blood is still red. Her hair still grows from her scalp rather than emerging along a prescribed seam. But a small translucent area has appeared at the base of her throat. In the coolest hours, fluid can be seen moving through it.

She works in the records office.

When a map develops an address that no one remembers, she sometimes knows the name of the person who lives there. These are ordinary names, she insists. People she once knew. A grocer. A cousin. Someone who repaired shoes.

The clerk removes those maps from circulation.

The woman does not ask for them back.

***

The inhabitants grow old by becoming less negotiable.

Their bodies stiffen. Formerly shifting vessels settle into fixed routes. Cuts heal slowly but retain their shape. An elderly person can stand at a corner for an hour without opening the ridges at their wrists.

This steadiness is useful. Old people supervise nurseries, weigh survey sheets, and sit in rooms where the partitions will not stay still.

Some become very light. Others grow dense enough that floors need bracing beneath their beds.

Toward the end, appetite diminishes. Speech becomes effortful. The person may still enjoy company, provided no one expects an answer. Families bring chairs close and discuss the ordinary business of the town: a belt wearing thin, a dispute about drainage, the good condition of this season’s crop.

There is no cemetery.

The dead are kept for a time in a cool room behind the nursery. A pruner examines them daily. When the chambers within have ceased to shift, the body is opened along its longest seam.

What can nourish the town is returned beneath the paving. What remains resistant is dried and stored.

Families receive a small piece, usually no larger than a finger joint. These pieces are not decorative. They have irregular surfaces and sometimes retain a faint smell. People keep them in drawers, wrapped in clean cloth, and take them out when a room feels unfamiliar.

***

The council consists of five inhabitants who would all rather be doing something else.

Sella serves because she sees nearly everyone. The map clerk serves because she can establish where nearly everyone was. A nursery keeper serves because construction plans must account for children. The other two seats are held by a surveyor and a man chosen principally for his ability to remain awake during long meetings.

They disagree about the central square.

The surveyor believes it will be a building. He has prepared measurements for several possible façades.

The nursery keeper has requested more water.

Sella wants to know whether it will need feeding.

The map clerk has begun finding a warm patch on the blank membranes reserved for next year’s records. She has told no one yet.

At meetings, the fifth council member sits with both feet flat on the floor. Lately he has complained that something below is matching his pulse.

For now, the inhabitants pass the square without crossing it. They carry meals to neighbors, argue over repairs, coax children into sleeves. En checks the edge of her shears with the pad of her thumb. The woman from elsewhere pauses beneath a lamp and touches the key through her coat.

At home, the pumping-station attendant hears his husband move on the other side of the partition. He fills their cup and waits beside the opening.

Beneath the streets, the measured water travels inward.

Forty-three inhabitants sleep, or work, or sit quietly until they are tired enough to sleep. In the nursery, one empty bed is kept warm.
