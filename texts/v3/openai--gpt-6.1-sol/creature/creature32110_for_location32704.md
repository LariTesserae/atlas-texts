The most permanent inhabitants are so thin that the hollow does not appear occupied.

They live at the edges of things: where a moving film of water thins against the stone, where a little oxygen enters the warm seep, where the heat becomes tolerable before the surface becomes dry. Their country is not the pool or the wall, but a narrow overlap of conditions. Move that overlap a few millimeters and the country moves with it.

The rust-colored colony is thickest underneath its dull outer stain. There, cells crowd in a soft matrix that holds water after the passing film has drained away. Fine particles lodge in it. Minerals form among the living cells and the remains of dead ones, so that the colony slowly acquires a crust of its own. Its lower edge is ragged, interrupted by places where hot water has stripped it back to bare stone. Along the upper edge, a few nearly invisible outposts occupy damp pits.

From a distance it would seem to be one small organism, flat and patient. Close enough, it becomes a multitude. Some cells are dividing. Others have slowed almost to stillness. Some occupy pockets favorable only because their neighbors have already altered the water passing through. Material released by one is taken up by another. What looks like a uniform stain contains gradients, sheltered interiors, exhausted patches, fresh margins.

A hot pulse kills cells along one edge. Their material remains caught in the matrix, and other cells begin to use it.

The gray colony lives a little farther from the inlet. It is less conspicuous when fully wet. Water fills its surface irregularities, and for a while it seems almost to disappear into the stone. As the film drains, its faintly swollen texture returns. It is a soft place in a room of hard surfaces.

Here the springtail comes to feed.

It is pale, but not quite white. There is a weak translucence to its body, especially at the joints, where the brightness passes through the thin cuticle. Its outline is easiest to see when it stands against the gray colony: a short segmented body, six small legs, a head lowered close to the surface. Its antennae move even when everything else is still.

The chip under which it shelters has fallen at an angle. One edge touches the floor; the other is raised by a grain. Beneath it is a wedge of moist darkness, scarcely larger than the animal needs. The underside retains a thin film when the exposed stone has dried. The springtail rests there for long intervals with its feet set against the roughness, its body clear of the water.

It does not leave simply because there is light. It leaves when the exposed route is wet enough, when the heat has subsided, when the microbial patch has something to offer. It tests the entrance with its antennae. Sometimes it comes forward until its head is visible, then reverses into shelter without turning.

When it does emerge, it grazes in small places. Its mouth works against the film, and its body advances by fractions of its length. The colony is not evenly edible. A mineral crust interrupts one patch; another has dried; a third is covered by water too deep for comfortable footing. The springtail follows these differences along the surface, doubling back where a straight route would take it through a bead.

A bead can rise higher than its head.

At the edge of one, the animal pauses with its front feet on wet stone and its hind feet still dry. A foot touches the water. The curved surface draws around it, and the springtail pulls free. For a moment it stands farther back, antennae extended. Then it takes another route beneath the broken ledge.

Its folded jumping organ remains tucked under its body. Most of its departures are walked. In this low room, a leap might end against the roof, on the hot shelf, or in the pool.

Sometimes a falling drop reaches the grazing patch. The springtail may already be beneath its chip when the water arrives. If it is not, the widening film can isolate it on a tiny projection of stone. It climbs that projection and waits. Water passes on either side, carrying loosened fragments of the colony. As the flow diminishes, the two channels separate again and a damp crossing opens below its feet.

The mite arrives less often.

It comes from above, along the seam, carrying no visible sign of the darker cavity it has left. Against the bright ceiling its body is a small opaque oval. Its legs touch the stone in sequence; the front pair reach and test before its weight follows. It pauses beneath droplets and works around crystal points, finding purchase on roughness too slight to cast a shadow.

Farther down the wall, its pauses lengthen. The stone is warmer there. It turns sideways, follows a cooler strip, and eventually ascends again. On some visits it enters the loose space behind a chip. On others it leaves without finding food.

Beneath the fragments there may be eggs too small and colorless to distinguish from adhering grains. In the damp film are cells that have arrived with the water and will not persist, cells resting until conditions change, and small pieces of living colonies carried away from their margins. Much of what lives here cannot be assigned a visible position. It is suspended in a clear lens, lodged in a fracture, or spread across a surface that still looks clean.

The broad wall remains almost bare. Light falls on it all day without producing leaves or a green covering. The warm center of the pool is empty of animals. Life gathers unevenly, leaving most of the available space unused.

As a mineral lip grows, it holds back a little water. A damp patch lasts longer. Cells extend into it. The springtail eventually reaches the new margin and lowers its head.

Later the lip breaks. The retained water drains, exposing the patch to heat and air. Some of the cells survive in its smallest hollows. The springtail retreats beneath its chip.

When the flow returns, it passes over the broken edge and wets the surviving colony again. Along that edge, too faint to see at first, growth resumes.
