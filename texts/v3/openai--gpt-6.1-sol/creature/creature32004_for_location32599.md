The first person you meet may be standing still beside a wall, with one hand laid flat against it.

She is not resting. There is a pencil behind her ear and a small brass instrument in her other hand. After a while she moves her palm a few inches, waits, and writes something on the back of an envelope. Then she lifts a loose tile from the path and takes out a key.

She has lived here long enough to remember when that wall was vertical. Its lean is slight; you would have attributed it to the slope beneath your feet.

Her work coat is pale on the shoulders and dark at the cuffs. Under it she wears a fine wool shirt, despite the light. Most people in the valley dress this way: clothes for cold air, hats for hard sunlight, sleeves that can be fastened above the elbow. Their exposed skin is weathered, but the palms of their hands remain soft. They wear gloves for carrying the mineral and remove them for almost everything else.

There are scars along the sides of some fingers. A sheet can look blunt until its edge catches the light.

She opens the processing house and holds the door for you. Inside, she speaks at an ordinary volume. The room is quiet enough that you hear her take the pencil from behind her ear.

***

The workers do not all live beside the factories. Their houses occupy older pockets of ground, places where soil has remained deep enough to support a kitchen garden and a foundation. You find them by following paths that branch away from the rails, or by noticing a clothesline behind an orchard.

The houses have more shade than the works. They have shutters, thick door curtains, shelves built into the cool inner walls. Several have an abandoned room below the inhabited ones. In one, the old kitchen window now opens level with a fern bed. The family uses the room for storage, lowering jars through the window rather than taking them down the stairs.

Meals are substantial. There is bread with a dense, damp crumb, soup made from orchard fruit and a sharp green leaf, beans baked slowly in covered pots. Fruit is eaten with salt. The succulent vegetables must be cooked soon after cutting; otherwise the cut faces close and the flesh becomes unpleasantly tough.

Water is brought home in narrow lidded cans. Each household knows which pools can be drawn from, which should be left to the roots, and which acquire a pearly skin toward the end of the dry season. A pool may be perfectly clear and still be unsuitable for drinking. Children learn the distinctions before they are trusted with a can.

The people have names for places that no longer resemble those names. The Lower Steps are above the orchard now. The Long Room was divided by a rising seam twenty years ago; its two halves belong to different families. Someone giving directions may say, “Turn where the fig used to be,” and then, seeing your uncertainty, walk with you.

Few possessions are too large to move through a window. Tables have legs that unbolt. Cupboards are shallow. The beds are sturdy but come apart without tools.

There are exceptions. One household keeps an upright piano, brought into the valley before the present cable system existed. Its owners have shifted it between three rooms and will discuss the next move with anyone who stops long enough to listen.

***

Children spend a good deal of time looking down.

They lie on their stomachs beside pools, watching the bare lengths of roots descend through the water. They place dry needles across cracks and return to see whether the needles have fallen. They collect discarded mineral, although adults insist that they carry it in boxes, never in pockets.

Their best playthings are pieces too clouded for the presses. Held at different angles, these show narrow rooms of light inside them. A child can spend an afternoon trying to make a particular brightness reappear.

They also make boats. The boats are folded from leaf skins and launched into condensation gutters before sunrise. Each travels perhaps six feet, catches at a joint, and must be rescued with a twig.

They are noisy among themselves. Their shouting comes through the trees surprisingly clearly. Near the buildings, they lower their voices and use the padded handrails to swing around corners. They know the machinery has places where a sleeve can be taken in, and that a carriage which seems almost motionless will still crush a foot.

At the first sound of a falling stone, they stop talking. Usually it is a small stone. They wait for a second one before moving.

***

The gray animals on the cables are difficult to see on the ground.

Their feet spread widely when they grip, exposing pale skin between the toes. Their tails are short and flattened. On a cable they seem quick; on a wall they move with a laborious, testing caution, pressing their bodies close to the surface before each step.

They eat the fleshy flowers and the dark paste that collects inside old pipe sleeves. Their nests are made from felt stolen a fiber at a time. A junction box with a warm relay inside may contain three adults and a litter, packed so tightly that opening the lid reveals what looks at first like one breathing animal.

The electricians leave certain disused boxes connected to very small heating elements. This keeps the animals out of the working relays. It does not work perfectly.

In houses, people call them by a name that is also used for a small child who has fallen asleep in an inconvenient place.

They make a sound, though visitors seldom hear it: a brief rubbing chirr, produced with the mouth shut. It travels well through metal. A worker holding a cable may feel it before hearing it.

There are other lives in the valley that scarcely emerge into sight. Under the moist collars of the trees, translucent worms feed among old leaf tissue. Tiny dark snails shelter beneath the rims of the fleshy flowers. The moths in the processing houses leave fine dust on the felt curtains and rest with their wings held so closely together that they resemble a lifted flake of wall paint.

The pearly pools are alive too. Once your shadow has passed, the skin slowly smooths itself. Near the edge, where a root enters, it draws into minute folds.

People wash their hands after touching those pools. They do not throw stones into them.

***

The oldest residents are attentive to maintenance in ways that can seem fussy.

A man may interrupt breakfast to wipe a damp patch from a hinge. A woman returning from work may stop to cut a climbing stem before it reaches a roof seam. They turn stored frames every few weeks, check the cloth between mineral sheets, keep grit out of tools that were designed to withstand much worse.

They have seen a small neglected pressure become an expensive one. They have also seen repairs outlast the thing they were intended to save.

In a house near the eastern ridge, a steel brace still crosses the ceiling above an elderly woman’s bed. The wall it once supported was removed when she was young. She hangs her winter clothes from the brace and will not have it taken down.

She remembers the roots arriving in the old loading tunnel. At first they were pale threads along the roof. Then they thickened, and the roof broke between them. For several years the tunnel remained usable, with daylight falling through a narrow strip overhead. She remembers which trees stood above it, and which one died after the opening widened.

Her granddaughter records cliff measurements for the works. Sometimes the two of them disagree about how quickly a surface is moving. The granddaughter brings her figures. The grandmother takes her to a particular tree.

***

There is music in the houses after work: singing, the piano when its room is warm enough, an instrument with strings stretched over a shallow box. The sounds carry through open windows and disappear surprisingly soon in the dense leaves.

People visit after supper. They bring something to mend, or fruit that will not keep, or simply themselves. A newly exposed seam is discussed alongside a marriage, an illness, a troublesome motor. Someone has heard from the next basin that a rail tower must be moved. Someone else wants a lamp panel from the locked sheds and has been waiting all month.

While they talk, moisture begins to gather outside.

Before bed, a household puts clean vessels beneath selected gutters. Shoes are brushed and left on a shelf above the floor. A jacket is hung away from the wall so that both sides can dry. The person sleeping nearest the window closes the shutter but leaves its upper catch loose.

Later, when the leaves turn inward, a few drops overflow onto the roof.

The gray animals wake inside their boxes. Roots hold their unbranched lengths in the pools. Behind the factory glass, the small motors continue turning.

In one of the houses a child wakes, listens to a faint clicking from the gutter, and goes back to sleep.
