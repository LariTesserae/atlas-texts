At first, the only inhabitant you can distinguish is a swelling moving along the base of a wall.

It is about the size of a closed fist, though it has no firm outline. Its forward end tightens; its rear end becomes a thin, wrinkled sleeve. A strand beneath it brightens faintly as it passes. When it reaches a junction, it stops long enough for three small openings to appear in its surface. Each admits a little air. Two close. Through the third, something pale extends and touches the adjoining wall.

The swelling follows that wall away.

It has not chosen a direction in any familiar sense. It has sampled a difference in heat, a trace of mineral dust, perhaps the electrical state of tissue farther down the passage. Yet it is more than a packet being delivered. Behind its tough skin is an unfinished arrangement of organs. Depending on where it stops, it may become a valve, a brace, a filter, or the swollen base of a new production stem. For now it carries several possible bodies.

There are thousands of these mobile buds. Most die without unfolding. The wall opens around them and retrieves their contents.

The ones that take root are harder to recognize.

A particular set of shutters, a knot of roots below a floor, and a resin-filled chamber three streets away may belong to one organism. The connecting tissue is sometimes no thicker than a wire. Elsewhere it occupies an entire wall, with other organisms growing through it. Cutting one connection does not necessarily divide the individual. Opening a passage may.

Its extent can be found by disturbing it. Press a cold object against a trunk, and a shutter overhead closes. Several minutes later, a groove in the pavement contracts. Farther away, a production leaf thickens along one edge. The responses reveal something with a body spread through the district, although not everything that responds belongs to it. Some of its neighbors have merely felt the change in load.

Such an inhabitant has no front. It has places of intake, places of manufacture, places where old tissue is surrendered. Its most sensitive surfaces are usually buried. What passes for skin is often expendable scaffolding.

There are individuals here that have occupied substantially the same volume for centuries. Others last less than a week. Age alone does not make them larger. A very old one may survive as a narrow strip of living tissue inside a support it originally grew, holding an awkward tension that younger growth cannot quite replace. Remove that strip and a roof half a mile away begins, slowly, to descend.

These old remnants receive nourishment. Conductive roots bring charge to them; fine tubes supply resin and powdered salts. Whether their neighbors sustain them because they remain useful, or because the network still follows an ancient pattern of supply, is difficult to establish. Both things happen.

The long production groves contain a more regular kind of inhabitant.

Each paired trunk in the sheet grove encloses a thick living cuff. The cuff advances around the trunks as they grow, remaining at roughly the same height. Inside it, carbon is gathered and laid down in layers. The emerging leaf passes through two hard lips, then between rollers whose surfaces are studded with microscopic teeth.

A flaw in the sheet produces an immediate response. The rollers stop. A fine root enters the damaged layer, extracts a particle, and carries it into a tasting cavity. The sheet is then repaired or severed.

These organisms are extraordinarily particular. One rejects material containing a trace of a salt that another needs. One produces leaves with a repeated crease even where no crease is useful. Across generations, these habits persist. There are groves whose output can be identified by a faint diagonal grain, and others whose sheets curl inward at the edges unless held under tension.

Their descendants inherit more than a means of making a body. They inherit the shape of what the body makes.

That is how much of the plateau’s oldest design survives. A doorway remains shoulder-wide because the tissues that grow doorways still produce that width. A recess is renewed because renewal includes a recess. No surviving organism needs a handhold there, but feeder roots find it a convenient sheltered route.

Some lineages have altered greatly. The plants that once made hollow stems now grow branching conduits without cutting them free. Others still produce thousands of identical lengths and push them into racks. If the racks fill, the stems buckle. The broken pieces are reclaimed below ground, and production resumes.

The plateau supports both kinds.

Competition occurs mostly out of sight. A root enters another root’s salt bed and lowers its conductivity. A wall grows a chemically resistant lining just ahead of an invading seam. Two organisms may hold a boundary for years, each depositing tissue as fast as the other removes it. The resulting wall is thick, warm, and unusually smooth.

Occasionally one loses too much at once.

Then the dying body is opened from within. Its resin chambers empty into neighboring tissues. Supports are reinforced before their outer skins are stripped. A few mobile buds escape along connections that remain active. They carry small portions of the dead organism’s working chemistry, enough to resume some of its habits elsewhere.

Death here seldom leaves a whole corpse. More often it leaves a change in the things being made.

For several seasons after a large sheet-maker disappears, nearby shutters may acquire its characteristic grain. A ventilation flower may begin closing at a slightly different temperature. The inheritance spreads unevenly, then is lost or becomes ordinary.

The waves that pass through whole districts are their largest shared activity.

Before the paving rises, the mobile buds stop. They flatten themselves into protected seams. Production slows. Roots that usually maintain steady pressure begin to pulse. Living chambers exchange heat and charge until many separate bodies enter a similar state.

There is no single organ directing the movement. Nor is every participant merely reacting to the next. Some tissues can register strains far beyond their own boundaries through long conductive connections. Others respond only when a neighboring wall leans against them. The result is uneven, full of delays and small corrections.

A new avenue may be the outcome of thousands of organisms relieving pressure in compatible directions.

It may also be an inherited operation whose purpose has vanished.

In a sealed district beneath the western terraces, growth periodically clears a broad internal space, holds it open, and closes it again. Fresh tissue lines the floor each time. The space has no route to the surface. Whatever was once meant to enter cannot now reach it.

The plants continue to prepare it.

There are reproductive bodies, though they are seldom found in the accessible lanes. They develop near the drop, where rising air moves steadily through the roots. Each begins as a dense black knot surrounded by overlapping scales. Over years, the knot gathers salts, stores a little water, and grows a casing threaded with conductive metal.

When released, it spreads several brittle vanes.

Most strike the root face and are absorbed. Some lodge on lower terraces. A few enter the warm updraft and vanish into the dark above the plateau. They contain no complete miniature grove. They contain a small set of instructions, reserves sufficient for a first attachment, and organs able to test whatever surface they reach.

A successful one might begin as an almost invisible stain. It would draw charge across a crack, take carbon from the air, and lay down a film. Much later, the film would acquire a raised edge. The edge would shelter dust. Roots would enter the dust.

Eventually it could make room inside itself.

There is little evidence that any inhabitant recognizes you as a separate living creature. Your footsteps register as intermittent loads. Your breath offers moisture and carbon. Your clothing sheds fibers with unfamiliar chemical properties. The soles of your boots leave insulating traces.

Different parts of the plateau respond differently to these facts.

A filter may open toward your breathing. A floor may stiffen under your weight. A feeder root may follow the salt deposited by your hand. None of these responses requires the place to know what you are.

On the exhausted eastern apron, the new root repeatedly touches the polished bark because contact has yielded a small discrepancy. The surface is warmer than it was. Something has settled there, and the weight persists.

After several touches, the root does not withdraw completely.

Its tip divides. One branch continues along the edge of the apron. The other lies flat against the old bark and begins secreting a thin adhesive layer. Dust catches in it. A faint current passes through the dust.

By the time you notice the pale line approaching your boot, the root has already begun the slow work of becoming a floor.
