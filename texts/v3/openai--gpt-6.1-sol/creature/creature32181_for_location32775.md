The narrow thing beneath the canopy stops when the frost stops moving.

For a while, the line it has left is easier to see than the animal itself: a shallow furrow through white crystals, ending at a place where several leaves overlap. There is no hole there. The leaves are still clasped tightly enough to keep out the wind.

Then one of them lifts.

What seemed to be the underside of a leaf has a small dark opening in it. The opening closes, opens again, and turns toward the disturbed frost.

The animal lies flat against the ground, almost wholly covered by growth. Its back is olive and silver. Along its sides, little leaves stand in two crowded rows, their edges browned by cold. Some carry ice. Beneath them is a body the color of wet ivory, thin enough in places for the shadows of its internal parts to show through.

It is longer than a human forearm, but its length is difficult to judge. The rear portion is tucked beneath a cushion of shoots, and the front is folded back beside it. It can turn in a space scarcely wider than its body. Nothing in it appears to require a straight line.

When it begins moving again, the leaves along its back remain still.

Underneath, many short limbs release and take hold in sequence. Each ends in two soft lobes that close around a stem without crushing it. The body passes between the attached growth and the ground, drawing itself through openings that seem too small until they widen around it. The frost rises along its sides, then settles behind it.

It has no fur. The leaves are alive.

They grow from shallow pockets in its skin. Fine roots spread through the pockets without entering the deeper body, and the animal keeps them wet with a clear secretion. At the shoulders, where movement is greatest, the plants are low and closely fitted. Farther back, a few longer stems trail over the flanks. One of these has rooted into the ground.

The animal pauses to release it.

There is a slight tightening beneath the skin, followed by a bead of moisture. The root withdraws intact. For several moments its pale tip waves in the air before the trailing stem folds it into a groove along the animal’s back.

An old one may carry plants older than itself. Small portions pass between bodies when the animals lie together. Other growth is acquired from the slopes they cross. Their coverings differ from ridge to ridge, and even over the course of a day: silver on an exposed crest, dark and broad-leaved in a sheltered hollow.

Only the bare underside and the mouth remain consistently animal.

The mouth is not at the end you first take for the head. It lies underneath, a little way back, surrounded by a ring of soft folds. Ahead of it are two blunt feelers, usually laid flat together. These search the ground independently, touching a stem, withdrawing, touching another. Their tips have no visible eyes, but become darker when a blue discharge passes through the roots nearby.

The animal feels that discharge before the vegetation responds.

It raises the front third of its body. The limbs beneath it brace. A moment later, the mat on which it stands loosens and slides downhill.

It does not ride the moving surface. It lowers itself into the seam as it opens, holding a deeper layer while the upper growth passes over its back. For perhaps a minute it is buried. Then its feelers emerge farther uphill, between stems still glazed with undisturbed frost.

Most of its journeys happen this way.

It moves through the differences between one layer and another: where an old mat holds while a new one slips, where a full conduit lies beside an empty channel, where a contraction has ended on one side of a hollow and has not yet begun on the other. On a stable slope it is slow. In ground that is rearranging itself, it may cross a considerable distance without taking many steps.

It knows very little of open air. Lifted clear of the surface, it folds inward, clasping its limbs over its underside. The little plants on its back close their leaves. Returned to the ground, it waits until its feelers have found two points of contact before unfolding.

There is almost nowhere here that it could be placed without finding them.

***

It feeds beneath the terraces, at the junctions of the pale tubes.

It does not chew through their walls. Its mouth settles against a swelling, and the ring of folds becomes closely fitted to the membrane. Both surfaces change. A cloudy patch clears; a small dark pore appears between them.

Then the animal grows thicker.

The change is slow enough to be missed. Its flattened body rounds slightly, and a faint amber color enters the tissues along its underside. The tube does not drain. Fluid continues past, pulsing toward a lower field of closed leaves.

What the animal takes is mostly the material that gathers where a flow hesitates: dense sugars, fragments of softened fibre, the fine deposits left at bends. It can stay at one junction for an hour. Sometimes it withdraws without feeding at all.

The ground closes the pore after it leaves.

A few grains remain in the animal’s mouth folds, too pale and hard to digest. These are carried until it reaches another channel. There it presses them into the wall, one by one, before drinking.

Behind it, the swelling may diminish. A cramped bend opens. Two flows that had been interfering with one another separate into different layers.

Elsewhere, its feeding has the opposite effect. A channel narrows after being emptied of its deposits. The field beyond it waits longer for water. Leaves that would have unfolded remain shut.

The animal returns repeatedly to such places. It has no fixed den, but it has a circuit: three or four feeding junctions, a warm seam beneath an old spur, a sheltered patch where the growth on its back can spread toward the light.

Those places move. The spur persists, but the seam migrates around its base. A junction is drawn deeper into the hill. The sheltered patch turns over and exposes an entirely different layer.

The animal follows some changes and abandons others. Occasionally it searches a small area for days, pressing its feelers into every opening, while the place it is seeking has already been carried far below.

***

In the warm seam, there are others.

At first they look like a handful of pale roots lying across one another. Their backs are turned inward, so that the leaves they carry meet in the center of the hollow. Their bare undersides face the enclosing walls.

The largest has a damaged flank. A long strip of its covering is missing, and new pockets are forming in the smooth skin beneath. Beside it lies one so small that it carries only six leaves. Its limbs hold the larger animal rather than the ground.

They are not asleep. Now and then a feeler moves along a neighbor’s body. Mouth folds touch the bases of the carried plants. Moisture passes between them. A tiny shoot comes loose from one back and remains against another.

There is no permanent pair among them. Over several nights, individuals arrive and depart by different openings. One returns with its covering almost entirely changed. Another brings a young animal attached beneath its body.

The young are born bare in these sheltered seams. They are translucent, folded into narrow loops, with their limbs already closing on whatever touches them. For their first days, they feed from pores along an adult’s underside.

Their first plants are pressed into their skin pockets by another animal.

Only some take hold. The unsuccessful shoots are eaten. The surviving ones spread tiny roots, and the young body begins to lose its naked gleam.

When the surrounding ground tightens, the gathering separates. Adults take the smallest with them. Larger young flatten themselves beneath the receding warmth and find holds of their own.

By morning, the hollow may have closed completely.

***

You would rarely see one in full daylight.

It may expose its back for a few minutes in a sheltered bowl, letting its plants unfold. Even then, its body remains below the surface. There is only a slightly unusual patch of leaves, breathing apart and drawing together as something beneath them changes position.

In winter, many settle deep among the old layers. They attach their mouths to slow conduits and grow cold. Their coverings lose leaves. Water withdraws from their limbs until each is a narrow, flexible hook.

Others remain near the vents, taking warmth where the buried tissue is breaking down. These are the ones whose tracks sometimes cross the frost.

Their deaths are difficult to distinguish from their resting.

A dead animal stays attached for a while. The plants on its back may continue growing. Their roots pass beyond the shallow pockets and enter the body beneath. A pale limb loses its shape; the feelers darken; the mouth releases from the conduit.

Eventually the whole covering lifts away on new stems.

Something of the animal’s outline remains in the layer below it, a soft curve among the compressed fibres. Later even that is drawn apart.

At dusk, on the ridge, one emerges beside the draining pool.

It touches the underside of the suspended ice with a feeler, then withdraws as the green tips rise. For a moment it waits with its front limbs lifted. The ice is carried upward, opening a small passage beneath it.

The animal slips through.

Its silver leaves turn edgewise. A trailing stem catches, bends, and comes free. Before the ice has disappeared into the thickening growth, the passage closes behind it.
