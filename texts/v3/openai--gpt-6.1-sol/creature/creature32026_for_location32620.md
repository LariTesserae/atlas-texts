It is easiest to find by what the others avoid.

Below one of the warmer overhangs, the droppings lie undisturbed in a shallow crescent. Beetles work through them, and crickets sometimes cross, but no bird settles on the stone above. The bats leave a little more room there than they leave elsewhere. When the chamber shifts in its sleep—when one restless body sets a whole fold of the ceiling whispering—the crescent remains quiet.

At its center is what appears to be a darker piece of the floor.

The animal has a broad back and almost no visible neck. Its fur does not stand away from its body. It lies in short, close whorls, with longer strands along the flanks that collect dust and fragments of down. Old droppings have whitened one shoulder. The other is rubbed nearly black by the underside of the rock where it sleeps. It would be difficult to say what color the animal is when clean, or whether it has ever been clean.

Its head rests between its forefeet.

These are the first parts of it that seem unmistakably alive. The toes spread independently, feeling along the stone without shifting the body. They have blunt claws and wide, bare pads. A forefoot can close around a projecting edge, but it can also flatten over a slab so smoothly that the animal’s weight makes no sound. When it rises, it does not push itself abruptly upward. It unfolds a little at a time: toes, wrists, shoulders, the heavy middle of the body, then the hindquarters drawing underneath.

It is smaller than its stillness makes it seem.

Standing clear of the overhang, it could fit inside a person’s coat. Its legs are short, its belly low, its tail thick at the root and tapering into a dark, sparsely haired length. The tail usually rests against the ground. On a climb it presses sideways into cracks, steadying the body while the feet search for purchase.

The ears are small but mobile. One may turn toward the entrance while the other remains directed into the chamber. Their edges are nicked. A pale scar crosses the muzzle, and one eye has a cloudy crescent at its lower rim. The clearer eye reflects faintly when the animal lifts its head toward daylight. Usually it keeps both eyes half closed.

There is little light worth watching.

Its whiskers are another matter. They project beyond the muzzle in a broad, uneven fan, some broken short, others long enough to touch both sides of a narrow passage. As it moves beneath a slab, they bend against the stone before its face reaches it. A few stiffer hairs rise above the eyes. Others grow behind the wrists. In tight places, the animal advances with its feet spread and its head slightly lowered, receiving the dimensions of the passage through all these small contacts.

It knows the hollow in pieces.

It knows a cool crack that carries the sound of movement from behind a pillar. It knows where a warm breath rises strongly enough to trouble loose fur. It knows the underside of a ledge from which young birds sometimes fall. There are passages it cannot enter but can wait beside, and stones it avoids because they tilt under its weight. One broad slab, apparently secure, gives a faint knock when stepped on. The animal goes around it even in pursuit.

The polished routes across the rubble are not all its own, but it uses them precisely. Its body can disappear behind one block and emerge beneath another without crossing the exposed space between.

Most of its hunting consists of waiting.

A cricket extending its antennae from a seam may touch the animal’s muzzle before discovering what is there. The jaws open only far enough to take it. A beetle is pinned beneath a forefoot and lifted to the mouth. These small meals occupy it for long stretches, and it turns scraps carefully, finding what remains inside them.

When a bird falls, the animal is quicker.

It can cross three slabs in a series of low bounds, landing with its feet already spread. It catches the bird before the bird can work out which way is open. Wings strike stone. The chamber answers with calls, and then the predator carries its catch beneath the overhang, gripping it close behind the head.

Feathers remain there afterward, some bitten through at the quill, some intact. They collect in the fur along its belly. When it cleans itself, it pulls them free with its front teeth.

Occasionally it climbs.

The ascent is laborious enough to seem unlikely to succeed. It wedges itself between a pillar and the wall, braces its back, reaches, and draws its hindfeet upward. It pauses frequently. At each pause, the ears search ahead.

The birds detect it before it reaches them. Their calls sharpen; bodies lean away from the rock. A bat opens its wings and cannot immediately find room to launch. The predator stretches into the disturbance with one forefoot, feeling for a grip or a body.

Sometimes it catches nothing. Sometimes it descends with an egg held so carefully in its jaws that the shell remains whole until it is under shelter. Once, after a long climb, it returns to the floor with blood on its nose and spends an hour licking one foot.

It drinks from the basin behind the slab. Reaching the water requires it to slide on its side through a space narrower than its standing body. Its fur has been worn thin along the ribs by this passage. At the basin it laps slowly, stopping whenever a vibration reaches the stone. The surface trembles against its mouth.

Farther in, beneath a tilted block, is a second place it uses. Warm air passes through the back of this pocket, and dry feathers have accumulated in front. There are moth wings, the curled skin of a small snake, and fibers pulled from fallen nests. The material is flattened into a hollow just large enough for its body.

For part of the year, it lies there with two young.

They are born dark and nearly bare, with disproportionately large feet. At first they move by pushing their faces into its belly. Later they crawl through its fur and grip the longer hairs with their mouths. Their ears unfold slowly. Before their eyes are fully clear, their heads already turn toward the drip behind the slab.

The parent brings food into the pocket and eats there, leaving pieces within reach. As the young grow, it spends more time outside, resting across the entrance to the nest with its back exposed.

Eventually one of the young follows it to the drinking basin. The other remains among the feathers, making a faint intermittent squeak that the chamber’s larger noises almost cover.

There is no season here without hunger. There are nights when fewer wings return, cold intervals when the crickets withdraw too deeply into the seams, days when the basin is only a dark dampness. The animal becomes narrower then. Its loose flank fur hangs below the ribs, and it sleeps close to a breathing crack with its feet tucked out of sight.

Still, it seldom approaches the entrance.

When a cloud enters the notch, it lifts its muzzle. Moisture settles on the whiskers. It may follow the cooler air as far as the last sheltered slab, where the stone brightens and the sounds of the roost loosen behind it. There it stops. Its ears flatten under the wind. Below, beyond anything it can see, the birds are feeding.

After a while it turns back into the warm rubble.

By dawn it is under its overhang again. Wings pass above it, claws find their ledges, and fresh fragments begin to fall. One ear turns. The forefeet open against the stone.
