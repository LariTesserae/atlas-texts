The pale foot beneath the seat has six digits, though only four touch the ground. The other two fold back against the ankle and carry a fringe of short, stiff hairs. When something falls nearby, the foot comes out before the rest of the animal. It feels around with the four working digits, finds the object, and turns it over. The folded pair tremble without touching it.

A fruit is brought inside. A dead insect is sometimes left. A stone may be carried under the seat and returned several hours later to almost exactly the place where it lay.

There are five animals in that shelter, or perhaps six. They lie so closely together that counting their breaths is more reliable than counting their bodies, and even their breathing sometimes falls into the same rhythm.

The smallest can fit beneath the lowest part of the seat. The largest has to flatten itself to enter. Its shoulders pass through first, followed by a long, yielding abdomen. Last come two broad hind feet, each with a dark patch on the sole. The patches leave faint marks on the polished stone when the animal is wet.

Their skin is pale only in daylight. In shade it has a faint green cast, with small brown stipples around the joints. It looks soft but is covered in minute scales, overlapping in one direction along the back and in several directions around the limbs. A drop of water placed there travels a surprising distance before falling off.

They have no tails. What first looks like a tail, curled beneath a sleeping body, is a limb.

There are three pairs of limbs. The front pair are short and delicate, usually held against the chest. The middle pair do most of the walking. The hind pair can spread flat against stone or fold into deep, muscular cups. An animal resting on the carved seat settles these hind limbs into the two worn hollows. Its weight is carried comfortably there, while the other four limbs remain free.

This fit is easier to see at dusk, when the oldest one comes out.

It is not much larger than the others. The skin along its sides hangs in loose folds, and one front digit is missing. There is a pale band across its back where the scales have regrown without their brown markings. It emerges slowly, feeling the floor ahead with a middle foot, then climbs onto the seat and turns twice before lowering itself.

For some time it does nothing you can recognize as an activity.

Its head is blunt, almost square when viewed from above. The mouth lies low, beneath a flexible upper lip. Its eyes are small and dark and set farther apart than seems useful. Between them is an indentation lined with fine hairs.

The hairs lift.

Along the animal’s throat, three slits open in succession. Each exposes a thin, wet surface. They close again without an audible breath.

A patch of leaves falls quiet. Farther up the wall, something answers with two knocks.

The animal changes position, and the hollow’s noise continues.

It is possible to spend several evenings watching this happen without deciding whether it has called, listened, or merely shifted its weight.

The younger animals are less patient. They come out while there is still sunlight on the floor and move along the margins with their bodies held low. They eat the yellow fruit by pressing it between their front feet and licking the split skin. The flies embedded in it are swallowed too. They also graze on the copper fronds, taking only the curled tips, and sometimes scrape a damp seam with the edges of their teeth.

Their teeth are black. There are not many of them.

One animal habitually visits the little pool. It lowers its head until the indentation between its eyes meets the water, then remains there while small creatures continue to walk across the bottom beneath it. When it lifts its head, the hairs are gathered into a dark point. It rubs them apart against its wrist.

Another drinks only beside the blurred window.

It approaches sideways and places a hind foot against the wall before leaning in. The foot remains there throughout the visit. Its tongue touches the beads on the lower edge, never the air beyond. Sometimes it stops with its mouth open and waits. On those occasions, the fine scales of its abdomen stand away from the skin.

The other animals do not crowd it.

They know the window in a way they do not know the pool. A young one once reaches toward the blur with a front hand. Before it gets close, the oldest animal rises from the seat and crosses the floor on all six limbs.

There is no cry and no struggle. The old one places its mouth around the youngster’s wrist, holds it briefly, and lets go. Both return to the tree.

For the rest of that evening, the youngster keeps the wrist folded against its chest.

The blue frame receives less caution. They pass through it carrying food, strips of bark, and occasionally their young. From one side, their bodies look briefly narrower as they cross. From the other, nothing unusual can be seen.

They dislike the suspended grit.

At night, one or two of them approach the layered crescent and draw their middle feet slowly through it. The grains separate around the digits without settling. A few adhere to the stiff hairs at the ankles. These are combed off beside particular plants: black grains at the base of the low tree, pale dust under the western shelf, red particles into a seam near the entrance.

The task takes much of the night. They interrupt it to feed, or sleep, or press their faces together.

By morning, the crescent is thinner.

This is the only work they perform regularly enough to be called maintenance. They do not polish the blue substance or clear the roots from it. They never try to enter the space behind the window. When a vine dies around the frame, they chew away some of the loose stem, but leave the portion that has hardened against the corner.

They keep objects under the seat.

Not a hoard, exactly. There are too few objects, and too little consistency in what they retain. A feather shaft. Three rounded pebbles. A segment of translucent insect wing. A narrow strip of material that may once have been cloth. These are brought out from time to time and laid in the shaded part of the floor.

The old animal examines them with its mouth and front hands.

One pebble is passed among all five visible inhabitants. Each holds it against the indentation above its nose. When the last has finished, the pebble goes back beneath the seat.

The strip is treated differently. They avoid getting it wet.

There are young in the hollow, but they are rarely seen. At first they look like soft knobs attached to the underside of an adult. Later, their folded feet can be distinguished. An adult carrying them lies with its abdomen against warm stone and arches the middle of its body so that the young are sheltered without being crushed.

The little ones make a dry ticking sound. It is almost lost among the insects.

When several begin at once, adults elsewhere in the hollow lift their heads.

After rain, the animals become unusually active. They walk among the new seedlings, pressing some flat and leaving others untouched. They carry scraps from beneath the seat into the plant islands. They push their narrow front hands deep into softened mineral seams and withdraw them coated in red paste.

For a day or two their pale bodies are stained.

They also spend more time in the open center then. Two will sit facing one another several feet apart, resting on their cupped hind limbs. A third moves between them, stopping at irregular intervals to put its forehead against the rock. In the immense noise of the hollow, these encounters appear silent.

Sometimes all of them are out at once.

They do not form a circle. There are gaps and unequal distances, and one may remain half-concealed beneath the tree. The youngest wanders away and is allowed to go. Still, their heads turn toward the same patches of wall. Their throat slits open and close. For a few moments, the calls nearest the floor become easier to distinguish: a low knocking from the roots, the scrape of a wing case, a brief whistle from somewhere behind the dripping shelf.

Then a fruit falls, or a predator moves, and the arrangement comes apart.

A visitor can stand among them. They will usually leave enough room.

They examine boots with their ankle hairs and occasionally touch bare skin with the backs of their front digits. Their hands are warmer than expected. If the visitor sits on the stone seat, the oldest waits nearby until it is vacated. It does not attempt to share it.

Once the person rises, it climbs up and feels both depressions thoroughly before settling down.

At night the pale bodies are difficult to see. Their eyes do not shine. What gives them away is the gentle interruption of the wet seams: a darkness moving across a darker line, then the faint gleam returning behind it.

Eventually they go beneath the seat. The smallest enters first. The others follow, compressing themselves into the shallow shelter until there is scarcely room for another foot.

The old one remains outside a little longer, seated beneath the flowers opening along the trunk.

Overhead, the wind is loud enough to be mistaken for water. In the hollow, the noise shifts toward its night voices. The animal’s throat opens once, twice, three times. It leans forward, listening with the hairs between its eyes raised.

Then it folds its hind limbs, lowers itself to the floor, and goes in among the others.

After a while, a foot reaches out. It finds a fallen yellow fruit, rolls it carefully out of a crack, and draws it into the dark.
