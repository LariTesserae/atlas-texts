The small animal beneath the ledge is not quite the color of the stone. Its back is darker, dust worked into the short fur, while its flanks are pale enough to show whenever it stretches. It has broad feet, a narrow muzzle, and ears that seem too large for the rest of it. In the starlight you can see the fine branching vessels inside them.

It waits with its mouth slightly open.

When the drop falls, it catches most of it. The rest darkens its chin. It remains where it is until a second drop begins to swell overhead, then steps aside and lets another animal take its place.

There are six of them in the crack.

They do not come out together. First a nose appears, then the ears, held forward independently as if examining different parts of the night. The body follows reluctantly. Each animal pauses with its hindquarters still under stone. Nothing in the open has changed, but each must establish that for itself.

The workers call them seam-runners. There are several kinds, though the distinctions are clearer to the animals than to the people. One has a white chin, another a dark line along the spine. The smallest can turn around inside a pipe barely wide enough for a finger. A larger kind lives among the abandoned trestles and leaves shallow beds in the dust beneath their cross-braces.

Their burrows are not deep. Depth is often the wrong direction here.

Instead, they occupy the spaces between fallen slabs, extending them with short excavations wherever the packed grit is cool enough to touch. A home may have a dozen entrances and no chamber much larger than a boot. The animals sleep pressed along its passages, sometimes singly, sometimes in a loose row, their ears laid against the bodies ahead of them.

They maintain these passages with considerable care. Loose grit is pushed out. Sharp flakes are carried away in the mouth. A warm entrance may be plugged during the afternoon and opened again after dark. Their rubbish heaps are easy to mistake for natural accumulations: beetle cases, grass husks, splinters of root, little pellets dry enough to crumble between two fingers.

Their feet know distinctions that a hand would miss. An animal crossing a basin seldom travels in a straight line. It turns around a patch of apparently ordinary gravel, follows a narrow strip beside a ridge, pauses on one stone and refuses the next. Occasionally it makes several quick hops with all four feet leaving the ground at once.

Seen from above, its journey looks indecisive. At its own height there is a continuous route of tolerable surfaces.

It eats whatever can be opened, softened, or pulled apart: pale beetles, seeds from the centers of the grass rings, the fleshy base of a moss pad, an occasional shed lizard egg that has failed to hatch. After rain, it spends hours digging in the damp silt. Its forefeet become dark mittens. It returns carrying pieces of root longer than itself, dragging them backward into the crack.

Water determines how far it can afford to go.

The young learn the drinking places before they learn the full foraging routes. They follow an adult beneath three ledges, across the base of a boulder, behind the rusted housing of a disused valve. At each stop they investigate with their noses. Some stops are dry. They are shown those too.

A seam-runner may continue visiting a dry ledge for many nights after the drops have ceased. Eventually it passes without stopping. When the ledge begins to yield water again, one animal discovers it, and within a few nights the dust below is marked by many feet.

Around the pump houses, their lives become a little less precarious and more complicated.

A leaking coupling can support two or three families. They excavate beneath the edge of the platform, where the stone stays shaded and a little moisture works downward through the fill. They chew the soft wrapping off cables. They take insulation for bedding. The workers fit wire mesh over openings, and the animals find the places where the mesh has been folded rather than fastened.

There is usually one worker who feeds them, although feeding them is discouraged.

She does not consider what she does feeding. When she rinses the grain pot, she throws the rinse water behind the condenser. There are things in it too small to recover. The animals arrive after she has gone indoors, except for an old female with a torn ear that waits beneath the bottom fin while the pot is still being emptied.

The woman has been stationed there for eleven years.

Her room occupies the shaded side of the pump house. It is narrow, with a sleeping shelf, a cupboard, and a table attached to the wall on hinges. She keeps the window covered through most of the day. Her shirts dry on hooks within an hour of washing, stiff at the seams unless she shakes them out twice.

She knows the animals chiefly by their inconveniences.

The torn-eared female gets inside the spare-parts locker. A smaller one steals the cotton ends from cleaning swabs. Once, an entire litter appeared in the housing of a pressure gauge that had been removed for repair. She carried the housing outside and left it beside the wall until they moved themselves.

She knows the birds by their schedules. The long-legged ones arrive at the drain before the evening valve cycle, walking with their heads held level. They probe the wet ground for larvae, lifting each foot clear before putting it down again. If she opens the door too quickly they stop, but they rarely retreat.

The lizards are harder to distinguish. There are simply many of them, and their distribution tells her things.

When they crowd the pipe on the northern side, she checks the southern line for a rise in temperature. When a slab that usually holds twenty is empty at breakfast, she takes the probe out before beginning her other work. Their departures have warned her of changes small enough to fall between scheduled readings.

They are not always right in ways useful to her. Sometimes the cause is a snake.

The snake is thin, grey, and seldom seen except while crossing from one refuge to another. It enters the seam-runners’ passages and emerges with its middle thickened. For several nights afterward, the surviving animals use different doors.

There are other deaths that leave less disturbance. A bird does not arrive at the drain. A beetle remains on its back beneath a lip of stone. An old seam-runner stops halfway across a familiar crossing, and by noon its body has dried enough that the ants can work underneath it.

The plateau supports a great number of lives, but it does not keep them long once they have ended. Fur loosens. Small bones come apart. The wind sorts what remains into cracks.

The woman sweeps her doorstep in the morning. She checks the condenser fins, writes down the pressure, and walks the line as far as the first cable support. Beside the path are tracks made during the night: bird feet, the paired marks of hopping mammals, a delicate wandering line where a beetle has dragged something home.

At one bend, the tracks have begun to leave her cleared path and cross a patch of rougher stone.

She stops there. Kneeling is uncomfortable, so she lowers herself onto one hand and holds the other just above the ground. Then she touches it.

It is warmer than it was.

That evening she moves the water from the grain pot to the other side of the building. The old female searches the usual place, circles the condenser, and finds it. Two smaller animals follow.

By the next morning, a new narrow trail has begun between the stones.
