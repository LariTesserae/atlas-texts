The creatures under the slabs are smaller than they first appear. Their coats make a broad, rounded shape, but when one slips into a fissure the fur folds against its sides and the body beneath passes through an opening scarcely wider than its skull. The hind feet go last. For a moment their pale soles face outward, the toes spread against the stone; then there is only a little gray hair caught on an edge.

In the open they look almost unfinished: short ears buried in fur, a blunt muzzle, forepaws held close beneath the chest. Their tails are thick at the root and short enough to disappear when they crouch. Everything that might lose heat or catch the wind has been reduced. Yet their faces are mobile. The nose lifts and wrinkles. Long whiskers reach ahead into shade. A feeding animal continually shifts its muzzle, sampling the air close to the ground, where the scent of a plant can persist even while the wind strips it from the space above.

Their eyes are dark and set wide. They notice a moving wing long before they notice a motionless observer.

One may remain beside its feeding patch while a person stands nearby, provided the person stays still. It chews, lowers its head, scrapes soil from a root. But let a sleeve flap, or a hand move across the bright stone, and it flattens at once. Around it, three other gray shapes do the same. None seems to have called.

There are calls, though they are difficult to hear. Close to a burrow mouth, a faint dry clicking sometimes becomes audible between layers of the roar. A mother makes it while nosing her young toward cover. Two adults exchange it before entering the same narrow passage. At greater distances they rely on posture: a raised muzzle, a forefoot held clear of the ground, the sudden exposure of a pale patch beneath the chin.

Their underground life has a different range of signals. A foot pressed sharply against a slab sends a small tremor through the chambers touching it. Animals resting below lift their heads. A succession of taps can travel where a voice would be swallowed by moving air.

They do not use every hollow. Some are too cold, some flood after the rare high rain, and some breathe so violently that loose fur collects against their walls. The occupied chambers lie off the main airways, reached through crooked passages. Dry grass, shredded roots, and bird feathers accumulate in shallow nests. The material is carried a mouthful at a time, often from far away. A feather caught beneath a stone can bring two animals together in a brief, silent struggle, each braced backward until the shaft breaks.

The feathers usually come from the cliff birds.

These birds have sturdy little bodies under their pale breast plumage. Their dark wings are long enough to cross a basin quickly, but they fold with remarkable neatness when the birds enter their nesting slots. The blunt face conceals a short bill with a hooked tip. At close range, the feet are conspicuous: broad pads, curved claws, toes that spread over irregular limestone.

A bird landing on a ledge seems to strike the cliff rather than alight on it. Its feet catch, its wings close, and it is gone into the seam.

Inside the colony, the neighbors are close enough to touch. Their voices, so sharp outdoors, become short rattles and breathy notes within the nest cavities. An arriving adult brushes its mate with the side of its bill. A chick pushes its face between the adult’s breast feathers, seeking food. Birds that have occupied adjoining slots for years tolerate one another at a few inches’ distance, yet fight bitterly over a loose tuft of nesting fiber.

They know the surfaces around their nests in extraordinary detail. A newly displaced flake may delay a returning bird. It lands, leans toward the unfamiliar edge, withdraws, and tries from another angle. By the following day it crosses without hesitation.

The mammals have a similar intimacy with the ground. What appears to be a bare stretch contains a chain of usable places: a shallow groove where the body can lie below the wind, a knob that conceals movement from above, a crack too narrow for a predator’s foot. Their crossings follow these particulars. Two animals approaching the same patch of leaves from opposite directions may take quite different routes.

The young learn by following. At first they run too far out from cover and stop in conspicuous places. An adult doubles back, touches one with its nose, and moves on. After several weeks the small animal begins to pause at the same depressions its parent uses.

The seed-breaking stone is one such destination.

Several animals visit it, though not together. Each brings a seed in its mouth, settles on its haunches, and works with both forepaws. The first blows are tentative. The seed is turned until a weak point meets the edge. An experienced animal can open a familiar shell with two or three strikes; an inexperienced one may drop it repeatedly into the fragments at its feet.

Sometimes the seed rolls beneath the slab. The animal reaches after it, whiskers pushed forward, shoulder nearly flat to the ground. If it cannot recover it, it stays there for a while before leaving.

The polished edge has no owner. It is useful, sheltered, and near the entrance to a dry chamber. Those advantages have brought animals to it for longer than any living one can remember.

Below their chambers, where daylight never reaches, other creatures occupy the limestone. Pale beetles move along damp boundaries. Fine-legged hunters wait beside trickles too small to hear. Here and there a root has descended from a plant above and spread a delicate mesh across the roof. Tiny mouths graze it. Water gathers on its fibers and falls.

The larger inhabitants seldom enter these depths. They drink near the surface, pressing their muzzles into pockets where condensation collects. After a cloud has crossed the rim, they emerge at unfamiliar hours to visit newly filled hollows. Birds gather there too. For a short time the different bodies stand close together: feathers, gray fur, wet black noses, claws against stone.

Then the pockets shrink. Each animal returns to its own routes.

By late afternoon, the feeding mammals are easiest to see at the edges of the plant cushions. They turn their faces into the last warmth while their bodies remain in cover. The birds make their final crossings toward the cliff. A youngster misses its slot, circles, and tries again. Far overhead, a scavenger tilts one broad wing and passes out of the basin.

A gray animal comes to the favored stone with nothing in its mouth. It sniffs among the shells, finds a fragment worth eating, and sits holding it. Its eyes half close in the sunlight. Behind it, two young wait near the crack.

When it leaves, they follow so closely that, from a little distance, the three seem to be one patch of shadow moving into the ground.
