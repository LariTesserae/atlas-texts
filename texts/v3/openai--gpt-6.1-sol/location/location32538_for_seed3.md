The city stands so high that weather reaches it from below.

Clouds gather under the viaducts, snag on the lower stairways, and slowly lift their damp backs through the streets. On some mornings a cloud enters a factory through its loading doors and remains there until the afternoon shift, silvering the belts and settling beads of water on the workers’ sleeves. Above the city there is little weather at all: a dark, clear sky, a few motionless streamers of vapor, and the faint light of a sun that appears to be shining somewhere behind the mountain rather than beyond it.

The mountain is still being made.

Its youngest surfaces are pale, almost luminous: broad planes of stone with sharp edges, uncreased by runoff. There are no screes beneath them. Nothing has had time to fall apart. A cliff may end in a seam of soft mineral paste, the consistency of wet clay, which hardens over several years into another hundred feet of precipice. Elsewhere the rock arrives already black and dense, emerging beneath a street with such gradual force that surveyors can mark its progress in chalk and return the following season to find their marks at eye level.

Most of the city occupies a vast shelf between two unfinished summits. You can see from one end to the other on a clear day, although the distances are difficult to judge. A municipal reservoir looks close enough to walk to before lunch; the railway timetable gives it as a journey of three hours. Behind it, the western summit rises in clean, overlapping slabs, each slab slightly out of alignment with the one below. There are places where the mountain’s growth has not yet settled on a direction.

The buildings are widely spaced. Each district has room around it: a foundry beside several acres of bare stone; apartment towers overlooking an empty basin; a school, a boiler house, and a tram stop sharing a terrace larger than a village square. Elevated pipes connect these islands of habitation. There are cableways, maintenance rails, long covered walks, and staircases descending into gaps where the rock will eventually be. The city is built for a population much greater than the one now living here, or for streets that have yet to rise into reach.

At night the electrical grid draws orange lines across the shelf. Lamps burn in cages of thick glass, dimmed by mineral dust that accumulates on their inner surfaces. The tallest buildings have lamps on every landing, useful to people on the stairs and to the flocks moving through the dark.

There are animals everywhere.

Small gray birds pack themselves into the warm housings of traffic signals. Their bodies are scarcely distinguishable from the felt insulation until the light changes and an entire signal erupts in wings. Goats move along the rims of cooling towers, grazing the mats of vegetation that grow where vapor escapes. Broad-backed, long-legged creatures browse in the gardens beneath the viaducts; from the roadway above, their branching horns resemble bare shrubs drifting slowly between the green ones.

The rail yards support a great population of pale moths, mice, hunting birds, and something narrow enough to move inside the rails. Track workers hear it passing: a quick sequence of taps under their boots. It feeds on the small insects that shelter along the warm steel. Nobody has managed to lift a length of rail quickly enough to see more than its tail.

Most animals leave people alone. They have routes through the city older than the roads, although the city itself is older than some of the mountain supporting it. A fox crossing a courtyard may vanish halfway across and reappear on a ledge beyond the outer wall. The ledge is entered on the municipal maps as a projected formation. Work crews are required to fence it three weeks before it becomes accessible to humans.

Water is scarce on the open ground. The rock takes it in greedily, and rain seldom falls for more than a few minutes. Dampness collects in particular places: beneath a pipe joint, along a shaded wall, in the folds of a stone surface that is still soft enough to hold a thumbprint. These places are intensely green. Ferns unfold beneath benches. Moss grows thick as upholstery around drain mouths. Dark-leaved trees stand in narrow beds beside the residential blocks, their roots following the condensation lines down through the foundations.

A whole grove flourishes under the eastern freight station. Its trunks rise between the columns, and its foliage brushes the underside of the platforms. The railway has cut openings in the roofs to accommodate the taller branches. Passengers wait in cool green shadow while heavy engines shudder overhead. Water drips steadily from leaves that have received no rain.

The vegetation advances slowly, by sheltered corridors. A moss colony may spend forty years crossing a dry courtyard, then cover the far wall in one season after a steam pipe begins to leak. There are gardeners employed to maintain these connections. They tend the plants, patch the pipes, and sometimes persuade the plumbing department to leave a useful fault unrepaired.

From a distance the city sounds continually occupied: the low laboring of compressors, wheel flanges grinding on curves, pumps knocking inside their concrete chambers. Up close, the noise comes apart. A factory operates for ten minutes and rests for an hour. A lift climbs a shaft so slowly that its motor becomes part of the sound of a room. There are long pauses between tram bells.

The mountain adds its own noises. New stone gives a deep report when it hardens, sometimes loud enough to shake crockery from a shelf. The sound travels irregularly. People in the northern district may hear a fracture on Monday; those living beside it hear the same fracture on Thursday. Windows are braced when a report comes over the municipal radio, and remain braced until the local sound has passed.

Time is kept carefully here, with allowances.

Every station has three clocks: one for the railway, one for the district, and one whose face is blank except for a needle that indicates how firmly the present hour has taken hold. When that needle trembles, trains wait in the open. Workers do not pour metal. Children are brought indoors, away from staircases and mirrors.

During an unsettled hour, the city’s edges become unreliable. A row of houses can be seen continuing past its actual end, their windows lit by households that have not arrived. A stair may acquire extra flights. In the unfinished southern quarter there is a bathhouse whose interior occasionally opens onto a cold slope of flowering plants. The attendants close the changing rooms, leave the outer doors unlocked, and wait. Animals sometimes come through: wet-furred little beasts with folded ears, birds carrying seeds in their crops. The bathhouse has consequently become one of the city’s richest gardens.

Such disturbances are usually quiet. A resident comes home to find the corridor longer than it was that morning and sits down beside the lift until it shortens again. Freight crews discover that a loaded wagon weighs less after crossing a particular bridge, so they weigh it twice and record both figures. The city has regulations for these things, printed on thin gray paper. They are revised slowly, after enough people have agreed on what happened.

Beyond the last inhabited terraces, paths lead through low vegetation to the exposed works of the mountain. Here the air is cold enough to numb the mouth. The city noise remains audible, carried up from below, but the nearest sounds are wings, grazing teeth, droplets striking hollow leaves. There are thousands of animals out on these slopes, dispersed through the enormous spaces, each finding some warmth or moisture invisible from the path.

Farther on, even the lichens cease. Fresh rock rises in broad, unmarred surfaces. At its seams you can watch darker material gathering, very slowly, as though the cliff were healing around something buried inside it. Survey stakes stand in orderly rows. Beside them are coils of cable, unopened crates of lamps, and lengths of tram rail laid ready on sleepers.

At the final stake, a worker has placed a wooden bench facing the new precipice. People come out here on their days off and sit with their coats buttoned to the throat. Sometimes they hear a stone harden. Sometimes a bird lands on a surface that was not there when they arrived.

Behind them, across the shelf, the factory windows brighten for another brief shift. Steam rises into the gardens. A tram begins its slow journey between districts, its bell sounding once in the dim air.
