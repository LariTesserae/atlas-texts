At first the plain seems to be under shallow water. There is a dim shine across it, broken by long islands of black material, and the few visible lights stretch themselves into trembling lines. But much of the shine is only wetness. Water lies in films over the ground, gathers in grooves too broad and worn to be called seams, and sinks through holes whose edges have been polished by centuries of drainage.

The ground is made.

So are the islands, the horizon, and the bed beneath every pool. There is no soil under the paving, no buried rock waiting below the foundations. A fracture reveals another manufactured layer: black ceramic, pale aggregate, woven metal, then a substance like glass with bubbles arranged in regular rows. Farther down, the layers become too compressed to distinguish. The deepest exposed surfaces have a dull, fibrous texture. They look less like building materials than something which has been pressed and folded until it forgot how it was assembled.

The country extends for many miles at nearly the same height. Its elevations are modest: a raised conduit broad enough to resemble a ridge, the rounded back of a buried reservoir, a series of low terraces descending toward a pumping basin. Whatever stood tall here has been brought down slowly. Towers have worn into knuckles. Loading platforms have become shoals. The faces of enormous buttresses slope gently outward beneath their own accumulated debris.

Nothing has fallen recently enough to retain the shape of a ruin. Even the broken pieces are old.

You can walk up a rise without realizing that it was once a wall. At the top, your boots cross the remains of windows, now no more than darker inclusions in the pavement. A little farther on, rainwater follows what used to be a stairwell. The steps have become a single shallow chute.

There is no silence anywhere.

The noise comes first through the soles of the feet, then through the ribs: a heavy, continuous vibration, with several rhythms struggling inside it. Pumps turn beneath the plain. Air is driven through buried chambers. Somewhere out of sight, material passes repeatedly under crushing rollers. The sound is so large that individual sources are difficult to locate. You might stand beside a vent wide enough to swallow a house and hear almost no increase in volume. Only the pressure changes. Your clothing flutters toward it, and the water around its lip acquires a fine, crowded pattern of ripples.

Speech works poorly here. A person a few paces away can be seen moving their mouth, but most of the words disappear into the industrial weather. Travelers communicate with lamps held low, where the mist is thinner, or with a hand laid against another person’s shoulder. Even then, the machinery can make a touch ambiguous. A sudden pulse through the ground passes up both bodies at once.

The air is cold and nearly saturated. Rain falls frequently, though sometimes it is hard to tell rain from condensation dropping out of the low haze. It collects on exposed metal and along the undersides of overhangs, then falls in thick, widely spaced drops. Warm exhaust rises from cracks and immediately turns white. A bank of vapor may move across the plain for an hour, carrying a faint mineral smell and enough moisture to wet the inside of a coat.

The light is insufficient to give the place a single color.

Near the service lamps, the ground is brown-black and streaked with gray. Farther away it becomes a broad absence, crossed by reflections. The lamps themselves are scattered: a blue point at a valve station, three amber bars marking the edge of a sump, a white glow beneath the lip of a maintenance opening. Many are sunk deep into protective housings. Their light illuminates the water before it illuminates anything solid.

Above, there is usually cloud. On the rare nights when it opens, the stars make the plain feel lower and more extensive, but do little to reveal it.

Vegetation survives where the workings provide an advantage. Thin reeds grow from mineral felt lodged around drain mouths. Their stems are colorless near the base and dark red at the tips. Along certain warm pipes, a low plant spreads in stiff, rectangular mats, its roots gripping old insulation rather than seeking earth. A few sheltered basins hold patches of pale growth with leaves so small they resemble damp scales.

These patches are far apart. Between them lie acres of exposed manufactured surface, wet and almost bare.

Their seasons follow maintenance cycles. When hot water is diverted into a channel, the plants along it wake, put out new shoots, and shed fine dust over the stream. When the channel is closed, they stiffen and lie flat. A colony can remain in that condition for years. Some of the reeds have grown through several generations of grating; their old stems are embedded in the mineral crust, while the living growth stands only a handspan above it.

There are no birds on the lamps, no insects in the vapor, no tracks along the pools. When something moves on the water, it is a pressure wave or a strip of loosened sealant. The scarcity of life does not make the plain still. Almost everything else is in motion.

Channels fill and empty. Hatches open slowly, draining sheets of water from their backs. A low section of pavement may rise a few inches as a chamber underneath fills, remain raised through several days of rain, then settle again. Far out on the flats, an entire row of rounded housings sometimes turns in sequence. They resemble low hills shifting their weight.

The water has several kinds of behavior.

Rainwater travels along the shallowest routes and tastes, if touched to the tongue, of metal and cold dust. Condensate runs clear from the steam collectors. Dark process water comes up through circular wells and moves in channels with no visible fall. It will cross a level basin, climb a low sill, and descend into a receiving slot on the other side. The force moving it is buried; the surface gives little warning of its strength.

At intervals, every exposed pool lowers by the width of a finger.

It happens across the whole district at once. Water slips down the sides of basins, wet lines appear on retaining walls, and reeds bend toward the drain mouths. The sound deepens. For a little while, shapes become visible in the pools: fitted plates, enormous fasteners, sections of old roadway that pass beneath the water and do not emerge again. Then the level returns. The edges disappear.

Travel routes depend on these changes. The safest paths run along raised joint lines where the material is rougher. Smooth expanses can conceal intake slots worn almost flush with the ground. A shallow puddle may be an opening with no reachable bottom. People who know the plain avoid bright reflections; light marks wetness, and wetness often marks a hollow.

Here and there stand shelters built much later than the land around them. They are small, squat structures with thick doors, external pipework, and windows no larger than a palm. Most have been placed on old structural ribs. Their inhabitants bolt new plates onto the walls, but the plates quickly acquire the same worn sheen as everything else. Inside, cups rattle continuously on padded shelves. Beds hang from springs. The noise persists, softened into a pressure that can be slept within.

From a shelter doorway, the nearest installation might be half a mile away: a ring of low exhaust stacks, or a dark rectangular basin edged with service lights. Beyond it, another arrangement can just be made out. There is plenty of space between these presences, yet the space is no less constructed than they are. The apparently empty flats contain passages, valves, reservoirs, load-bearing webs. Every open acre has work beneath it.

What that work serves is difficult to establish. Pipes continue beyond the district. Material arrives from below and is sent down again after processing. Vast amounts of water are lifted only a little way, cooled, filtered, warmed, and returned. Replacement parts emerge from machines whose outer casings have almost entirely worn away. New surfaces are laid beneath old ones, where they cannot be seen.

On a low ridge near the center of the plain, water seeps out through a long horizontal joint. It has done so long enough to smooth the ridge into an even slope. At its foot is a narrow belt of pale plants. Behind them, half submerged, a maintenance lamp burns inside a clouded housing.

The seep thickens when the pumps change phase. The plants lean under the added flow. Water spreads across the pavement, finding each shallow groove in turn, and the lamp’s reflection divides into dozens of trembling fragments.

For a few minutes, that little stretch of ground is visibly changing. Then the flow eases, the grooves empty, and the worn surface emerges again, slightly cleaner, under the same immense noise.
