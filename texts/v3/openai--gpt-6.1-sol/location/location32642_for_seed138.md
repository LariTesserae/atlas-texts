The district occupies a shallow basin behind the seawall, small enough that you can hear the same pump from every street. There are four streets, a freight yard, two rows of workers’ rooms, and a concrete drainage court at the center. Beyond them, the warehouses close off the view. The only long horizon is a narrow strip of dull water visible through the floodgate.

Everything is low. Doorsteps stand a hand’s breadth above the pavement. The rail siding rises just enough to shed water into its gutters. Even the seawall, seen from inside, resembles the back of a bench more than a fortification. The land beneath the district is newly made: packed dredging spoil, crushed brick, a skin of poured concrete. Where a service trench has been left open, its sides show clean layers of gray sand and red rubble. There are no rounded stones, no old gullies, no deep soil. The corners of the curb are still sharp enough to scrape a shin.

Yet the place is thoroughly wet.

Water stands in the joints between paving slabs and beads along the underside of pipes. It occupies shallow rectangular depressions in the freight yard, giving each stack of pallets a dark, wavering duplicate. Behind the workers’ rooms, a ditch carries warm gray water toward the pumping station. Nothing rushes. The flow is thick-looking and almost level, betrayed by scraps of film that move steadily past a bolt embedded in the concrete.

Rain is common here, but much of the moisture has nothing to do with rain. Cooling pipes sweat. Steam condenses under the loading canopies. The water table lies close enough to the surface that a hole dug for a fence post fills before the post can be lowered into it. Beneath the paving, the district rests on saturated fill, held in place by sheet piling and kept habitable by machinery.

The heat collects under the roofs.

High above the court, broad corrugated awnings project from opposing buildings, leaving a crooked slot of sky between them. The sky is usually white behind low cloud, but little of that brightness reaches the ground. Down here the light is greenish, filtered through old translucent roof panels, or amber beneath wire-caged lamps. A person crossing the court passes from one kind of dimness into another. At midday, the lamps remain on.

Metal handrails are hot to the touch. The walls give back heat well after dark. Inside the pump house, you can feel the engines through the floor before you reach the doorway, and the air has the close, damp warmth of a laundry room. Outside is scarcely cooler. There is sometimes a breath from the floodgate, carrying a saline smell, but the warehouses break it up before it crosses the yard.

The buildings are simple and widely spaced for an industrial district. Their blank walls face broad working surfaces: turning space for trucks, access space for cranes, clear strips beside drains. Most of this space is empty. A yellow hose lies in a careful loop. Six steel drums stand beneath an awning that could shelter sixty. There is one parked flatbed with rainwater pooled on its deck.

Construction is dense in its arrangements rather than its crowds. Pipes run in parallel ranks along the walls. Cable trays cross above doorways. Every corner has a numbered inspection cover, every drainage channel a removable grate. The freight tracks disappear beneath a warehouse shutter and emerge again on its other side. Small bridges let workers cross the main ditch without stepping over hoses. Almost everything has been positioned to allow something else to be reached, repaired, flushed, or lifted out.

Vegetation finds little room. A tuft of coarse grass grows where a stair footing meets the pavement. There are a few purslane plants in the sandy margin of the rail siding, their fleshy leaves dusty on top and wet beneath. A young fig has rooted behind a downpipe, but someone cuts it back whenever it reaches the lowest window. Its surviving leaves spread flat against the wall. Algae make a slick dark band at the ditch’s waterline; above that, pale salt stains trace the limits of old splashes.

The animals are much more numerous.

Pigeons fill the shaded roof trusses. They sit far apart until a forklift passes beneath them, then shift and clap their wings in a brief, irritated commotion. Small fish gather where a cooling-water outlet stirs the canal. Their backs appear as short black strokes, vanishing whenever the surface trembles. Crabs work along the floodgate steps, slipping into the gap between the concrete and its steel facing. The ditch has frogs in the quieter reaches, although you are more likely to hear them than see them.

There are mosquitoes under every stair landing. Geckos cling beside the lamps, thick-bodied from feeding. On the warm outer wall of the pump house, a row of them waits with remarkable spacing, each facing its own patch of illuminated concrete. Rats use the dry ledges behind the cable trays. They leave bright, clean tracks through the dust and avoid the open court.

The sound rarely falls below the level of raised conversation.

A deep mechanical beat comes from the pumping station. Ventilation fans add a continuous, uneven roar. Somewhere behind a shutter, a compressor releases air with a hard hiss, pauses, and does it again. Loose roofing trembles in the fan wash. Water clicks against grates. Pigeons call from inside the canopy, their soft notes enlarged by the metal around them. Near the floodgate, the crabs’ movements are inaudible, but the hinge of a floating trash screen knocks steadily against its stop.

People speak close to one another. A worker leans toward a doorway to ask a question; someone inside answers without leaving the cooler strip beside the fan. The few open windows have mesh screens and curtains darkened by damp. Laundry hangs beneath the residential eaves, sheltered from rain but unable to dry completely. Shirts remain heavy at the seams.

The place changes very little.

The pump keeps the ditch at almost exactly the same height. A float switch starts the second motor when the water rises; a valve admits canal water when the cooling system needs more. The floodgate closes against the higher tide. Rain adds to the work without much altering the appearance of the court. Water covers the same low patches, and the same patches reappear when the pump catches up.

Maintenance keeps the young surfaces young. Cracked paving is cut out in neat squares. Corroded brackets are replaced before they sag. Grass is scraped from the access lanes. There is little chance for a ruin to begin, or for a garden to establish itself. The engines run through the night because the ground would become waterlogged without them, and the plant runs through the night because stopping and restarting costs more than continuing.

After dark, the slot of sky disappears. The lamps show the undersides of roofs, the shining handrails, the pale breasts of pigeons above the cable trays. Beyond the floodgate, boat lights move across the narrow opening and pass out of sight.

Inside, the water holds its level. The fan turns. A gecko crosses the wall to take an insect beside a lamp, then returns to its place. Under the court, the saturated sand presses against the foundations, and the pumps continue their work.
