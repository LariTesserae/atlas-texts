The settlement lies under the lip of a mountain so old that its summit has lost the shape of a summit. Above it is a broad, worn table of stone; below it, a drop into pale air. Clouds pass far beneath the houses. When they part, other ridges appear, their peaks blunted and their valleys filled with distances too large to judge.

Here, on the sheltered ledge, everything is close enough to touch.

There are perhaps thirty houses, though it is difficult to count them. A roof becomes a courtyard, a courtyard narrows into a passage, and the passage disappears beneath another roof. The lowest buildings lean against the highest. Their walls are made of small, flat stones fitted without mortar, the gaps packed with clay and wool. Doorways have been polished by shoulders, baskets, and the flanks of animals. Most doors are too low to enter standing upright.

The whole place occupies a fold in the cliff scarcely larger than a cathedral precinct. At one end, the ledge pinches away to nothing. At the other, steps climb through a crack in the mountain. Between these boundaries, the settlement has grown thick: dwellings, animal pens, store chambers, planting beds, stairways, shrines, piles of fuel, all pressed into the same shallow shelter.

The overhang keeps the streets dim. There is always enough light to see, but seldom enough to cast a clean shadow. Even at noon, the undersides of the roofs remain brown and cool-looking. Light reflected from the cloud plain below climbs the outer walls, so that a person standing near the parapet may have a pale chin and dark eye sockets. For a short interval in the late afternoon, sunlight reaches sideways beneath the rock. It warms the thresholds and reveals dust suspended in the rooms.

The ledge is warm for its height. Its outer face looks toward the sun, and the enormous roof of stone blocks the winds that scour the exposed plateau. Masonry holds the day’s heat after sunset. On the narrow paths, the air smells of warm mineral, wool grease, crushed herbs, and cooking smoke. Beyond the parapet, the same air would strip warmth from a body in minutes.

There is no stream here, no pool, no dripping cistern mouth. The paving is dry. Dust gathers in the stone gutters. Rain rarely enters beneath the overhang, and a cup left outdoors will collect grit long before it collects anything drinkable.

Yet the place is full of plants.

They grow out of long stone troughs along the lanes, from baskets wedged onto roofs, from dark pockets behind retaining walls. Their roots descend into carefully guarded seams of soil, where scant moisture persists beneath slabs and thick mulch. Some reach fissures too deep to follow. The oldest shrubs were here before the first walls; houses have been bent around them, and their roots run underneath several households.

Junipers spread low, dense limbs over courtyard edges. Silver-leaved herbs crowd beneath them. Fleshy rosettes fill every warm crevice, their outer leaves dusty and scarred, their inner leaves tight and green. Tough grasses hang in dense brushes from roof gardens. Thorny bushes bear small, bitter fruits that are dried on strings above the doorways.

There is very little bare earth. People cover it with pebbles, broken pottery, old cloth, and mats woven from dead stems. A planting bed may be only the size of a sleeping mat, but it contains several layers of growth: low herbs, a shrub, a vine trained across a wooden frame. In the narrow lanes, branches meet overhead. Passing through, one brushes against foliage on both sides, releasing sharp scents into the warm shade.

Almost everything growing here looks touched. Leaves have been plucked, stems tied back, branches notched to hold cords. A juniper’s lower limb has become a handrail. Another supports a shelf of clay pots. The inhabitants know which plants tolerate a little foot traffic and which must be enclosed behind a ring of stones. Children learn these distinctions before they learn the names of the distant mountains.

Animals occupy the remaining spaces.

Small, heavy-coated goats stand on roofs and descend steps with the seriousness of old people. Their hooves click against the paving. They nose at closed shutters, reach for drying herbs, and complain when a gate interrupts a route they remember. Their horns have worn pale grooves into the corners of walls.

Choughs nest along the underside of the overhang. They come and go in black groups, dropping into the gulf before rising again on the cliff wind. Their calls strike the stone ceiling and return from several directions. Sparrows quarrel inside the roof gardens. Moths rest in the dark entries. Beetles work through dry dung and fallen leaves; bees move between the sheltered flowers with a sound like a small tool held continuously against wood.

At dusk, rodents emerge from the masonry. They know the grain stores, the loose paving stones, the places where husks collect after threshing. Cats wait for them under benches. Occasionally a cat leaps onto a wall and looks outward, ears lifted, as if it has only just noticed where it lives.

The settlement is loud, though little happens quickly.

A pestle knocks in a courtyard. A hand loom clacks behind a curtain. Someone shakes a hide full of seed, and the dry rattle carries down the lane. Goats bleat, birds answer, a child calls from a roof. The enclosing rock gathers these sounds and passes them back. From the outer steps, the village sounds larger than it is.

There are no wheels. Loads travel on backs, in slings, or in narrow baskets hauled by rope. The ropes pass over wooden rollers darkened by years of handling. Tools are simple and repeatedly repaired: short knives, stone weights, bone needles, chisels with small metal tips. A broken handle is spliced; a cracked pot becomes a planter; a worn stair is turned over if its underside remains sound.

The stone itself has been worked far longer than the settlement has existed. The ledge is not a fresh fracture. Its edges are rounded, its exposed surface worn into broad, shallow hollows. In places, harder mineral ribs stand only a finger’s width above the surrounding rock. Ancient channels cross the paving beneath the houses, traces of water that ran here when the mountain had another climate and perhaps another height.

The villagers use those channels where they can. One holds a row of troughs. Another makes an easy path between two courtyards. A third disappears beneath the oldest house, and nobody living remembers where it goes.

Change arrives in small increments. A wall settles and is packed with another stone. A shrub extends across a passage and is tied upward. A family encloses part of a roof; years later, that room gains a hearth. The same stair is swept every morning, though its middle has sunk into a smooth depression.

Near evening, people sit along the outer parapet to mend straps and sort seed while there is still light. Below them, the clouds slowly close over the distant ridges. Warmth comes through their clothes from the stone seats. Behind them, the lanes remain crowded with leaves, animals, smoke, and familiar voices.

Then the reflected light fails. Doorways glow faintly with small hearths, and the black birds become indistinguishable from the recesses of the cliff. The last clear sounds are close ones: a goat shifting against its tether, grain poured into a jar, the scrape of a wooden latch.
