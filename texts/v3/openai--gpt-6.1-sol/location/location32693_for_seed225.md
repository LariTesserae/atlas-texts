The hollow lies just below the roots of the grassland, shallow enough that warmth reaches it, deep enough that no grass grows down to its floor. From above, its entrance looks like a crack around a loose stone. You could step over it without noticing. Beneath that crack, the earth opens into a chamber about the size of a small orchard.

There is almost nothing in it.

A pale floor slopes toward a shallow depression. One wall curves inward; another leans away. A few roots hang from the ceiling, stopping well short of the ground. Three patches of vegetation occupy the places where the wall meets the floor. Between these presences, the chamber is empty enough that a fallen leaf would alter its appearance.

Yet it is extraordinarily bright.

The ceiling is made of a thin, cream-colored stone through which daylight passes. Not in beams, except directly beneath the entrance, but through the whole breadth of it: a broad, milky radiance that leaves scarcely any shadow. The stone contains cloudy seams, amber freckles, the occasional dark inclusion shaped like a seed. You can see these overhead as you might see imperfections in a shell held against the sun.

At noon, the chamber is brighter than the land above. Outside, leaves and soil absorb the light. Here, every surface returns it. Even the underside of your hand seems illuminated.

The brightness does not quite end at sunset. It withdraws slowly from the ceiling, gathering first in the thicker places, then lingering in a few rounded patches. For an hour or two after dark, the hollow resembles a room in which someone has forgotten to cover several bowls of warm milk. Then those patches fade too.

The floor is limestone, but not the rough, sharp limestone of a newly opened cave. Its edges have been worn down over a very long time. Low ridges pass through it like wrinkles in a palm. Small hollows have smooth lips. Here and there a darker bed of stone has resisted the wearing and stands a finger’s breadth above the surrounding surface.

There is no rubble beneath the walls. Anything that once fell has either softened into the floor or dissolved away. The chamber seems to have finished settling centuries ago. What changes now is too slight to notice during a visit.

The air is warm, with a dry mineral smell. Moisture is mostly a matter of touch. A stone may look dusty and leave your fingertips damp. In the morning, beads of condensation form along the hanging roots. They grow slowly, holding the white ceiling upside down inside themselves, and fall only when the heat of the day has nearly arrived.

A drop striking the floor makes a small, definite sound.

It is often the loudest thing happening.

After rain on the grassland, water appears through seams near the entrance. It does not rush. It spreads in thin sheets, finding old channels so shallow that they are invisible until wet. For a day, perhaps two, the central depression holds a clear pool no deeper than a thumb. Then its edges draw inward. The last of it remains in a narrow oval, precisely where it remained after the previous rain.

There is a faint brown ring around this oval. It looks like a stain until you kneel.

It is alive.

Hundreds of minute animals stand along the waterline, each on six translucent legs. Their bodies are narrow, their eyes black and widely set. They are so nearly the color of wet stone that only their shadows reveal them—and in this light their shadows are no more than a gray thickening beneath their feet.

As the pool contracts, they move with it. Not all at once. One takes a step; later another follows. Over an afternoon, the whole ring shifts inward without ever seeming to be in motion.

The chamber is full of animals like this: abundant, but difficult to find in the emptiness.

Flat snails adhere to the ceiling, their shells clear enough that the stone shows through them. Fine-legged grazers occupy the small patches of growth. Pale larvae lie in the shallow channels, sealed inside skins that hold a little water through the dry weeks. On the leaning wall, a colony of winged insects rests with its wings spread so closely against the rock that it resembles a patch of slightly different polish.

Nothing here moves far unless it must. The animals are small, thin, economical. There is little to eat and almost nowhere to hide, so most have become hard to distinguish from light.

Sometimes a ceiling snail releases its grip.

It falls slowly, opening a transparent fringe around its shell. The fringe catches the still air and turns the fall into a long, wavering descent. It may take half a minute to cross the chamber. When it reaches the floor, it folds itself shut and waits. Hours later, it begins the journey back up the wall.

The vegetation is confined to three damp ledges. It has no familiar green. There are low, fleshy rosettes the color of unripe pears, and threads of rust-red growth tucked beneath their leaves. Each rosette holds its leaves upright in the morning, then lowers them as the ceiling brightens. At noon they lie almost flat, exposing only their narrow edges to the light.

Their roots travel much farther than their leaves suggest. Some enter cracks and descend below the floor; others spread over bare stone as translucent cords. The grazers follow these cords to feed on the thin films around them.

One kind of grazer uses tools. It is no larger than a fingernail, with a blunt head and two forelimbs stronger than the others. It chooses a loose grain of stone, grips it, and scrapes the wet surface beneath a leaf. The grain is abandoned afterward. Nothing accumulates into a workshop or a nest. A place where several have fed can be recognized only by a clean patch on the rock and a few pale grains lying nearby.

There are no paths, no hearths, no marks left by hands. The chamber has never needed to be arranged.

Its few sounds carry unusually well. A leg slipping on limestone makes a tiny tick. A wing opening produces a dry whisper. When the pool is full, you may hear a faint tapping from beneath its surface: the waterline animals testing the stone with their forefeet. Between these noises there are long intervals of complete quiet.

The entrance admits almost no wind. Occasionally, a breeze above draws cooler air out of the hollow, and the hanging roots sway once. Dust rises a little way from the floor and settles again.

By late afternoon, the central pool, if there is one, becomes difficult to see. The reflected ceiling and the ceiling itself have the same color. Only the animals around its margin show where the water ends.

You could sit beside it for hours and believe the place unchanged. Then you would notice that one rosette had lifted a leaf, that a snail had crossed a seam overhead, that the damp outline of the pool had retreated past a tiny ridge.

Under the ground, in all that borrowed brightness, these are large events.
