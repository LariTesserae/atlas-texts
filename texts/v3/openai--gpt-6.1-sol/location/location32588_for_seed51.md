The ground is never quite out of the water.

Across the whole basin, the land rises only far enough to divide one kind of wetness from another: shallow lakes from reed beds, reed beds from moss flats, moss flats from banks of dark clay that shine even when no rain has fallen. The highest places are low-backed ridges of stone, worn smooth and split along old seams. Water beads inside those seams, gathers weight, and drops. Some of the ridges have been weeping for so long that the rock beneath each drip has become a rounded cup.

There is no clear shore. You can walk what looks like firm ground for a hundred paces and find the prints behind you filling with water. Elsewhere, water lies under a thin, tough skin of roots, carrying your weight until the roots shift. The basin extends beyond sight in several directions. Low islands, drowned groves, sheets of open water, and the occasional long stone ridge recur through the haze, each part concealing another. A distant grove can look close enough to reach before dark. It may take two days to get there.

The light comes through a deep gray ceiling. There is enough to distinguish the blue-green of rushes from the brown-green of moss, but little enough that the hollows beneath roots remain black all afternoon. Every wet surface carries a pale reflection. These reflections give the landscape much of its brightness: the sky itself is almost without depth, while its image in the water appears spacious and faintly luminous.

It is cool here. Mist collects on eyelashes and the edges of leaves. The wind moves low across the basin, finding the open channels and spreading along them. In sheltered places it seems absent, though the reeds overhead continue leaning and straightening.

The plants grow densely wherever the ground offers purchase. Sedge fills the shallow water in thick stands, its leaves sharp and heavy with droplets. Between the sedges rise clusters of fleshy, translucent stalks, each containing a dark thread that branches near the tip. Low trees occupy the ridges. Their trunks spread into broad buttresses, and their crowns lean over the pools until the outer branches trail in them. Pale growths cover the submerged wood. Moss fills nearly every fork.

There are few flowers. Most of the vegetation reproduces through drifting fragments, spores, or roots that travel slowly through the mud. During the wettest weeks, whole strips of moss loosen from the banks and move into the channels. They float with the current for a while, catch against something, and establish themselves there. The older rafts are thick enough to support bushes. They have undersides crowded with fine white roots, all reaching down into the dim water.

Nothing here has a clean edge. Decay softens the wood, clay merges with silt, and living roots enter the cavities of stones. Even the exposed bedrock has the worn appearance of something repeatedly handled.

That appearance is partly misleading.

At the bottom of the stone cups, where the dripping water settles, you can sometimes see a fine regular grain. It is too small to resolve except at certain angles. Turn your head, and a smooth patch becomes a tightly ordered field of minute hexagons. Water moves across them in thin bands, occasionally flowing uphill along the side of the cup before spilling from the rim.

The same grain occurs in the translucent stalks, in the pale coatings beneath floating moss, and in layers of black sediment exposed by a falling water level. It extends through the basin. There is no central object to discover, no buried room, no broken casing. The operative material is dispersed among stone, soil, and living tissue, much of it smaller than a cell.

It exchanges heat between separated pools. It sorts dissolved minerals. It sustains connections between volumes of water that have no ordinary route between them. Whatever its origin, it has been present long enough for the plants to grow around its workings and for the basin to wear into its current form.

A pool beside your foot may be exchanging water with a lake several miles away. You recognize this when the pool develops a gentle inward current on every side, taking leaves down without rising. In another part of the basin, those leaves surface beneath a stand of reeds. Root fragments and spores travel the same way. Occasionally a whole fallen branch disappears into water scarcely deep enough to cover it.

These exchanges are noisy.

From a distance, the basin sounds like heavy rain falling steadily on broad leaves. Closer, the sound separates into dripping, reed stems knocking together, water rushing through submerged roots, and deep concussions that travel through the ground. Several times an hour, a low rolling noise moves from one lake to the next. Mud trembles around the sedge bases. The trees release showers of droplets. Then a channel begins to flow in the opposite direction.

There are also sounds whose sources arrive late.

A splash can be heard from a still pool. A few seconds afterward, its surface breaks, and concentric rings spread toward the bank. You learn to wait before stepping across narrow water. The warning is reliable, although the delay varies with the weather. On cold mornings there is barely time to lift a foot. Before a change in pressure, the sound may precede the disturbance by half a minute.

The landscape has settled around these discrepancies. Trees beside certain pools grow bowed away from them, though no prevailing wind explains the angle. Their new shoots correct the bend each spring, then gradually acquire it. Sedges along other channels bear droplets that remain attached through gusts and detach together at irregular intervals. On falling, the droplets stop briefly just above the surface. For a moment the channel is covered in suspended points of gray light. Then they enter the water, making a sound like a sack of grain being emptied.

Animals are uncommon. Tiny crustaceans live among the submerged roots, visible as fleeting movements in clear pockets of water. Once in several days, a long-legged bird crosses an open reach. It flies low and keeps its beak pointed toward the surface. When it lands, it chooses bare mud well away from the inward-flowing pools.

There is another animal you are more likely to hear than see: a large amphibian living beneath the floating moss. Its call is a rough, brief note, almost lost beneath the water noise. One calls, and often there is no answer. Their numbers are small, and much of the basin seems unoccupied. Yet sometimes a moss raft lifts along one edge, roots hanging clear, and something dark passes beneath it with surprising speed.

The weather changes without clearing. Rain thickens the air until distant ridges disappear; afterward, the clouds remain, slightly higher and brighter. Water drains from the foliage for hours. Channels broaden. A grove that stood on mud in the morning is knee-deep by evening.

During these rises, the basin’s connections shift. Pools that once emptied begin receiving water. Old paths sink. New shallows emerge elsewhere, laid with clean silt and scattered leaves from trees you cannot see. There is a season when the western marsh receives red leaves, although no tree within the basin turns red.

At dusk the pale coatings under the moss become visible through the water. Their light is weak and intermittent. It spreads in broad patches, revealing roots, submerged stone, the slow settling of debris. Across the open lakes, the patches sometimes join into long, dim bands.

The water level steadies then. For an hour or two the deep concussions cease, leaving the more immediate noise of dripping and flowing. Mist settles into the channels. The low ridges lose their outlines, and the trees stand in layers, each farther grove less distinct than the one before it.

Underfoot, a faint vibration continues through the clay. The basin is still exchanging water and heat, adjusting somewhere beyond sight. By morning, a few pools will have cooled, a few banks will have slumped, and roots will have reached into the fresh mud. The stone cups will be full again.
