The basin lies low enough that water reaches it reluctantly and leaves it badly. Rain from the surrounding uplands arrives through cracks in the rock, sometimes weeks after the weather that produced it. There are no streams on the upper slopes. Down here, a wall may begin to weep along half a mile of its length, darkening from top to bottom before the first drops fall.

Most of the basin is dry at any given moment. Almost none of it is entirely dry.

It extends beneath a broad, sagging roof of basalt. The roof is too high to see, except when a pale discharge moves through the mineral seams overhead and briefly reveals another layer of darkness beyond the first. Along the basin’s margins, the ceiling descends into shelves and folds; toward the middle, it withdraws far enough that the place feels open. You can walk for hours without approaching a wall. Low ridges divide the floor into districts of rubble, clay, shallow water, and densely inhabited stone.

The ground has been worn smooth and broken smooth again. There are slabs with rounded edges, boulders hollowed from below, channels that no longer lead anywhere. A dark grit fills the spaces between them. It looks like ordinary sand until something beneath it moves and the grains flow uphill.

Nothing grows upright here. There are no trunks, no leaves, no soft green surfaces to intercept moisture. The stone is exposed, but rarely bare.

Animals occupy it in layers.

The lowest layer is nearly transparent: flat creatures spread against the rock with their mouths sealed around small imperfections. A thousand might cover a single slab without being visible until the slab is disturbed. Then their edges lift, letting trapped water escape, and the whole surface wrinkles.

Above these come the jointed grazers, although there is nothing here that a person would ordinarily call grazing. They scrape at the pale films gathered around mineral seeps. Their mouths work sideways. Their legs are short, numerous, and tipped with hooks that fit into holes smaller than a pinhead. They move in crowded files along routes polished into the rock, carrying on their backs a second population of tiny scavengers. The scavengers, in turn, carry eggs.

Larger animals stand among them on folded limbs.

At first they resemble stones resting at unnatural angles. They are cool-skinned and heavy, with broad jaws and sensory fringes trailing over the ground. Some remain motionless for days, feeding on what passes beneath their bellies. Others make slow circuits between damp hollows. Their tracks overlap so completely that no individual trail can be followed for more than a few steps.

There is scarcely an empty crevice in the basin. Every sheltered surface has something pressed against it. Every pocket of water contains bodies beneath the bodies you first notice.

And all of them make noise.

The sound is immediate and physical. It pushes against the ears before the place has resolved into shapes. There is a deep continuous clattering from beneath the rubble, sharper clicks from exposed ridges, and an uneven bellowing that travels through the low passages. Some creatures strike their shells against stone. Others rub ridged plates together. The flat animals release sudden jets of trapped water with a noise like cloth tearing close to the face.

The basin magnifies all of it. A call made nearby returns from several directions with its edges altered. A rockfall a mile away may arrive as a hollow cough, followed much later by the distinct impacts of the falling stones. The air is full of sounds that are no longer being made.

Communication happens inside this interference. Each species occupies a narrow register or a particular rhythm. A hunter can follow a quarry’s repeated scrape through an uproar that makes human speech useless. Some animals signal through the floor instead, striking with a thickened limb and receiving the answer through their feet.

Where the stone is thin, these exchanges produce a constant trembling.

The coolness is steady rather than sharp. Wet skin loses heat quickly; dry stone holds the same chill through every cycle. There is no dawn to warm it. Light enters only at the far western margin, through a fissure that opens onto an overcast gorge. Even there, it reaches the basin as a dim gray reflection. Farther in, sight becomes a matter of nearby textures and uncertain edges.

A few animals emit light, but sparingly. A row of dull blue points may travel beneath a sheet of shallow water. A pale ring opens on the underside of a body, closes, then opens again farther away. Most of these lights are concealed when the creature moves. They are displayed only while mating, threatening, or feeding, and sometimes a predator imitates the display precisely enough to draw the wrong animal close.

The energy supporting this abundance comes from below.

Cold water rises through deep mineral beds carrying sulfur compounds and dissolved gases. Around certain cracks, the rock wears a thin, greasy pallor. Microbial films renew themselves there almost as fast as they are eaten. Animals concentrate around these places in such numbers that their passage keeps the stone clean, exposing fresh feeding surfaces. Waste is carried outward, worked through by successive mouths, and finally laid down in the darker hollows as a fine sediment.

The richest seep is not necessarily the wettest. Some productive seams remain merely damp. A grazer will follow a line of condensation invisible to an eye, its mouth touching down every few inches. Behind it, others wait. Occasionally the pressure below changes, a seam dries, and the queue becomes a slow migration.

These movements reshape whole parts of the basin without urgency. Over several days, one ridge empties and another fills. The noise shifts with them. A passage that was deafening becomes quiet enough for dripping to be heard, while a distant hollow begins to thunder.

There are predators in nearly every size.

Small ones live inside the breathing chambers of larger creatures and take a little at a time. Long, flexible ones lie in the drainage grooves, indistinguishable from the black water until they tighten around a passing foot. The largest hunters move rarely. Their bodies are low and wide, their upper surfaces studded with worn mineral nodules. They settle across busy routes and allow the smaller traffic to climb over them.

When they feed, they raise themselves.

What looked like a bank of stone lifts on six thick limbs, revealing a damp chamber beneath. Animals fall inward from its underside. The hunter lowers again with a weight that can be felt through the soles of the feet, and the surrounding traffic closes over the place.

There is no general flight from danger. In such density, movement away from one threat means movement toward another. Many animals simply flatten themselves and wait. Others climb onto whatever is beside them. For a few seconds, a patch of the basin becomes a living knot; then the knot loosens, and the usual layers return.

Water gathers in shallow pans among the ridges. Their margins are rounded and stained, with no reeds to mark where land ends. In some, the surface is broken continuously by mouths taking air. In others, it remains still because the inhabitants breathe through the submerged stone.

After a strong seepage pulse, the pans connect. Water inches across the polished saddles between them, carrying eggs, shed skins, and small animals that cannot resist the current. The basin acquires a network of quiet channels almost hidden beneath its greater noise.

Then the water sinks away. What remains is caught in dents, under bellies, inside empty shells. The animals redistribute themselves around these remnants. Some enter the drying mud headfirst and stay there. Their bodies become hard enough to walk on until moisture returns.

Along the eastern margin is a wide field of old shells. They have accumulated so slowly, and been ground and dissolved so often, that the distinction between shell and stone is difficult to keep. Living animals excavate chambers in their predecessors. Seeping water cements the fragments together. A low escarpment has formed there, ribbed with hollows, all of them occupied.

Its weathered face carries the same soft contours as the older rock. Nothing in the basin looks newly arrived for long.

To cross it, you choose the less crowded ground, though this is often the least stable. You place a hand on a cool slab and feel movement within it. You step over a channel and discover that its banks are breathing. Far ahead, an unseen mass shifts, sending a series of impacts through the floor. The sound passes beneath you and continues toward the western fissure.

You wait for it to fade.

Before it does, another begins.
