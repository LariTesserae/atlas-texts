The place lies beneath a fallen doorstep, in the shallow space between the foundation of a house and the rock on which it was built. From outside, there is only a dark seam, partly screened by a curl of old plaster. Heat comes out of it. So does a low, uneven clatter, as though someone inside were sorting pots.

There is no one inside.

The hollow is scarcely six paces across. Its ceiling descends toward the edges, and its floor is almost level, with a slight sag at the center where the finest dust collects. Within it stands a little town.

The buildings are made of fired earth, mica flakes, and thin pieces of pale stone fitted together without mortar. Most are no higher than a hand. There are square houses with inward-sloping walls, a row of open-fronted workshops, storage chambers sunk halfway into the floor. Narrow lanes run between them. In several places, one generation of construction rests directly on another: a threshold above a buried roof, a stair descending into compacted dust, a newer wall borrowing the corner of an older one.

Nothing here was made with elaborate tools. Clay was pressed around reeds or rods that have since burned away. Stone was scored and snapped. Door lintels retain the small, repeated dents of patient hammering. On one roof, a repair has been made with mismatched fragments, each rubbed until it fitted.

The town occupies less than a quarter of the hollow. Beyond its last wall, the bare floor continues beneath the low ceiling. A single fallen tile lies far out on this open ground. Around it there is so much unmarked dust that it seems more remote than it is.

No water reaches the chamber. There is no dampness under the stones, no condensation on the roof, no hidden reservoir. The dust would rise at the lightest touch. Even the darkest recesses are hot and dry.

Yet vegetation grows here.

It gathers chiefly along the lanes and in the lee of buildings: small, crowded thickets of black stems with leaves like folded copper foil. Some plants spread low over the ground in dense cushions, their overlapping scales dull green underneath and nearly black above. Others climb the walls on stiff, branching hooks. At the town’s center, where an open court was once kept clear, growth has become luxuriant enough to obscure the paving.

These plants take their nourishment from the gases rising through the floor. Their roots follow cracks rather than seeking depth. Where a fissure exhales, they flourish; a few finger-widths away, nothing grows. The boundary between abundance and barrenness is abrupt. A green-black bank of foliage ends at a lane of untouched dust.

The leaves contain no soft tissue that can be bruised into wetness. They are thin and tough, layered around a flexible mineral skeleton. New growth emerges pale, almost translucent, then darkens in the heat. Old leaves curl inward and become brittle. They fall in small spirals that collect against thresholds, and eventually break into glittering flakes.

There are no flowers in the familiar sense. At intervals, the tips of certain stems loosen into branching yellow combs. These open when the floor begins to tremble. A fine powder shakes free and travels on the rising air, briefly visible in the scant light.

Light enters through the seam beneath the doorstep and through two smaller cracks overhead. It is never enough to illuminate the whole chamber. A roof edge catches a dull red gleam; a strip of paving appears; several leaves flash and disappear again. Most of the town is understood by its interruptions of darkness.

The heat is strongest near the central court. A broad stone there has split into three pieces, and beneath it something deep in the foundation exhales at irregular intervals. The gas itself is invisible. Its arrival can be seen in the plants: leaves lift, climbing stems straighten, the yellow combs open one after another. A movement passes outward through the court and stops at the walls.

Then comes the sound.

Air runs through buried passages beneath the buildings. Some passages end in little clay chimneys, others in broken drains or the hollow walls of houses. Each gives a different note. One whistles sharply. Another produces a thick, wavering hum. A cracked storage jar knocks against the stone beside it whenever the pressure rises.

The chamber is loud during these breaths. Its low roof holds the noise close. Small sounds become disproportionately large: a dry leaf striking a roof, grit rattling down a stair, the sudden snap of a cooling tile. Afterward there may be several minutes of stillness, but never quite silence. Somewhere in the town, a stem is slowly scraping a wall.

The buildings have endured this for a long time. Corners are rounded by airborne grit. The sides facing the main vent are smoothly worn, while sheltered surfaces still carry thumbprints from their making. Dust has buried the lowest courses evenly enough that the town seems to have settled into its own foundations.

Its destruction is slow and orderly. A wall falls, catches leaves, and becomes a new growing place. Roots enter the opened joints but do not tear them apart quickly; they thicken by increments, lifting a stone perhaps once in many hot cycles. Loose fragments migrate into the lanes. The lanes narrow. Where a passage becomes blocked, the next exhalation finds another route, and a different cluster of plants begins to prosper.

There is no animal movement, no nest, no chewed edge, no track in the dust. Nothing visits the thickets or carries away their fallen growth. The empty workshops remain empty. A tiny bench stands behind one doorway, with three shallow bowls on it, all filled to the same level with powder.

Near the entrance, the air is less hot and the plants are sparse. One long runner has crossed the bare ground toward the light. It lies beside the fallen tile, its newest leaves still pale. During each upward breath they open slightly. Between breaths they fold close around the stem.

Beyond that, the dark seam leads out into a much larger ruin. But the little town has its own weather, its own seasons of pressure and release. Beneath the doorstep, the court stirs again. Leaves rise over the roofs, the buried passages begin to sound, and dust lifts from streets that no feet use.
