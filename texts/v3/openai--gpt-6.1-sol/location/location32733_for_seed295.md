The first impression is of a hillside heard rather than seen.

Something is calling from every height: a hard, repeated note close to your ear, a rasp farther up, a wavering chorus beyond the next ridge. Beneath these sounds runs the continuous friction of insects. Sometimes a wing strikes a leaf with a noise like a hand clapping. Sometimes thousands of small voices coincide, and the air seems briefly to acquire a rough surface.

It is warm, though the place stands high above the lower country. The wind arriving from below has lost its moisture on the outer slopes. Here it moves through the vegetation in tired breaths, lifting the smell of crushed leaves, dry droppings, and the sour warmth of inhabited hollows.

There is almost no light at ground level. A little remains overhead, divided into greenish slits by leaves. You can see enough to distinguish a bank from an opening, but not enough to know what is inside the opening until something moves.

The ground is built.

What seems at first to be weathered earth is the joined work of colonies: clay grains carried individually, chewed wood, fibers, saliva, fragments of fallen leaves. For generations, insects have raised walls, bridged cracks, roofed passages, and repaired the damage left by roots and rain. Their constructions have widened until neighboring mounds meet. Old galleries have collapsed and been filled; their roofs have become paths; paths have been covered by new chambers. Beneath the thin litter, there is no ordinary surface left exposed.

The hillside has rooms all the way down.

In the few places where a bank has broken, you can see its substance: a dense brown crust around a pale, delicate interior, perforated with passages no wider than a stem. Larger cavities occupy the remains of abandoned chambers. Beetles live in some. In others, the droppings and shed skins have accumulated deeply enough to hold roots. A rat slips into one opening, and the sound of its feet continues somewhere beneath you.

The builders themselves are mostly hidden. A line of pale workers crosses a split in the wall under a fresh roof of damp pulp. By tomorrow, the split will be closed. The great rounded shoulders of the terrain change much more slowly. Their corners have softened through decades of wetting, drying, occupation, and repair. A mound that has ceased to belong to its first colony is not abandoned for long.

Vegetation makes a second landscape over the first.

Ferns rise from joints where moisture lingers. Creepers lie thickly across the ridges, descending into hollows and climbing out again. Small trees stand in pockets of deep compost, their roots spreading sideways through the old chambers. Here and there a root has broken a wall and become part of its support: the insects have plastered around it, leaving a swelling seam. Leaves overlap so closely that walking means entering one layer after another—broad leathery leaves at the shoulder, narrow leaves at the knees, fine new growth brushing the face.

There is lushness here, but little open water. Rain comes hard and briefly. For an hour, every leaf tip drips and the paths become slick. Then the water disappears into a thousand seams. The exposed ridges dry first, becoming dusty enough that a passing animal leaves a clear print. The hollows remain damp. At the bottom of one, a shallow pool holds the reflected underside of a fern and three mosquito larvae.

That small pool is crowded beyond its apparent capacity. Tiny frogs cling to its rim. Ants drink where a twig touches the water. A beetle waits just beneath the surface. Something larger has pressed a narrow track through the mud beside it, then gone away.

Every available space is occupied on more than one scale.

Lizards sleep against warm walls while mites move between their scales. Moths gather under projecting lips of hardened clay. Spiders suspend themselves across passages too large for the original builders and too small for most of the animals that now use them. Bats hang inside cavities high in the banks, packed so closely that the ceiling looks furred. Their smell reaches you before their bodies become visible.

Above, birds roost in the trees that have rooted into the oldest structures. Even in darkness they argue, shuffle, dislodge one another. A large bird landing sends a tremor through a whole patch of foliage. Below it, a small mammal freezes with a fruit held against its chest.

The noise is not evidence of haste. Most of the place proceeds patiently. An animal spends half the night working through the same cluster of leaves. A vine advances a little during each wet season. A colony seals one passage and opens another over the course of weeks. The overwhelming chorus is the sound of countless small routines continuing together.

Beyond the immediate banks, the country opens into a succession of rounded ridges and densely planted depressions. You glimpse them from the higher paths: dark shoulders receding across the upland, each wearing its own canopy, each divided from the next by a narrow trough. Occasionally the foliage parts enough to reveal the distant, lower country, faint and cool-looking beneath the haze. Then a branch shifts back, and the view closes.

There are recognizable routes through this density. Heavy animals favor the crests, where the constructed crust is thick and dry. Smaller ones travel along the walls. The deepest hollows belong to creatures that tolerate wet feet and very little air. A fresh, smooth patch of clay may be sound underfoot; an old, leafy hollow may conceal a chamber that will collapse beneath a careless step.

At night the paths grow busier. Wings pass close without being seen. The bats leave their cavities in uneven streams, and for a while their squeaking sharpens the larger din. Fruit falls. Something scrapes steadily at a bank, stopping whenever you move.

Then a little rain reaches the canopy.

For several minutes it is only another sound overhead. Eventually one drop comes through and strikes the back of your hand, warm as the air. More follow. The dry crust darkens in scattered spots; leaves begin to shed water from their tips. In a broken gallery beside the path, pale insects retreat from the exposed edge. Others arrive carrying material.

By morning, the opening will be smaller.
