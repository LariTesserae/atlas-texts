Inside the underside of the ice is a room no larger than a closed hand.

It cannot be seen from above. There the ice is milky, roughened by snow, and apparently solid. Below, a narrow seam admits seawater to a hollow that has not yet decided its shape. Its ceiling is still freezing. Its floor is still being made.

The room is almost entirely water: cold brine, faintly denser at the bottom, carrying a sediment so fine that individual grains stay suspended for days. There is no horizon. The farthest distance is the width of the hollow, and even that is interrupted by pale towers, filaments, and the slowly passing bodies of animals. Light arrives through the ice as a diluted blue. Near the entrance it is enough to distinguish a translucent shell from the water around it. Deeper in, things become visible only when they move across something darker.

The settlement occupies the lower half.

It has grown from a crust of animals that attach themselves to whatever is firm. Their first shelters were single chambers, chalk-white cups scarcely wider than a grain of salt. New chambers have budded beside the old ones, then above them. They meet, join, and leave narrow passages between. Some stacks lean against the ice walls; others rise from the bottom on translucent foundations. The oldest chambers remain occupied. Through their thin partitions, a moving shadow can sometimes be followed from one cell into another.

There are streets, though nothing here has laid them out. They are the gaps that persist because water uses them. Broad at the seam, they divide into lanes, then into channels through which only the smallest larvae can pass. Food collects at the corners. Waste settles in the sheltered courts. Along the most reliable currents, ranks of soft mouths open from their chalk rims.

The architecture is still wet and unfinished. Fresh walls bend slightly when touched. A tower acquires its next storey as a clear blister, almost indistinguishable from an egg, then slowly whitens. On one side of the room, six such blisters have appeared since the last exchange of water. On the other, a wall has not hardened enough to support what has grown above it; its upper chambers have sagged into a low arch. Animals continue feeding beneath the arch. A thin new seam is forming where it touches the floor.

The ground has no weathered surfaces. Nothing has been here long enough to become smooth through wear. Its small ridges are deposits, its hollows are spaces left between things arriving. Pale mineral dust adheres to mucus and becomes a bank. A bank supports a cluster of chambers. The cluster diverts a current, which begins another bank somewhere else. Even the darkest material at the bottom is fresh: compressed food remains, empty membranes, and the first bodies the room has failed to keep alive.

Life is crowded into every available interval.

Flat, glassy animals slide between walls with their legs folded beneath them. Worms thinner than hairs extend from pores, test the water, and withdraw. Small crustaceans hang head-down under the ceiling, their antennae held apart like delicate branches. Egg clusters fill the sheltered recesses. Some eggs contain an obvious creature, curled and waiting; others seem empty until a point inside changes position.

In the lanes, swimmers turn sideways to pass one another. A transparent hunter holds itself motionless beside a busy opening. It is nearly invisible except for two dark specks and a gut containing something pale. When a larva strays close, the hunter folds around it. The movement is brief and local. A few nearby animals draw back into their chambers, then emerge again.

There is very little green. A thin olive stain follows a crack in the ceiling where the light is stronger. Lower down, three tufts of algae cling to the rim of a mineral shelf. They grow slowly and are grazed almost as soon as they lengthen. Their surviving strands are stiff, short, and dark. Much of the room’s food comes through the seam: suspended scraps from the larger sea, caught and passed inward through successive ranks of mouths.

It is quiet enough that the place’s activity seems separate from sound. No surf reaches it as surf. No running water can be heard in its lanes. A swimmer’s limbs beat rapidly without disturbing the silence. Feeding fans open. Fine particles change direction. Somewhere within a wall, a body contracts and pushes a little cloud out through a pore.

The room has tides, but they are not quite the tides outside.

As the roof freezes, it rejects salt. Threads of heavier water descend from it, visible only because the suspended dust follows them. They drop between the towers and spread along the floor. At these times, animals climb. The lower mouths close; upper galleries grow crowded. A passage that was busy becomes empty, and a previously unused ledge receives a row of resting bodies.

Then a slight easing of pressure opens the entrance seam. Less concentrated seawater enters, lifts the heavy layer, and sets the whole settlement moving again. Feeding fans unfold in succession from the doorway to the back wall. The three algal tufts lean the other way.

Occasionally the ice produces a needle that grows down into an occupied lane. Animals gather along its sides, but cannot attach there for long. The needle may thicken, melt, or vanish at its base and drift loose through the room. One lies caught among the upper chambers now, turning almost imperceptibly as the current passes.

At the darkest end of the hollow, there is still a patch of bare floor. Sediment is gathering along one edge. Two clear chambers have anchored beside it, each with a small animal working inside. Between them, a passage is beginning to hold its shape.

For the moment, water goes through.
