The first thing you notice is how far below you the birds are.

They wheel beneath the parapets in bright, loose companies, crossing the faces of buildings whose lower stories disappear into haze. Now and then one rises past your feet with something green in its beak. Beyond it, other terraces stand at different heights: a garden level, a roof of white metal, a row of chimney pots, then another garden, each partly obscuring the next. The view extends for miles, but almost nowhere does it reach an unobstructed horizon. There is always another planted balcony, another bridge, another great wall with trees leaning out of it.

The district occupies the summit of a constructed mountain. Its foundations lie somewhere below the cloud layer, at an altitude nobody up here has reason to visit. Everything underfoot has been poured, fitted, riveted, or packed into place. The cliffs are retaining walls. The gullies are service channels worn smooth by centuries of runoff. What looks from a distance like a broken ridge is the roofline of an old generating station, its successive additions rising toward the sun.

Even the loose brown material caught around tree roots is manufactured: crushed brick, spent charcoal, ground ceramic, fiber from the mills, all softened by generations of fallen leaves.

It has the smell of earth now.

At this height the air ought to be cold, but heat comes through the structure. Warm pipes run beneath the paths. Ventilation grilles exhale into hedges. The largest trees grow above machine halls, where their roots spread over thick beds insulated from the hotter chambers below. On a still morning, the district smells of warm iron, bruised leaves, and the slightly sour water standing in drain traps.

The sunlight is fierce. White roofs and pale paving send it upward under leaves, so that even the shaded passages have a green luminosity. People have stretched woven awnings between buildings, but vines weigh them down. Some awnings have disappeared altogether beneath flowering creepers; you can locate their old edges by the straight line of blossoms hanging above the street.

There is little open water. A few long reservoirs occupy terraces where buildings once stood, their sides too steep for reeds except in baskets fastened to the masonry. More often water appears briefly: a sweating pipe, a dark patch below a valve, droplets gathered along the underside of a footbridge. Thin rain arrives from clouds snagged against the lower levels, sometimes while the upper gardens remain in full sunshine. You can stand at a railing and watch a shower falling across a neighborhood a hundred yards beneath you.

Most of the vegetation lives on these small provisions.

Every ledge has a catchment lip. Roofs slope toward planted troughs. Condensation is gathered from cooling chambers and sent through shallow channels that can be opened with a wooden lever. In the morning, gardeners walk the channels, lifting screens clogged with petals and feathers. They know which beds can wait another day and which need their allotment before noon. A tree with a spreading crown may be fed by a trickle no wider than a finger, running somewhere inside the wall.

The result is lush but uneven. Broad leaves crowd the warm outlets. Ferns fill recesses around inspection doors. Flowering trees cast dense shade over certain courtyards, while the passage next to them is dry enough to whiten a dropped leaf in an afternoon. In places the greenery is layered so closely that it hides the depth beneath it: creepers over railings, shrubs in balcony beds, taller crowns rising from the terrace below. A gap between leaves may reveal not a patch of ground but a hundred feet of open air.

Nothing grows entirely unattended. Even the apparently wild growth depends on a basket, a seam, a pocket deliberately left in the masonry. Workers prune roots away from expansion joints and replace corroded planting cages. Old trees are braced with beams whose paint has blistered in the sun. Their trunks swell around the collars, and small lizards sleep in the warm gap between bark and metal.

There are animals everywhere.

Swifts nest behind loose flashing. Doves gather around the grain elevators and settle on moving conveyor housings with the ease of creatures accustomed to their speed. Bees occupy hollow balustrades; each colony has a little numbered plate beside its entrance, because somebody must remember not to weld there. Green beetles turn over wet mulch in the planters. Monkeys cross the district along pipe racks, pausing to look down through workshop skylights. At night, small pale geckos emerge around the lamps and remain nearly motionless until insects come within reach.

The larger animals know the maintenance routes. There are gates in improbable places, high on walls or halfway along suspended stairs, to keep them out of fan chambers and cooling ducts. A broken latch can leave a herd of little horned goats browsing an entire avenue of ornamental shrubs. Their bells become part of the industrial noise until someone comes to lead them home.

The sound is substantial, though seldom abrupt. Fans revolve deep inside the mountain. Pumps send a slow thudding through the pavement. Somewhere a belt squeals for a few seconds at each revolution; elsewhere water knocks through a pipe before settling into a steady rush. Voices carry along enclosed walkways. Birds call over the machinery, and the machinery continues beneath their calls.

Near the generating station, conversation requires a raised voice. Two streets away, behind a wall thick with climbing figs, you can hear bees working a row of flowers.

Much of the place has been repaired so often that its original shapes are difficult to read. Stairs have shallow dips in their centers. Handrails shine where palms have passed, then vanish into rough sleeves of oxidation. Runoff has cut grooves into soft mortar, and those grooves have been lined with tile, cracked, and lined again. Some bridges are supported by arches added beneath arches, each generation carrying the older one.

The district changes slowly. A pump is taken out of service, and the adjoining garden cools over several seasons. Leaves become smaller there. A broad, sun-loving tree dies back, allowing another tree to expand beneath it. A new heat exchanger opens farther east, and gardeners begin moving tender plants toward that quarter.

Such changes are noticed. People remember when a particular stair was always damp, when swallows nested in a vent that is now screened, when the upper orchard first reached across the gap to the mill roof. They measure time by replacements as much as by growth.

Walking here involves repeated adjustments to height. A level street ends at a stair. The stair opens onto a market built over an engine room. Beyond the market, a narrow bridge crosses a shaft so deep that its bottom is invisible even at noon. Ferns descend partway down the shaft in rings of suspended beds. Below the last of them, lights mark a maintenance landing; below that, only a faint mechanical hum rises through the warm air.

At the bridges, people tend to move toward the middle. The railings are sound, but the drop remains persuasive.

By late afternoon, the west-facing metal is too hot to touch. Workers lower shutters over the brightest windows. The upper gardens release the smell of sun-warmed leaves, and birds settle in the cooler foliage beneath them. Long shadows stretch from one level to another, skipping the intervening air.

Far below, cloud thickens against the structure. It conceals the lower towers first, then the undersides of bridges. For an hour, the district appears to float on a white plain. Only the descending cables suggest anything beyond it.

Along the upper walkways, the gardeners begin the evening round. They open small valves. Water passes from trough to trough with a quiet, practical splashing, scarcely audible over the fans. Leaves tremble where it reaches them. A few drops escape through an old joint and fall toward a roof below, where a pigeon steps aside, then returns to the same warm patch of metal.
