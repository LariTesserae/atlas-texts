The city lies high enough that the clouds sometimes pass through its lower floors, but there are no peaks around it. Whatever mountains lifted this country have long since worn down. The horizon is a low, continuous curve, interrupted by the swollen foundations of distant districts and the pale chimneys of pumping stations. On clear days you can see across three valleys, though none looks deep enough to deserve the name. They are shallow depressions in a vast, weathered surface, like places where something heavy once rested.

Everything underfoot is alive.

The ground resembles gray cork, close-grained and slightly yielding. Its fissures have rounded lips. Where traffic has worn it smooth, it shows the dark sheen of old wood handled for generations; where nobody walks, thin silver leaves lie pressed against it, so tightly overlapping that they seem to be its scales. There is no exposed rock, no soil, no grit blowing along the streets. What looks like dust is dry pollen. What looks like gravel is a scatter of dormant buds.

Vegetation does not grow *on* this plateau. Vegetation is the plateau.

That does not make it lush. There are no crowded forests, no green walls tangled with flowers. The living surface extends for miles between widely spaced structures, keeping its leaves small and its moisture deep inside. A person crossing one of the outer squares might walk twenty minutes without passing anything taller than an ankle. Yet the square itself is a single plant organ, old beyond counting, and the streets are narrow seams where neighboring growths have learned to separate.

The city has been persuaded out of this material.

Its buildings rise as hollow trunks, most of them broad and low, with windows grown in regular courses around their circumference. Their outer skins are pale, fibrous, and worn almost featureless by centuries of wind. Some retain the ridges where branches were once trained into balconies. Others have settled so far that their upper stories are now the only stories, a ring of doorways opening onto ground that slowly rose to meet them.

Between districts lie enormous plazas. Their emptiness is intentional. It gives the foundations room to breathe, keeps the older structures from sharing diseases, and leaves space for a building to lean without bringing down its neighbors. Along the margins are drainage grooves, tramways, inspection paths. From a distance, the city looks scarcely inhabited. Close up, every long gap has markings: a line of pale buds indicating a buried conduit, stakes showing how far a root may advance this season, small doors set into the ground for maintenance crews.

The tram rails are paired lengths of black heartwood, polished by wheels. They cross the squares in long, direct lines, vanishing into the low haze. Tramcars are grown around reusable molds and fitted with engines that burn dried sap. Their wheels have replaceable wooden teeth. Their exhaust smells faintly of apples scorched in a pan.

There are factories, although at first you might mistake them for groves of dead trees.

Each is a stand of tall, hollow columns joined below the surface. Inside, pistons lift and fall in cylinders kept smooth by patient layers of living tissue. Steam passes through bundled vessels. Broad belts of woven fiber transmit power from one shaft to another, and the belts need feeding along their return runs. Workers carry buckets of a dark solution to the places where they pass close to the floor.

The machinery is loud. It has to be. A healthy engine knocks hard enough to be heard across several streets, and the superintendent listens for it through the wall of the office. A softened note means that a cylinder is swelling. An extra click may be a new shoot catching on a belt.

Across the plateau, presses hammer, ventilators bellow, and saw frames make a high, relentless rasp. Beneath those sounds is the slow creak of the foundations shifting weight. There are hours when it seems the whole city is grinding its teeth.

Then a machine stops, and the sudden hole in the noise reveals smaller things: a window membrane tapping against its frame, dry leaves sliding over a plaza, drops falling inside a drain.

Water arrives intermittently. For several days the air may be clear and cool, the light so ordinary that every balcony casts a sharp shadow. Then a bank of cloud rolls over the western rim and enters the streets. Condensation beads on the buildings. The gray ground darkens. Little cups that had looked like scars open along the tramways and catch the dripping water.

It rarely rains hard. The city takes most of its water from mist, drawing it down through fine roots that emerge at night and withdraw before the tram service begins. There are public cisterns in the deepest folds of the plateau. Each holds only a few feet of dark water beneath a roof of interlaced leaves. Nothing grows visibly around them. They are guarded because a careless incision in a cistern wall can drain a whole neighborhood.

The great living mass survives this dryness by being very slow.

A square may take a human lifetime to put out a new layer of tissue. Some of the oldest buildings have not grown noticeably for centuries. They are still alive, but narrowly: a thin wet boundary beneath a thickness of spent fiber. Window tenders scrape and irrigate that boundary each spring. They know where the doors must be discouraged from closing and where a wall can be allowed to thicken.

Age has rounded everything. Stair treads sag in the middle. Towers end in blunt caps. Even the youngest factory is built with curved corners, because straight edges do not last well here.

The wind is cold enough to keep the workers in heavy coats, even in strong sunlight. At midday, the pale plazas reflect so much light that people squint crossing them. By evening, their hands are stiff. There is little stored warmth in the buildings unless an engine is running inside.

Animals are uncommon. A few small, white-bodied insects live in the warm joints of the power houses. Once or twice a season a bird arrives from below the plateau and spends a day moving between rooftops, apparently unable to find anything it recognizes. Its call carries remarkably far when the factories pause. People come to their windows to listen, then ask one another which roof it is on.

The stranger difficulties of the place tend to emerge during routine work.

A pressure gauge is fitted to a pumping station, tested, sealed, and entered in the maintenance book. The next morning, there are two gauges. Both carry the same serial number. Both show a plausible pressure. Disconnecting either one makes the other stop.

A new doorway sometimes opens into a room that already belongs to another district. For a few days, the inhabitants can step through and find themselves miles from home. Freight handlers hurry to make use of it. Then the room begins to narrow, or the floor develops a slope, and the doorway is boarded over before anyone is trapped.

The city surveyors work in pairs. One carries the instruments; the other remembers the route. Distances are not entirely stable here. A street can remain the same length when measured from either end and still acquire an extra block in the middle. Usually this happens after a long dry spell. The added block is old, complete with worn steps and tenants who insist they have lived there for years.

There are procedures for such events. Deliveries are suspended. Water access is checked. The new addresses are entered provisionally in the directory. Nobody argues with the tenants.

The oldest districts are the least reliable. Their passages descend through layers of abandoned architecture: tram sheds squeezed flat, rooms whose ceilings have become floors, workshops still connected to shafts turning somewhere far above. A stairway may take you down for an hour and bring you out into afternoon sunlight at the edge of town. Returning by the same stairway seldom works.

For that reason, repair crews carry enough food for several days, and apprentices are taught never to leave their coats at the entrance.

At dusk the factory windows glow amber through their thin inner skins. Trams move across the empty squares with a clattering that can be heard long before they appear. Vapor gathers around the exhaust stacks, briefly outlined by the lamps, then tears away into the cold.

Beyond the last buildings, the plateau continues in low, silver folds. Far out, a few inspection posts stand over buried roots large enough to carry water between towns. Their warning bells sound when pressure rises. Sometimes they ring in sequence, allowing the night watch to follow the movement of water for miles.

After midnight, most of the engines stop. The ground remains warm beneath the tram sheds and hardens slightly in the open squares. Condensation begins to collect.

Near morning, in a district where all the presses have been shut down, one starts knocking again. The watchman walks out across the long yard to check it. Through the factory windows he can see the belt hanging slack, the flywheel motionless.

He waits beside the wall until the sound finishes, then notes the hour in his book.
