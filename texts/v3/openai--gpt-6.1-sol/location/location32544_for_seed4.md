The town is inside a bud that has not opened.

It grows on the underside of a fallen branch, a finger’s width above the black water. From outside, there is little to distinguish it from the other buds along the wood: a swollen green shape, its scales closed tightly against the cold. One scale has lifted at the lower seam. This is the harbor entrance. Water enters there in small, irregular pushes, and the inhabitants keep a comb of root fibers across it to catch anything too large to be allowed inside.

There is no stone in the town. Even the hard things are plant.

The ground is the inner surface of the bud, pale tissue thickened into ridges. It rises toward the walls and sinks at the center, where a shallow pool occupies what will eventually become the flower’s throat. Streets follow the veins. Their surfaces are scraped smooth with flakes of seed husk, and at crossings the veins have been pinched to make steps. Everything still carries sap. A freshly cut stair is wet for several hours; a doorway, unless regularly trimmed, begins to close.

The town is young enough that the marks of its making remain sharp. No foot has hollowed the steps. The little drainage channels retain the exact width of the jaw that cut them. On the waterfront, several houses are still recognizably the folded leaf lobes from which they were made, their edges pegged together with thorns. Farther uphill, older dwellings have grown into one another. Their residents share a wall that occasionally thickens overnight and has to be pared back in the morning.

Morning is a word for a change in temperature.

There is almost no light here. The scales admit none, and the harbor seam faces down toward water shaded by the branch. Occasionally, something luminous swims past outside, and a thin blue line appears beneath the root comb. Everyone near the waterfront pauses. The line moves across the wet floor, catches on a doorway, vanishes. The town has a small vocabulary for these appearances, distinguishing the broad, steady passage of a luminous swimmer from the brief green flash of something being eaten.

Most work is done by touch.

Guide cords run along the streets, each knotted according to its destination. The market cord has five close knots, the cistern cord a long smooth interval followed by a loop. A cord leading to a dangerous place is left damp with bitter sap. Public notices are cut into soft pith and hung at the junctions, where anyone passing can feel them. Some are ordinary: a bridge closed, a new clutch hatched, a request for help moving an egg. Others concern the growth of the town itself. A familiar passage has narrowed. A room has become warmer. The floor under the northern ward has begun to separate into petals.

The inhabitants are animals, although at this scale the distinction between citizen and wildlife is less secure than they would like.

There are jointed creatures with transparent bellies, pale mites carrying their eggs beneath them, flat grazers that move like scraps of living skin. Slender many-legged animals work the upper streets, where the slope would defeat the round-bodied residents. A colony of nearly invisible worms lives in the public cistern and is tolerated because it eats the film from the water. Above the cistern, tiny hunters hang from threads. Beneath it, smaller hunters wait in the seams.

Almost every animal carries others.

Under a translucent shell, a cluster of still smaller bodies shifts when its host turns. Fine fur shelters whole grazing populations. A single fallen egg can feed a neighborhood of scavengers, some of whom will never encounter anything larger than the egg. The town’s quiet spaces are crowded with life too small to trouble its occupants. A room may have no resident and still contain several hundred feeding mouths.

Yet the place feels spare. Broad stretches of living floor lie between the houses. A guide cord crosses an empty slope without branching. The market consists of three low platforms around a patch of smooth tissue, with enough room between them to turn while carrying a reed. The inhabitants avoid building across every available surface. They need places where condensation can gather and move, where the walls can swell without crushing a roof, where a predator can be heard approaching.

Cold makes silence possible.

For long intervals the animals barely move. They press themselves into folds or gather beneath the walls of occupied houses, taking what little warmth passes through the plant. The leaf tissue is cold enough to numb an exposed limb. Water beads on every ceiling but falls slowly, each drop enlarging until its weight overcomes its hold. Below a favored drip stands a basin made from half a seed coat. A worker waits beside it with a hooked grass fiber, ready to draw the water away if the basin fills.

A drop falling from the roof is an event. It strikes with a soft, heavy sound and flattens into a lens. Streets disappear beneath it. Animals caught at its margin pull against the surface with all their legs, while others approach to drink. The drainage crews cut a narrow groove into the floor, and the drop gradually lengthens toward the harbor. It takes part of the day to restore the crossing.

Elsewhere, water climbs.

It creeps along braided fibers, enters wall joints, swells the wooden pegs. The town’s builders understand this more reliably than they understand distance. A house may be safe ten body lengths above the pool but flood because someone has laid a damp cord from its threshold to the shore. Every inhabited building has a dry break in its foundations: a little air gap crossed by a removable twig. At the onset of heavy condensation, these bridges are lifted. Neighbors remain close enough to touch across the gaps but cannot visit until the floors dry.

The central pool is dark, shallow, and almost always moving.

Its surface dimples under walking animals. Below them, larvae browse the submerged walls, leaving pale tracks where they scrape away soft growth. Some tracks widen into tunnels. Water then enters the tissue and brings out a sweetness that draws more grazers, until a whole section of bank loosens and floats. The shore ward spends much of its time anchoring these pieces with root ropes. Occasionally, a piece is deliberately cut free and used as a raft.

The rafts are poled with dry needles. They carry building fibers, eggs, pieces of edible lining, and passengers who cannot manage the steep streets. Their owners keep them tethered well apart. Anything touching the living wall for too long begins to adhere to it; by the next cold interval, a neglected raft may have become a new ledge.

Predators use the water as well. A narrow-bodied hunter can pass beneath the harbor comb and remain motionless among its roots. It waits until the town warms a little and traffic resumes. Then there is a brief disturbance: a cord shaking, a raft turning, several bodies scattering over the pool. Afterward the hunter returns to stillness. The crossing remains closed until someone locates it by lowering a fiber into the water and feeling where the fiber catches.

There are larger dangers above.

In the ceiling lives an animal too large to fit through any street. Most of its body is concealed between the scales, but one hooked limb reaches down into the town. It has occupied the same fold for so long that houses have been built outside its reach. The edge of that reach is marked with small thorn posts. Newcomers sometimes mistake the posts for a boundary fence.

Now and then the limb moves. Its passage makes a faint scratching sound overhead, and the nearest neighborhoods withdraw into doorways. It takes whatever has strayed close enough, then folds back into the roof. No one has seen the whole animal. There are workers who spend their lives in its neighborhood, cutting gutters and repairing cords, and know it only as a pressure through the ceiling and the occasional appearance of a claw.

The town’s own activity comes in pulses. A slight warming of the branch wakes the grazers first. Their scraping can be felt through the floor. Then the carriers emerge, the raft owners push away from their moorings, the market platforms fill. Fresh plant tissue is traded in damp curls. Thorn points are tested against the seller’s shell. A builder fits a strip of husk over a leaking seam, holding it in place until the sap begins to bind.

Later, the cold returns. Those who are far from home shelter where they can. An unfinished roof sags with water. On a public platform, a pile of cut fibers freezes lightly at the tips.

Meanwhile the bud continues to grow.

Its inner ridges separate by imperceptible degrees. New folds appear behind the houses. One season’s narrow storage cavity becomes large enough for a workshop; another disappears as the tissues press together. Surveyors visit the outer wards carrying lengths of root marked with bites. They measure from fixed thorns, record the changes in pith, and argue over how soon a move will be necessary.

They have already abandoned a district near the top. Its floor became thin and flexible, lifting whenever the outer air stirred. The empty dwellings are still there, their roofs pulled oblique by growth. Small animals have entered them through cracks no citizen could use. In the darkness, they eat the lashings and leave the walls standing.

At the very center of the pool, a pale column is rising. It was no higher than the waterfront steps when the town was founded. Now its tip reaches above the lowest guide cord. The water around it is warmer, and densely crowded with swimmers. Their movement keeps that part of the pool clear while the margins gather film.

Nobody builds on the column. Crews clear away the debris that catches at its base, and boats give it room. It grows whether the town is busy or sleeping.

One day the scales will loosen enough to admit light. The floor will unfold, the central pool will drain, and these close streets will be exposed to the air beneath the branch. The inhabitants have begun storing dry fibers near the harbor. Some have already moved into the neighboring bud, crossing outside during the stillest part of the cold.

For now, the entrance remains a narrow seam over black water. Inside, a worker follows a knotted cord toward a dripping roof. Larvae turn slowly against the submerged walls. The great concealed animal adjusts its grip. A new doorway is being cut, and beside it a thin shaving of green tissue curls away from the living wall.
