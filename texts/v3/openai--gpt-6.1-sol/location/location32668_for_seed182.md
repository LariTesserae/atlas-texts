The neighborhood lies one level beneath the city, in a hollow that was not there forty years ago.

It is small enough that, from the uppermost landing, you can see nearly all of it: six narrow terraces descending toward a central court, a roof of dark stone overhead, and the thick supports left standing when the excavation crews took out everything else. But you cannot see the ground. Buildings, leaves, pipes, hanging wash, and smaller buildings occupy the space between the terraces. The hollow has been filled with such care that even its emptiness has tenants.

The stone is still young in its exposure. Behind the houses, where no one has plastered or tiled it, the walls retain the angular scars of cutting teeth. Pale mineral seams end abruptly at drill holes. A few corners are sharp enough to slice a cloth drawn across them. There are no rounded boulders, no old ravines, no deep accumulations of soil. The little earth that exists was carried down in sacks or made here from kitchen waste, shredded packing fiber, and the slow decay of roots.

Heat comes through the floor.

You notice it first through the soles of your shoes, then in the railings, then in the faint warm breath rising from gaps between the paving tiles. Below this hollow are the city’s older service chambers: buried boilers, electrical transformers, conduits that were too expensive to move. Their waste heat has collected here for decades. Even at night, when the upper streets cool, the neighborhood remains hot. People sleep near the stair openings. They leave their doors ajar, though a door opening onto another room’s window admits very little breeze.

The machinery is mostly elsewhere. What reaches this place is a steady, almost inaudible pressure: a tremor in a cup touching a wall, a loose screw that turns slightly in its hole over the course of a week. The exposed equipment moves slowly. Near the eastern terrace, a flywheel taller than a person makes one revolution every eleven minutes. It drives a ventilation bellows through a series of reduction gears, all enclosed behind oily glass. A faded sign asks residents not to hang plants from the mechanism. Roots have entered through the bottom of the enclosure anyway.

There is greenery everywhere, but almost none of it grows where it began.

Vines emerge from wall cupboards and travel along power lines. Ferns crowd the gutters of houses with no exposure to rain. Waxy, broad-leaved plants occupy little balconies too shallow for a chair; their leaves overlap those on the balcony opposite, so that the lane beneath has a roof of green. Thick roots descend beside stair rails. Some have been wrapped in cloth where people grip them. Others have been fitted with brass collars and numbered, because they have become important to the stability of the smaller buildings.

Light enters through three shafts. At noon, each lays a thin, pale rectangle across a different part of the neighborhood. The shafts were meant to be wider, but the surface city built over their edges, and the plants have narrowed them further. Most leaves turn toward these openings. On the lowest terrace, where direct light never reaches, they turn instead toward a row of old nursery lamps hung above a communal table. The lamps are amber and greenish white. Their casings are crusted with minerals, and their light is so weak that the faces below them remain indistinct.

Much of the planting is edible, though not immediately recognizable as food. There are bitter greens with stems as thick as fingers, tubers grown in stacked drawers, and climbing beans trained up the ventilation ribs. Fruit appears infrequently. When it does, people support it in little net hammocks so its weight will not tear the vine away from a pipe.

The place smells of warm leaves, iron, cooking oil, and plaster drying too slowly.

Water arrives unevenly. A roof leak may run for three days and then stop for a month. Condensation beads on the few cold pipes descending from above, and every such pipe has a collar, a funnel, and a vessel underneath it. The vessels range from glazed cisterns to cut-open detergent cans. Nobody lets a drip fall freely if it can be caught.

After rain on the surface, water takes hours to find its way down. It appears first as dark patches in the stone ceiling. Later, individual drops gather on the fresh-cut ridges and fall through the leaves. For a while the whole neighborhood becomes faintly audible: water tapping sheet metal, pattering into soil, striking a stretched scrap of canvas. Residents move pots into the lanes. Children hold cups beneath the most dependable cracks.

By the time the dripping reaches the central court, it has passed through so many roots and roofs that it is warm.

The court contains a shallow basin, roughly the size of a room, surrounded by tiled edges and the backs of buildings. Most days there is only a handspan of water in it. Pale roots spread through the basin from planting boxes along its rim. They are kept trimmed away from a submerged intake grate, a task performed by an old woman who comes down every afternoon with scissors and a low stool.

There are no fish. Occasionally a small translucent snail appears on the tiled wall above the waterline. No one knows where they lay their eggs. Once, years ago, a moth came down through a light shaft and remained in the neighborhood long enough to appear in several family photographs. Insects are otherwise uncommon. The plants have been selected for forms that do not require them: greens that reproduce from cuttings, beans pollinated by hand, fruiting vines whose flowers are gently brushed each morning.

People do not keep birds here. There is too little room above the leaves.

The buildings are narrow, dense, and carefully adapted to inconveniences. A bakery shares its hottest wall with a communal bath. The bath’s exhaust passes through a drying cupboard before entering the ventilation duct. A repair shop stands on short legs over the main drain, and its floorboards lift in numbered sections when the drain needs clearing. One apartment is entered through the storeroom of another, by longstanding agreement. A tiny square of bare stone behind the western stair serves as the neighborhood’s cool room, though it is only cool in comparison.

Everything has more than one use. Stair treads are hinged over seed drawers. The handrails carry water. The thicker support pillars contain niches for shrines, tool storage, and sleeping children during the hottest afternoons. At several points the lane runs directly through a house; there, the paving changes to tile, and visitors lower their voices even further.

Mostly, the hollow is quiet.

Its inhabitants have padded the metal doors, wrapped the chain drives, and laid rubber beneath the pumps. Noisy work is done aboveground or in a small insulated workshop on the top terrace. Down here, speech carries too easily. A spoon dropped beside the basin can be heard from every landing, so people move with the caution that comes from sharing a wall, a ceiling, and a little air with many others.

You hear the bellows drawing in, very slowly. A leaf rubs against a window. Somewhere, behind a curtain, someone turns a page.

The stranger qualities of the place are easy to miss at first.

The roots run warm even where they leave the heated stone. On some evenings they are warmer than the pipes they embrace. Residents say the plants store the day’s heat, although the nursery lamps give off very little, and there is scarcely any day down here to store.

More noticeably, the mineral seams in the walls seem to continue into the plants. A white band in the stone meets a root and becomes a pale stripe along its bark. Higher up, the same stripe follows a stem into a leaf, where it ends as a row of hard, translucent dots. These leaves are not harvested. They are allowed to grow until they stiffen, then used as shingles over small leaks.

The seams also cross repairs. A crack plastered over in spring will sometimes show its old mineral line again by winter, as if the wall has slowly worked it through the new surface. The residents date this process with pencil marks. They do the same where roots press against doorframes, or where a tread has begun to tilt. There are decades of measurements in some houses, written beside one another in different hands.

Change here is usually small enough to accommodate. A sill is shaved down. A pipe bracket is loosened. A landing is raised by the thickness of a tile. The neighborhood adjusts gradually to the stone and plants, and they adjust to it.

At the bottom of the hollow, beside the basin, a passage disappears behind a curtain of heavy leaves. It is only a few steps long. At its end stands a door made from fresh timber, swollen tightly into its frame. Beyond it is a room that has never been occupied, excavated at the same time as the rest but left unfinished when a mineral seam began shedding heat.

The room is hotter than anywhere else in the neighborhood. Its floor is bare, its walls sharply cut. On the ceiling are rows of small green shoots growing directly out of the stone.

Once a month, someone opens the door to look. There is no lamp inside, so they bring one. They count the shoots, measure the longest, and check for water. So far the room has remained dry.

Then they close the door and return through the leaves, past the basin and up the crowded stairs. The hollow settles around them: warm railings, dim windows, pots placed beneath silent cracks. High overhead, the narrow shafts retain a little surface light. By the time it reaches the lowest terrace, the lamps have already come on.
