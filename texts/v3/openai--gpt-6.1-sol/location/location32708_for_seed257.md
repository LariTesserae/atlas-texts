The water reaches almost to the crown of the vault. A narrow pocket of air remains there, caught between the rounded ceiling and the surface: warm, stale, too shallow to sit up in. Once in a long while, a drop gathers on the ceiling and falls into it. The small sound travels surprisingly far.

Below that pocket, the place is full.

It is a chamber about the size of a village meeting hall, built at the summit of a much larger construction. Beyond its walls, flights of stone steps descend for hundreds of terraces into darkness. The chamber stands higher than the weather usually reaches. On the rare occasions when its upper vents clear, clouds can be seen moving below the sill.

But from inside the water, elevation is felt mostly as an absence. No soil washes in. No river brings leaves or seeds. Nothing drifts down from a bank. Whatever arrives must have come up through the masonry, or crossed the high threshold from the narrow causeway outside.

There is no natural floor here.

The bottom is laid in fired-clay plates, each originally small enough for one person to carry. Thick pillars rise from it, supporting arches assembled from short wedges of stone. The wedges were shaped with blows rather than sawn. In sheltered joints, their old tool marks survive as rows of shallow dimples. Everywhere else, the work has been softened by water until it resembles something grown.

The pillars have lost their corners. Stair treads are shallow scoops. A low partition, perhaps once waist-high, is now a ridge with a broad, polished back. Where water passes through a doorway, the jambs have worn inward together, making the opening look gently pinched.

There are no sharp edges left except those made recently by a break.

The age of the place is difficult to grasp because so little happens in it. Water moves, but never with the force of a stream. It rises through a pair of clay-lined shafts in the floor, warm enough that the stone around their mouths is pleasant to touch. Then it spreads through the room and leaves through slots beneath the far wall. The shafts descend into the heat of the immense masonry beneath the summit. Their linings have been replaced in short sections over many generations; the oldest pieces are thicker, less even, and darkened all the way through.

Everything visible could have been made with a hammer, a wooden gauge, a basket, and patient hands. There is no machinery hidden behind the walls. The water runs because its source is higher than its outlet. A slab lowered into a groove regulates the passage. Its lifting holes are empty now.

The chamber is dark enough that distances have to be learned.

A little gray light enters through three openings above the waterline. It reaches the tops of the nearest pillars and dissolves before reaching the floor. There, the clay is almost invisible. You find it by the pale animals resting on it.

They are everywhere.

Small shrimp stand in the seams between plates, their bodies clear except for a soft white line of organs. Their antennae overlap until a joint looks fringed. Flat snails crowd the undersides of lintels. Blind fish, no longer than a finger, hold themselves in the warmer currents with tiny corrections of their fins. Some have rounded mouths for grazing the films on clay; others remain motionless beside the grazers until one comes close enough to swallow.

The largest animals are broad, pale crabs. They occupy the hollows beneath the steps, where the masonry has worn away faster than the tread above it. One old crab may fill such a hollow almost completely. Its shell is the color of soaked plaster, with brown staining at the joints. Young ones shelter beneath it without any obvious permission.

There are no plants, not even a thread of green at the light openings. The life begins in thin, colorless films around the warm shafts and along seams where mineral-rich water seeps through the mortar. The films wrinkle slightly as they thicken. Shrimp comb them with their forelegs. Snails remove them in narrow tracks. In some places the animals keep the surface bare; in others a soft, cloudy skin hangs between adjacent stones.

Near the outlets, the floor is layered with shell fragments, shed skins, and fine powder worn from the building. This deposit is only a few fingers deep, but it contains so many small bodies that stirring it would release a dense pale cloud. Usually it is undisturbed. The fish feed above it. The crabs take what settles. Tiny worms thread through the spaces beneath the fragments.

Even the empty places are occupied at another scale.

The chamber has alcoves along both sides. Each contains a stone bench with a channel cut down its center and a round depression at one end. Whatever work was done here required water, or washing, or something to be held steadily while it was rubbed. The benches are submerged now. Their depressions make excellent nurseries. Shrimp larvae gather in them, nearly motionless, turning together when a larger animal passes.

In one alcove, the bench has split lengthwise. The two halves have settled less than a hand’s width apart. Shells have lodged in the gap. A crab has dragged other fragments there, and minerals have begun to bind them. The repair is rougher than the original work but already feels solid beneath a probing finger.

This happens throughout the room. Breaks fill. Open joints acquire crusts. A fallen stone becomes the foundation of a low heap, then disappears beneath it. The building is still losing material, slowly, but some of that material comes to rest close by. Its ruin is dense with additions.

At the far end is a stairway rising out of the water.

The bottom steps are broad and almost level from wear. Higher up, they become narrower, retaining more of their original shape. The last two enter the air pocket beneath the vault. They are damp rather than submerged, and their surfaces carry a dull salt bloom. No animal lives permanently on them. A snail sometimes climbs too far, pauses for hours at the wet boundary, and turns back.

Beyond the stair is a door of fitted slabs. It stands partly open onto a covered landing. There is no view from the chamber itself, only a paler rectangle and, occasionally, the slow passage of mist.

Outside, the high construction falls away in tiers. Small catchment courts, roofed channels, retaining walls, and empty rooms descend beyond sight. All are built of the same manageable pieces: clay plates, rough blocks, mortar pressed into joints by hand. The scale was achieved through repetition. A person could have made any one part. No person could have seen the whole work completed.

Up here, only this room still holds its water to the vault.

It does not feel abandoned in the ordinary sense. Too many bodies use every ledge and passage. The benches are grazed clean. The thresholds shelter fish. The drain slots are crowded with animals facing into the current, catching what comes toward them.

Nor is it busy. A crab’s journey from one stair hollow to the next may take most of the day. The fish advance a little, stop, and remain suspended. Changes in water level are measured against pale bands on the pillars that took centuries to form.

Sound is mostly touch transmitted through the building: the faint scrape of a shell against clay, a stone shifting somewhere far below, a dull tick when a claw closes. Sometimes a bubble escapes one of the warm shafts and rises along a pillar. It enlarges as it goes, slips beneath an arch, and vanishes into the shallow air above.

Then the room returns to its small motions.

Warm water passes over the old floor. Antennae sweep the softened joints. At the highest remaining step, the wet line advances by the thickness of a shell, stays there for several days, and slowly retreats.
