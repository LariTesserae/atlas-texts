The country begins above the clouds, but there is no summit.

There are escarpments, certainly: walls of dark stone dropping through miles of white weather, their upper edges so sharp that snow cannot find a place to lie. There are ridges and enormous tilted slabs. Yet beyond each apparent crest, the land continues at almost the same height. Another wet plain opens. Another range rises out of it, still bearing the clean fractures of its emergence. The horizon is not a boundary so much as the point where the eye gives up distinguishing stone from air.

Underfoot, the ground looks recently made. Black glass runs in seams through pale, fine-grained rock. Some surfaces have the smoothness of a fresh break in pottery; others are jagged enough to cut a glove. There is little gravel. The cliffs have not shed the rubble that should lie beneath them. Cracks are full of water, but their lips remain square.

The cold is steady and penetrating. It enters through boot soles and settles against the teeth. Nothing is quite frozen. Shallow pools hold clear water over green bottoms, and every exposed metal rail wears a film of moisture. A drop placed on the back of a hand feels colder than ice without becoming ice itself.

Vegetation occupies the sheltered places densely. In the fissures grow low, dark cushions of leaves, each leaf thick and polished, overlapping its neighbors like the scales of a closed pinecone. Pale root fibers lace the wet stone. Along the broader terraces, a growth like reeds stands waist-high, although its stems have no joints and its narrow blades branch repeatedly near the tips. It forms immense soft fields, gray-green in daylight, almost black when viewed against the sky.

Walk into one of these fields and it closes behind you. The blades are cold enough to numb your wrists. They do not smell sweet or rotten. Their smell is mineral, like a cellar cut into living rock.

There are no insects in them. No grazed leaves, burrows, nests, or tracks. The pools contain nothing that swims. On a distant slope, a dark shape can hold the eye for a long time, inviting the expectation that it will turn its head. It is a pipe outlet, or a boulder, or simply a gap in the vegetation.

The emptiness lies mostly above everything.

Between the high terraces are gulfs so wide that their opposite sides take on the faint blue of distant mountains. Clouds occupy only a small fraction of them. Most of the space is clear, cold air. A bridge thrown across one of these openings looks substantial at its anchorages, where steel members rise thicker than houses, but dwindles almost immediately to a thread. From the middle, neither end appears close enough to belong to the same construction.

There are cities here, though it is difficult to decide where any one city ends.

At long intervals the vegetation gives way to a station: a cluster of residential blocks, machine halls, water towers, and loading platforms set against a cliff. Covered streets connect the buildings. Their roofs are steep, ribbed metal; their lower walls are tiled in dull green ceramic that remains wet without staining. Staircases descend to terraces of pumps and climb to galleries cut into the rock.

A station may contain several thousand windows. Beyond it, a service road runs for a hundred miles through empty upland before reaching another lamp, another switchyard, another group of buildings. The road is carefully maintained. Its white edge markings are crisp. Water fills its drainage channels exactly to the bevel of the concrete.

Seen from sufficiently far away, all the stations are one city: a continental arrangement of narrow inhabited-looking knots joined by rails, cables, pressure mains, and bridges. Seen from within a station, the city consists of one street, some blank windows, and an immense amount of elsewhere.

The windows are lit. There is no movement behind them.

Inside the machine halls, the lights are brighter. Banks of generators occupy tiled floors. Their housings are painted cream, their inspection covers marked with numbers and small red arrows. Tools hang in outlines on boards beside the doors. A coat remains on a hook above a pair of dry boots. None of this appears abandoned. There is no dust, no corrosion, no sign of an interruption.

The machinery is deafening.

Even out on the reed plains, far from the nearest station, the sound comes through the ground: a low, massive vibration with several higher tones laid across it. At a bridge anchorage it becomes a roar. Within a pump hall it is too large to hear as one thing. The floor trembles, the railings tremble, and pressure passes through the chest with each apparent beat of the engines.

Yet the shafts do not turn.

One can stand at a guard window and inspect a flywheel for an hour. The same bolt remains at the top. A piston stays halfway through its stroke, its polished rod exposed. The great electrical buses emit their unwavering hum. Gauges indicate pressure. The cables carry heat into the walls.

The machines have reached some condition beyond running or stopping. They deliver the noise, warmth, and force of operation without completing another cycle.

Water behaves in the same way.

It falls from the high terraces in broad transparent sheets. In places the falls are miles tall, hanging against the dark cliffs like strips of torn glass. Close up, each sheet contains bubbles, folds, and thin white veins of spray. Those features remain exactly where they are. Put a finger into the water and the finger gets wet; withdraw it and the sheet is whole. The water makes a continuous crashing sound, but no mist drifts away from its base.

Below the falls lie pools with no worn basin beneath them. Water rests on the sharp original stone.

This is why the land still looks young. It has arrived at its present form and ceased to proceed. There has been no time for scree to gather or roots to pry apart the cliffs. The green growth is lush because it is held at fullness: no leaf has browned, no new shoot has displaced an old one. Moisture stands everywhere at the edge of freezing. The weather has all the apparatus of weather—cloud, condensation, light, falling water—but none of its departures.

There is ordinary daylight. Blue opens above the plateau, and sunlight strikes the upper windows and catches in the wet leaves. Far below, under the main cloud layer, the cliff faces fade into a white brilliance. The sun does not perceptibly cross the sky. Shadows rest beside the buildings with clean, unwavering edges.

A person can move here. That is perhaps the most unsettling fact.

Your footsteps happen. Your breath clouds the air and disappears. A door opens under your hand, and you can leave it open. But beyond these small disturbances the place offers no evidence that your afternoon has occurred. The same condensation bead hangs from the corner of a sign. The same bright crease remains halfway down a waterfall. Returning along a service road, you find the vegetation at the precise angle at which you first passed it.

At one station, an observation platform projects over the eastern gulf. It has a waist-high railing, two fixed benches, and a coin-operated telescope aimed at the opposite shore. Below it, a pipe as broad as a street leaves the cliff and descends out of sight. Its whole length sings.

From the platform, the opposite shore appears empty until the eye adjusts. Then windows emerge: tiny gold rectangles arranged in rows beneath a black overhang. Beside them stands a bank of silver tanks. Farther north, almost lost in the blue distance, another bridge reaches toward a landmass beyond sight.

All the way across the gulf, the water shines. The clouds retain their folds. The engines are loud enough to make the bench vibrate beneath you.

There is no birdcall to rise above them, and no interval in which to expect one.
