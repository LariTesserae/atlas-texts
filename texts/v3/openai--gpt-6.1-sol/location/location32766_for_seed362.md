From a distance, the plain looks upholstered: pale ribs running to the horizon, shallow depressions between them, a close white nap catching the light. There are no mountains. The highest features are long swellings, scarcely taller than a warehouse, that continue for hundreds of miles without breaking. Their rounded backs carry the same fine texture as the hollows.

Up close, the nap resolves into leaves.

Each is narrow, stiff, and jointed at the base, with a translucent edge and a chalk-colored face. They stand so closely together that there is nowhere to put a foot without bending them. Underneath lies a felt of smaller leaves, beneath that a mesh of branching stems, and beneath that further compressed growth: living layers laminated into a floor. A cut through it would show something like plywood, except that every layer would turn slowly toward the blade.

There is no soil.

Even the dark material lodged in the joints is manufactured pollen, worn fiber, and the powder of old conducting tissues. Roots enter other roots. The deepest ones are bolted into a foundation grown from the same stock as the surface, though its cells have become square and mineral-hard under pressure. Everything that bears weight is alive, or was alive, or is waiting to be made alive again.

The air is cool enough to sting the inside of the nose. It has the dry smell of cut stalks and electrical insulation. Moisture is almost absent. Nothing beads on the leaves, nothing trickles through the hollows. The occasional silver sheen on a stem is a bloom of metal salts.

These plants work without sap. Their tissues move nutrients as dust, charge, and thin gases through microscopic passages. A damaged branch gives off a little pale smoke. A severed root spills grains that roll uphill toward the nearest intact root until they are taken in.

The light is immense.

It arrives from the sky and from the ground, from leaf edges, from the undersides of things. Shadows survive only as faint gray seams. Above, a low, luminous ceiling is divided into panels too large to understand as panels until one has traveled for days beneath a single joint. The ceiling is botanical as well: a suspended canopy of broad leaves woven into load-bearing sheets. Their veins carry power. Their pale inner surfaces release the light absorbed elsewhere, on some exterior face no one here can see.

Occasionally a whole district brightens. The ground’s fine blades straighten; long shadows briefly appear where none were before; then the light settles back into its almost unbearable evenness. This is the nearest thing to a passing cloud.

At intervals across the plain stand the mills.

They are low structures, wider than towns, assembled from columns of densely bundled trunks. Their roofs resemble leaves pressed in books. Around them, huge belts of braided vine move through slots in the walls, dragging nothing visible. Some slide lengthwise; others rotate in place, exchanging their outside and inside surfaces as they pass around living pulleys. Vast wheels turn a few inches below the ground cover. You can see their motion in the procession of shallow bulges traveling through the leaves.

There is almost no noise.

The bearings secrete dry lubricating spores. Gears have soft teeth that harden only at the instant of contact. Exhaust enters the ceiling through hollow pillars and is separated there into useful elements. A mill can manufacture several square miles of new floor in a day with less sound than a hand drawn across cloth.

Closer still, there are little noises: a dry tick as a leaf unlocks, the faint crunch of a seed casing, a long thread slipping through an eyelet. At times these converge into a soft, local pattering. It travels past you in a band perhaps fifty feet wide, and the leaves behind it have changed their angle.

What the mills make is largely the place itself.

Inside their open bays, stems are extruded through ceramic mouths and trained immediately into joists, ducts, ladders, wall panels. Sheets of dormant leaf tissue pass between rollers, receive a pattern of veins, and wake as they emerge. Thick roots arrive braided and leave separated into cables. Along one wall, a row of flower heads opens and closes with absolute regularity, stamping serial marks into the bark moving beneath them.

The serial marks remain legible for a century or two. After that, the bark thickens over them.

Much of the plain is considerably older.

There are districts where generations of production have settled into broad, comfortable forms. Corners have rounded. Access trenches have narrowed into leafy seams. Once-identical columns lean at slightly different angles, and the mills accommodate them with curved drive shafts. Abrasion has polished the leaf floors into white paths. These paths do not indicate travelers so much as prevailing traffic: sheets of material dragged across them, maintenance growth extending and withdrawing, seasonal rearrangements of the ground.

In the older hollows, the leaves are short and almost transparent. You can look down through them into the moving layers underneath. Cables cross there. Pale nodules crawl slowly along roots, repairing worn insulation. A deep belt advances one notch, waits, advances again.

Standing still, you begin to notice how busy the silence is.

A seam closes beside your shoe. Far away, a roof lowers a fraction. Several acres of leaves reverse their grain, carrying dust toward a collection slot. A row of pillars contracts in sequence, pumping cold gas into another district. The plain has weather, though its weather seldom touches the sky: pressure fronts pass through the substrate, electrical seasons migrate beneath the floor, and great pollen drifts accumulate against the ribs.

Sometimes the pollen rises.

It hangs in sheets so thin that they are visible only where they interfere with the light. A sheet may extend from the floor to the ceiling and move steadily across the plain for weeks. Leaves open their edges as it passes, taking in a measured ration. Behind it, the surface acquires a faint greenish cast. Ahead of it, everything looks white and exhausted.

The coldest weather comes after a long period of flowering. So much energy has gone into making the airborne grains that the whole district cools. The joints stiffen. Vine belts slow. The ceiling lowers slightly, conserving heat, and the luminous air becomes close and hard. There is still no frost. The leaves fold their transparent margins over their faces, giving the ground a dull, quilted appearance.

The animals are easy to miss.

One might spend months here and see only a single narrow creature crossing between two maintenance slots. It has six legs, a body the color of old ivory, and a blunt head held close to the floor. It eats defective seeds. Its tracks disappear as soon as it lifts its feet, since the leaves close behind it.

There are also things that rest beneath the ceiling, flattened against the light panels. They resemble scraps of torn insulation. Once in a very long while one releases its grip and drifts down through the bright air, opening a pair of wings so delicately veined that it becomes almost invisible. The plants beneath it stop moving until it has passed.

No one knows whether this is deference, alarm, or an instruction older than the mills.

Distance is less dependable here than machinery.

You can walk toward a factory whose roof seems a mile away and arrive before your shadow has taken three steps. On another day, a nearby column stays beside you until evening, its pale trunk sliding along the horizon as you travel. Routes are reliable only when their conducting veins are warm. A cold route may preserve the length it had before the most recent expansion, or borrow length from an adjacent district.

The survey markers acknowledge this. They grow at crossings: stiff, rectangular leaves printed with destinations, production classes, and distances in three different states. One distance is for walking. One is for transporting material. The third is for returning.

The return distance is usually the largest.

There are places where manufacture continues beyond the space available for it. A corridor of pillars holds more pillars between its columns than can be seen from outside. A hollow under a low roof contains a horizon. Through inspection windows no wider than a hand, entire plantations can be observed leaning under their own separate weather.

The workers, if there ever were workers, designed doors into these places. The doors remain. They are green, heavy, and equipped with handles of polished root. Most open onto storage tissues packed solid with leaves. A few open onto a stretch of plain indistinguishable from the one behind you, except that all the machinery is moving a little faster.

Nothing announces these differences. No alarm sounds when a district folds inward. The floor adjusts its veins, the belts find their new pulleys, and production continues.

Far out in the oldest quarter, where the ceiling’s great seams have almost vanished under successive growths, the plain becomes gently corrugated. Broad ribs rise and fall. The mills there have no distinct walls; their mechanisms have spread into the surrounding vegetation, their roofs have joined the canopy, and their names survive only in clusters of seed heads bearing worn impressions.

Here the brightness is softer. Layer upon layer of translucent growth filters it into the color of bone held up to a lamp.

For a while you can believe that the whole place has finally come to rest.

Then a strip of ground several miles wide lifts along the horizon. It turns slowly, showing its dark, finely rooted underside. Below it is another white plain, fresh-made and already trembling with activity. The lifted surface rolls onward without a creak, feeding itself into some distant, concealed apparatus.

The new leaves begin to open before the old ones are gone.
