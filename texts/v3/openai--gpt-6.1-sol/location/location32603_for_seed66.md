From the rim, the city looks like another form of erosion.

It occupies the high basin in pale terraces, following the curves of rock so closely that streets and drainage channels are difficult to tell apart. Buildings have rounded shoulders, shallow roofs, walls darkened at their bases by water. Beyond them, across distances that turn the farther districts blue, stand other terraces and other cities. A train moving between them is visible first as a thread of smoke, then as a short black stroke, then not at all.

The basin is high enough for clouds to catch against its southern escarpment. Yet the air is warm. Warm water rises through the stone, gathers in the foundations, runs under the roads. On a clear morning the gutters steam while sunlight lies white and ordinary on the upper windows.

There are no trees.

There is nothing tree-shaped even in the vacant places: no scrub on the spoil heaps, no grass between rails, no moss in the gutters. An abandoned courtyard remains a courtyard of mineral dust, shell fragments, and damp masonry. A roof left untended acquires birds rather than weeds.

The oldest visible surfaces are limestone, although most have lost the crispness associated with broken rock. The basin has been wet for so long that its exposed stone resembles something handled. Stairways have sunk into broad scoops. Pillars wear narrow waists where people and animals pass. Along the edges of the city, cliffs descend in overlapping folds, their ledges softened, their fissures enlarged into chambers. Water appears halfway down a wall without any visible crack, beads there, and falls.

Some drops take several minutes to form.

Below the inhabited terraces lies a sequence of shallow lakes. They are not especially beautiful from close by. Their margins are gray, greasy with fine sediment, and crowded with bodies. Long-backed animals stand belly-deep in warm water. Small shelled things occupy every stone that protrudes above the surface. Birds cover the mud in such numbers that, when one flock lifts, another layer seems to remain beneath it.

From a distance, however, the lakes become silver plates. Sunlight catches on them between the industrial districts. Their glare reaches high into the houses.

The life of this place begins underground.

Beneath the basin, water moves through hot seams of sulfur and iron. Where that water meets cooler air, pale bacterial films grow across the stone. In some springs the films lie flat and slippery. In others they hang in thick folds, cream-colored at the edges and rust-red within. The city cultivates them in long covered galleries, regulating the water with sluices and letting air enter through low chimneys.

At the mouths of uncultivated springs, animals eat them directly.

There are snails as large as buckets, with chalky shells and soft black mouths. There are flat-bodied grazers that adhere to wet walls and move almost too slowly to be noticed. There are jointed scavengers that squeeze into drains, rasping clean the undersides of grates. Their tracks cross the thin mineral deposits like writing rubbed out before it can be read.

Much of the larger life feeds on these creatures, or on what the city discards.

The birds are the most immediately overwhelming. They occupy parapets, bridge cables, balconies, lamp standards, the backs of slow vehicles. White birds with blunt yellow bills gather around the warm outfalls. Narrow-winged gray ones nest in ventilation shafts. Heavy, dark birds wait beside slaughterhouses with the solemn patience of workers waiting for a gate to open.

A roof can hold several thousand and still look empty from the next district.

Their feathers collect in the channels. Their droppings crust the statues. Every public square has someone whose job is to walk slowly through it with a wheeled scraper.

There are animals at street level, too. Broad-footed, hairless creatures the size of oxen rest against the retaining walls, their hides the color of wet putty. They are domesticated only in the sense that people know which ones will move when touched with a pole. Most pull barges, turn low-speed capstans, or carry loads between stair landings that engines cannot reach. Others belong to nobody. They sleep in the warmth above buried pipes and rise reluctantly when a cart needs to pass.

Smaller animals live in the spaces around them: blunt-faced burrowers under paving slabs, red-legged scavengers in the market drains, translucent eels in the household cisterns. Some are pests. Some are food. Some remove enough waste to be protected by law, though this does not make anyone fond of finding them in a kitchen.

The city smells of wet stone, hot metal, salt, feathers, and the faint sourness of living tanks.

Its industry has been fitted into the basin rather than set against it. Factories occupy natural shelves. Their cooling ponds fill old depressions. Freight lines enter tunnels that began as water passages and were widened, one century at a time, to take larger wagons. In many places a conduit, a street, and a geological seam share the same course.

The machinery is substantial and slow.

Flywheels turn behind tall windows. Pump rods rise and descend in brick towers. Chains drag loaded pans through mineral baths. At the edge of the deepest lake, dredges work day and night, lifting a black sediment that is washed for metals and then spread in the feeding galleries. Their buckets emerge shining, empty with a heavy splash, and return.

A dredge may stay in the same reach for twenty years. Its crew can tell, from the color of a bucketful, which buried wall it has reached.

There is noise almost everywhere, but little of it suggests haste. Pumps thump through the foundations. Water strikes iron plates. Barges rub against their moorings. The birds make a coarse, continuous racket that strengthens around feeding time and settles afterward into scattered arguments.

Under those sounds is the movement of water through the city: a hiss in the wall beside a bed, a hollow rush under the market, a measured gulp from the overflow shaft in a courtyard. People learn to hear changes in it. A drain that has gone quiet may be more dangerous than one that has become loud.

The houses are built accordingly. Their lowest rooms are washable. Furniture stands on stone feet. Doors open a little above street level, and thresholds have grooves to carry seepage away. Many homes keep a narrow indoor channel running along one wall. Its water is warm enough to bathe a child, wash utensils, or keep a room comfortable after dark.

At intervals, the channels deposit pale scales. These are scraped away and carried to the municipal kilns. Much of the city is made from what its water leaves behind.

New masonry is sharp-edged and almost white. It looks strangely temporary beside older walls. Within a few decades its corners soften, its seams darken, and small ledges acquire their permanent allocation of shells and nesting birds. Repairs are judged partly by how well they will drain after a hundred years. The cheapest buildings are those that require dry foundations.

Across the upper basin, streets can be narrow and crowded, with windows nearly touching and overhead bridges blocking strips of sky. But the city never quite excludes the larger landscape. At the end of an alley there may be a lake extending past sight. Between two warehouses, an exposed cliff rises farther than the roofs. A tram emerges from a packed district onto an embankment so long that its next station seems to belong to another weather system.

Rain often falls in one quarter while the central terraces remain sunlit.

It comes from low clouds assembled over the warm lakes, or from higher banks pushed across the escarpment. The rain is usually gentle. It polishes roofs, fills worn hollows, and makes every road resemble a recently washed work surface. Afterward the air holds a close, luminous haze. Distant cranes become faint; nearby animals gleam.

There is little wind inside the basin. Out on the rims it blows steadily enough to keep the rock dry and the birds leaning. Those heights are bare, broad, and worn smooth. Survey towers stand there with their iron feet embedded in old concrete. From their platforms one can see the true scale of the place: not a single crowded valley, but a succession of high wet basins divided by low stone saddles, each with its lakes and settlements, each sending water toward an eventual outlet no one in the central cities ever sees.

At dusk, the industrial windows turn amber. Lamps come on along the towpaths. The pale animals beside the pipes are easier to see after the streets themselves darken.

Birds rearrange themselves on the roofs for a long time. The last barge passes under a bridge with its cargo covered against droppings. Workers close gates in the culture galleries, check the flow through the feeding beds, and leave the warm rooms to the grazers.

The pumps continue.

Over years, water lowers a sill, thickens a deposit, opens a passage behind a cellar wall. A terrace is rebuilt farther uphill. A lake gains a narrow strip of shore. Families remember a bridge as always having been there, though its middle pier has been moved twice in living memory.

In the oldest district, there is a stairway that descends directly into a pool. Its upper steps are still used, and their centers are deep with wear. Below the water, the steps continue, visible on bright days through a wavering skin of sediment.

Shells cling to them. Small fish pass over them. At noon, the sun reaches the lowest one that can still be seen.
