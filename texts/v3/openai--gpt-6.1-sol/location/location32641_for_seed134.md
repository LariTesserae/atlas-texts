The summit is underwater.

It stands high above the surrounding seabed, close enough to the surface that daylight arrives with most of its colors intact. Far below, the mountain descends into blue darkness; here, on its crown, a shallow hollow holds a cold, crowded world no larger than a village square. Three walls of black stone enclose it. The fourth has split open, leaving a narrow view of open water and, beyond that, another pale peak.

There is nowhere to stand in air. Even the highest tooth of rock has several fathoms of water above it. Waves passing overhead lay broad moving bands of brightness across the hollow, though almost none of their motion reaches this far down. The water is clear in the gaps and milky around the animals. It carries a taste of iron, were there anything here that tasted as we do.

The stone is young. Its fractures are sharp enough to part soft flesh, and the smaller splinters have not yet been rounded. Along the walls, fresh mineral growth outlines every crack: white rims, yellow beads, translucent blades standing edgewise. Some blades are longer than an arm and thin enough that their centers disappear when viewed straight on. Their edges remain visible, blue and bright.

No weed softens them. There are no trailing leaves, no grasslike fringes, no brown canopy moving in the current. What first looks like a sparse coating of moss resolves into thousands of tiny jaws, each set directly into a limestone bead.

The hollow is full of animals.

They occupy it in tiers, with scarcely a hand’s breadth left unused. Flat, pale creatures cover the sloping floor, their shells fitted together without quite touching. Above them hang leathery sacs on mineral stalks. Between the sacs, schools of small transparent fish hold position in facing rows. Larger bodies are wedged into fissures, so deeply anchored that only a rim of flesh or a revolving mouth shows.

At the center of the hollow, where the rock is stained orange, several enormous animals lie side by side. Each is the length of a small boat. They have low armored backs, six broad feet, and a fringe of jointed feeding limbs folded beneath the head. Their plates are a dull, pitted gray. Along their flanks are narrow openings from which water pours in steady jets.

They do not travel much. One may shift the placement of a foot over the course of a morning. Another may turn its head a few degrees between one tide and the next.

Inside them, things move very fast.

Through gaps in the armor, you can see wet ivory discs revolving on fleshy axles. Tendons pass continuously over their edges. A ring of toothlike nodules engages another ring, smaller and turning more rapidly, then vanishes into a dark chamber beneath the shell. The animals pump water through beds of living tissue that separate dissolved metals from the sea. Their undersides rest against the mountain’s mineral seams, drawing up the warm, bitter seepage that comes through the cracks.

Their bodies make fuel from the meeting of those two waters.

The whole hollow is loud with their work: a deep rolling clatter, a higher whine, the sharp knock of valves closing. Sometimes the rhythm of one animal settles into the rhythm of its neighbor, and the sound becomes a single dense chord. It passes through the stone and through every creature touching the stone. The transparent fish tremble in it. The hanging sacs draw their feeding fringes inward.

No structure has been built here. Nothing has been assembled by hands. Yet the place has the close-set organization, vibration, and continuous appetite of an engine room. The resemblance grows stronger the longer you remain.

The little shell animals around the central bodies collect their waste. Fine gray powder drops from the flank openings and is caught in fans of hair. What escapes the fans feeds the jaws on the walls. Fish graze the jaws, clipping them away in quick, almost invisible bites. The jaws regrow slowly around the same small holes.

Predators stand among this abundance with extraordinary patience. One resembles an open cage made from six curved ribs, each rib clothed in clear muscle. It is suspended beneath a stone overhang. A fish swims through it unharmed; another does the same. Hours may pass before the ribs close around a third. The movement is gentle and complete. Afterward, the animal opens again, with no visible change in its shape.

Elsewhere, thin silver creatures are packed vertically into a fissure, all facing outward. They have no eyes. A continuous line of red organs runs along each side, pulsing in sequence. When one of the great armored animals changes its rhythm, these lines brighten. The silver creatures extend farther from the crack, testing the water with blunt mouths.

Here, vibration is as useful as sight.

Some animals follow the faint mineral currents. Some orient themselves to pressure. Most listen through their feet, their shells, the tips of their anchored limbs. The hollow has reliable signals: the double knock that precedes a feeding discharge; the lower note that means a seam has begun to open; the sudden absence of a familiar pulse.

Silence would be an event here. If one of the central animals stopped, everything around it would know.

For now, they continue. The water stays cool, except within a finger’s breadth of the orange seams. There, a warm distortion makes the straight mineral blades appear bent. Small creatures crowd these boundaries, half their bodies in one temperature and half in another. Their translucent skin contains moving black grains, shaken and sorted by tiny internal currents.

A few give off light. It is scarcely visible at noon, only a greenish pallor under the ledges. As the sun declines, their outlines become clearer. There are branching lines beneath shells, dots inside mouths, long narrow lights that illuminate the transparent bodies around them. Nothing here needs to grow toward the sun. Daylight is useful chiefly because it reveals neighbors, openings, food.

The mountain rose recently enough that the hollow still bears the shape of its making. A whole section of its wall has lifted away from the bedrock, leaving a dark slot below it. The slot is crowded with new settlers. Their white shells are smaller than fingernails. Above them, a slab the size of a room remains caught between two points of stone.

Soon, in the slow reckoning of this place, the slab will move.

It has already moved a little. A fresh strip of black rock shows along one edge. Each time the pumps reach a certain shared pitch, a pale dust comes out from beneath it. The animals nearest the slot withdraw their soft parts. Then the pitch changes, the dust settles, and they unfold again.

Beyond the split wall, the open ocean looks almost empty. A few distant bodies cross it, dark against the blue. Inside the hollow, every surface is occupied, every current is intercepted, every falling particle finds a mouth.

An afternoon passes with little outward change. The same fish hold their rows. The same immense backs rest over the warm cracks. Sunlight shifts from one wall to another and reveals, for an hour, a patch of red mineral hidden behind a feeding sac.

Then one of the armored animals lifts its forward foot.

Beneath it is a clean, bright impression in the stone, ringed with tiny white shells. It sets the foot down slightly farther away. The whole hollow hears the new contact. For several minutes the smaller animals adjust themselves around it, and the great clattering sound continues in a subtly altered key.
