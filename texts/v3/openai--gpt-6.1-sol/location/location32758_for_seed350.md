The water is warm enough that the iron sweats beneath it.

Along the drowned plain, beads of darker liquid gather on pipes and girders, swell, and fall sideways into the current. They do not mix with the sea. They travel in slow, trembling strings until they reach the black leaves below, which fold around them. In this way the machinery feeds the forest, though much of the machinery has no visible source of power.

There is no shore within sight. The surface hangs perhaps twenty meters overhead, an uneven ceiling of pewter light. Sometimes it lowers almost to the treetops; sometimes it withdraws until the tallest growth disappears into a greenish dusk. This happens without a corresponding change in pressure. A length of chain can lie on the bottom and trail its upper end across the surface, while the distance between its links quietly increases.

The country is low and broad: shallow basins, long mud flats, ridges no higher than a house. You could cross it in an afternoon if the currents held still. From the top of a ridge, visibility opens into submerged avenues, pale channels, and stands of vegetation extending toward the blurred outlines of three or four distant structures. Then the water turns, something vast exhales beyond sight, and everything close comes rushing back into view.

The ground is new. It has the sharpness of things that have not yet learned how to endure.

Fresh black shelves project from the sediment with edges thin enough to cut drifting leaves. Clay walls stand almost vertical beside channels that were not there yesterday. Where a current scours the bottom, it exposes pale seams of material resembling porcelain, still soft inside. Some ridges have cooled only on their outer skins. Their cracks shine a dull orange, and the water above them shivers with heat.

Whole patches of terrain occasionally arrive.

There is a concussion, a rain of silt, and then a low promontory occupies what was open water. Roots are already threaded through it. Sometimes a section of pipe is embedded in its flank, complete with a valve and a pressure gauge trembling in its glass housing. It is difficult to tell whether these are things buried during the plain’s formation or things the plain has formed.

The vegetation makes that distinction less useful.

It grows thickly over almost everything: jointed green stems, broad translucent fronds, mats of fine red filaments that flatten in the current and spring upright in its wake. Some plants resemble reeds until their stems open lengthwise, revealing rows of smaller leaves tucked inside. Others spread like submerged orchards, each low trunk supporting a dense crown of rubbery blades. Their roots descend into the mud, climb neighboring trunks, pass through bolt holes, and emerge from drainpipes several meters away.

Much of the forest grows sideways. A stable current can serve it as ground. Roots grip a band of moving water, and the entire stand leans across the channel, suspended above the silt. When the current shifts, these groves sag slowly, as though their soil has been removed from underneath them. They may settle on the bottom or find another flow and rise again.

There are no animals among the leaves. No fish turning in the open lanes, no shrimp beneath the plates of bark, no small mouths opening in the mud. The enormous movement here belongs to water and growth. Seeds drift in translucent pouches. Loose fronds roll through the channels. Root tips explore the interiors of switch boxes.

The absence becomes most apparent in the clearings. There is food everywhere, shelter everywhere, warm water full of suspended matter, yet nothing arrives to feed.

The sound is continuous and immense.

It comes through the ground as much as through the water: a deep industrial pounding, overlaid by the shriek of stressed metal, the crack of new stone, and the roar of currents entering passages too narrow to contain them. Several rhythms can usually be distinguished. A slow hammering proceeds somewhere beneath the southern flats. A higher, irregular chatter moves between the tree stands. Farther away, a single sustained note rises until nearby stems begin vibrating in sympathy.

There is little silence between these sounds. Their pauses expose other sounds underneath.

Sometimes the pounding goes through the body before it is heard. Sometimes a sharp report arrives from a structure that has already collapsed. Silt can be seen lifting from the ground in anticipation of a noise, arranging itself into low cones; then the noise reaches it, and the cones burst.

The loudest places are marked by vegetation. Around each engine house, the leaves grow thicker and darker, and the trunks develop ridged surfaces like heat sinks. Plants nearest the great turbines have narrow, rigid blades. They vibrate so fast that their edges blur. During a shutdown, these plants loosen almost at once. The blades broaden, the stems droop, and pale new shoots begin appearing around the foundations.

There are only a few buildings, widely separated across the plain.

One is a brick pumping station with a flat roof and windows filled by roots. Another consists of four concrete legs supporting a metal floor; the rest has either fallen away or not yet arrived. Farther north stands a corrugated shed, its walls flexing inward and outward with a regularity the surrounding water does not share. Between them run scattered lengths of rail, isolated pylons, and cables that disappear into thickets.

The pumping station is still operating.

Inside, beneath a ceiling crowded with hanging leaves, flywheels turn in flooded housings. The motor windings shine faintly blue. Water enters through open doors, passes through the pumps, and leaves through a pipe whose broken end lies just outside the wall. There is no apparent reason for the circuit, but the outflow is colder than the intake and carries rectangular bubbles.

These bubbles rise a short distance, hold their shape, and darken. Within some of them, a dry corridor becomes briefly visible: tiled floor, yellow lamp, a closed door at the far end. The current can stretch such a corridor into an impossibly long room before the bubble ruptures. What spills out is warm water and a few brittle flakes of paint.

The forest grows especially lush beneath that pipe.

Beyond the station, the plain opens into a wide, pale basin. Here the water is crowded with layers moving in different directions. A descending sheet carries leaves downward while an adjoining flow lifts heavy fragments of masonry toward the surface. Narrow columns rotate between them. The basin floor is repeatedly uncovered and buried, uncovered and buried, each exposure showing a slightly different arrangement of channels.

A ridge may split while you watch it. The two halves move apart without disturbing the plants rooted across the opening. Their stems lengthen to bridge the gap. Elsewhere, roots pull so fiercely at the young ground that they raise it into walls. Water strikes these walls, breaks through, and fills the new passage with uprooted growth. Within minutes the loose vegetation has attached itself again.

Almost nothing here remains loose for long.

Even debris becomes part of the landscape. A drifting sheet of metal gathers filaments, slows, and anchors itself edge-on in the mud. Silt builds behind it. Stems thicken along its upper edge. By the time another surge tears it free, it carries a small wooded bank with it, traveling across the plain like a raft without a surface.

Above, the dim ceiling brightens occasionally with a broad, colorless flash. Its light reveals a region much larger than ordinary visibility allows: the pumping station, the northern shed, acres of dense growth, and beyond them a low line of newly formed ground. Every edge appears hard and recent. Every hollow is already full of roots.

Then the light fades, the water closes around the nearer trees, and the pounding resumes its usual distance.

Near the station door, a pressure gauge remains readable beneath a clear film of growth. Its needle advances through numbered marks, stops, and advances again. Each time it reaches the red sector, the foundations lift a few centimeters from the mud. Water rushes underneath. Roots tighten around the brickwork.

The pumps keep turning while the building settles into its new position.
