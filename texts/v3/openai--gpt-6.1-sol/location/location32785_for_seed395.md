From the edge of the basin, the green looks almost continuous. It lies low beneath the sky, interrupted by pale towers and the darker seams of passages between them. There are no distant mountains. The horizon is a shallow rise of the same occupied ground, so far away that heat has taken the detail out of it.

Down inside, there is hardly anywhere to put a foot.

Succulents grow shoulder to shoulder in the warm mineral soil: low mats of fleshy leaves, branching spurges, aloes with skirts of old brown blades. Some bear a dusty blue bloom that rubs away where animals have brushed against them. Others are glossy on the shaded side and dull on the side facing the sun. Thorn shrubs fill the spaces above them. Their crowns overlap without quite touching, leaving narrow channels of brightness through which small birds move.

The soil is dry beneath all this growth. Scratch it and a pale powder falls apart between the fingers. There is no stream, no pool, no wet margin where a different sort of vegetation begins. Water is held out of sight—in the thick leaves, in tuberous roots, in the bodies of insects, deep beneath the compacted surface. Rain comes seldom enough that much of the basin’s activity concerns keeping what the last rain left behind.

But the place does not look starved. There is too much living matter for that. New leaves crowd the tips of old stems. Fallen seed capsules accumulate in the forks of shrubs. Flowers stand above the thorn canopy in small yellow clusters, each with a shifting attendance of flies and tiny wasps.

The pale towers are termite mounds.

At first they seem scattered among the plants. Farther in, it becomes clear that they determine the arrangement of almost everything else. Some are broad, rounded masses with several blunt peaks. Others rise in fluted columns, their lower walls hidden by vegetation. The largest are taller than the shrubs, but most remain close to the ground: roofs over chambers, low ridges over covered routes, clustered swellings built around the remains of an older colony.

Each is a separate construction. Together they form something like a city extending beyond sight.

There are crowded districts where mounds stand only a few paces apart, and quieter stretches occupied by the collapsed shells of abandoned ones. Trails run between them. Under the trails are galleries; beneath the galleries lie storage chambers and the passages that reach toward deeper, more humid soil. The visible structures are only the upper part of a much denser occupation.

No wall has a clean edge for long. Workers carry dampened grains to a breach and fit them into place. A beetle excavates beneath a ledge. Roots enter a crack, swell there, and split it wider. Lizards enlarge cavities left by other animals. The wind lays fresh dust against one side of a mound while ants remove the same material from the other.

The ground is still being made.

Beyond the basin, broad salt and silt flats supply fine sediment whenever the wind rises. Here, the thickets slow it. Dust collects under every branch and on the sheltered side of every wall. A rooted plant becomes the center of a small rise. Several rises join. Termites bind the loose material into hard surfaces, and those surfaces catch more dust.

There has been little time for deep gullies or worn rock. The newest banks are soft-faced and steep, patterned with the drag marks of leaves. In places, yesterday’s deposit remains as smooth as sifted flour. A line of footprints crosses it, disappears beneath a shrub, and emerges on a roof.

The landscape advances by small additions. At its outer edge, isolated mounds stand among widely spaced plants. A little farther in, thorn branches meet. Farther still, there is no clear distinction between a mound’s apron, the earth held by a shrub, and the low bank built by generations of burrowing animals. All have become one uneven floor.

In the middle of the day, the basin is remarkably quiet.

Heat presses scent out of the vegetation: warm resin, bitter sap, the faint sourness of an opened succulent stem. Most animals stay beneath something. Beetles rest against the cool undersides of leaves. A tortoise occupies a shallow scrape beneath an aloe, its shell so thickly powdered that it resembles a piece of the ground. Small rodents sleep in chambers borrowed from old mounds.

The absence of obvious movement is deceptive. Ants pass under a continuous skin of shelter, crossing from root to root through tunnels only a grain wide. Termites work on the shaded side of a wall. A hunting spider shifts one leg, then waits. A leaf gradually tilts away from the sun.

Now and then there is a dry click: a seed case opening, a bird landing on a brittle twig, a little section of hardened earth breaking from a roof. Wind produces a low brushing sound in the thorn canopy. Because there is no running water and few large leaves, nothing sustains a roar or a rustle. Even a busy part of the basin can be heard as a series of separate, small events.

Toward evening, those events become more frequent.

Lizards appear on the mound walls while the earth is still warm. Sandgrouse walk through the broader passages, picking fallen seeds from the dust. Mice emerge at the bases of shrubs. A narrow-bodied snake follows a route worn smooth by many smaller animals, pauses beside an entrance, and folds itself into the shade.

The plants cast long, interlocking shadows. These offer temporary lanes across ground that was exposed an hour earlier. Animals follow them. A beetle can travel from one patch of cover to another without crossing direct sunlight; by sunset, the patches have joined into a continuous dim floor.

There are no broad avenues. Passage depends on size. A bird sees a lightly broken canopy with pale towers projecting through it. A fox finds a maze of thorn openings, mound bases, and dead ends. For an ant, there are whole neighborhoods under a fallen branch, including covered roads, feeding grounds, and boundaries it cannot cross without being challenged.

The basin is crowded enough that an empty space seldom stays empty.

When a shrub dies, its branches remain useful. Birds perch on them; climbing stems take advantage of the light. The roots decay slowly, leaving tunnels through which insects and new roots descend. When a mound is deserted, its upper chambers become nesting places. Its walls shelter seedlings until they grow large enough to break them. Collapse opens a sunny patch, and the patch fills with low, fleshy growth.

Even the bare surfaces have occupants too small to notice from standing height: mites in the dust, larvae beneath flakes of earth, minute lichens on the cooler faces of old walls.

At night, the stored heat leaves the ground gradually. The air above the roofs cools first. The lower passages remain warm, and animals gather there, moving between feeding places without climbing into the exposed air. Far off, another part of the basin is still releasing the day’s heat. A gentle current develops and travels through the thickets, carrying dust and the smells of thousands of colonies.

It takes hours for that current to cross even a small district.

Under the stars, the towers lose their separate shapes. The basin becomes a dark, close-textured expanse, quietly shifting at innumerable points. By morning there will be a repaired wall, a new opening, a cleared trail, a layer of dust caught beneath a branch. Near the outer edge, somewhere too distant to see, another small patch of bare ground will have begun to hold together.
