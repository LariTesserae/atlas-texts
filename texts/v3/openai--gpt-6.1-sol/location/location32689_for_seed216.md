The first thing you notice is how much room there is.

You have come through a passage scarcely wider than your shoulders, its walls soft with overlapping leaves, and expected a forest on the other side. Instead there is an opening so large that the far side has weather. A faint gray shower descends there, stops short of a distant ledge, and hangs in the air as a darkened patch. Below you, other ledges become indistinct. Above, a few long green ribs cross the gloom.

Everything you can touch is alive. Almost everything you can see is empty.

The path beneath your feet is a braided root, flattened across its upper surface and covered in a short, resilient nap. It is broad enough for six people to walk abreast. Water has gathered along its edges, where little leaves turn their tips downward and discharge it drop by drop. The drops fall for a long time. You lose sight of them before hearing any impact.

There is no stone under the path. No soil beneath the nap. If you scrape it away, you find pale fibers, then tougher green fibers, then a moist layer that closes slowly over the injury. The darker seams that look like mineral veins are old bindings: strips of bark wrapped around the root when it was young and still narrow enough to bend by hand.

This entire country was made that way.

***

It stands at an immense height, though height here is difficult to establish. Openings in the outer wall sometimes admit a view of clouds far below. Mountains appear through them on clear days, small, blue, and apparently deserted. At other openings the same mountains are overhead. The inhabitants use those apertures for ventilation and avoid making maps from them.

They agree on local directions. Toward the growing tips is outward. Toward the oldest knots is inward. Down is wherever loose water goes, until it changes its mind.

The air is warm. Even in the highest galleries, where a steady draft pulls loose clothing tight against the body, the living walls give off the warmth of sunlit skin. Thick sap moves inside them. If you sit against a trunk long enough, you can feel a slow change of temperature, a warm passage followed by a cooler one. There are no fires in the settlements. Food is cooked in folded-leaf pockets pressed into the hot sides of the major stems.

The settlements themselves are small.

A dozen dwellings might occupy the junction of three immense branches. Their doors are woven shutters; their rooms are hollows trained into swelling tissue. Between one settlement and the next there may be several days of walking, with nothing to interrupt the view except distant suspended structures and the occasional movement of an animal. The whole inhabited region could fit within one of the spaces it overlooks.

Yet every bridge, every shelter, every branching support bears evidence of work. Old cuts have become oval scars. Grafts are marked by ridges. A railing thick as your waist retains the paired indentations of a wooden clamp used centuries ago.

The makers had knives of shell, wedges of bone, cord, ladders, and patience. Their descendants still use those things. They cannot raise a beam weighing a thousand tons, but they can persuade a shoot to grow into the place where a beam will eventually be needed. They can bind two stems together, keep the wound clean, and leave instructions for their grandchildren.

Some bridges took nine generations to meet in the middle. A few are still approaching one another across miles of open air.

***

At first the landscape seems unfinished. Then you begin to see how old it is.

The great supporting limbs have settled into broad curves. Their undersides are ridged where runoff has worn away generations of bark. Dry surfaces carry a silver fleece of dead root hairs, but rain-facing surfaces are smooth and dark. Where two structures once rubbed together, they have grown a shared cushion of dense tissue and ceased moving independently.

There are abandoned routes, too: stairways narrowed by growth until only a child could pass; a row of doorways now pointing downward; bridges that have become so deeply bowed that travelers prefer the long way around. Nobody removes them. The old construction feeds the new, and a failed bridge may make a good reservoir or a nursery for hanging roots.

Erosion is patient here. Water loosens the outer fibers, which drift away in translucent ribbons. Grazers crop those ribbons before they fall. Beneath them another layer has already matured. Large shapes change slowly enough that people name the changes rather than the shapes: The Bend That Has Stopped, The Hollow Still Widening, The Stair That Will Be Lost.

The names are useful. They tell you where it is safe to sleep.

***

There is water everywhere, but rarely much of it in one place.

It beads on leaf ceilings. It runs along the bottoms of handrails. It gathers in the cupped surfaces of old joints, where a few inches of dark water may cover an acre. These shallow pools are warm enough to steam when a cooler current of air arrives. Their margins creep through the living floor, leaving bands of fine white rootlets.

Rain usually forms inside the larger chambers. Vapor rises from the warm supports, collects under a high canopy, and falls in scattered showers. Sometimes a rain takes three days to descend from its cloud to a walkway. You can watch it coming and move your bedding.

The broad leaves above the settlements are adjusted seasonally with ropes. A household can direct a shower into its cistern, provided no one farther along the branch has a prior claim. Water disputes are settled by examining the old binding scars. Some claims are older than the houses that depend on them.

Mist moves through the open volumes almost without sound. It reveals distances that the dimness conceals: a nearby rail, then an isolated hanging root, then a wall so far away that its individual leaves cannot be distinguished. Occasionally the mist passes behind something you thought was distant, and you realize it has been hanging a few yards from your face.

***

Light enters through thin membranes in the exterior growth. It comes through green, diluted, and directionless. Deep inside, a second light seeps from the pale seams between grafts. You can read by it if you hold the page close.

There are vast surfaces overhead that might be ceilings or might be the undersides of other countries. They receive so little light that their vegetation grows slowly, with leaves as broad as sails. When one turns to catch a brighter interval, the motion takes hours. A whole district may darken beneath it.

Flowers are uncommon near the paths. Those that appear are small, heavy, and almost black, their pollen carried by animals rather than air. Farther out, on the remote spans, flowering happens in sheets. Once in many years a structure on the horizon becomes faintly violet. Messengers travel to see it. By the time they arrive, it may be green again.

No one speaks loudly in the large chambers.

Sound does carry, but unreliably. A shouted greeting may reach a neighbor, or may be heard the following evening in a settlement below. Quiet sounds remain close to their source. People converse at arm’s length, and the animals have mostly abandoned calls.

***

The animals are abundant wherever you look closely.

Flat-bodied grazers cling beneath the wet paths, licking the films that form there. Small translucent creatures live between layers of bark; their shadows pass through the pale walls of bedrooms. The shallow pools hold jointed swimmers that emerge at night and climb the warm stems to shed their skins.

There are larger inhabitants in the open air.

Long, narrow-winged animals drift between the supports, scarcely moving their wings. Their bodies trail root-colored filaments in which smaller creatures shelter. They land only to feed or give birth. When a group passes near a walkway, you hear a faint dry brushing, like fingertips across cloth.

Predators wait in folds of leaves. Some are distinguishable from the leaves only when they open their eyes. Their prey is plentiful, but widely scattered, and a successful hunter may remain still for weeks. Beneath one overhang is a mound of shed skins and polished bones that has been accumulating longer than anyone remembers. The animal above it is still young, according to the people who maintain the path.

Nothing roars. Nothing needs to.

***

Travel requires a few precautions beyond food and a sound pair of shoes.

A bridge must be crossed while looking at some part of it. Looking continuously into the gap can make the far end recede. This is a practical difficulty, not an illusion: travelers have exhausted their supplies on spans that others cross in an hour. Railings are therefore planted with flowers, bright fungi, or contrasting strips of bark—anything that gives the eye a succession of nearby places to rest.

Do not cut a hanging root unless you know where its upper end is. Some descend from the past. Severing one may close a passage before its builders were born, leaving you in a country where the passage never existed. The locals recognize these roots by their leaves, which bear the marks of weather that has not yet happened.

And if you find a stair with wet footprints going upward, wait.

There may be someone descending it.

***

Toward evening the warmth leaves the smaller paths first. Water begins to gather on their edges. Grazers turn over beneath them, exposing pale bellies to the last heat.

In the nearest settlement, a person comes out carrying a wooden lever and a coil of cord. They are going to adjust a growing tip. The work takes only a few minutes: loosen a binding, insert a wedge, pull the shoot a little toward the empty air. In seventy years, if it remains healthy, it will touch the corresponding shoot across the gap.

Beyond them, rain falls in a chamber large enough to contain a horizon.

A winged animal passes through it and emerges dark with water. Far below, a small light moves along a route you cannot see. The person finishes tying the cord, tests the knot, and goes home.

The bent shoot remains, held in its new direction.
