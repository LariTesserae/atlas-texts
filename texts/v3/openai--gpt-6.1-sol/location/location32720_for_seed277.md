The upland begins above the clouds, though there is little light to tell you so. Beneath its southern edge, cloud sometimes shows as a paler darkness, rising until it touches the cliffs and then withdrawing without rain. The plateau continues beyond it in broad, shallow steps. A person could walk for weeks along one of these steps and never reach the place where it rises to the next.

The ground is warm.

It is not volcanic in any obvious way. There are no red fissures, no fountains, no cones smoking against the sky. Heat comes through a dark, close-grained stone, evenly enough that lying down feels like resting on an animal much larger than yourself. Here and there, the warmth gathers beneath a plate of rock. The plate expands, lifts a little at one edge, and settles again after a few hours. Dust slips into the opening. Something underneath exhales.

Most of the country consists of low ridges, long hollows, and exposed shelves rounded by weather that has worked slowly for a very long time. Nothing looks freshly broken. Even the cliffs have blunt lips. Their fallen stones lie half sunk in their own debris, fitted into the slope so thoroughly that it is difficult to imagine them ever having moved.

The surface is charcoal, brown-black, and, where rubbed bare, a dull metallic gray. Small grains adhere to a damp fingertip. Some are magnetic. Others leave a yellow stain that smells faintly of hot copper.

There is no road, no building, no abandoned machine.

Yet the place has the atmosphere of an immense industrial district after the lights have gone out.

Heat passes upward through seams. Pressure collects in chambers below the plateau, then escapes through thousands of narrow vents. Mineral-rich vapor condenses along their mouths, laying down collars of pale scale. In certain basins, electrical charge migrates through wet layers buried between sheets of conductive stone. Stand barefoot there and you feel a delicate prickling before you hear anything.

The sounds are infrequent and widely separated: a click from somewhere underfoot; a short, low vibration passing along a ridge; the distant release of pressure, soft at this distance as someone drawing breath through their teeth. Most of the time there is only the blood in your ears.

What resembles machinery is the ordinary work of the ground. It has no controls and produces nothing intended for use. It keeps the upland warm, lifts moisture from deep reservoirs, and deposits minerals wherever that moisture escapes. The living things have arranged themselves around its output.

Water is scarce on the surface. It appears overnight on the cooler sides of stones, making their edges shine for an hour or two. A shallow depression may hold a spoonful. In the shaded throat of a ravine, droplets gather on mineral teeth and fall into a pool too narrow to see unless you put your face close to the opening.

Occasional rain arrives from weather formed over the plateau itself. Warm vapor rises through the hollows and spreads beneath a high ceiling of cloud. Days later, perhaps hundreds of miles away, it falls in a brief, heavy shower. Dust darkens. Dry channels run. For a short time, the country smells of wet stone and bruised roots.

The water sinks quickly. It leaves behind dark tidelines and small accumulations of seeds.

Plants grow far apart. There are knee-high shrubs with black, folded leaves and roots that follow warm seams deep into the rock. Their branches are stiff, polished by wind, and carry little bunches of translucent berries. Near vents, coarse straw-colored grass grows in rings. Its blades remain tightly rolled during dry periods; after rain they open, exposing green inner surfaces that seem startlingly tender in this place.

In the hollows, broad mats lie almost flush with the soil. They resemble old felt until moisture reaches them. Then their margins lift and reveal the fine stems underneath. Tiny animals shelter in the gap between mat and ground.

There is very little visible growth across the great distances. You might see a shrub, then walk half an hour before seeing another. But the bare intervals are inhabited.

Under loose flakes of stone live flat, many-legged grazers whose bodies are the color of ash. They feed on films that develop wherever damp mineral meets warm air. When disturbed, they do not scatter noisily. They slide into cracks, dozens at once, leaving the exposed stone apparently empty.

Small furred animals travel between condensate pockets. They have long hind feet, narrow faces, and dense whiskers that touch the ground ahead of them. They lick moisture from stone before it evaporates, then retreat into burrows insulated with dry grass. Their droppings gather around favored hollows. In the darkness, these little deposits are often the first sign of how busy the plateau is.

Larger animals move farther.

A herd may pass as a slow rearrangement of shadows: heavy shoulders, high backs, pale undersides briefly visible when they step across a ridge. They browse the scattered shrubs and spend the hottest hours in shallow caves. Their feet have broad, soft pads. Even a hundred of them crossing hard ground makes less sound than you expect. Only an occasional stone turns beneath their weight.

Predators follow the routes between warm hollows and water pockets. Some wait beside vents where small animals come to drink. Others range behind the herds, keeping low, stopping whenever the herd stops. Their eyes show no shine unless light is brought close to them. Most hunting here takes place by scent, by warmth, and by the vibration of a footfall carried through stone.

Overhead, wings pass unseen.

The flyers nest in cliff recesses and descend when a vent releases fresh vapor. Small winged insects breed around the damp collars of mineral, sometimes emerging in such numbers that the warm air thickens with them. The larger hunters pass through that rising column with open mouths. You hear one dry flutter, then nothing. By morning, the insects that survived have settled into cracks, and the vent is quiet again.

Darkness persists through most of the day. The sun, when the cloud thins, is a dim, rust-colored patch with no clear edge. It gives enough light to distinguish the next ridge, seldom enough to see the one beyond. Distance is usually discovered by sound or by walking into it.

There are other lights, too weak to illuminate much: a blue bead at the tip of a mineral needle, a brief green seam exposed when a rock plate lifts, pale flecks in the damp lining of a burrow. During dry electrical weather, the fur of a passing animal may glow faintly along its back. The glow fades when it enters a sheltered hollow.

Wind moves slowly across the open ground, warm and carrying fine grit. At the cliffs it accelerates. There, the darkness has a continuous breath, and the few shrubs lean permanently away from the drop. Far below, cloud presses against the rock. Sometimes a break reveals another dark terrace beneath this one, equally broad, with its own vents and faint drifting weather.

The plateau has been settling into these forms for longer than its present creatures have lived on it. Channels deepen a little each rainy season. Mineral collars grow and collapse. A blocked vent forces moisture toward a new opening, and over several years a ring of grass dies while another takes hold a mile away.

The animals learn the change before it is obvious. Their paths shift. A familiar pool dries. Predators wait at the old place for a few nights, then leave.

After hours of walking, what first seemed empty begins to resolve into activity. A shrub trembles though the wind has stopped. A loose flake tilts under the weight of something beneath it. Warm damp air brushes your ankle and is gone.

Beyond the ridge ahead lies another hollow. Its floor is bare, its edges rounded, its darkness almost complete. Somewhere in it, water is gathering.
