The plateau is so pale that distance appears first as a change in texture, not color. Near at hand, the stone is pitted and granular, crowded with sharp little ridges. Farther out, those ridges become fins, then clustered towers, then a white unevenness beneath the white sky. Only the deepest openings are dark. They disclose the scale of the country: a cleft that looks narrow enough to step across continues down through several hundred feet of rock.

There is no soil. Nothing has taken root in the cracks, and no green tint softens the wet faces. The darker streaks are manganese; the yellow is sulfur; the faint pink along certain seams belongs to the stone itself. Even where a hollow has collected fine sediment, it holds only crushed mineral, pale and heavy as flour mixed with water.

The whole plateau is densely occupied by its own substance. Blades of limestone stand shoulder to shoulder. Rounded blocks lodge between them. Beneath the blocks are smaller stones, and beneath those, gravel fitted so tightly that the water can pass through it without leaving any obvious channel. There is scarcely a level place large enough to set both feet. Yet the view is open for miles. You can see over this crowded surface to successive basins, each filled with another congregation of pale ribs and towers, all the way to a mountain wall whose lower slopes are hidden by brightness.

Water is everywhere, but almost nowhere deep.

It emerges from horizontal seams halfway up the cliffs, spreads across the rock in transparent sheets, and disappears into slots. It fills thumb-sized cups. It beads along the undersides of overhangs. In the larger depressions, shallow pools lie so clear that their presence is visible only where the air touches them: a hard silver line, a slight distortion of a submerged edge. Their bottoms are white stone, without weed, mud, or any loose organic litter.

The water is cold enough to ache in the fingers. The air is cool rather than freezing, and the wet rock draws warmth out of anything laid against it. There is no snow on the exposed plateau. Its whiteness belongs to the limestone and to the light, which comes through a high, thin ceiling of cloud with almost no loss of intensity.

Shadows remain small and blue. Under a ledge there may be a hand’s breadth of shade; inside a fissure, a narrow darkness. Everywhere else, the brightness enters from several directions at once, reflected off wet surfaces and pale stone. Looking up offers little relief. Looking down means meeting the sky again in a thousand shallow pools.

The sound is enormous.

At first it seems impossible that so much noise could come from water this thin. There is no great river in sight, no single fall to account for it. But every cleft has its own drop, every ledge its pouring edge, every hidden cavity its resonant trickle. Close by, water strikes a hollow slab with a hard, rapid tapping. A few paces away, a crack exhales a deep continuous rumble. Beyond it, an unseen cascade makes a broad tearing sound that fills the basin.

These sounds overlap until there is no silence between them. A voice spoken normally disappears before it reaches another person. Even a shout has little range.

Much of the noise comes from beneath the visible surface. The plateau is perforated. Under its crowded spires are chambers, descending passages, and narrow throats through which the water falls. Some openings draw the sound inward; others throw it back enlarged. Standing beside a dark slit, you can feel the vibration faintly through the soles of your feet.

Despite all this movement, the place has an extraordinary steadiness.

The seams give water at the same rate. The pools hold the same level. The sheets crossing the rock are so even that they resemble clear, flexible material laid over it. There are no flood marks above them, no stranded debris, no fresh scars from a collapse. Water arriving from the higher massif is balanced by water draining through the plateau. Where it dissolves a face, it deposits a thin mineral skin elsewhere. Old edges are rounded; newer surfaces have already lost their rawness.

A narrow shelf below one spring has grown thick with calcite. The neighboring shelf is being worn away at nearly the same rate. Over a lifetime the difference would be difficult to measure.

In some channels the water looks still while the noise proves that it is running. A tiny bubble catches against an edge and holds there, replenished as quickly as it breaks. Beneath it, the current passes continuously through a polished groove. Such details give the landscape its peculiar fixedness: each small event persists in the same location, fed by the same conditions.

Life is scarce and easily overlooked.

Under submerged stones, where spring water brings dissolved iron from deep in the mountain, thin colonies of microbes form colorless films. They do not spread across the sunlit surfaces. Their habitat is the narrow boundary between two kinds of water, one rising from within the rock and one moving across it.

A few small, blind crustaceans live in the flooded passages. Occasionally one appears in a clear pool, almost transparent except for a darker line inside its body. It rests beneath the lip of a stone, moving its limbs in the current. There are no tracks along the pool margins, no nests on the towers, no wings crossing the open sky.

The absence of plants makes every surface legible. You can follow a fracture through an entire hillside. You can see where one bed of rock overhangs another, where pressure folded a seam, where centuries of dripping have cut parallel grooves. Nothing covers these marks except water.

From a higher rib of stone, the plateau opens into a broad basin. Its floor is not flat but packed with hundreds of low, blunt pinnacles, each wet on one side, each separated from the next by a shining slot. Beyond them stand taller formations, close-set and irregular, with rounded shoulders and deeply fluted bases. Their resemblance to anything made is brief. Up close there are no repeated proportions, no entrances, no surfaces intended for use—only the patient consequences of stone meeting water.

Mist hangs above the basin at a constant height. It does not thicken into cloud or clear away to reveal more. Through it, the far formations lose their edges while retaining their brightness.

There is nowhere dry to sit. There is no soft ground and little shelter from the light. The plateau offers no obvious center toward which to travel. Every basin leads to another crowded reach of stone, another set of wet ledges, another dark opening carrying the same immense sound.

After a while, the ear can distinguish one nearby fall from the rest. It drops out of sight just below a rounded slab, steady and forceful. You listen to it, then look outward again. Across miles of pale rock, the water continues at its appointed levels, and the bright mist holds exactly where it was.
