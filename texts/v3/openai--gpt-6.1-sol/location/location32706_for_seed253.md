The sea is held above the mountains.

Its bottom lies higher than the highest clouds, though there is little here by which to judge height. Beyond the outer banks, water falls into darkness. It falls so far that no sound of its arrival returns. Within the banks, the water is cold, almost black, and very clear. A swimmer can see a long way if something luminous passes; otherwise, distance is a pressure on the eyes.

There is no stone under this sea. The entire basin is living wood.

Not wood as it appears in a forest: no trunks standing separately, no crowns, no leaves turning toward a sky. Here the trees have grown sideways and into one another, laying their roots across the summits, then across the gaps between summits, until they made a continuous floor. Other growths thickened that floor from beneath. Still others rose along its edges and became the walls that hold the water. Whatever mountains once supported it have worn away far below. The basin now carries its own weight.

Its underside hangs over empty air in great, dark folds.

Inside, the landscape is open. Broad submerged terraces extend for miles, separated by shallow descents. The floor is a pale vegetable substance, close-grained and smooth, with the faint parallel marks of growth visible beneath a skin of transparent algae. Here and there it rises into a long ridge, a rounded enclosure, an arch large enough to admit a migrating shoal. These features are far apart. Between them lie expanses of bare-looking floor, though nothing is bare and nothing is dead.

Even the silt is alive. It is a low, soft crop whose filaments lie flat under the water, so fine that a hand passing over them would feel only a slight resistance, like the nap of worn cloth.

Everything has been made, but nothing has been assembled.

The arches grew around spaces that needed to remain open. The retaining walls thickened where the water pressed hardest. Corridors formed along habitual passages, and hollows deepened beneath the places where animals rested. The basin has the proportions of an inhabited place: sheltered bays, generous routes between them, broad courts where currents meet. There are thresholds without doors. There are ledges at convenient heights. There are openings that seem intended for bodies of particular sizes.

Whatever first guided this growth has left no separate sign. No tools are buried in the floor. No inscription interrupts the grain. The intention is distributed through the place, as thoroughly as the sap.

It is immensely old.

Water has softened every exposed edge. The ribs of the walls have merged into broad buttresses. High projections have subsided, their matter drawn back into the living structure below. Old chambers are visible only as faint changes in colour, ovals and rectangles sealed beneath newer growth. Some of the deepest terraces have been worn so evenly that they resemble fields seen from a great height.

Yet they do not continue wearing down. The plants replace precisely what the water removes. A grazing animal leaves a pale scrape; by the time it returns along its circuit, the surface is restored. The current hollows a channel, and the channel grows inward at the same rate. The whole sea has reached a shape it can keep.

There is movement everywhere, but very little happens.

Cold currents travel the same routes through the arches. Fronds lift and settle within the same narrow range. Shoals emerge from distant darkness at intervals so regular that their arrivals feel like part of the current. There are no storms in the basin, no seasons, no advancing shore. Along the outer wall, the overflowing water is replaced by a slow seep through the floor. The level never visibly rises or falls.

The animals are abundant, though the openness can conceal them.

On the terraces lie flat, many-finned grazers, dark as the water until they turn and show their pale undersides. They move in loose companies, leaving ample room between one body and the next. Small translucent creatures live within the fine crop of the floor. A single patch that appears empty may contain thousands, each occupying a tiny hollow in the living nap.

Long silver fish pass overhead in sheets so thin that they are nearly invisible edge-on. Their movements briefly draw out the scale of the basin: a procession begins above one ridge and continues until its leading edge has disappeared beyond another.

Larger animals inhabit the deeper courts. They have broad heads, slow tails, and loose folds of skin that tremble as they swim. They eat the soft buds growing beneath the ledges. Sometimes one rests its jaw against an arch and remains there for hours, opening and closing its gill covers. The arch grows smooth around these resting places.

Light comes chiefly from mouths, eyes, and the undersides of fins.

It is weak light: green points, a blue seam along a flank, the dull pearl glow of eggs carried beneath a body. The plants themselves shine only where the water rubs them hardest. Along a few narrow passages, the walls give off enough light to reveal the grain and the mouths of small sheltering animals. Beyond those passages, the landscape disappears again.

Far above, the surface is occasionally visible as a lighter thickness in the dark. There is no clear sky through it. The sea seems to have a ceiling of cold water.

It is a loud place.

The falling water at the perimeter produces a continuous low concussion, carried through the basin floor rather than through the air. Arches deepen it. Hollow ridges return it in separate tones. Beneath that sound are the animals: scraping teeth, clattering plates, abrupt knocks made by bodies against resonant wood. The large grazers call to one another with sounds that travel for miles, descending through several notes until they become indistinguishable from the vibration of the floor.

Nothing rustles here. Even the smallest leaf is submerged. Sound arrives through the bones, through a palm laid on a ledge, through the soft skin of the throat.

Near the outer bank, the floor slopes upward and the vegetation becomes thicker. Broad straps of growth overlap like shingles, each one fixed beneath the next. Between them are quiet cavities where young animals shelter from the circulating water. The bank’s crest is smooth and low. Water passes over it in an unbroken sheet.

From there, the basin can be felt extending behind you: terraces, distant arches, the slow traffic of feeding bodies. Ahead is the drop. The wall descends beyond sight, clothed in pale roots that have no earth to enter.

For a little distance the overflow remains transparent. It carries the faint lights of small animals swept over the lip, animals adapted to that passage, their fins folded tightly against their sides. Then the falling sheet narrows into the dark.

The old basin holds steady above it, full to the same worn edge.
