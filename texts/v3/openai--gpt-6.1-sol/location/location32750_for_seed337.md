The town begins below the level of the fields.

From the surrounding country, you see almost nothing of it: a few roof ridges, pale smoke lying low, the tops of ladders leaning against the sky. Then the ground ends at a cut bank, and the settlement opens beneath you. It occupies a broad excavation, shallow enough for daylight to reach every street but deep enough that, from the lower courtyards, the horizon is made entirely of earth.

The bank is yellow-gray clay. Fresh spade cuts show in it, each with a small lip where the blade was withdrawn. Here and there a darker seam runs through the exposed face, level and uninterrupted, disappearing behind houses and reappearing three streets farther on. The inhabitants use these seams to judge height. A threshold is laid along one; the floor of a storehouse is measured from another. On the north side, where the digging is newest, the seam passes through a row of doorways that have frames but no doors.

There is very little old surface here. Corners are sharp. The clay has not had time to slump into comfortable shapes. Even the footpaths are still being established: a hundred passages have darkened them, but their margins retain the teeth of the digging tools.

The excavation is nearly a mile across, widening at one end into a second, less finished hollow. Between the two stands an uncut rib of ground carrying a narrow road. People cross it with baskets balanced on their backs. Seen from below, they seem to be walking across the top of a wall.

Within the first hollow, almost every piece of ground has a use.

Houses stand shoulder to shoulder, built of unfired brick, packed earth, woven reed panels plastered with clay. Their roofs are low and slightly pitched, weighted with flat stones. A roof edge often serves as the handrail of the passage above it. One family’s courtyard is another family’s route to the communal oven; a stair descends beside a bedroom wall and ends in a place where three households keep their water jars.

There are larger spaces, though none are empty. The broad square near the southern bank contains timber frames, stacked bricks, drying mats, a covered well, and a little fenced patch where an elderly woman grows onions. Its openness is overhead. You can see a wide piece of sky there and feel the weather coming before it reaches the lanes.

Most buildings are occupied before they are finished. Blankets hang where walls will eventually rise. A wooden shelf is wedged into a niche still bearing the marks of the hand that scooped it out. Beside a sleeping platform, someone has left a basket of clay with a damp cloth over it, ready for the next morning’s plastering. Construction advances a little at a time around meals, births, sleep.

The air is cool. Cold stays in the earth overnight and lingers indoors well past midday. At dawn, moisture beads beneath roof stones and darkens the bottoms of walls. People wipe the droplets from lintels with scraps of wool. In the deeper storage rooms, jars sweat even when the streets outside are dry.

Water comes down from the fields rather than rising abundantly from below. After rain, thin streams arrive at the rim and enter channels lined with split stones. The channels bend around buildings, pass beneath small bridges of wood, and empty into settling pits. Someone is always tending them: lifting a leaf from a grate, pressing clay into a leak, shifting a stone to divide the flow.

There is enough water to keep certain places perpetually damp, but not enough to waste. Each household knows which channel it may draw from and at what hour. Drinking water is carried from the covered well. Roof runoff is caught for washing and brickmaking. The dull green water at the bottom of the lowest pit belongs to neither purpose; it is left for soaking reeds and softening the hard clods brought in from the newest cuts.

Rain changes the town quickly. The pale walls deepen in color. Roof edges begin to tick. Streets that had seemed level reveal small slopes, and people follow the trickles with bowls of clay, repairing the ground while the water shows them where it fails. On a rainy afternoon, half the settlement may be outdoors doing this, quiet figures crouched along the lanes.

Vegetation survives mostly at the margins. Grass hangs over the rim in short, torn roots. There are a few thorn bushes on the unexcavated rib, their lower branches clipped for kindling. In the town itself, green appears in carefully defended fragments: a pot of bitter herbs on a roof, bean vines trained along a cord, moss in the shaded seam behind the well.

Seeds sometimes sprout in the plaster. A narrow blade of grass will emerge from a wall just above someone’s shoulder, live there for a week, then yellow as the brick dries. People leave these small growths alone unless they open a crack.

Animals are uncommon. A pair of swallows nests beneath the eaves of the grain house. One lean dog makes a slow circuit of the cooking yards, accepted everywhere and fed sparingly. Beetles are discovered when a new layer of earth is opened. They lie still in the exposed clay for a moment, then find a joint between bricks and disappear.

The human activity is much greater than the sound suggests.

There are no wheels in the lower lanes, and little metal. Earth is loosened with wooden spades whose edges have been hardened in fire. Where the clay resists, workers use stone picks, striking patiently rather than swinging hard. Loads travel in baskets, on shoulder poles, or along short lines of people passing them hand to hand. Wet clay receives each basket with a soft, heavy settling sound.

From the rim you hear an occasional knock, the scrape of a blade, a cough. Most voices remain within the courts. Cloth screens and thick walls absorb conversation; the banks keep the wind from moving through the lanes. Even at the busiest hour there are stretches when the loudest sound is water dropping into a jar.

At the unfinished end of town, the work follows a strict sequence. First the upper soil is lifted away and carried to the rim, where it will support gardens. Then the clay beneath is cut into blocks. Some blocks become bricks; others are broken and mixed with straw for plaster. The hollow left behind becomes a room, a passage, or a court. Each day the builders decide which walls of untouched earth to keep.

A newly opened chamber smells different from the houses around it: mineral, close, faintly sweet. Its walls are cold to the palm. Before anyone moves in, a shallow groove is cut around the floor to catch seepage, and a lamp is left burning there through the evening. In the morning, the workers inspect the flame-blackened roof and the water in the groove. Only then do they bring in the first household’s belongings.

By late afternoon the western bank casts a broad shadow, but the upper walls remain bright. People move their work toward the remaining sun. Bricks are turned on their mats. A reed screen is carried up a stair and laid across a roof to dry. Someone tests a newly plastered wall with two fingertips, finds it still soft, and goes away to do something else.

Beyond the rim, the fields look level and remote. Down here, every few paces brings a change in height: a step, a ramp, a sunken hearth, a platform raised against damp. The town is dense with these adjustments. It has grown around the earth’s coolness, the route of a little water, the reach of daylight over a bank.

At dusk, the day’s final baskets of soil are carried upward. Below, a family sweeps loose clay from a room that did not exist yesterday. They spread a mat, set down a jar, and hang a curtain across the entrance. Through the curtain comes the faint sound of another household still digging next door.
