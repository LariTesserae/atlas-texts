The district is scarcely larger than a courtyard, though it has its own power station, waterworks, and street names. Six buildings stand around a sunken square. Their upper stories lean toward one another, joined by enclosed bridges, cable trays, and thick pipes wrapped in tarred cloth. From below, the sky appears in three narrow pieces. Even at noon, the light reaching the square has the greenish weakness of light at the bottom of a bottle.

Everything is wet at a slightly different rate.

The brickwork darkens from its foundations upward. Water stands in the mortar joints and gathers beneath projecting bricks, where each drop swells for a long time before falling. The metal staircases are slick but not rusty: a dark mineral glaze has formed over their treads. Along the warm pipes, moisture disappears in thin, wavering bands, only to return farther down, where the pipes pass through shadow. The concrete pavement carries a shallow film that moves so slowly its direction is visible only where it bends around a stem or a bolt.

There is no dry route across the square. There are, instead, routes of different depths.

A raised line of paving stones leads from the generating house to the western arcade. Most remain just above the water; two have subsided, and their positions are marked by white ceramic studs on the adjacent wall. Elsewhere, narrow steel walkways cross channels too deep to step into. Their handrails are polished beneath a coating of moisture. Ferns grow through the mesh flooring, making some sections look soft until the grid catches a little light.

The channels were once straight. Their concrete banks still preserve the geometry of the first survey, but the water has made its own adjustments. Corners have become round pools. Outlets have widened into dark mouths. Beneath one bridge, a smooth tongue of deposited silt divides the flow, and a stand of glossy, broad-leaved plants occupies it so completely that their roots now hold the division in place.

The oldest structures are difficult to distinguish from the ground. Brick steps descend into beds of moss. A retaining wall has worn into a shallow cliff, streaked with lime and studded with iron brackets whose original purpose is gone. Where a drain empties against it, the wall has developed a deep vertical hollow with a fluted floor. Water runs down this hollow without splashing. It has been doing so long enough to find a surface that fits it.

The vegetation is vigorous but confined. It fills available seams rather than overwhelming the district. Liverworts spread across ledges. Long fronds hang from the underside of a loading platform. In the windows of the old pattern shop, climbing plants press against reinforced glass from both sides, their leaves flattened into overlapping green palms. A fig-like tree grows inside a disconnected boiler. Its trunk emerges through the inspection hatch, bends under a low beam, and branches along the beam as though the obstruction were part of its anatomy.

There are no birds in it. No insects collect around the lamps. Nothing rustles independently among the leaves.

The absence becomes noticeable only after some time, because the place is loud. Behind the eastern wall, an engine turns with a low, continuous hammering that can be felt through the soles of the feet. Water strikes metal somewhere overhead. A ventilation fan labors in a shaft, each revolution accompanied by a scrape. The square gathers these sounds and returns them from several directions. A small drip beneath the arcade seems close; the next seems to fall inside the wall behind you.

Most of the machinery moves too slowly to watch with satisfaction. A broad wheel in the generating house advances a few inches while water sheets down its paddles. Its axle passes through three separate buildings. In the middle building it drives a pump; in the last, a vertical conveyor whose rubber cups carry black sediment upward, one after another, into a tiled hopper. The machinery is not making goods anymore, if it ever did. It keeps water circulating, lifts what settles, spreads what rises, and supplies enough electricity for the lamps and the patient rotation of the fans.

The lamps are amber bulbs enclosed in heavy glass. Their interiors have clouded with deposits, so they illuminate themselves more clearly than anything around them. Beneath each is a small patch of pavement where algae grow in a precise oval.

Warmth rises from the lower rooms. Through open service grilles comes the smell of heated iron, wet roots, and old oil. The air is mild, almost comfortable, until you pause in a doorway and feel how little it moves. Condensation forms on the cooler walls at shoulder height. Higher up, where the bridges trap the heat, leaves remain dry and dusty on their upper surfaces while their undersides shine.

At the center of the square stands a shallow stone basin, broad enough to have once held a fountain. No pipe feeds it. Its water rises from below.

This happens gradually. The level climbs until it touches a brass mark on the basin’s rim. Then, somewhere under the paving, a valve closes with a heavy knock. For several hours the channels reverse. Water returns toward the buildings, slipping into cellar grates, and the steps of the central basin emerge one at a time. Thin roots trail across them. The exposed stone is warm.

The reversal does not empty the square. It merely changes which surfaces are submerged. Plants on the lower walls fold their leaves flat when the returning water reaches them. Those in the channels stand more upright as the current lessens. By the time the next rise begins, the leaves have opened again.

High on the generating house is a row of flood marks. The lowest bears a date. The others bear only short horizontal cuts, several of them passing through bricks laid over earlier cuts. Whatever once demanded that the water stay below a particular height has been rebuilt to tolerate it. Doors have become windows; windows have become vents. The lowest bridge has no entrances left, but still carries pipes.

Viewed from the western arcade, the whole district fits between two pillars: the basin, the steaming grilles, the leaning walls, the foliage caught among cables. Yet the depth is hard to judge. Every gap contains another surface, another drain, another dimly turning part. Behind a curtain of fern, a glazed door reflects a staircase that is not visible from anywhere in the square.

The water keeps moving. It passes under that door, beneath the stair, through the old foundations. A drop forms on the lowest fern tip, grows heavy, and falls into the channel. The engine continues its uneven stroke. Above, in one of the narrow pieces of sky, the clouds are pale enough to suggest that it may be afternoon.
