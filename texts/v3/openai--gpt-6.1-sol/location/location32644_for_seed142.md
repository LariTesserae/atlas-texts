The city occupies the top of a mountain that has forgotten how to be a mountain.

Its summit is a plain, broad enough that the opposite districts lie beyond the horizon. The last slopes are so long and gentle that they cannot be seen from the streets; only at the outer freight yards does the ground finally begin to fall away. There, a few obsolete rails continue over the rim, bending downward into darkness. Nothing has come up them in a very long time.

The rock is warm. Not hot enough to burn a hand, but warm through its whole thickness, with no cooler layer underneath. Buildings draw heat from their foundations. Doorsteps, roof tiles, the undersides of bridges all have the same dry, bodily temperature. There is no weather to speak of. The air moves slowly from one district to another, carrying a little mineral dust, and returns by routes too broad to feel as wind.

Above is a black sky without a visible ceiling or a visible star.

At first, the darkness makes the place seem smaller than it is. A wall comes within reach; a doorway opens beside it; somewhere ahead, three pale marks indicate the edge of a loading platform. Then the eye begins to collect the weaker lights. Across several miles of open ground, inspection windows shine from the sides of furnaces. Each is no brighter than a coal buried deep in ash. Their arrangement reveals the city: separate clusters of workshops, long residential bars, an elevated conduit crossing an empty square, a low bank of storage vaults. Enormous spaces intervene. You can stand in what was once a crowded commercial district and see only five illuminated windows.

Most light is turned inward. Lamps sit beneath machine housings or at the bottoms of stairwells, where it is needed. Door numbers are cut deep into the stone, their grooves filled with a faintly luminous ceramic. They can be read from a few paces away. From farther off, they disappear.

Everything has been worn down, including the evidence of what did the wearing.

The old ridges of the plateau are smooth swells beneath the paving. Exposed rock has the finish of a tool handle used for generations: dull, close-grained, rounded wherever anything could catch. There are no sharp fragments at the feet of cliffs. There are scarcely cliffs at all. Beyond the western warehouses, a shallow depression occupies the site of an immense quarry. Its floor and sides have settled into one continuous curve. Looking across it, you would never guess that stone was once removed there in blocks larger than houses.

The buildings are younger than the mountain, though not by enough to retain their original shapes. Cornices have dwindled to soft shoulders. Window ledges slope into the walls beneath them. Some older entrances are little more than oval openings in thick masonry. Newer structures stand within their shells, assembled from dark metal plates whose edges were rounded before installation.

There is no paint. Districts are distinguished by the grain of their materials: fine black basalt around the foundries, brown ceramic in the dwellings, a pale, porous stone along the administrative avenue. The pale stone does not make the avenue bright. It merely holds a little more of the scant light.

No gutters run beside the streets. There are no fountains, reservoirs, tanks on roofs, or pipes laid for water. Narrow channels between the paving slabs collect dust. At intervals, a moving brush draws it into a covered slot. The brush passes slowly and makes a sound like a sleeve rubbing against a wall.

Beneath the city, machinery separates the dust into its mineral constituents. Conveyors return them to the appropriate works. Iron enters the rolling houses. Ceramic fines go to the kilns. A particularly dense black powder travels through enclosed chutes toward the district at the plateau’s center, where squat engines turn continuously.

Their operation can be felt more easily than heard. Set a palm against a foundation and there is a faint, evenly spaced pressure in the stone. Loose objects do not rattle. The engines were balanced long ago, and the balance has held.

The factories are still in use. Their output is modest but exact: a bearing to replace a worn bearing, a section of conduit, a door hinge, a slab for a street. Nothing is produced in heaps. There are no warehouses full of goods awaiting demand. Finished pieces move outward on narrow rail carts; the parts they replace return along the same tracks.

A cart might take most of a day to cross the open ground between two buildings. Its steel wheels turn without haste. Far away, their sound is a delicate ticking that stops whenever the track passes through deep dust.

The city is inhabited, although the dwellings offer little evidence of it. Curtains of linked ceramic plates hang inside the doorways. In some rooms, a low table stands within reach of a heated wall. Shelves hold smooth objects fitted to the hand. Footpaths connect the dwellings to the works, but almost no one is visible on them.

At the edge of one square, a figure stands beside a freight signal. It remains there so long that it could be mistaken for part of the installation. Eventually it turns its head. The signal shows the same dim mark before and after.

There is other life, rarer still.

Under the warm conduit housings live small, jointed creatures with overlapping gray plates. They have no eyes that can be found. Once in many nights, one crosses a patch of exposed paving, placing its feet between the grains as carefully as a watchmaker. It stops over a mineral seam and lowers the underside of its body against the stone. Afterward, a faint discoloration remains.

Nothing grows from it. There is no moss in the channels, no roots beneath the slabs, no lichen on the oldest walls. The creatures do not gather, call, or leave nests. A shell found beneath a stair may lie there for centuries before being carried into a dust intake.

The quiet is not complete. A relay clicks inside a locked cabinet. Ceramic settles with a small dry note. Somewhere under the plain, a load passes from one machine to another, sending a single soft tremor through the foundations. Between these sounds are long intervals in which the ear searches too hard and begins to hear its own workings.

The streets have distances posted at their junctions, but no dates. The clocks in the factories show position within a cycle. Their hands return to the same marks. No bell announces the return.

Far out on the summit, beyond the last inhabited district, the paving gives way to bare stone without a step or seam. Low humps extend into the dark. Some contain buried rooms; some are the remaining heights of ridges older than the city. Here and there, a ventilation cap projects from the ground, polished smooth by dust. Warm air issues from it at the same rate, carrying the faint smell of fired clay.

From this distance, the city is a loose constellation near the ground. Its few lights do not flicker. The freight carts are too small to distinguish, and the factories make no visible smoke. Behind it, the mountain’s broad back continues until the eye can no longer separate rock from sky.

There is no dawn to wait for. A hand resting on the stone remains warm. Somewhere within the darkness, a replacement bearing reaches the workshop that ordered it, and the cart begins its journey back.
