The city fits inside the hollow of a seed.

The seed itself lies buried in the oldest part of a root, below the place where the root ceased to grow downward and began, very slowly, to spread. Nothing outside it has moved far in a long time. The surrounding earth has been taken up, grain by grain, into vegetable tissue. What was once stone is now a hard, pale rind threaded with fibers. What was once a slope has become a slight difference in pressure. The root has accommodated the weight above it so completely that there are no longer separate things called ground and burden.

Inside the seed, there is room.

Almost all of it is empty.

A thin living lining follows the inner shell. From this lining, the city projects: shallow terraces, folded partitions, doorways pinched between ribs. Its tallest structures reach no farther inward than the thickness of a fingernail. Beyond them extends the hollow, immense in proportion to the buildings, though the entire place could be covered by a thumb.

There is no light. The darkness does not lift at any hour. Leaves have no faces turned toward the sun, and windows have no reason to open outward. The plants here have inherited another method of feeding. Their buried filaments enter the mineral rind and slowly separate its constituents, passing the useful fractions inward. They have been doing this for so long that the root contains cavities where whole seams of stone once lay.

The living tissue is cool, dry, and slightly resilient. Pressed gently, it gives; held under pressure, it becomes as hard as horn.

There is almost no water. None gathers on the walls, and none falls from the ceiling. The city transports its nourishment in a dense oil that moves through channels too fine to admit a hair. In the cold, that oil advances reluctantly. A portion released from one chamber may take many years to reach the next. Old passages have thickened around it, leaving small amber plugs that the surrounding tissue gradually consumes.

The scent, if something capable of smelling were brought here, would be faint: cut cork, bitter seed, the dust made by rubbing two nutshells together.

But the sound would be overwhelming.

The outer root carries strain down from the huge, dry body of the plant. Somewhere far away, a fiber slips against another fiber. The shell receives the movement, and the hollow enlarges its sound into a deep wooden report. It rolls through the terraces, enters the narrow streets, and returns altered from the far side.

Between these reports there is a continuous rough humming. Thousands of stretched membranes rub at their edges as the root settles. Some produce low tones. Others rasp. One loose partition gives a rapid clatter without moving far enough to be seen, even if there were light to see it by.

The city itself changes scarcely at all. It is the sound of larger, slower changes arriving here.

Along the lower curve of the shell runs a broad avenue. Broad, here, means that two seed husks could lie abreast. Its surface is a compressed layer of dead plant cells, worn smooth by repeated tightening and release. Shallow grooves lead from the avenue into the buildings. These carried oil once, or pollen, or bundles of fiber. Most end in sealed doors.

The doors have no hinges. A strip of tissue was left alive along one edge, and the rest was dried under tension. To open one, the living strip must swell. To close it, the strip draws inward. Several doors have remained half open for so long that their edges have grown into their thresholds.

Beyond them are chambers with careful, unmistakable workmanship.

There are shelves woven from strips of rind. There are low bins lined with overlapping scales. There are bundles tied with single fibers, each knot tightened around a thorn. Some rooms contain wedges cut from old seed coats; others hold combs whose teeth are the stiffened veins of leaves.

Nothing here is metal. Nothing requires fire.

The tools were made from parts of the same plant that supplies the walls. A thorn scores a panel. A strip pulled taut cuts softer tissue. A wedge holds apart a growing seam until the seam hardens around the required opening.

No animal made these things.

Certain branches of the plant once grew fine, jointed tendrils within the hollow. Their tips could grasp and pull. Guided by differences in pressure and by the chemical marks laid down along the shell, they folded rooms, tied fibers, and cleared channels. They worked in the dark, taking decades over a difficult knot.

A few remain attached to the ceilings. They are shriveled now, no thicker than dust caught in a cobweb. One still holds a sliver of rind against an unfinished doorway. The doorway has grown around both the sliver and the tendril. Whatever operation was intended there has become part of the building.

Elsewhere the construction is complete, though completion has taken strange forms. A stair climbs three shallow steps and enters the wall. A bridge crosses a crack that has since closed. A row of storage chambers has been compressed into a single rib, with the mouths of their entrances preserved as faint dents.

The avenues are level because everything that was higher has gradually yielded. Corners have rounded. Roofs have sunk until they rest on the partitions beneath them. Loose fragments have been caught, overgrown, and absorbed. Even the dust is scarce. The lining draws it back into itself.

Along one side of the hollow stands a district of very thin towers. They resemble rolled leaves, though their edges are joined with elaborate rows of fiber stitches. Each encloses a long shaft, and each shaft ends at a different depth in the shell. When the outer root shifts, air is driven through them.

These are the loudest structures in the city.

Some bellow. Some whistle harshly. The smallest makes a piercing note that persists long after the others have quieted. Their pitch changes as the tissue ages, so the sound of the place is never exactly repeated. A tower may take centuries to lower by a single note.

The towers stand far apart. There are no houses clustered around them, only stretches of bare lining with occasional sockets where older structures have disappeared. Across the great central vacancy, their voices meet.

A little growth continues beneath the avenue. There, a pale sheet advances under the dead paving, separating it from the shell. It raises nothing yet. Its leading edge is blunt and soft. Eventually it will reach the nearest terrace and enter the joints between its rooms. Perhaps it will repair them. Perhaps it will lift the whole district loose and fold it flat.

For now, the shelves remain level. The knots hold. The oil moves through its narrow channels.

Deep in the surrounding root, something settles.

The hollow fills with a crash that seems far too large for the small place containing it. The doors tremble in their frames. The unfinished doorway sheds one minute curl of rind.

It rests on the avenue until the living surface, slowly swelling beneath it, takes it in.
