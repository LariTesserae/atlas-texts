Behind the courtyard wall, below the level of the paving, is a chamber full of warm water. From outside, it is easy to miss. Its opening looks like a narrow gap left between two buildings: a rectangle of fierce daylight, a wet coping stone, a little white crust where splashes have dried. Only when you lean over does the depth become apparent.

The chamber is scarcely larger than a small room. Three steps descend along one side, all submerged. The lowest disappears beneath the glare. Across from them, a clay spout projects through the wall and pours continuously, striking the water with enough force to keep that corner white and broken.

Everything below has been put there by hand.

The floor is laid in thick fired tiles, their edges still square, their joints packed with pale mortar. The walls are brick under lime plaster. Even the little ledge around the bottom, which might at first resemble a shelf of sediment, is deliberately shaped: a rounded fillet where wall and floor meet, smoothed with a wet tool so dirt cannot lodge in the angle. There is no exposed earth, no bedrock, no accidental hollow. The water occupies a space made expressly to hold it.

Seen from beneath the surface, the chamber is extraordinarily bright. The opening overhead becomes a trembling white rectangle. Sunlight falls through it and folds over the tiles in broad, moving bands. Every disturbance at the surface redraws the floor. The corners are shaded, but even there the plaster gives back enough light to reveal the grain of sand in its finish.

A few small irregularities survive the brightness: a thumbprint beside the spout, a shallow trowel ridge, one tile set slightly higher than its neighbors. The room is new enough that these marks have not softened. Along the steps, the plaster has hard, clean edges. Water has darkened them but has not yet worn them round.

The incoming water has traveled through a shallow channel across the sunlit roof. It arrives warm. Near the spout it is a little warmer still, and the falling stream carries bubbles down almost to the floor before they loosen and rise. Farther away, the water settles into a slow circulation: outward along the bottom, upward at the opposite wall, back toward the splash at the surface.

Nothing here swims.

A thread of green clings to the mortar beneath the second step. Another patch has established itself behind the spout, where the plaster stays wet even when the water level drops. These are thin beginnings, translucent at their edges. They move when the current reaches them and lie flat again when it passes. Most of the chamber remains bare.

The empty space between these few living patches is conspicuous. A single flake of lime drifts down. A grain of grit crosses a tile, pauses against a joint, then moves another finger’s width. In the bright, shallow water, such small journeys can be followed all the way.

The sound is much larger than the place. The pouring spout fills the walls with a hard, continuous splatter. Beneath the surface, that sharpness becomes a dense rushing vibration, strongest near the falling column. Each burst of bubbles adds a brief rough flutter. There is nowhere in the chamber far enough away for complete quiet.

At the far end, an overflow notch keeps the water at a fixed height. A thin sheet slips over its lip into a covered drain. When the incoming flow increases, the sheet thickens and begins to pulse; its sound joins the spout in a lower, hollow gulping. The chamber’s level rises only slightly. For a few minutes the top step vanishes entirely, then reappears as a wavering pale shape.

The arrangement is simple: a roof channel, a fired-clay inlet, a plastered basin, an outlet set lower than the rim. No mechanism is needed. The height of the spout provides the force. The notch limits the depth. The sun supplies the warmth.

Near midday, the light reaches almost the whole floor. Later it withdraws toward the steps, leaving the opposite wall blue-gray beneath the water. Heat remains after the direct light has gone. The tiles release it slowly, and the inflow continues to stir it through the chamber.

Along the overflow, the first signs of age are already gathering. A fine chalky seam marks the usual waterline. Beneath it, the plaster has acquired a faint slickness. At the foot of the falling stream, a small fan of reddish grit rests between the tiles, washed in from somewhere along the roof channel. The next strong surge lifts its outer grains and spreads them farther across the floor.

For now, though, the basin retains the clarity of something recently finished. Its depth is modest, its boundaries close. From the edge you can see nearly every surface at once. Yet the moving light, the warm circulation, and the loud water pouring into that enclosed space make it difficult to take in as merely a room. The steps descend into a bright little underwater court, with its own currents, its few green footholds, and its steady work of wetting and wearing everything that was made to contain it.
