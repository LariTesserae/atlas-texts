From the upper service road, the whole basin is visible: six kilometers of terraces descending into cloud, their retaining walls dark with moisture, their roofs almost indistinguishable from the gardens they support. Beyond them, another set of terraces rises toward the opposite rim. Nothing in the basin has been left as bare ground. The slopes are concrete; the ravines are drainage channels; even the ridgeline is a succession of covered reservoirs, joined by low buildings with rounded, weathered roofs.

At this height the air stays cool through most of the year. Cloud catches on the upper works and drifts down between the buildings. On some mornings it is impossible to see the other side. On others, the cloud thins just enough to reveal an extraordinary amount of detail: a thousand wet surfaces, bands of foliage, pale pipework, staircases crossing staircases, a train moving along a gallery halfway up the far wall.

The place is old enough that its engineering has acquired the irregularity of terrain.

Concrete lips have worn round under decades of overflow. Mineral deposits hang beneath joints. A spillway once built straight now has a shallow, polished groove along one side, where water found a slightly easier route and enlarged it year after year. Repairs follow these changes rather than erasing them. New patches are shaped around old hollows. Replacement handrails bend to accommodate the roots of trees planted in roof beds long before the current maintenance crews were born.

There is earth here, but it was brought here. It lies in trays, vaults, retaining pockets, and enormous lined beds. Beneath every patch of growth is a membrane, a drain, a slab, and another occupied level.

The vegetation is lavish.

Alder and willow fill the broadest terraces, kept low by wind and pruning. Ferns crowd the damp recesses beside stairwells. Bramble runs over obsolete cable trays. There are beds of cabbage with leaves almost blue in the cold light, and rows of berries under nets beaded with rain. Moss covers the rough aggregate of old walls wherever inspections allow it to remain. In the narrow spaces between greenhouse blocks, climbing plants have grown into thick vertical masses, concealing the steel except where hinges and access panels are regularly cleared.

The greenhouses are not bright glass palaces. Their glazing is clouded, reinforced, streaked by runoff. From outside, the plants within appear as packed, dark shapes. Condensation gathers overhead and falls at irregular intervals, so that even during dry weather the internal paths are wet.

There is scarcely an unoccupied surface. Where a wall carries no plant, it carries a conduit. Where a roof supports no garden, it holds tanks, heat exchangers, bundled ducts, or the rails of a maintenance crane. Under the rails are lockers; under the lockers, drainage access. A gap between buildings proves to be a freight lift. A seemingly empty terrace is marked with flush hatches and the painted outlines of loads that must not be exceeded.

Yet the basin feels open. Its long sightlines run across all this occupation. You can watch a bank of mist move through several districts, obscuring one level after another. You can trace water from the high catchment roofs to the lower settling pools, losing it beneath a building and finding it again two terraces down. Distance is measured in repeating structures, in the gradual disappearance of individual leaves, in signal lights dwindling along the elevated rail.

Water is frequent rather than deep. It lies close to everything.

The paving darkens before rain begins, as moisture condenses on the cold slabs. Thin streams travel along curb channels. Stair treads hold little crescents of water against their raised edges. Behind retaining walls, drainage outlets weep steadily into planted troughs. Most pools are shallow and rectangular, with numbered rims and floating mats of vegetation. Their surfaces can look still while a considerable volume passes beneath.

The basin never quite stops rearranging its water.

A change in the weather above the rim can set an entire district in motion. Gates descend. Pumps reverse. Reservoirs that were full an hour ago expose stained walls, while other tanks rise almost to their overflow slots. Bridges over diversion channels are lifted clear. Wheeled crop beds withdraw beneath shelter roofs. Freight platforms travel sideways to leave emergency drainage routes open.

Much of this happens quietly. The machinery is electric, enclosed, mounted on old vibration isolators. Pumps are heard chiefly through the soles of your feet. Behind a wall, a motor runs with a low note that is difficult to distinguish from wind passing over a pipe opening. Gate mechanisms make a brief, deliberate clicking, then fall silent.

The loudest sound is often a drop striking a hollow stair rail.

This quiet makes the motion more noticeable. Water accelerates through a channel without much warning. A sheet of it spreads across a roof and vanishes into slots. A crane, unattended from where you stand, begins turning above the ferns. Indicator lamps change from amber to green along three levels at once. Far across the basin, a whole row of greenhouse vents opens, each panel lifting a few degrees until the roofs look finely ridged.

The turbulence belongs to the weather and to the work of accommodating it. Cloud pours over the rim in fast, low streams. Rain arrives unevenly: one terrace glistens under a shower while the next is merely damp. Gusts flatten the tall grasses in roof beds, and minutes later cold air settles into the lower galleries, fogging every exposed window.

During heavier storms, the lower routes close in sequence. People know the order. They move their carts upstairs before the warning lights begin, carry loose pots indoors, and check that nothing has been left over a drain. The place is not perpetually in disaster. It is perpetually making room.

Human movement follows narrow, well-used paths through the abundance. Workers in waterproof coats pass beneath hanging leaves, carrying pruning tools or electrical test cases. A small rail vehicle delivers crates to a packing hall whose roof supports a grove of birches. Apartment windows overlook filter beds. Laundry dries slowly in covered recesses warmed by recovered process heat. At shift changes, there are briefly more footsteps and low voices; then the galleries empty again.

Animals are scarce. Screens keep birds out of the growing houses, and most open water has no soft bank on which anything can burrow. Occasionally a wagtail follows a drainage run, stopping on numbered valve covers. Tiny flies hover in the sheltered warmth near a vent. Their presence seems disproportionate: one bird on a railing draws the eye more readily than a moving crane.

Toward evening, the basin darkens without much change of color. The cloud remains gray, the foliage deepens, and the wet concrete loses its last pale reflection. Lamps come on beneath walkways rather than above them. Their light reaches shoes, steps, the edges of channels. Higher surfaces stay dim.

From the rim, the terraces are still moving. Gates adjust in the gloom. A train passes behind a curtain of willow. Somewhere below, unseen water is being transferred uphill to make space for the next rain.

On an old retaining wall beside the service road, ferns grow from a long horizontal seam. Their fronds tremble whenever a pump starts inside. A worker has clipped them back around an inspection plate, leaving the rest untouched. Water beads on the cut stems, gathers, and drops onto the channel cover below.
