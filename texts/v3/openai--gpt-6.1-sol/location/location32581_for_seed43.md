The hollow lies beneath a paved square, shallow enough that daylight reaches its floor but deep enough that the weather arrives altered. Rain comes down as a few hard streams through the roof holes. Wind enters sideways, already warmed by the passages it has traveled. Voices from above lose their words and become a rolling disturbance in the brickwork.

Nothing here is exposed earth. The floor is tile, the walls are brick, the pillars are stacked rings of fired clay. Even the mound surrounding the hollow was built: generations of broken vessels, kiln rubble, and discarded roof tiles laid down in courses and enclosed by retaining walls. If a wall gives way, it reveals another wall, or a packed layer of potsherds with the curved pieces carefully turned to shed water. Digging does not lead out of the made world. It leads into older workmanship.

The whole place would fit beneath a modest meeting hall. From any unobstructed position, one could see almost all of it. There are very few unobstructed positions.

Shelves project from pillars. Small stairs climb behind larger stairs. Drying racks hang across the gaps between balconies, and narrow footbridges connect doors that have gradually become inaccessible by any other route. Clay pipes descend beside ladders; ladders stand inside disused chimneys; a row of wicker cages occupies a landing scarcely wider than a person’s shoulders. The spaces left between these things are the streets.

At the center is a low, roughly circular court, worn smooth by feet and by the dragging of baskets. Its tiles are no longer flat. Each has developed a shallow dip, so that after rain the floor holds hundreds of separate spoonfuls of water. The tiles nearest the steps have been polished to a dark shine. Farther out, their surfaces retain a pale, rough texture that catches husks, feathers, and the fine dust produced by grinding clay.

It is warm here. The walls keep the previous day’s heat through the night, while cooking fires and a little charcoal kiln replenish it. By afternoon, warmth seems to rise from every surface. People leave doors open and sit on thresholds with their work in their laps. Dogs sleep stretched across the least convenient routes. Lizards flatten themselves against the curved sides of storage jars, shifting around them as the light moves.

Light enters through six square openings in the roof. Each is surrounded above by a low curb, and below by a widening shaft lined with pale limewash. The shafts spread the daylight rather than concentrating it. At noon, faces are easy to recognize across the court. The recesses beneath the balconies remain dim, but nothing about the place is permanently nocturnal. A whitewashed board, suspended on cords, can be tilted to throw light into a room. Someone adjusts these boards several times a day, pulling the cords with the same casual attention given to opening a shutter.

There is little standing water. The old communal cistern has been partitioned into three covered tanks, and its former overflow basin now holds baskets of kindling. What moisture remains is scattered: a leaking pipe joint, a wet rag wrapped around the neck of a drinking jar, the dark seam behind a wash shelf, condensation inside a cool storage niche.

The leaks have their own ecology.

Below one elbow in the main water pipe, a cushion of moss grows on a strip of coarse cloth tied around the brickwork. It is only the size of two hands, but it is the greenest thing in the hollow. The cloth is replaced when it rots; the moss is transferred intact. Tiny snails graze its edges. A little farther down, water runs through a trough made from overlapping roof tiles, passes beneath a washing stone, and collects in a bowl where two toads live. Their bowl is not ornamental. They eat the insects attracted by the damp and are left alone.

Most plants grow in vessels too broken for any other use. A fig has been trained sideways along a balcony rail, its roots confined to a deep jar repaired with cord and plaster. Its few leaves are dusty, broad, and carefully kept clear of the laundry. Bitter herbs occupy cups along a kitchen ledge. In the brightest roof shaft, three vines climb a lattice made from split basket handles. Together they produce less shade than one good awning, but their new shoots are watched closely.

Animals are much more numerous than plants.

Pigeons nest in the thick rims of unused drain mouths. Small brown birds pass through the roof openings to search the grain baskets, and leave by routes too quick to follow. Beetles live in the wicker. Moths gather beneath shelves where the air hardly moves. Ants use the plaster seams as roads, disappearing behind a patched wall and emerging, undeterred, on the next level down.

There are hens in a pen beneath the broadest stair, rabbits in cool compartments behind the jars, and several cats whose sleeping places mark the points where warm air leaks from the kiln room. Rats occupy the inaccessible layers of the hollow. Their movements can be heard through the walls after dusk: a dry pattering that travels behind cupboards and stops whenever someone strikes the brick with a spoon.

The place is loud even when nobody is speaking.

Grain falls onto trays with a continuous sandy hiss. A treadle turns a grinding stone. Wooden pestles knock in mortars, each station keeping a slightly different rhythm. The hens complain beneath the stair; the pigeons roll their calls through the empty pipes. A cord-operated bucket scrapes the side of a water shaft. From the kiln room comes the shuffle of fuel being moved and, occasionally, the sharp report of a flawed pot breaking in the heat.

Conversation passes over all of this at full volume. A person upstairs asks for a basket; someone below answers without looking up. Children can be located by the changing echoes of their feet. During a shower, the rain striking the roof becomes so loud that work continues by gesture until the downpour eases.

Yet the hollow is old enough to have settled into its congestion. The narrowest passages are precisely those that need to be narrow. Loads are divided before they reach them. There are depressions in the wall where shoulders habitually turn, and rounded notches where carrying poles have struck the same corner for decades. No stair has its original profile. The steps have become shallow scoops, patched at the fronts and hollowed again at the centers.

Repairs seldom conceal damage. A crack is bound with fiber, a gap plugged with a shaped sherd, a broken tile replaced by whatever will sit level enough. The hollow carries these repairs openly: red clay beside yellow clay, blackened brick beside white plaster, a blue fragment from a glazed bowl embedded in an otherwise plain drain.

Water is admitted at the high side and encouraged toward the low side, except where it is deliberately detained. Smoke follows the warmest shafts. Food is stored above the reach of damp and below the reach of roof birds. Nothing is allowed to block the little channels crossing the court. Even the dogs step over them, though they will sleep in a doorway without hesitation.

A storm briefly changes the place. The roof openings become bright gray squares. Water rattles down the shafts, strikes the stone splash plates, and races through the gutters. People lift baskets onto hooks. The hens crowd together. Children push floating husks along the channels until an adult sends them away from the tank mouths.

Then the rain stops. The drains continue speaking for a while. Warm brick drinks the thin films left on its surface, and the separate puddles in the court shrink back into their tiles. Feet resume their routes. The grinding stone turns again.

By evening, the hollow is almost dry, apart from its familiar wet seams. Behind the pipe elbow, the small patch of moss has taken on a brighter green.
