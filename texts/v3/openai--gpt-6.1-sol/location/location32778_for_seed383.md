The city lies beneath a plain so worn down that the surface has almost no landmarks. Above it are gravel, pale clay, and a few stones too hard to have weathered at the same rate as everything else. Below it is a broad, shallow hollow: not a deep cavern, but a valley with a ceiling.

That ceiling has worn thin in places. Long openings admit the daylight, and smaller holes scatter it across the ground. At noon there is enough light to cast hard shadows beneath the lintels. The limestone takes on a white glare; the streets seem brighter than the sky visible overhead. People shade their eyes with strips of woven hide, though the air is cool enough that their breath occasionally shows in doorways.

The hollow extends for several miles. From the western steps you can see much of the settlement: low walls, isolated clusters of rooms, squares without crowds in them, wide pale intervals crossed by paths. It looks sparsely inhabited until you notice how many of the paths are in use.

There are no trees. There is no grass in the cracks, no root holding a bank together, no green stain on the cistern walls. The builders use stone, bone, fired clay, and cords braided from animal hair. Doors are hide stretched over rib frames. Where a roof is needed, they lay thin limestone slabs across carefully fitted arches. Most courtyards remain open to the cavern ceiling, which already keeps off the weather.

The oldest buildings are difficult to distinguish from the ground. Their corners have rounded away. Doorsteps are shallow bowls; staircases are slopes with faint horizontal interruptions. A wall sometimes continues through three younger houses before ending as a hump in an empty square. There are streets whose names outlasted their paving, and paving that outlasted the streets it was laid for.

Still, this is a city, not a scattering of shelters. Its channels connect. Its public stairs lead somewhere. There are districts for washing, cutting stone, curing hides, and keeping animals away from the sleeping rooms. Along the central avenue, a row of low pillars marks the stalls of a market. Traders set their goods on the broad worn tops: translucent flakes of salt, little bowls, needles, rolled hides, lamp grease, pale eggs packed in powdered stone.

Almost everything can be carried away quickly.

The ground is always rearranging itself.

Not violently, at least not on the scale of a single footstep. Underfoot, the fine limestone dust moves over buried slopes. A terrace subsides by the width of a finger. Farther off, an old chamber loses its roof and leaves a fresh depression in the street. Loose stones stream slowly down a bank that looked level that morning. The city stands on centuries of collapsed rooms, channels, and cavities, all gradually finding less precarious positions.

Across an empty court you may see a slab tilt, slide, and settle. Dust rises around it without much sound. Another slab follows. By evening there may be a new passage between two districts, while yesterday’s shortcut ends at a wall.

The inhabitants watch this motion with an attention that can look like idleness. A mason sits beside a doorstep with one hand resting on its stone. Children lay small bones across cracks and return to see which have fallen. At intervals along the avenues, bowls balance on narrow pegs. When a bowl begins to lean, the nearest household moves its sleeping mats.

The oldest law concerns what you may leave in a doorway. Nothing heavy; nothing tied down; nothing that prevents a neighbor from passing through your house if the street closes. Houses have several exits. There are no cellars.

Construction is done with wedges, mallets, scraping stones, and patient hands. Mortar is used sparingly. A wall that can be dismantled in an afternoon is more valuable than one that will remain upright forever but trap its occupants when the floor beneath it moves. Some families inherit numbered sets of building stones. The numbers are shallow cuts, renewed whenever wear makes them illegible.

Water arrives unevenly.

Rain falls through the openings in the ceiling, striking a few bright patches of the city while leaving everything around them dry. The first drops vanish into dust. Then the hollows fill, and narrow streams begin moving through channels that may have changed course since the last shower. People come out with jars, not running, but moving promptly.

More often, water gathers overhead. The cool ceiling beads with condensation, and certain ledges drip throughout the night. Under these ledges stand tall, narrow vessels with thick rims polished by generations of adjustment. A family may own the right to a drip rather than the ground beneath it. Their jar is moved a little each season as the wet line creeps across the stone.

In the lowest district there is a chain of shallow pools. They are clear, mineral-heavy, and very still. White shelves have grown around their edges, layer by layer, partly enclosing old steps and the bases of walls. In one pool a doorway remains visible below the surface. Small animals shelter in its lintel.

There are animals everywhere, though they do not fill the place with calls.

Long-legged beetles hurry along the shadow edges. Pale crabs inhabit the drainage channels, carrying dust on their backs until the rain washes them clean. Soft-bodied creatures lie beneath lifted paving stones, flattening themselves into almost invisible films. In the high openings, small winged hunters wheel through the light and descend after insects disturbed by a moving bank of rubble.

Most conspicuous are the slate-colored grazers. They are about the size of dogs, with low bodies, broad feet, and blunt, flexible mouths. They move in loose groups across the unbuilt spaces, scraping the limestone with their teeth. What they eat is difficult to see: a faint waxy coating that forms where damp air crosses freshly exposed rock. It has no leaves, no fibers, no visible shape. On a dry wall it disappears; on a wet one it returns overnight.

The grazers follow subsidence. When a street opens into a new hollow, they are often there before the people, feeding on the exposed surfaces. Their hoofless feet make little noise. At rest they tuck their heads against one another’s sides, so that a group resembles a heap of rounded stones.

Much of the city’s food comes from these animals and from the small life gathered in the pools. There are eggs drying on racks, strips of flesh curing in the cool drafts, shellfish kept alive in porous bowls. The settlement smells faintly of wet chalk, hides, and mineral water. Near the cooking courts there is a sharper smell of heated fat. Fires are small and sheltered. Fuel is precious, brought down from the bare upper plain or made from carefully dried scraps.

For all its activity, the city is quiet.

The dust softens footfalls. The rounded walls scatter voices. People use hand signals across the open courts, partly from custom and partly because a shout carries less reliably than a lifted arm. Work produces a few distinct sounds: a scraper drawn over stone, a jar set down, three restrained taps from a mason testing a slab. Occasionally there is a deep settling noise somewhere beyond sight. Everyone pauses long enough to judge its direction.

Then movement resumes.

A market shifts from one row of pillars to another. A herd pours through a gap that was not there yesterday. Two households exchange rooms because one floor has become damp. Children redirect a thread of water with handfuls of clay. On the eastern side, dust is rising from a fresh collapse; on the western side, people are already carrying stones toward a new stair.

Late in the afternoon the daylight turns almost horizontal through the larger roof openings. Every moving grain throws a minute shadow. The bare courts are full of sliding brightness, animal tracks, and the marks of things recently dragged away.

From a distance, the city appears settled into its final shape. Its low roofs and worn streets belong to the same pale surface as the cavern walls. Only when you stay for a while do you begin to see how much is changing: a doorway narrowing, a gutter filling, a line of jars advancing one by one beneath a drifting seam of moisture.

By dusk, the mason has taken his hand from the doorstep. He calls someone over, and together they lift the threshold free.
