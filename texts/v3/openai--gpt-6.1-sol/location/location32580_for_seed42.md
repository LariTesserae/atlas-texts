The town lies beneath a lake on a mountain saddle. Its highest roofs are three arm-lengths below the surface; its lowest street ends in blue water where the saddle drops away. On a clear day, sunlight reaches almost every doorstep. It comes down broad and pale, broken by the waves into moving patches that travel over walls, stairs, and the backs of fish.

There is very little stone to be seen.

The mountain is there, certainly. It holds the lake between two worn summits, and sometimes a bare edge shows through at a threshold: gray, smooth, cold enough to numb a hand. But almost everything above it is alive. Roots sheath the slopes. Moss fills the joints. Great trailing plants descend from the surface and join the growth below, so that the town is anchored both to the mountain and to a loose green ceiling.

From above, it looks like a dark patch in clear water. From within, it has streets.

They are wide for such a small place, and mostly empty. A lane curves between two houses, crosses a level square, then passes under a root arch into another lane. There are perhaps thirty dwellings, although it is difficult to count them: a wall continues into a hedge, the hedge thickens into a workshop, and what seems to be the back of one house turns out to be the front of another. The town has grown around a few careful decisions made long ago. Since then, people have kept those decisions alive.

A forked trunk forms a doorway. Bundles of flexible stems, bent while young and bound with bark cord, make a vaulted roof. The binding has disappeared into the wood in most places, but an occasional frayed end still protrudes. Window openings are held apart with short wooden braces. At their edges the wood has swollen into smooth lips, rubbed pale by hands.

Everything has to be tended, though very little has to be replaced.

The houses grow slowly in the cold water. A branch takes years to become thick enough to obstruct a passage. Once each season someone goes through with a chipped stone blade and a bone wedge, cutting back the tender growth. The cuts are small and clean. Long pale roots are guided into floor mats; tougher ones are persuaded around the rims of sleeping chambers. The residents know which plants will tolerate bending, which must be split, and which will die if their outer bark is scraped.

The oldest walls are wider than a person’s outstretched arms. They are not solid walls. Shrimp live within them. Water passes through dark channels between the roots, and slender fish slip in and out through holes no builder intended. A house has an inside and an outside, but the distinction matters more to its inhabitants than to the lake.

Under some roofs are pockets of air.

You see their surfaces from below as trembling silver ceilings. A stair made of root loops leads up into each pocket; a swimmer can pull through and sit with their head and shoulders above water. There are woven seats there, shelves of dry bark, little cups carved from dense wood. The air smells of wet leaves and people and, faintly, the sharp resin used to seal a roof. In the larger chambers, tubes made from hollow stems climb all the way to the lake’s surface. Their mouths are fastened beneath floating bundles of reeds, where they take in air without taking in much rain.

The tubes must be checked every morning. A beetle nest, a broken tie, a mat of drifting weed can make a room close and sour by evening.

Most of the work happens underwater. People descend with stones tied at their waists, brace a foot beneath a root, and work until they need to rise. They keep their tools on short cords. Dropping a knife into the street is inconvenient; dropping one over the southern edge of town means losing it into the deep basin.

That edge is marked by a low rail of braided willow. Beyond it, the living cover continues down the mountain, but sunlight does not. The roots become thinner, longer, less branched. Far below, pale fish appear and vanish without showing which way they have turned. No one cultivates that slope. When a roof needs a new support, the townspeople grow it inward rather than building farther out.

The central square is broad enough that you can float in it without touching anything.

Its floor is a clipped meadow of water moss, springy under bare feet. The clipping keeps it low but does not make it flat. Generations of footsteps and resting stones have worn shallow hollows into it. Here and there stand single tall plants with feathery crowns, each surrounded by several body-lengths of clear water. Their stems are tethered to the bottom with old woven collars. Young shoots are moved elsewhere before they crowd the square.

This is the town’s greatest expense of labor: preserving open water.

Every surface wants to grow into it. Every loose cutting can take root. A neglected basket becomes a thicket; an abandoned room closes gradually, first at its windows, then across its door. There are two such houses at the western end, recognizable only by their roof curves. Small fish breed in one. The other holds a mass of translucent eggs, each with a dark fleck suspended inside.

Animals are everywhere, although the streets remain spacious. Schools of silver fish turn through the square in bright sheets. Snails rasp the house walls. Broad, dark newts lie along the warmest upper roots, motionless except for their throats. In the moss, tiny shelled creatures scatter ahead of a foot and settle behind it. Larger fish wait beneath the workshops for scraps of peeled stem and soft root. They have learned the sound of a knife striking its cutting block.

Sound carries strangely here, and there is a great deal of it.

You can hear work taking place two streets away: the blunt knock of wood on wood, a stone weight being shifted, the rasp of a saw drawn slowly through a submerged branch. Fish make sudden hard clicks among the roots. Air released from a house climbs past the windows with a hollow gulping sound.

Above it all is the outflow.

At the northern end of the lake, water escapes through a notch in the mountain. The fall itself is outside the town, down a long slope of naked rock, but its vibration travels back through the lakebed and the rooted walls. It gives the place a continuous, heavy roar. In some rooms it is scarcely more than a pressure in the bones. In others, where a thick support reaches deep into a fissure, the walls hum audibly.

People choose their sleeping chambers partly by this sound. A quiet house is not necessarily the best one. Those who grew up near the northern gate find the softer southern rooms difficult to sleep in.

During rain, the surface becomes loud as well. Looking upward, you see thousands of small impacts whiten the water. The mountain summits disappear. The town’s light remains, but loses its direction; roofs, hands, and fish are illuminated evenly in a cool green dusk. Work continues. The rain is several arm-lengths away.

There is no surf down here, no repeated breaking and withdrawal. The current is mild enough that a loose thread takes a long time to decide which way to trail. Only near the northern channel do the leaves lean consistently. Elsewhere they stand nearly still, their tips moving a little when someone passes.

The lake has occupied this saddle for longer than anyone remembers. Its shoreline is rounded and stable. The flooded paths that climb toward the surface have been used so often that their root handholds fit the palm. Above water, they emerge among low trees bent by mountain wind. People go there to dry cord, gather seeds, and work things that cannot be worked wet. From those small landing places they can see distant valleys, pale roads, clouds lodged far below them.

Then they descend again.

The cold is most noticeable on the way down: around the ankles first, then the ribs, then the face. Below the upper leaves, the water clears. A familiar roof comes into view. Someone has hung a basket outside a window to keep it cool. A school of little fish parts around the basket and closes behind it. Across the square, a worker steadies a sapling against a frame, waiting patiently for another person to bring the cord.

Nothing in the town appears new, even where it is still being made. Fresh growth emerges from old walls. Old walls continue into the floor. The floor rises gently into streets that have kept their shape through years of pruning, use, and repair.

At evening, the last sunlight reaches the northern arch and leaves it. The square goes blue. One by one, people climb into the silver pockets beneath their roofs, drawing their baskets and tools up after them.

Outside, the fish keep moving through the open lanes. The moss lifts where a dropped stone has been removed. Water passes through every wall, and the mountain’s distant waterfall goes on sounding through the roots.
