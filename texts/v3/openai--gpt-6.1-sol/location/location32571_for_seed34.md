The city begins before you can distinguish it from the plateau.

From a distance, both are the same pale gray: long horizontal shelves, darker vertical seams, shallow steps ascending into the clouds. Only when the light shifts do the walls separate from the cliffs. A line that seemed to be a fault in the rock becomes a street. A scatter of shadows becomes a thousand doorways. Higher up, where the hillside appears to have broken into narrow ledges, there are terraces, each carrying another row of houses.

Beyond them are more terraces. Beyond those, broad uplands with their own valleys, their own districts of pale stone. Rain is falling on one of these distant districts while sunlight lies across another. The city is large enough that from almost any roof you can see weather that will never reach you.

There are no towers. The buildings spread rather than rise, sharing walls, leaning into the slopes, accumulating in low, close-set ranks. Their roofs are mostly intact: heavy stone slabs pitched just enough to shed water, their edges rounded by centuries of frost. Some have settled until one corner touches the ground. Others have become small elevated marshes, with black soil caught between parapets and a few stiff grasses standing in it.

Water is everywhere, though little of it is deep.

It beads along the underside of lintels. It darkens the lower courses of walls and fills the worn hollows of thresholds. In the streets, it travels through grooves so shallow that the current is visible only when a fleck of grit passes. Whole avenues carry a thin sheet of water over their paving. Beneath that sheet, the stone is greenish, finely pitted, almost soft-looking; above it, the reflected sky breaks around every raised joint.

The plateau holds rain close to its surface. Beneath the masonry lies a bed of tight clay, and beneath that, rock. Water arriving on the upper slopes moves sideways for long distances before finding an edge through which to escape. Every retaining wall has its damp line. Every stair has a lower step that stays wet through the clearest weather.

At the edges of the city, these small currents gather into marshes suspended above the lowlands. Their pools lie between rock ribs, with no reeds tall enough to conceal them. A little sedge grows around the margins. Flat cushions of moss cling where there is shelter from the wind. Most of the ground remains exposed: stone, gravel, wet clay, the dark skins of old mud drying and splitting between rains.

From the outermost terrace, you can look down through a great depth of air. The lower country is often hidden beneath cloud. On clear days it appears in fragments—another slope, the pale bend of a river, a brown plain so far below that it seems to belong to a warmer season. Here, breath is cool even at noon. Shade preserves a night’s chill until late afternoon.

The sunlight is ordinary and generous. It enters empty rooms through square windows, warms the faces of walls, catches on puddles. There is no permanent gloom over the place. After rain, a bright day can make the whole upper city glitter. Yet warmth remains shallow. Turn a loose paving stone and the underside is cold and wet, smelling faintly of mineral earth.

No one lives here now.

There are no tools left in the rooms, no vessels beside the hearths, no doors to knock against their frames. The buildings retain the shape of habitation without its loose contents. Courtyards open into courtyards. Narrow passages lead to communal washing floors, long dry basins, covered recesses whose purpose is no longer evident. Some walls have hand-sized niches at regular intervals. Some rooms are windowless but have carefully sloped floors.

In places, thousands of houses meet around a single sunken square. Its paving has subsided unevenly, and rain gathers there in connected pools. You can cross by following the higher stones, passing through the reflections of doorways on every side. The water is clear enough to show fine sediment arranged in fans around the drains.

The drains still work.

Their openings are small, cut beneath the rims of stairs or at the foot of sloping lanes. Where one has clogged, the water has found another route, sometimes through a house, sometimes down a passage that was once meant for feet. Lower walls have fallen under this persistent soaking. Upper walls remain, with daylight passing through the gaps below them.

Much of the city’s present order depends on these failures. A collapsed roof admits rain into a room; the room becomes a pool; the pool spills across a threshold and keeps a strip of moss alive in the lane. Farther downhill, that same water undermines a corner, carrying grains of mortar into a broad settling basin. There the sediment accumulates slowly, and grass takes root along its firmer edges.

The growth is sparse but exact. Each species occupies a narrow opportunity. Yellow lichens favor the sun-facing parapets. Dark moss follows seepage. Small plants with thick leaves grow in joints where soil is deep enough for roots but too thin to support taller stems. In early summer, their flowers appear close to the stone, easily overlooked until you kneel.

Animals are harder to find. A small gray bird sometimes works along a gutter, searching the wet grit. In the warmer courtyards, insects emerge briefly when sunlight reaches the floor. Their movement draws an occasional bird down from the roofs. By the time a cloud passes over, both are gone.

The place is quiet enough that these appearances seem substantial.

Water mostly slides rather than falls. Where it drops, the distance is short: from a roof edge into a shallow pool, from a wall seam onto a step. You hear individual drops if you stand nearby, then lose them as you move away. The wind travels above the buildings. At street level, it arrives as a cool change against the face, a slight disturbance of reflected windows, a few dry grass stems brushing one another.

But the quiet is never stillness.

Cloud shadows move over entire neighborhoods. Rain advances across the plateau in gray veils. Pools rise, touch, separate. Frost opens a hairline joint during the night, and by afternoon water has entered it. A loosened flake of stone slips from a wall and comes to rest on the pavement. Behind it, a paler surface is exposed.

These changes have had a long time to repeat themselves. The city has lost its sharp edges without losing its streets. Stairways remain passable because broken pieces have lodged among the lower steps. Fallen walls form low banks that hold soil and turn water aside. The plateau and the construction now settle together, each giving the other something to lean against.

Toward evening, sunlight reaches beneath the cloud and enters the west-facing passages. Wet stone becomes silver. The shallow pools brighten until their bottoms disappear in reflection. Far out on the uplands, rain continues over districts too distant to resolve into houses.

Nearby, a thread of water crosses a threshold, bends around a grain of sand, and runs down the next street.
