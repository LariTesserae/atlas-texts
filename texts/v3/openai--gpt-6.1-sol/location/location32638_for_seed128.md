Behind the uppermost rain baffle, there is a space large enough for a person to crouch in, though no one was meant to go there. It lies high in the inhabited structure, above the clouds on most days, tucked between the outer shell and a warm-water return manifold. From outside, it is only a seam beneath an overlapping plate. From inside, it is a little basin, a sloping ledge, and a low ceiling that sweats.

Nothing here is stone.

The floor is a ceramic composite, once pale blue, worn gray wherever water has passed over it for long enough. The ribs along the walls are cast rather than grown. Even the fine grit gathered in the hollows comes from manufactured things: gasket dust, flakes of shielding, tiny glass beads released by an old filter. There is no buried earth beneath it. Under the basin are more plates, then coolant channels, then an insulated void deep enough to hold a building.

But the floor has the shape of terrain now. A shallow ridge divides two drainage routes. Beyond it, a fan of sediment spreads from a split coupling, its smallest channels branching around grains too heavy to carry. One corner has become a shelf of brittle white mineral crust. Another is dark and smooth, polished by the bodies that cross it.

The space is warm. Not sun-warm: the heat comes steadily through the wall, enough to make the air feel recently breathed. Water arriving from higher channels is cooler, and each trickle draws a narrow ribbon of chill across the floor. Small animals gather along these ribbons. When the warm return line changes pressure, the entire basin clouds with vapor, and the ribs overhead disappear for a few seconds behind a low, moving whiteness.

Light comes through the entrance seam as a dull silver blade. The clouds outside brighten and darken it, but almost none of the open sky can be seen. Farther in, two inspection marks glow beneath translucent enamel, one amber and one a failing green. They are not lamps. Their purpose is to show the stress inside the material, and the green mark has dimmed as the plate has slowly settled into its present curve. Together they provide just enough light to distinguish moving legs from shadows.

There are many legs.

At first the basin seems to be covered in loose fragments of dark foil. Then the fragments separate. Flat, six-legged grazers work over the mineral skin, scraping the thin bacterial film with pale mouthparts. Their backs are nearly the same color as the floor. They can fit beneath a flake of enamel with less clearance than a fingernail needs.

In the deeper puddle live transparent, shrimp-shaped animals, each about the length of a grain of rice. Their digestive tracts show as green-brown threads. They swim in short, nervous pulses and settle upside down against the surface tension. Where a pipe crosses close above the water, they hang beneath it in a crowded fringe.

Larger creatures occupy the dry ceiling seams: thumb-sized climbers with gripping toes and blunt, soft heads. They descend to drink, moving one foot at a time through the condensation. Sometimes one stays motionless beside a runnel until a grazer comes within reach. Its strike is almost invisible. Afterwards, its throat moves slowly while water continues to gather around its feet.

None of them needs much vegetation. There is very little to be had. A comb of stiff green filaments grows under the light seam, each blade no taller than a knuckle. Their roots hold to pits in the enamel and catch whatever the water brings. Farther back, a few small translucent fronds open against the amber inspection mark. They spend most of their time folded tightly, and unfold only when the air is wet enough.

The real food of the place is spread too thinly to look lush: microbial varnish, suspended cells, soft colonies tucked inside the porous mineral crust. The return water brings traces of nutrients from gardens several floors below. Heat keeps the colonies active. The animals graze, hunt, molt, die, and are reduced to a film that another animal can eat.

All this happens inside an almost continuous roar.

Outside the shell, wind strikes the baffles hard enough to make the plates hum. Inside, water is forced through a narrow throat in the manifold, producing a heavy, steady rush. The small chamber catches both sounds and holds them. Close to the floor there is a deep vibration felt in the jaw. Near the entrance, the wind breaks into a sharp metallic shriek. Every loose rib contributes its own buzzing note.

A drop falling into the basin cannot be heard. Neither can the rustle of a hundred grazers. A person speaking here would have to put their mouth almost against another person’s ear.

The animals do not depend much on airborne sound. The ceiling hunters feel movement through their toes. The swimmers read disturbances in the water. The grazers communicate by knocking their hard forelimbs against the ceramic, brief paired taps that travel through the floor beneath the machinery’s larger vibration. Put a hand on a dry patch and, after a while, those little knocks become distinguishable: a scattered, busy traffic under the roar.

The basin is old enough that its wear has become orderly. Early leaks once cut new paths across the floor; now they mostly follow the same softened grooves. Mineral deposits narrow a channel, water backs up, and a neighboring groove begins to carry the excess. The white shelf grows at its edge and dissolves beneath, maintaining a small overhang. The low ceiling is stained in overlapping rings from generations of droplets gathering at the same points.

The structure still repairs itself, but not perfectly and not everywhere. Beneath the enamel, layers of active material move heat without a measurable flow of matter. Strained bonds loosen and reset. Hairline cracks close overnight, leaving raised scars that divert water by a millimeter or two. The system restores strength, not finish. It has no reason to remove the puddles, provided their weight remains small and the insulation stays dry.

So the landscape is allowed to persist.

Once in a cycle, the manifold reverses. The roar deepens. Warm water rises through a hole at the back of the basin and spreads over the dark floor, carrying pale flecks of shed shell. Grazers climb the central ridge. The transparent swimmers leave the puddle and disperse through water that, a moment earlier, was inaccessible to them. The green filaments bend flat under the passing sheet.

For several minutes, nearly every exposed surface moves.

Then the pressure falls. Water slips back into its established channels, and the ridge emerges shining. A ceiling hunter comes down to pick through the stranded debris. The fronds by the amber mark unfold. Along the entrance seam, the wind has dried a strip no wider than a finger.

Beyond that strip is the sheer exterior of the structure and a long fall into cloud. Here, under the baffle, there is only a warm ledge, two pools, a mineral shelf, and enough room between closely fitted parts for a crowded life to continue.
