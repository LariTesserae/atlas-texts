The hollow is easy to miss. From the high plain it looks like a darker patch beneath a folded lip of stone, not much wider than a room. There is no path down to it, only a shallow runnel worn into the rock, with footholds where pale mineral knobs have resisted the weather. The last step brings your head below the rim. The wind vanishes. Heat comes up through your soles, and the sound arrives all at once.

A tremendous rattling, clicking, whistling sound lives in the hollow. It is difficult to believe so much noise belongs to somewhere you could cross in thirty paces.

Above it, the plateau stretches nearly level beneath an ordinary blue sky. Its mountains have worn down to long, low shoulders. Even the stones lying loose on the surface are rounded on their undersides, as if they have been sitting in the same places long enough for the ground to wear against them. Far off, beyond a white band of haze, there may be a drop into lower country. Here, elevation is felt mostly in the thinness of the air, in how quickly a cloud’s shadow passes, and in the hard brightness of sunlight on bare rock.

Inside the hollow, the air is warm and thick with smells.

The rock is a reddish gray, rubbed smooth rather than broken. No sharp ledges remain. The walls curve into the floor through shallow shelves and sloping seams, their oldest fractures packed with mineral paste. Some seams contain a black material that softens in the afternoon heat. It has trapped feathers, insect legs, a little silver clasp. Around each embedded thing, the black surface has slowly closed.

There is very little standing water. A thumb-deep pool occupies the lowest place, clear enough that its depth is difficult to judge until a tiny animal walks across the bottom. Elsewhere, moisture exists as beads beneath leaves, dark patches at the roots, a wet shine inside cracks. Rain reaches the plateau only occasionally, but when it does, the whole hollow changes color. Its gray walls become red, the black seams glisten, and narrow passages that looked like solid stone begin to drip.

Between rains, water arrives at dawn.

The underside of the western shelf gathers condensation even when everything around it is warm. Droplets fatten along its edge and fall at irregular intervals onto a mat of roots. Each fall makes a small, precise noise that can sometimes be heard through the clamoring animals. The roots shiver where the water strikes. Nearby insects turn toward it before resuming their work.

The plants grow in islands, leaving most of the floor exposed. Each island is lush enough to seem crowded: glossy leaves overlapping in thick tiers, copper-colored fronds, succulent stems wound around one another, fine flowering vines spilling down the shelves. Between them are expanses of bare stone broad enough for two people to sit without touching anything alive.

One low tree grows against the northern wall. Its trunk is thick and almost horizontal, polished by the passage of bodies. It carries only a few branches, but each branch ends in a dense spray of long leaves that turn their pale undersides toward the afternoon sun. Beneath it, the shade smells of warm fruit and damp bark. Small flowers open along the trunk itself. Their petals are so short that they resemble pores.

Nothing grows casually here. Seedlings appear in profusion after rain, then disappear within days. The surviving plants occupy very particular seams, some no wider than a fingernail. Move a little soil from one side of a seam to the other and it dries into dust. A few inches away, roots remain wet.

There are three built things.

The first is a low seat cut into the rock beside the tree. Its surface has the shallow double hollow of long use, though it is hard to say what shape of body used it.

The second is an upright frame, waist-high, made from a dull blue substance. It has no visible joints. Only one side of it can be seen at a time: walk around it, and the far edge never comes into view. Animals pass through the opening without hesitation. Dust does not. On windy days, a thin crescent of grit accumulates before the frame, each grain hovering just above the floor. By evening, the grit has arranged itself into layers—pale dust over red, heavy black grains underneath—as neatly as sediment laid down over centuries.

The third structure is beneath the dripping shelf.

It resembles a little window set flush into the wall. There is no glass, only an oblong of air that stays faintly blurred. Behind the blur, the stone is farther away than it should be. Sometimes you see a narrow room. Sometimes a pale shaft descending beyond sight. Usually there is only darkness.

The hollow remains warm at night. Beside that window, however, the air is cool enough to raise gooseflesh. Water beads on its lower edge. Small animals gather there to drink, careful never to put a foot across the boundary.

Whatever sustains the difference has continued without visible maintenance for a very long time. Roots cross the frame but do not enter it. A vine has grown around one corner, thickened, died, and been replaced by another vine following the same curve. Under their tangled stems, the blue material is unmarked.

The animals are what make the place difficult to take in.

At first there seem to be only insects, an impossible abundance of them. Amber-backed bodies cover the undersides of leaves. Long-legged creatures stand on the wet mineral seams, so still that their shadows give them away. Beetles with soft, translucent wings push through the flowers on the tree trunk. The soil beneath each plant island is continually lifted and resettled by things moving below it.

Then you notice the larger inhabitants.

Flat, velvet-skinned animals cling inside the wall’s shallow depressions. Their mouths open sideways when a flying insect comes close. A narrow reptile lies along the tree’s upper surface, matching the bark except for the quick blue flash of its eyelids. Under the stone seat, several pale bodies breathe together. One occasionally reaches out a handlike foot, grips the floor, and withdraws.

Above the rim, small winged animals wheel so densely that the sky seems to flicker. They feed on the insects rising from the hollow, then dive into holes around its edge. Their calls are shrill and repetitive. Lower down, concealed animals answer with hollow knocks. Some creature beneath the roots produces a rolling sound like pebbles poured into a metal bowl.

The noise has intervals, although they are hard to learn. An abrupt silence passes across one patch of leaves while another erupts. A predator moves, and the nearest calls stop; farther away, animals take up the gap. At midday, when the stone is hottest, the entire hollow pulses with a deep buzzing that you feel in your teeth.

Yet you can stand in the middle without brushing against anything. The bare spaces stay bare. Even the insects cross them quickly, flying low or following the margins. The concentration of life belongs to the edges, the undersides, the narrow protected places. It leaves an open center, bright and warm, as if a gathering were about to begin.

Late in the afternoon, light reaches beneath the western shelf. For a few minutes the droplets there glow. The little pool reflects a patch of sky, and the blue frame casts a shadow with an extra bar in it, though no such bar spans the opening.

This is when the tree releases its fruit.

They are small, yellow, and nearly weightless. One falls, pauses a little above the rock, then drifts sideways toward a planted seam. Several animals hurry beneath it. Most are after the tiny flies caught in its skin; one carries the fruit itself into a hole. Others remain suspended in the still air until a leaf touches them and draws them into the shade.

As evening approaches, the plateau wind strengthens overhead. You can see grass along the rim bending flat, but the leaves below scarcely stir. The hollow holds its warmth. Its stone gives off the day’s heat, the wet seams darken, and another set of animals begins to emerge.

Outside, the old high plain is already losing color. Inside, there is enough light to watch a pale foot reach from beneath the seat and feel carefully around for something that has fallen there.
