The land is so high that its underside is sometimes visible above it.

Across the valley, beyond the groves, pale cliffs hang in the dimness with their roots exposed. They are not floating. They belong to the same mountain as the ground beneath your feet, but the mountain has not yet decided how to connect its parts. A ledge can continue upward for half a mile, turn slowly through a direction that has no name, and arrive beneath itself. From certain places you can see the foundations of the path you are standing on. From others, only the immense depth where those foundations will eventually be.

There is no summit. There are summit conditions everywhere: thin cold air, brittle surfaces, distances emptied of haze. Yet the valley is broad enough to hold several settlements, and a traverse from one end to the other would take days. The far slopes are visible in such fine detail that they look close. A small grove on the opposite rim shows every fork of every branch. Between here and there, the eye cannot find anything by which to measure the crossing.

The ground is dark, nearly black, interrupted by seams of fresh white mineral. Nothing has rounded its edges. Stones are sharp without being broken. Their faces have the slight inward tension of things still setting.

In sheltered cuts, the rock is softer. Press a thumb against it and the mark remains, although the surface feels cold and dry. Return much later and the print may have deepened into a small architectural recess, complete with a lintel. The mountain takes suggestions badly. It enlarges incidental details and neglects load-bearing ones.

No rain has ever fallen here. There are no old channels, no polished pebbles, no damp hollows. White material gathered at the foot of a cliff is not snow but fine splinters shed by unfinished stone. They cling to boots and hems. Rub them between finger and thumb and they separate into smaller splinters, never into powder.

The plants grow out of this.

Most of the valley is bare, but where vegetation takes hold it is extravagantly full. Groves stand far apart on shelves and broad inclines, each with a dense interior and a sharply defined edge. There is no thinning fringe. The last plant of a grove may have a hundred leaves; one pace beyond it, nothing grows.

From a distance they look blue-green. Nearer, their colors separate into dull jade, bruised violet, and the yellow of old wax. They bear thick leaves arranged in overlapping fans, with pale undersides and dark rims. Some leaves are as long as a person. Others are tightly pleated, packed around a stem like folded garments. Low growth tangles beneath them: curls, branching cords, little stiff cups filled with nothing.

None of it contains sap. A cut stem shows dry chambers and fine internal ribs. Roots enter the unfinished mineral and draw up the strain by which it is becoming solid. The plants spend that strain on making leaves, branches, and more room beneath their canopies. A grove can be lush while possessing almost no substance. Fallen leaves are astonishingly light. Held flat, they conceal the hand; turned edgewise, they are difficult to see at all.

The tallest growth resembles a tree until you try to follow a branch from its tip back to the trunk. Some branches have no point of attachment. They emerge from a close arrangement of smaller branches that, together, seem sufficient to support them. The whole canopy shifts very slowly as the mountain below firms. A branch seen against a cliff in the morning may have moved the width of a finger by the next darkening.

There are no animals. No birds cross the empty spaces between groves. Nothing nests in the folded leaves, and nothing runs when a stone is lifted. The abundance is unvisited. Broken growth remains where it falls, accumulating in tall, airy heaps that can last longer than the shelves supporting them.

It is coldest inside the groves. The leaves take warmth as well as strain, though warmth alone cannot sustain them. Touching one leaves a numb patch on the skin. The outer fans are often edged with a white frost of brittle crystals, but these crystals are dry too. They grow most heavily on the side turned toward an open drop.

Over all this lies an enormous sound.

It is already present when you arrive, so complete that for a while it is hard to identify as noise. The jaw aches. Loose clothing presses against the body in pulses. A cup set on stone creeps toward the edge.

Then individual parts become audible: a deep, continuous wrenching beneath the valley; the flat crack of a cliff accepting another few inches of thickness; a shrill overtone sustained by all the vacant space between the mountain’s parts. There is no echo. Sound does not return from the far wall. It remains wherever it was made, and fresh sound is laid over it.

This is the noise of the land forming.

The changes responsible are almost too slow to observe. A ridge rises less than the thickness of a fingernail in a day. An unsupported shelf takes generations to acquire a base. But the effort is deafening. The gentlest incline contributes a low grinding pressure that can be felt through the soles of the feet. At the edges of the groves, the plants add a thin metallic shriek as their roots hold the young rock open.

The few buildings are built to interrupt this sound.

They stand well apart: a low shelter near a grove, a roofed platform above an exposed seam, three narrow rooms facing a depth that has not yet become a ravine. Their walls are slabs of finished stone carried from the oldest parts of the valley. No mortar binds them. Wooden wedges hold some upright; woven leaf-fiber straps secure the roofs. The work is plain and careful. Every joint can be repaired by hand.

Inside, hangings of dried leaves divide the rooms into cramped pockets. The leaves absorb no sound, but their edges cause it to lose its place. Beyond the second hanging, the roar becomes intermittent. Beyond the third, it may be possible to hear a tool touch a bench.

There are benches, stone hammers, split stalks shaped into levers, trays of wedges sorted by thickness. Beside one doorway lie two ear covers carved from dense mineral, each lined with layers of leaf. There are no signs of a recent occupant, but little here is recent in an ordinary sense. A shelter may stand empty through the entire growth of a grove and still bear a fresh-looking scrape where its door was dragged open.

Paths are marked with upright shards. They follow strips of ground that have become consistent enough to cross. Unmarked ground is not necessarily dangerous; it is unreliable in more modest ways. A long stride may place the foot only a handspan ahead. A level patch may require the effort of climbing. On certain slopes, a dropped object rests where it is, while a person must lean hard uphill to avoid falling.

The markers are therefore close together near unfinished places and far apart on old stone. Their spacing is more useful than the apparent shape of the terrain.

Light comes from narrow openings behind the cliffs. There is no visible sun. A pale luminosity catches the white seams and the undersides of leaves, leaving the open valley dim. Sometimes a distant ledge closes over one of these openings, and an entire slope disappears for years. The groves there do not die. They fold their broad leaves inward and continue working through their roots.

At the valley’s oldest end, there is a small clearing where the stone has fully hardened. The sound is weaker there. The ground bears shallow tool marks, and the surrounding plants are modest, no higher than the knee. Beyond them, the towering fresh growth stands cold and crowded on the raw slopes.

From that clearing, the mountain’s exposed underside can be seen descending by an almost imperceptible degree. One day it will meet the rising valley floor. The gap will close, the sound will diminish, and much of the luxuriant vegetation will have nothing left to feed on.

For now, there is room between them. The groves spread their leaves into it, and the unfinished heights keep bearing down.
