The city stands on a plain that has not finished becoming land.

Beyond its last warehouses, the ground is pale and almost level, extending to a low horizon beneath an ordinary winter sky. There are no cliffs, no old river valleys, no slopes softened by centuries of weather. Here and there a shallow fold lifts the soil a few feet, carrying a whole block of buildings with it. Elsewhere, a new seam opens between two streets. Its edges are clean, without crumbling, and the material inside is the color of cream before it sets.

By afternoon the seam may have closed. By tomorrow there may be another street where it was.

The daylight is clear enough to show everything: the red enamel of electrical cabinets, the green undersides of leaves, a worker’s breath hanging briefly outside a station door. The strangeness does not depend on darkness. At eleven in the morning, with delivery vehicles waiting at an intersection and a woman carrying bread home, a strip of unused ground can thicken into a ridge and push a tramline six inches sideways. The vehicles wait. Someone marks the displacement in yellow chalk. The woman steps over it.

Cold holds the whole region. Not the killing cold of high mountains, but a lowland cold that settles into sleeves and masonry, a cold without much wind. Frost remains on the north sides of buildings until midday. Metal handrails take skin if gripped carelessly. Behind windows, offices are crowded with coats drying on hooks, their wool smelling faintly of leaves.

Yet the vegetation is extravagant.

It fills whatever the buildings leave available, and much that they do not. Thick, dark shrubs climb the supports of loading platforms. Ferns spread beneath parked freight cars. Flat-leaved trees stand shoulder to shoulder in courtyards too narrow for their crowns, so that branches pass through windows and continue growing along the ceilings. Some factories have surrendered their upper floors entirely. Their lower levels remain in production, while overhead, behind broken glass, a dense canopy presses against the roof trusses.

The plants are cold to the touch. Their leaves are firm and fleshy, with a dull blue sheen where the light falls across them. They do not need much free water. Most collect it during the night, when condensation forms beneath every horizontal surface: leaves, eaves, bridges, the bellies of machines. At dawn, the city turns these hidden beads toward the ground. For half an hour there is a small, scattered dripping. Then it stops.

There are no broad rivers. Water persists in overlooked places: a black pool beneath a stairwell, a gutter that stays wet for three weeks, a reservoir occupying the basement of a demolished department store. The pools are deeper than they look. Workers lower measuring rods into them and sometimes bring the rods back polished, their calibration marks removed.

After rain, which is brief and infrequent, every leaf brightens. People hurry to collect the runoff before the new ground draws it down.

From a distance, the city appears continuous. Chimneys, apartment blocks, grain silos, elevated walkways, cooling towers and trees fill the plain from one edge of sight to the other. There is scarcely a bare space large enough to sit in. Even the railway yards are crowded: stacked containers on one side, vegetable plots on the other, saplings growing between the sleepers. Above them run cables in such quantities that they cast a second, finer street plan onto the ground.

It is possible to travel outward for hours and remain among buildings. The districts differ in height and purpose, but not in their occupation of space. A cluster of foundries gives way to workers’ housing, then to greenhouses built around engine sheds, then to another concentration of foundries. Small shops fit into the gaps between structural braces. A pharmacy occupies the hollow beneath a freight ramp. There are bedrooms in the thick walls of a pumping station, and a school with classrooms arranged along both sides of a conveyor belt.

The belt still works. During lessons it moves slowly, carrying pale mineral blocks through the building. The children are forbidden to touch them until they have passed the third inspection gate.

Much of the industry is concerned with keeping the ground usable.

In the stabilization works, great presses descend upon fresh material, giving it weight, grain, and the habit of staying where it is placed. The machines are taller than houses. Their steel feet rest on hundreds of smaller adjustable feet, each of which can withdraw and find purchase again as the floor changes beneath it. Operators watch banks of needles. When one needle begins to turn backward, a team goes down into the foundation chambers.

Other works manufacture joints: sliding couplings for water pipes, expanding collars for columns, rails that can bend without losing their gauge. Roads are laid in overlapping plates. Buildings are assembled in sections with narrow, dark gaps between them. At night those gaps sometimes widen enough to show a lit room on the other side.

The oldest buildings are not necessarily the safest. A newly erected warehouse can follow the ground’s movement with its whole frame. An old brick house may have become too rigid, and split before breakfast.

Still, little of this is loud.

The presses lower without a crash. Freight locomotives slide along their tracks with a soft electrical whine. At a busy junction, a hundred people can cross among moving loads and the strongest sound will be a loose buckle tapping a coat. The vegetation absorbs ordinary noise, but there is more to the quiet than that. Fresh ground takes sound into itself. A dropped wrench makes its first sharp contact and then seems to lose the rest of the ring.

Workers put an ear to unfinished walls to hear whether they are settling. Sometimes, through the cold surface, they hear the accumulated noises of a district: an engine starting, crockery breaking, a crowd leaving a cinema. These sounds arrive weeks late and from the wrong direction.

The animals know the delays better than people do.

Small gray birds gather before a wall begins to speak, placing their feet against it and cocking their heads. Long-bodied rodents nest in the warm insulation around power cables, feeding on seeds and the waxy skins of industrial fruit. There are pale moths in the boiler houses, foxes in the conveyor tunnels, and broad-backed beetles that remain active even on frosted mornings. The beetles favor new cracks. Within minutes of a fissure opening, they arrive from beneath paving stones, carrying fragments of leaves toward the exposed material.

Where the city becomes uncertain, larger animals appear. Deer browse among unfinished columns. Their antlers are flattened, suitable for pushing between closely spaced branches. They move cautiously through the crowded ground floors of abandoned buildings, stepping over wiring and broken furniture. A deer may stand all day in a lift shaft, listening, then leave shortly before the shaft seals itself.

Nothing stays abandoned for long.

A district can alter radically in a week without ever becoming empty. When one factory shifts off its foundations, its workers move production into the neighboring streets. Folding roofs open between buildings. Cables are rerouted overhead. The local trees are pruned into arches to clear a route for machinery. A market forms around the disruption, selling hot food, spare bearings, protective gloves, cut branches, maps.

The maps are dated by the hour.

This continual upheaval has a oddly patient appearance. People stand in orderly lines beside a street that is slowly buckling. A repair crew eats lunch on a platform while the platform rotates beneath them. On a roof, gardeners lift entire beds of shrubs onto skids, preparing to transfer them to the next roof before the two buildings drift apart. Every visible task has another task fitted close against it. Every passage is used. When a gap opens, someone enters it carrying tools.

In the outer districts, the land’s newness is easier to see. Between closely planted shelterbelts lie fields of unweathered ground, broad, faintly gleaming surfaces crossed by the tracks of inspection vehicles. There are no loose stones here. Nothing has yet broken down into gravel. The first roughness comes from roots, which press into the surface and draw up small ridges around themselves.

Low structures stand across these fields: testing sheds, power substations, workers’ hostels, each connected to the city by several routes in case one disappears. Their doors are raised a little above the present ground level. Beside them are stacks of additional steps.

At sunset the windows reflect a long band of yellow light. The cold deepens. Leaves turn slightly, exposing their paler undersides to the coming condensation. Across the industrial plain, lamps come on in offices, kitchens, control rooms, and rooms whose purpose has changed since morning.

From a high walkway, the whole expanse seems almost still. Smoke rises straight from a few chimneys. A train crosses slowly behind the trees. Far away, a line of apartment roofs lifts together, very gently, as if something beneath them has drawn breath.

Down below, a surveyor kneels beside a new curb. She sets a small brass pin into it, checks the time, and waits to see whether it will remain.
