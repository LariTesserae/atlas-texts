The hollow is small enough that, from its center, you could touch almost everything with your voice. A low word would reach the enclosing rock, the roots above it, the black water at your feet. There would be little echo. Too much of the place is soft, wet, or furred.

It lies high on a mountain, tucked beneath the folded lip of a ridge. The open heights are somewhere overhead, but nothing here gives a view of them. The walls lean inward. Shrubs extend from their seams, and the shrubs carry moss, and the moss supports smaller growths whose leaves are scarcely larger than fish scales. What little sky the hollow possesses is a narrow, crooked opening, almost entirely filled by interlocking branches.

The darkness is not the darkness of night. Night would imply a morning somewhere behind it. This is the darkness beneath a stone that has never been turned over.

There is just enough light to distinguish a wet surface from a dry one, though almost nothing is dry.

At first the ground appears to be a single bed of moss. Closer inspection reveals how little ground there actually is. The hollow is crowded with rounded stone shoulders, black pools, root knots, and low shelves of vegetation. Between them run narrow channels filled to their rims. Water occupies every place where stone and root fail to meet exactly. It is present as a skin on the walls, as beads along the underside of leaves, as a thick gloss over exposed wood. The air tastes faintly of it: cold, mineral, already close to becoming liquid.

No bead falls.

Along one wall, hundreds of them hang beneath a fern, each swollen to the same fullness. The fern is bent under their weight. It has always been bent that way.

The stone is old and close-grained, gray where a broken corner shows, nearly black everywhere else. Long erosion has softened it without making it shapeless. There are sockets as smooth as the inside of a mouth, ridges rounded like knuckles, shallow bowls whose lips have worn down to the level of the water they hold. Nothing looks recently broken. Even the fallen stones fit the hollow with the settled intimacy of teeth in a jaw.

The largest pool is barely wider than an outstretched body. Its surface is so still that it seems less like water than a dark opening between two layers of moss. Pale roots descend into it. Below them, visible only when the eye has learned the dimness, is a dense congregation of animals.

Small salamanders lie against the submerged rock. Their bodies overlap without quite touching: slate-colored backs, amber throats, translucent toes. Beneath them are flat snails and cases of aquatic larvae, each case made from a precise selection of grit. The bottom is almost entirely occupied. Where no animal rests, an egg adheres; where no egg adheres, a film of minute life coats the stone.

The water has no empty depth.

Nor does the vegetation. What looked like a patch of shaggy moss is the folded fur of a little mammal, its head buried beneath its own flank. What looked like a fork in a branch is the foot of a bird. There are birds throughout the shrubs, small and broad-bodied, their feathers dark green, brown, or the gray of damp ash. They sit so closely among the leaves that a branch seems to carry two kinds of foliage. Their eyes are open.

In the cracks of the wall, spiders occupy webs so saturated with moisture that the strands resemble tiny roots. Moths lie flat beneath bark. Pale slugs fill the spaces between overlapping leaves. Beetles are wedged into the deep nap of moss, their shells giving back a dull gleam. A larger animal rests against the uphill wall, shaggy and low, with short horns following the curve of its skull. Ferns obscure its legs. Its coat holds the same cold moisture as the rock behind it.

There is no clear boundary between a hiding place and the creature hidden there.

Yet the hollow is quiet. Nothing calls out to claim it. There is no wingbeat, no scramble, no sudden disappearance into cover. Beneath the silence is a faint, continuous sound, too low to identify at first: water sounding inside the stone, perhaps, or the combined warmth of so many living throats. It has neither pulse nor interruption. It is simply another property of the enclosure, like its coolness.

The cold here is gentle but complete. It keeps the leaves small and thick, the stems close to the ground, the animals compact. It lives in the roots and under the coats of fur. There is no sunlit patch to cross into, no sheltered pocket where the air becomes warmer. The whole hollow has reached the same temperature.

There are no paths. No stones have been stacked, no branch cut, no surface made useful. The few objects resembling tools belong to the animals: a sharpened twig held beneath a bird’s foot, a thin flake of stone beside an otterlike creature at the pool’s edge. Both are worn smooth with handling. Neither has altered the place around it.

The unsettling thing is not any single creature or growth. It is the completeness of their arrangement.

A curled leaf supports a clutch of white eggs. Below it, a beetle rests with its mouth against the leaf’s edge. The leaf is not becoming less. The eggs are not approaching hatching. Nearby, the hooked jaw of a larva closes around a smaller creature without finishing the bite. Nothing looks dead; nothing looks interrupted. Each has the composure of a living thing entirely at home in its condition.

Even decay is settled. A fallen limb is soft enough to receive a fingertip, crowded with fungal threads, yet its hollow center remains exactly hollow enough for the animals inside. The moss has reached the limit of every shelf. Roots fill every usable crack. Water stands at every lip without spilling.

You could spend a long time learning how much is here. A face would resolve in the moss, then another beneath a root. A patch of darkness would prove to be feathers. The place would become more crowded as you understood it, though nothing had arrived.

And when there was finally nothing left to discover, the hollow would still be as it was: high in the mountain, densely inhabited, cool and wet beneath its closed canopy, with the full drops held under the fern.
