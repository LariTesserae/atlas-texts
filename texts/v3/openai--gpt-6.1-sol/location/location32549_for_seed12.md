The town lies under warm water, near the summit of a mountain that has not finished becoming stone.

There is no shore. The slopes descend through green dimness, steepening beyond the last houses until they are lost to sight. Far below, beneath the submerged terraces, clouds pass across the mountain’s flanks. On clear days their pale backs can be seen through several hundred feet of water. Lower still is another sea, a colder one, with weather moving across it.

The upper water does not fall.

It gathers around the unfinished mountain as sap gathers around a wound. Where the rock is growing, water clings to it; where the rock hardens, the water slowly releases its hold. This is why the town cannot remain in the same place forever, although no one living has seen it move more than the width of a street.

Everything is warm. The water is warm against the eyelids, warm beneath the tongue. Heat comes up through the ground without boiling or bubbling. In hollows between the houses, it gathers into nearly motionless pools distinguished only by a trembling of the view: a doorway seen through one seems to bend, then straighten as you drift past. The inhabitants plant their most tender gardens around these hollows.

The mountain is pale, with seams of dull rose and yellow. Much of its surface has the softness of wet clay, though it is not clay. A thumb pressed into it leaves a shallow hollow that may remain for years. Deeper down, under the paths, it is firmer. The oldest foundations rest on a blue-gray substance that rings faintly when struck.

New ground appears without upheaval. A rounded swelling rises beside a stair. A narrow ridge develops beneath a garden. Over several seasons, the ridge may become high enough to require a bridge. There are no shattered slopes, no screes of broken rock. Even the steep faces look freshly drawn, their curves uninterrupted except where someone has cut a foothold or tied a rope.

The town occupies three broad shelves on the upper mountain, with smaller neighborhoods tucked into folds between them. From a distance it resembles a thicket caught in the act of arranging itself.

Houses are made of woven stems, broad leaves, and thin plates cut from the firmest rock. The stems remain alive. Their lower ends are buried in planting baskets, and their branches are bent over frames until they join. A roof must be pruned or it will close its own doorway. Walls thicken during the warm season, requiring householders to trim them back with sharpened shell.

Most buildings are low, crowded close together, with narrow passages between their leafy walls. Small courts open unexpectedly in the density: a place for grinding seeds, a communal oven, a nursery where rooted slips hang in rows from a crossbeam. Above each court, long blades of vegetation lean across one another, leaving an irregular chimney through which a little light descends.

The surface is far overhead. It is visible as a dark silver ceiling, but rarely bright enough to cast shadows. Fine mineral particles cloud the water, and a floating forest spreads across much of the summit. Its roots hang down in ropes and fringes. Some reach the highest roofs. Others stop short, swaying above the town like loose ends waiting to be tied.

People do tie them. A useful root is guided around a roof beam, or lowered into a basket of young stone. Over the years it becomes a pillar. There are houses supported partly from below and partly from above, held between the growing mountain and the drifting forest.

Inside, air collects beneath the roofs.

It is brought down from the surface in broad seedpods, their openings plugged with wax. The pods are lashed to stones, hauled through the water, and tipped beneath the ceiling of a room. Air spills upward and stays there. Each house has a shallow upper chamber where people can lift their heads, speak, eat, and sleep on suspended mats with their bodies partly immersed.

The lower rooms are flooded. Workbenches are anchored to the floor; tools hang from loops rather than lying loose. Shelves have retaining lattices. A dropped cup may take a long time to settle, turning gently all the way down.

There are no fires in the houses. Food is cooked in stone-lined recesses where the mountain’s heat is strongest. These ovens are shared by a whole lane. In the evening, people gather around them with covered baskets, waiting for tough root flesh to soften. A meal can take most of a day to prepare. Nobody expects cooking to be hurried.

Movement through the streets is equally patient. Hand ropes run along the walls. At crossroads they gather into smooth wooden rings, polished by generations of palms. People pull themselves from house to garden, from garden to workshop, keeping close to the ground where the water is least likely to carry them sideways. Children wear little stone belts until they learn how much breath to hold.

Above the lanes, the gardens occupy almost every free surface. Soft red fronds spread over low walls. Dark green ribbons grow in beds edged with woven hurdles. Translucent leaf fans face the dim light, opening so slowly that their movement is noticed only by returning the next morning. Some plants bear clusters of milky beads. Others are harvested for fibers, wax, or a sweet paste found inside swollen stems.

A neglected garden becomes an obstruction long before it becomes a ruin. Leaves fill a passage. Roots thread the latch of a door. Eventually neighbors cut a new way around it, and the old house remains within the growth, its roof still holding a pocket of air.

Animals are seldom seen. Tiny pale snails live inside the folds of certain leaves. Once in a while, a narrow silver creature passes beyond the terraces, too far away to make out its fins. The inhabitants stop to watch until it disappears. Their nets usually return empty, so the nets are used mostly for gathering fallen pods.

The town is quiet, but it is not silent. Rope creaks against a wooden ring. A pruning blade clicks shut. Somewhere beneath the paths, a new seam in the mountain gives a low, soft knock.

Voices are heard mainly inside houses, muffled by the water between them. From the street, a room full of people speaking sounds like a hand gently rubbing the mouth of a jar. The air carriers announce their arrival by tapping a roof beam. Three slow knocks, then the faint rush of a pod being emptied.

Beyond the outer gardens there is an unfinished district. Its paths have been laid, but some end in open water where the next shelf is expected to form. Bundles of building stems wait in stone cradles. A few doorframes stand with nothing around them, their leaves growing inward until someone comes to prune them.

Here the view opens across the mountain: gardens on separate ridges, dim houses nested in green, ropes stretching over deep clefts. Warm water fills the whole distance. Far below, a cloud moves slowly out from beneath the town, and daylight rises through the place it has left.

The oldest people sit beside these open paths. They watch for new ground. When a pale swelling becomes firm enough to bear weight, someone descends with a basket, presses a young root into it, and marks the place with a length of cord.

A street may begin there. For now, it is only a plant, a knot, and a small patch of stone still warm enough to yield under the hand.
