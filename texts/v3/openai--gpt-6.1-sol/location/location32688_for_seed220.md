The district lies beneath the city’s lowest foundations, in a hollow scarcely wider than a public square. Its ceiling is so high above the floor that darkness makes the space feel deeper than it is. You can walk from one end to the other in a few minutes, provided you know where the channels cross the paving. You cannot see both ends at once.

Most of the district is empty.

Its urban character survives in the edges: nine levels of galleries, stairheads, loading doors, recessed windows, each following the slight curve of the enclosing wall. Balconies project over the hollow, but nothing hangs from them. The shops have their shutters down. A broad stair descends from a station entrance and finishes halfway to the floor, where a former landing has been taken away. There are street names impressed into the stone beneath layers of mineral scale. All the routes are still named. Very few remain connected.

The floor is a single shallow basin of dark paving, gently tilted toward an opening no larger than a dinner plate. Around that opening the stone has worn into a low funnel. Centuries of drainage have polished its lip, though little water reaches it now.

The air is warm enough that sitting still brings moisture to the back of your neck. Heat comes through the walls rather than from below: the buried city conducts it down its structural ribs, sheds it here, and draws it away again through shafts too narrow to enter. Close to a rib, the air smells faintly of hot iron. Farther out, it smells of limestone, old laundry, and the sweet, earthy odor of roots confined in darkness.

There is almost no light. A few handrails carry a dim brownish glow beneath their translucent surfaces. It shows where to put a hand but scarcely illuminates the hand itself. Across the hollow, the same rails appear as short, disconnected strokes. Sometimes a maintenance fitting opens in a wall and reveals a pale interior. For several seconds, a balcony, a row of sealed windows, and the glossy backs of leaves become visible. Then the fitting closes, and you retain an uncertain arrangement of distances.

The sound is immense.

Every few minutes, a low report travels through the district. It begins somewhere behind the northern wall, rolls across the paving, and rises through the galleries in successive, sharper echoes. Loose shutters chatter. Water in the drain opening wrinkles and jumps. You feel the first part through the soles of your shoes before you hear it.

This is the city adjusting its weight.

Above, buildings are supported on foundations that slowly exchange their loads. Beneath each tower, fields of engineered ceramic carry pressure sideways through the bedrock, passing it from one pier to another as rooms fill, trains arrive, reservoirs empty. The hollow was made to give those exchanges somewhere to occur. Its walls flex by fractions too small to see. Its old paving has been moving with them for so long that the joints are no longer straight.

Between the reports comes a continuous rush, like heavy rain just beyond a window. It belongs to the ventilation system. Air enters along the upper galleries, spirals down, crosses the floor, and leaves through the drain. There are places where it can pull at your coat hard enough to stop you walking. Elsewhere, behind a stair or beneath a balcony, it is still.

Those still places hold the vegetation.

A pale creeper grows out of mortar seams on the eastern side, where condensation is most reliable. Its leaves are small, thick, and almost black. Each leaf lies flat against the wall, taking warmth from the stone. The stems spread along old water stains and seldom cross dry masonry. Over several decades, a plant may reach from one window recess to the next.

At floor level there are two patches of coarse sedge, one beside a broken drinking fountain and one in the sheltered angle of a freight door. Both patches are narrower than a bed. Their roots have raised individual paving blocks, creating small, precious cavities underneath. Beetles live there. So do translucent, long-legged insects that emerge when the air changes and stand motionless with their antennae extended into the draft.

Water arrives irregularly, but there are several ways for it to arrive.

After rain far above, droplets gather on the undersides of the western balconies. They fall singly at first, then in short showers, loud against the paving. A dark strip advances across the basin. The sedge bends under the impact. For an hour or two, the old fountain leaks from its base, although its spout has been dry for generations.

On hotter days, moisture condenses inside the load-bearing ribs and seeps out through their inspection joints. This water is warm and faintly salty. It leaves pale crusts around the joints and shallow, scalloped shelves beneath them. Some shelves resemble stone bowls. Most are empty. One, just above shoulder height, holds enough water to support a skin of brown algae and a few swimming larvae.

There is also water that moves uphill.

You notice it along certain grooves in the masonry: a bead separating from the wet floor, entering a narrow channel, climbing slowly toward the ceiling. The grooves belong to an old recovery system. A field beneath the stone lifts liquid without drawing in air or dust. Where the channel is intact, the water ascends quietly. Where weathering has opened a gap, beads collect, bulge, and drop back to the floor. A fault near the southern stair has been raining on the same square paving stone for years.

The stone has a deep, smooth depression in its center. Around it are older depressions, now dry, marking the system’s gradual retreat.

Much of the district has settled into patterns like this. The polished paths of water, the bowed stairs, the salts spreading around warm seams—all have had time to find durable forms. Nothing looks freshly ruined. Even the detached landing beneath the station has been worn round at its broken edges. It rests against the wall at a slight angle, and a creeper has crossed it.

The largest animals are a few small, blunt-headed lizards. They shelter in the warm freight-door tracks and hunt the insects beneath the sedge. Their eyes reflect the handrail light with a dull copper glint. During the pressure reports, they flatten themselves against the stone. As soon as the echoes fade, they lift their heads and resume watching.

Occasionally a bird comes down a ventilation shaft. You hear its wings before anything else: a frantic clatter moving through the galleries. Most find the upward current again. Some spend a day on a balcony, calling into the hollow, each short cry returning from too many directions.

There are no permanent human inhabitants now, though the services still admit people. A working lift descends to the third gallery twice each day. Its doors open whether anyone is inside or not. A square of white cabin light falls across the opposite wall, briefly revealing a pharmacy sign, its raised letters softened by deposits. The lift waits, shuts, and rises.

Maintenance workers sometimes cross the basin in pairs. They carry their tools close to their bodies because the ventilation can catch a dangling strap. They talk into throat microphones; ordinary speech is swallowed by the air and struck apart by the echoes. Their lights reveal surprisingly little. A bright circle on a wall makes the rest of the hollow darker, and above it the balconies continue beyond the beam.

Near the drainage opening, a brass plaque remains legible if you rub the condensation away. It gives the maximum permitted occupancy of the district, instructions for evacuation, and the telephone number of an office that no longer exists. Beneath it is a newer ceramic strip, installed by someone still responsible for the place.

It says to wait on the raised paving when the drain begins to whistle.

Usually the drain only breathes: a deep, continuous intake, edged with small clicking sounds from grit striking its interior. Before a transfer cycle, the pitch rises. The air across the floor accelerates. Dust slips out of the dry joints and runs in narrow streams toward the center.

Then the intake stops.

For a moment the hollow becomes quiet enough to hear the warm water dripping from the southern wall. Moisture clouds form close to the floor. The lizards have already withdrawn. The leaves lie hard against their stones.

The drain exhales once, with enough force to lift every loose thing in the basin.

It is over in seconds. Grit settles. A few leaves turn slowly through the dark. Water trembles on the shelves, spills over their rims, and begins its familiar routes downward—or, where the recovery grooves still work, upward into the wall.

The ventilation resumes. Somewhere high above, behind all the sealed shopfronts and empty rooms, the next load exchange begins to gather.
